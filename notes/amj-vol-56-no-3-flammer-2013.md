# Corporate Social Responsibility and Shareholder Reaction: The Environmental Awareness of Investors

**Abstract**
This study examines whether shareholders are sensitive to corporations’ environmental footprint. Specifically, I conduct an event study around the announcement of corporate news related to environment for all US publicly traded companies from 1980 to 2009. In keeping with the view that environmental corporate social responsibility (CSR) generates new and competitive resources for firms, I find that companies reported to behave responsibly toward the environment experience a significant stock price increase, whereas firms that behave irresponsibly face a significant decrease. Extending this view of “environment-as-a-resource,” I posit that the value of environmental CSR depends on external and internal moderators. First, I argue that external pressure to behave responsibly towards the environment―which has increased dramatically over recent decades―exacerbates the punishment for eco-harmful behavior and reduces the reward for eco-friendly initiatives. This argument is supported by the data: over time, the negative stock market reaction to eco-harmful behavior has increased, while the positive reaction to eco-friendly initiatives has decreased. Second, I argue that environmental CSR is a resource with decreasing marginal returns and insurance-like features. In keeping with this view, I find that the positive (negative) stock market reaction to eco-friendly (-harmful) events is smaller for companies with higher levels of environmental CSR.

**Research Question**
Do shareholders reward eco-friendly corporate initiatives and penalize eco-harmful behavior, and how have these reactions changed over time? How do firms’ existing environmental strengths and concerns relate to these reactions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Shareholders react positively to announcements of eco-friendly corporate initiatives.
- H2: Shareholders react negatively to announcements of eco-harmful corporate events.
- H3: Shareholders’ negative reaction to eco-harmful events increases over time.
- H4: Shareholders’ positive reaction to eco-friendly initiatives decreases over time.
- H5a: Stronger environmental performance is associated with a less positive shareholder reaction to eco-friendly initiatives.
- H5b: More severe environmental concerns are associated with a more positive shareholder reaction to eco-friendly initiatives.
- H6a: Stronger environmental performance is associated with a less negative shareholder reaction to eco-harmful behavior.
- H6b: More severe environmental concerns are associated with a more negative shareholder reaction to eco-harmful events.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Announcements of eco-friendly corporate initiatives or eco-harmful corporate behavior, analyzed separately.
- DV(s): Shareholder reaction, measured as cumulative abnormal stock returns (CARs) around announcements.
- Mediators: None tested; competitive resources, reputation, goodwill, and trust provide theoretical explanations.
- Moderators: External pressure to become green, proxied by time; internal environmental strengths and concerns, measured by lagged KLD indexes.

Environmental CSR is theorized to generate competitive resources, whereas environmental harm can waste resources, damage reputation, and incur legal and cleanup costs. As going green becomes an institutional norm, eco-friendly initiatives offer less differentiation and eco-harmful behavior attracts stronger punishment. Existing environmental resources are proposed to exhibit decreasing marginal returns while providing insurance-like protection against adverse events. The empirical tests examine announcement returns and their correlates, rather than directly testing these mediating mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
Factiva searches identified WSJ environmental news from January 1, 1980, through December 31, 2009, matched to CRSP stock returns. After excluding mixed friendly/harmful reports, articles mentioning other significant activities, firms not traded on a US stock market, unavailable estimation-period returns, and previously published articles, the sample contains 273 events: 117 eco-friendly and 156 eco-harmful. These counts are announcements, not unique firms.

Day 0 is WSJ publication; the main event window is (−1, 0). Appendix B estimates each firm’s market model by OLS over 200 trading days, (−240, −41), using the equally weighted CRSP market portfolio. Actual daily returns minus model-predicted returns yield abnormal returns, which are summed within the event window to obtain firm-event CARs; average CARs are then calculated separately by event type. This observational event-study design examines short-run market reactions, without directly identifying the causal effect of external pressure.

The external-pressure proxy is a calendar-year trend. Separate KLD environmental-strengths and environmental-concerns indexes each add one point per qualifying item, range from 0 to 7, and are lagged one year; their coverage reduces the friendly/harmful samples to 55/47 events. Firm controls are log total assets, log years since first Compustat coverage, net income/total assets, market equity/book equity, and log analyst count from IBES. Table 5 progressively adds SIC-division and environmental-issue fixed effects; Table 6 omits these fixed effects because of the smaller KLD samples. Regressions use heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors.

**Key Findings**
- H1 and H2 are supported: Table 2 reports average two-day CARs of +0.84% for eco-friendly events (z = 3.57) and −0.65% for eco-harmful events (z = −3.49), both p < .01. CARs in the reported windows before and after the event window are nonsignificant.
- H3 and H4 are supported by the time patterns. In Table 4, harmful-event CAR changes from −0.42% in 1980–89 (nonsignificant) to −1.12% in 2000–09 (p < .01); friendly-event CAR changes from 1.19% (p < .01) to 0.68% (only p < .10). Table 5 model 3 estimates negative time-trend slopes of −0.049 and −0.026 percentage points per year for harmful and friendly events, respectively (both p < .01).
- Table 6 model 2 yields the predicted directions, with important significance qualifications. For eco-friendly events, environmental strengths predict lower CARs (b = −0.206, p < .10; H5a) and concerns predict higher CARs (b = 0.128, p < .10; H5b). For eco-harmful events, strengths predict less negative CARs (b = 0.259, p < .10; H6a), whereas concerns predict more negative CARs (b = −0.286, p < .05; H6b). Thus H5a, H5b, and H6a receive only marginal support; H6b is supported at the .05 level.
- Table 3 retains the positive/negative announcement pattern under dependence adjustment, precision weighting, excluding Exxon or confounding announcements, alternative asset-pricing models, industry adjustment, and longer windows. Precision weakens for the eco-friendly (−1, 3) window: CAR = 0.66%, p < .10.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends environment-as-a-resource reasoning by connecting its stock-market value to external institutional norms and firms’ existing environmental resources. Its time-series comparison and KLD analyses provide evidence consistent with diminishing rewards for green initiatives, increasing penalties for environmental harm, and insurance-like features of environmental CSR, while the KLD evidence is mostly marginal.

**Practical Implication**
The author suggests that managers and boards may find it worthwhile to design environmental CSR policies and environmental innovation/R&D programs in pursuing long-term objectives, given shareholders’ favorable short-run response to eco-friendly behavior. Legislators may also benefit from coordinating environmental regulations with companies because shareholders and legislators appear to share basic environmental views.

**Limitations**
The event study addresses short-run stock-market reactions, leaving long-run shareholder value and operating performance unresolved. The author also notes difficulty constructing a comparable measure of event severity within environmental-issue categories, possible power loss from omitted keyword-search results, and changing KLD item coverage; an alternative index restricted to consistently surveyed items produces similar results.

**Future Research**
The author proposes studying long-run firm value and performance while addressing CSR’s likely endogeneity. Further research could examine whether the framework extends to CSR generally, including changes in shareholder perceptions over time and decreasing marginal returns.
