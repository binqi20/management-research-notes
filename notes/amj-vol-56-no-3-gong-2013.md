# A Multilevel Model of Team Goal Orientation, Information Exchange, and Creativity

**Abstract**
Adopting a multilevel approach, we examined how team goal orientation may relate to team creativity and individual creativity. We also theorized and examined the bottom-up process linking individual creativity and team creativity. Multisource data were collected from 485 members and their leaders within 100 R&D teams. The results indicated that a team learning goal and team performance approach goal were positively related—whereas a team performance avoidance goal was negatively related—to both team creativity and individual creativity through team information exchange. Furthermore, a trust relationship with a team leader played a moderating role: when the trust was stronger, the indirect positive relationship with team creativity and individual creativity was stronger for the team learning goal but weaker for the team performance approach goal. We also found that average individual creativity within a team was positively related to team creativity (going above and beyond the effect of team information exchange) through a supportive climate for creativity.

**Research Question**
How does team goal orientation relate to team and individual creativity through information exchange, and how does trust in the team leader condition these relationships? How is individual creativity related to team creativity through a supportive climate for creativity, beyond team information exchange?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Team learning goal has an indirect positive relationship with team and individual creativity through team information exchange.
- H2: Team performance approach goal has an indirect positive relationship with team and individual creativity through team information exchange.
- H3: Team performance avoidance goal has an indirect negative relationship with team and individual creativity through team information exchange.
- H4: A stronger trust relationship with the team leader strengthens the indirect relationships between team learning goal and both creativity outcomes through team information exchange.
- H5: A stronger trust relationship with the team leader weakens the indirect relationships between team performance approach goal and both creativity outcomes through team information exchange.
- H6: Average individual creativity is positively related to team creativity through a supportive climate for creativity, beyond the relationship explained by team information exchange.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team learning, performance approach, and performance avoidance goals; average individual creativity in the bottom-up model.
- DV(s): Team creativity and individual creativity; team creativity in the bottom-up model.
- Mediators: Team information exchange for the goal-orientation relationships; supportive climate for creativity for the individual-to-team creativity relationship.
- Moderators: Affect-based trust relationship with the team leader, strengthening the learning-goal pathway and weakening the performance-approach pathway at the information-exchange stage.

Shared learning goals motivate acquiring and exchanging knowledge, while shared performance approach goals motivate exchange in pursuit of collective success; avoidance goals discourage exchange because exposing ideas or knowledge gaps risks negative evaluation. Trust is theorized to release learning-oriented teams' willingness to exchange ideas, but to reduce externally motivated striving in performance-approach-oriented teams. Information exchange supplies knowledge and perspectives for creativity at both levels. In the bottom-up argument, members' creative acts provide social cues that establish expectations and support for creativity, enabling collective creative endeavors; these are proposed mechanisms tested with cross-sectional associations.

**Data & Measures**
The main sample comprised 485 completed member-leader surveys from 564 distributed surveys (86%), covering 100 R&D teams in 19 South Korean companies. Members reported team goals, information exchange, trust, and supportive climate; leaders rated both creativity outcomes. Collection dates were not reported. A separate validation sample of 206 members in 45 R&D teams from ten Korean companies examined the information-exchange measure, rather than testing the substantive hypotheses.

Team learning, performance approach, and performance avoidance goals used referent-shifted VandeWalle measures with five, four, and four items, respectively. Within-team information exchange used two adapted items concerning exchanging information, learning from one another, and exchanging ideas to solve problems; trust used McAllister's five-item affect-based scale. Supportive climate used Anderson and West's eight-item innovation-climate scale; reported analyses retained that full scale, with substantively identical results using its five creativity-focused items. Team creativity used Shin and Zhou's four-item leader rating relative to similar R&D teams on a seven-point scale; individual creativity used Zhou and George's scale on a five-point scale. Member reports of team constructs were aggregated after agreement and ICC checks; the bottom-up predictor averaged leader-rated individual creativity within teams, with the maximum member score examined separately.

HLM models nested teams within organizations, or individuals within teams within organizations, with an organization-level intercept. Individual-creativity models controlled gender, organizational tenure, education, team size, team tenure, task interdependence, and R&D task indicators; team-outcome models additionally listed team face saving. Bottom-up models also controlled within-team dispersion of individual creativity, labeled its standard deviation in Table 3. Indirect relationships multiplied the component regression coefficients and used 5,000 bootstrap samples for asymmetric confidence intervals; moderated path analysis tested conditional indirect relationships. The design is associational.

**Key Findings**
- H1–H3 were supported. Learning, approach, and avoidance goals were related to information exchange with γ = .54, .21, and −.20, respectively (Table 2, Model 1; all p < .01). Their indirect 99% confidence intervals were, respectively, [.19, .28], [.06, .13], and [−.11, −.07] for team creativity, and [.06, .17], [.01, .10], and [−.07, −.02] for individual creativity. Information exchange was positively related to individual creativity (γ = .22, p < .05; Table 4, Model 2). For team creativity, γ = .40: Table 3, Model 2 marks p < .05, whereas the p. 837 prose reports p < .01.
- H4 was supported. Learning goal × trust positively predicted information exchange (γ = .30, p < .05; Table 2, Model 3). The reported conditional indirect relationship with team creativity was significant at high trust but nonsignificant at low trust; the individual-creativity relationship was significant at both levels and stronger at high trust. Both high-versus-low contrasts were significant.
- H5 received partial support. Approach goal × trust negatively predicted information exchange (γ = −.32, p < .05). The conditional indirect relationship with team creativity was significant and positive at low trust but nonsignificant at high trust. For individual creativity, neither conditional relationship was significant, although their difference was significant; the interaction's indirect 99% intervals excluded zero for both outcomes: [−.37, −.01] for team creativity and [−.12, −.01] for individual creativity. Avoidance goal × trust was nonsignificant (γ = −.06; Table 2, Model 3).
- H6 was supported. Average individual creativity was positively associated with supportive climate (γ = .38, p < .01), and climate with team creativity after controlling information exchange (γ = .61, p < .01; Table 3, Model 4); the indirect 99% interval was [.04, .42]. Using the maximum member-creativity score also yielded a positive indirect relationship through climate, but only partial mediation (99% interval [.02, .09]).
- Supplementary quadratic terms for all three team goal orientations were nonsignificant. Results were substantively identical after excluding teams with low agreement on the relevant goal-orientation measure and when using the five-item climate measure.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends goal orientation theory and the componential model of creativity to team and cross-level relationships, identifying information exchange as an explanatory process and leader trust as a boundary condition with different implications for learning and performance approach goals. Its positive linear learning-goal relationship with creativity contrasts with prior efficiency-based findings, suggesting that outcome type matters. Social information processing theory explains a bottom-up climate pathway, treating team creativity as a distinct collective outcome rather than simply average individual creativity.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that managers foster team learning goals through leadership, assigned objectives, and recognition, cultivate trust to support learning-oriented exchange, encourage performance approach goals, and avoid performance avoidance goals. Managers may provide platforms for exchanging ideas and knowledge to support creativity at both individual and team levels. They should consider the intended outcome, because a strong learning goal does not necessarily benefit efficiency-based performance.

**Limitations**
The cross-sectional design cannot establish causality or exclude reverse relationships, and the high learning–approach goal correlation prevented examining their interplay. The study lacked team-role information for role-weighted aggregation, used an innovation-climate scale, and did not measure novelty and usefulness separately. South Korea's power distance, collectivism, and ongoing formal team leadership may condition the observed relationships and the role of trust.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining reciprocal relationships, combinations and diversity of goal orientations, and whether information exchange, error discussion, and experimentation have different mediating roles. Further work could test role-weighted bottom-up aggregation, alternative mechanisms such as cognitive stimulation, and organizational learning behavior as a possible moderator of nonlinear learning-goal relationships. They also call for refined creativity/climate measurement and replication in other cultures.
