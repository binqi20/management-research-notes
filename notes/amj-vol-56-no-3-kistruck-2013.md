# Mitigating Principal-Agent Problems in Base-of-the-Pyramid Markets: An Identity Spillover Perspective

**Abstract**
The potential for profitably distributing products to previously underserved “base-of-the-pyramid” (BOP) markets as a means of poverty alleviation has received growing interest within the management field. However, such business models often struggle with the agency costs that arise between the firm and local sales agents as the institutions and infrastructure in BOP markets make traditional contractual and monitoring mechanisms difficult and expensive to employ. We present the results of two complementary studies which were both conducted with salespeople in rural Guatemala. The first study employed a quasi-experimental field-study combined with in-depth interviews, while the second study was a laboratory experiment. The results of the studies suggest that identity-based mechanisms can potentially mitigate agency costs through a positive identity spillover effect in multiproduct settings.

**Research Question**
Can assuming ownership of one product mitigate principal-agent problems in BOP markets by increasing sales of both that product and other nonowned products? Does functional product similarity cue an ownership identity that spills over across products?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1 (Study 1): Arrangements in which BOP salespeople assume ownership of a product produce higher sales of that product than arrangements without ownership.
- H2a (Study 1, agency-theory prediction): Ownership of one product produces lower sales of other nonowned products than arrangements without ownership of that product.
- H2b (Study 1, competing identity-theory prediction): Ownership of one product produces higher sales of other nonowned products than arrangements without ownership of that product.
- H3 (Study 1): Ownership of one product is more likely to increase sales of similar nonowned products than sales of dissimilar nonowned products.
- H4 (Study 2): When a salesperson assumes ownership of one product, identity spillover is more likely for a similar than a dissimilar product.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Ownership of eye drops rather than consignment in Study 1; an experimentally induced ownership experience with Lifestraw in Study 2.
- DV(s): Sales quantities of owned and nonowned products in Study 1; ownership identity ratings for other, consigned products in Study 2.
- Mediators: Ownership identity salience and spillover are the proposed psychological mechanism; the studies do not estimate formal mediation of sales outcomes.
- Moderators: Functional similarity between the owned product and nonowned products.

The authors argue that product ownership can create an owner identity that is more prestigious and salient than an agent identity in BOP markets. Similar nonowned products act as nonsocial cues that sustain this identity and associated responsibility and effort across tasks. This predicts positive spillover, competing with agency theory's prediction that stronger incentives for one product divert effort from others. Study 1 examines sales and qualitative accounts of the process; Study 2 directly examines identity ratings under experimental ownership cues.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 involved salespeople in six rural Guatemalan regions served by one US-based multinational organization. Geographic treatment allocation was nonrandom: 37 salespeople initially participated, four left the organization and two treatment salespeople could not afford the required purchases, leaving 31. Treatment salespeople bought eye drops up front at 22 quetzales rather than the 27-quetzal consignment price; all other products remained on consignment. Outcomes were average monthly quantities sold per salesperson during January–April 2010, with corresponding September–December 2009 sales as baseline covariates. Nonowned products were divided into vision-related products, such as glasses and accessories, and non-vision-related products, such as water purifiers, light bulbs and seeds. ANCOVAs also controlled for prior business ownership, villages lived in, organizational tenure in months and trust in the organization. Follow-up interviews covered the 31 salespeople, six regional managers and country director.

Study 2 randomly assigned 32 consignment salespeople from the same organization to ownership (17) or control (15); participants were former Study 1 controls or newly recruited salespeople. Treatment combined handling and wearing Lifestraw with verbal cues about upfront payment, responsibility, loss, control and higher margins; controls viewed Lifestraw under a consignment description. Both groups evaluated consigned Germ-X hand sanitizer and Body-Guard pepper spray in counterbalanced order, responding about how a typical Guatemalan salesperson would react. Ownership identity used six five-point items covering time dedication, responsibility, control, motivation, damage responsibility and theft concern (α = .71; Appendix A). A single five-point similarity item confirmed that Germ-X was perceived as more similar to Lifestraw than Body-Guard. Mixed ANOVA combined ownership condition between participants with product similarity within participants; realized sales were not measured in this experiment.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: Eye-drop sales were higher in treatment than control (monthly means 8.15 versus 4.49 bottles; F = 4.39, p < .05), supporting H1. All nonowned-product sales were also higher (16.93 versus 10.90 units; F = 4.48, p < .05), supporting H2b and not H2a. Means are from Table 1 and ANCOVA tests from Table 3; the p. 669 prose labels both nonowned-product groups as treatment, whereas Table 1 identifies 10.90 as the control mean.
- Study 1: Vision-related nonowned-product sales were higher under treatment (10.43 versus 6.49 units per month; F = 4.70, p < .05), whereas the non-vision-related difference was nonsignificant (6.35 versus 4.45; F = 1.47, p = .238). The authors interpret these separate comparisons as supporting H3. Interviews suggested increased effort, responsibility and protection of products, while also raising cross-selling as a possible alternative or complementary explanation.
- Study 2: The ownership-by-similarity interaction supported H4 (F[1, 30] = 6.11, p = .019; Figure 1). Ownership identity ratings for Germ-X were higher under ownership than control (3.91 versus 3.56; t[30] = 2.29, p = .029), but Body-Guard ratings did not differ (3.71 in both groups; t < 1). Neither the overall ownership effect (F[1, 30] = 1.36, p = .253) nor the overall similarity effect (F[1, 30] = .12, p = .733) was significant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
Integrating agency and identity theories, the paper challenges the assumption that agents respond to each task's incentives in isolation: an owner identity associated with one product can inform responses to other, similar products. Functional similarity offers a contingency for incentive-incompatibility arguments. The paper also extends identity theory by identifying nonsocial environmental cues, including physical products, that can activate one identity over another.

**Practical Implication**
For organizations pursuing BOP markets, the authors suggest that creating ownership identity around one product line may mitigate agency concerns across similar product lines and offer a potentially less expensive alternative to monitoring and contractual incentives. They caution such organizations to limit product scope when scaling rather than diversify extensively; the studies do not establish cost savings directly.

**Limitations**
Both studies use relatively small samples associated with one organization in a single BOP market, limiting claims about other organizations and settings. Study 1 cannot fully randomize geographic groups, lacks prior-year seasonal sales controls and leaves cross-selling as an alternative explanation for higher sales of similar products. Neither study establishes that the created identity remains effective in changing behavior over years.

**Future Research**
The authors call for further study in other BOP organizations and markets and in developed markets, where formal business ownership is more common and owner–agent status differences may be less salient. They also identify the long-term persistence of identity-related behavioral effects as unresolved.
