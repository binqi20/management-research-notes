# Apprentice, Departure, and Demotion: An Examination of the Three Types of CEO–Board Chair Separation

**Abstract**
Past research has consistently shown that separation of CEO and board chair roles has no systematic effect on firm performance. In this study, we introduce the different forms of such separation: apprentice, departure, and demotion. In a study of Standard & Poor’s (S&P) 1500 and Fortune 1000 firms, we find that separation of the two leadership roles positively impacts future firm performance when current performance is poor, but negatively impacts future firm performance when current performance is high. We find that this effect is most dramatic for demotion separations. Finally, we test theory about the permanency of CEO–board chair separation. Our results show that the different types of CEO–board chair separation have very distinct consequences.

**Research Question**
How do current firm performance and the type of CEO–board chair separation condition its subsequent performance consequences? How do separation type and postseparation performance relate to the likelihood of recombining the two roles?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Separation decreases future performance when current firm performance is good, but increases it when current performance is poor.
- H2a: The interaction of separation with current performance in predicting future performance is stronger for demotion than apprentice separations.
- H2b: This interaction is stronger for demotion than departure separations.
- H3a: Recombination is more likely following apprentice than departure separations.
- H3b: Recombination is more likely following apprentice than demotion separations.
- H4: The likelihood that a departure separation leads to recombination increases with postseparation firm performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO–board chair separation, distinguished as apprentice (the incumbent remains chair), departure (the incumbent retains neither role), and demotion (the incumbent remains CEO).
- DV(s): Future firm performance; subsequent combination of the CEO and board chair roles.
- Mediators: No mediation test is reported; independent oversight, unity of command, and strategic redirection are theorized mechanisms.
- Moderators: Current firm performance for separation’s performance consequences, with differences across separation types; postseparation performance for departure separation’s relationship with recombination.

The authors argue that independent oversight can redirect a poorly performing CEO but disrupt successful unity of command. Demotion imposes oversight on the incumbent and therefore presents the greatest potential for performance reversal; apprentice arrangements preserve the predecessor’s influence, while departure combines succession with governance change. Apprentice separations commonly serve a passing-the-baton process, making recombination likely; after departure separations, good performance is proposed to demonstrate that the new CEO merits the chair role.

**Data & Measures**
Corporate Library governance data cover firms in the S&P 1500 and Fortune 1000 during 2002–2006; performance data come from Compustat, CRSP, and IBES. The performance sample comprises 1,095 firms eligible to separate combined roles, with 309 separations: 202 apprentice, 78 departure, and 29 demotion. These analyses examine events during 2003–2005, exclude always-separated firms and separations reversed the following year, and use 2,403 firm-year observations in 1,053 firms for stock returns and 2,355 observations in 1,033 firms for analyst ratings (Tables 2–3). The recombination sample includes 843 firms with separate roles, including 272 apprentice, 105 departure, and 34 demotion separations; its logit models use 1,844 observations in 829 firms during 2002–2006 (Table 6).

Separation and combination are binary transitions between the previous and focal year’s leadership structures; separation types track which role, if any, the incumbent retains. Stock performance is the calendar-year holding-period return. Analyst performance is the annual mean composite IBES recommendation, reverse-coded from the original 1 = strong buy to 5 = strong sell scale so higher ratings are favorable. Analyst-rating predictors are standardized in the performance regressions; future analyst rating remains unstandardized (Table 1).

Performance models use firm fixed effects and control for current firm performance, current and future industry performance (four-digit SIC medians), board independence, CEO ownership, age and tenure, firm size (natural log of annual revenue), CEO change, CEO-change × performance, and year indicators. Recombination models use year fixed effects and firm-clustered standard errors, controlling for stock return, analyst rating, their industry counterparts, board independence, and CEO ownership, age and tenure; firm size is excluded because of multicollinearity. Supplementary instrumental-variable regressions use board independence and CEO age as instruments for the performance tests. The design is observational.

**Key Findings**
- **H1 supported:** In Table 2, Model 3, separation × current performance is negative for future stock return (β = −0.32, p < .01) and future analyst rating (β = −0.08, p < .05), consistent with improvement after poor performance and deterioration after good performance. Separation’s main terms are nonsignificant.
- **H2a supported; H2b qualified by outcome:** Demotion interactions are negative for stock return (β = −1.41) and analyst rating (β = −0.22), both p < .001 (Table 3, Model 3). Wald comparisons show stronger demotion interactions than apprentice for stock return (F = 11.28, p < .001) and analyst rating (F = 15.45, p < .001). Relative to departure, the stock-return difference is significant (F = 9.90, p < .01), but the analyst-rating difference is only marginal (F = 3.69, p < .10; Table 4). Apprentice’s stock-return interaction is negative (β = −0.26, p < .05); its analyst-rating interaction and both departure interactions are nonsignificant. Demotion also has a positive analyst-rating main term (β = 0.18, p < .05).
- **H3a and H3b supported:** Apprentice separation positively predicts recombination relative to firms without a separation event (β = 1.33, p < .001; Table 6) and exceeds departure (Wald χ² = 7.49, p < .01) and demotion (χ² = 4.60, p < .05). Departure and demotion main terms are nonsignificant.
- **H4 supported:** Departure × postseparation performance is positive for stock return (β = 2.19, p < .05) and analyst rating (β = 0.81, p < .05; Table 6, Model 3). Additional analyses find positive departure associations with recombination above mean performance and nonsignificant associations below the mean for both measures. Additional apprentice/demotion performance interactions in the recombination analysis are nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates agency theory and unity of command by treating separation as a contextual governance change whose consequences depend on when and how it occurs. Its apprentice/departure/demotion distinction connects CEO duality with succession theory and distinguishes temporary passing-the-baton arrangements from changes oriented toward independent oversight.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise boards to assess whether performance under the incumbent is poor enough to warrant changing leadership structure and to consider the type of separation. Their conclusion recommends separation selectively under weak performance, specifically through demotion, rather than treating separation as a universal governance prescription.

**Limitations**
Leadership structures are proxies for CEO–chair working relationships, rather than direct observations of boardroom processes. The study cannot fully account for the circumstances or initiator of each succession and separation, despite controlling for CEO turnover. Its focus on separation consequences leaves a broader theory of role combination and the antecedents of separation types undeveloped.

**Future Research**
The authors call for closer examination of CEO–chair interactions, including perspectives on specialization, synergy, and resource- and knowledge-based views. They propose studying why departing CEOs retain or relinquish the chair, how these choices relate to heirs apparent, and how performance, CEO age and tenure, and board independence predict separation types. They also call for distinct theory on role combination, distinguishing a CEO becoming chair, a chair becoming CEO, and a successor assuming both roles.
