# The Double-Edged Sword of Decentralized Planning in Multiteam Systems

**Abstract**

The literature on small stand-alone teams has suggested that decentralization has predominantly positive features. However, in multiteam systems, the presence of other highly interdependent teams adds a level of complexity that may preclude generalizing from teams to multiteam systems. We studied the effects of decentralized planning in 210 multiteam systems, each composed of three six-person, functionally specialized component teams. As has previous research, we find that decentralized planning has positive effects on multiteam system performance, attributable to enhanced proactivity and aspiration levels. However, we also find that the positive effects associated with decentralized planning are offset by the even stronger negative effects attributable to excessive risk seeking and coordination failures. We discuss the implications for theories of intrateam and interteam dynamics, along with the applied implications for designing empowerment interventions in team and multiteam contexts.

**Research Question**

Does decentralized planning improve or impair multiteam system performance when its positive pathways through proactivity and aspirations are considered alongside its negative pathways through risk seeking and coordination failures? The authors leave the overall net effect an open question.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1a: Decentralized planning increases planned proactivity.
- H1b: Planned proactivity is positively associated with actual proactivity.
- H1c: Actual proactivity is positively associated with multiteam system performance.
- H1d: Planned proactivity mediates decentralization's positive performance effect; the hypothesis is printed as “Planned productivity,” although the surrounding argument and tested model concern proactivity.
- H1e: Actual proactivity mediates decentralization's positive performance effect.
- H2a: Decentralized planning increases planned aspirations.
- H2b: Planned aspirations relate positively to actual aspirational behavior.
- H2c: Actual aspirational behavior is positively associated with multiteam system performance.
- H2d: Planned aspirations mediate decentralization's positive performance effect.
- H2e: Actual aspirations mediate decentralization's positive performance effect.
- H3: Pursuing higher aspirations positively influences risk-seeking behavior.
- H4a: Decentralized planning increases planned risk seeking.
- H4b: Planned risk seeking is positively associated with actual risk seeking.
- H4c: Actual risk seeking is negatively associated with multiteam system performance.
- H4d: Planned risk seeking mediates decentralization's negative performance effect.
- H4e: Actual risk seeking mediates decentralization's negative performance effect.
- H5a: Decentralized planning increases coordination failures.
- H5b: Coordination failures negatively affect performance.
- H5c: Coordination failures mediate decentralization's negative performance effect.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Decentralized rather than centralized initial planning authority.
- DV(s): Multiteam system performance.
- Mediators: Planned and actual proactivity; planned and actual aspirations; planned and actual risk seeking; coordination failures. Actual aspirations also predict actual risk seeking.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper

Giving component teams initial planning authority should encourage proactive exploration and higher aspirations, with plans translated into behavior. However, ambitious goals and decentralized authority can also encourage excessive risk, while independently developed plans can produce coordination failures across tightly interdependent teams. The authors argue that system performance has conjunctive properties, so favorable component-team dynamics need not outweigh these system-level costs.

**Data & Measures**

The situated experiment involved 2,940 United States Air Force officers in 210 multiteam systems during a five-week leadership development course. Each system contained 14 distinct people in operations, intelligence, and leadership teams of six members each; directors and assistant directors belonged to both their functional team and the leadership team. The simulation lasted approximately three hours excluding training; calendar collection dates are not reported. Systems received the same task, training, and time limits and were comparably composed on age, experience, gender, occupational category, and ASVAB scores. No additional model covariates are reported.

Decentralization was manipulated by having boundary-spanning directors and assistant directors plan first with their component teams and then with leadership; centralization reversed this order. The simulation recorded subsequent behavior objectively.

- Proactivity: Planned percentage of single-sourced intelligence missions, coded 1 = 0% to 5 = 100%; actual proactivity was the total number of intelligence missions sent.
- Aspirations: The performance goal chosen using normative performance information; actual aspirational behavior was the number of missions flown north toward high-value targets. The prose describes normative categories from 1 (top 10th percentile) to 5 (bottom 10th percentile), whereas Table 1 reports planned aspirations with mean 180 and SD 28.0; the mapping is not explained.
- Risk seeking: Planned strategy coded 1 = 100% double-escorted missions to 6 = 0% double-escorted missions; actual risk seeking was the number of unescorted missions into previously unexplored space.
- Coordination failures: Harmful leadership edits measured vertical failures; operations missions unsupported by the shared common operational picture measured horizontal failures. The variables were standardized first and then averaged.
- Performance: Points gained by destroying targets minus points lost from destroyed assets and base hits.

Incomplete planning worksheets yielded available samples of 167–210 systems for planned-goal measures. Maximum-likelihood structural equation modeling used all 210 systems with full-information maximum likelihood for missing data; bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals assessed indirect effects.

**Key Findings**

- Proactivity received partial support: H1a and H1b were positive (β = .18 and .33, respectively; both p < .05), but H1c was only marginal (β = .11, p < .10). The indirect effect through planned and actual proactivity supported H1d/e (95% CI [0.01, 1.69]).
- Aspirations supported H2a–e: decentralization predicted planned aspirations (β = .25), planned aspirations predicted actual aspirations (β = .15), and actual aspirations predicted performance (β = .17; each p < .05); the indirect-effect 95% CI was [0.05, 1.8]. Higher actual aspirations also predicted greater actual risk seeking, supporting H3 (β = .27, p < .05).
- Risk seeking supported H4a–e: decentralization predicted planned risk seeking (β = .32), planned risk seeking predicted actual risk seeking (β = .26), and actual risk seeking related negatively to performance (β = −.26; each p < .05). The indirect-effect 95% CI was [−4.2, −.7].
- Coordination failures supported H5a–c: decentralization predicted more failures (β = .23), which related negatively to performance (β = −.39; both p < .05); the indirect-effect 95% CI was [−14, −3].
- The net relationship was negative (r = −.14, p < .05), with the direct path becoming nonsignificant after including the mediators (β = −.06). The reported total effect through planned and actual behaviors was −.10 (p < .05); the model explained 24% of performance variance. Numerical path estimates follow the results prose on pp. 747–748: Figure 4 prints `11` and `15` without the decimal points given in the prose as `.11` and `.15`.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The study identifies multiteam systems as a boundary condition for theories treating decentralization as predominantly beneficial in small stand-alone teams. Integrating decentralization, goal setting, and proactivity distinguishes beneficial pathways from risk and coordination costs and separates planned activities from subsequent behavior, capturing goal setting and goal striving as distinct phases.

**Practical Implication**

Component-team leaders should guard against overly optimistic plans because system-level goal accomplishment may be a nonadditive function of component-team success. Multiteam systems may need greater emphasis on advance planning, and routine recommendations to decentralize as part of empowerment interventions may be inappropriate when teams are embedded in larger, tightly interdependent systems.

**Limitations**

The simulation's behavioral measures imperfectly represent their constructs, cognitive and emotional drivers were unmeasured, and the scale of simultaneous sessions precluded direct observation or extensive surveying. A single organizational simulation limits generalizability, particularly because Air Force officers may be trained to respond to centralized planning. The authors also caution that 59% of the teams in Davison et al.'s earlier sample participated here, making performance observations across the two papers nonindependent.

**Future Research**

Replications should use other measures, samples, tasks, and organizational contexts and examine whether less interdependent divisional structures reduce or reverse decentralization's disadvantages. Longitudinal work should test whether learning mitigates these costs or poor performance induces further risk seeking. Proposed moderators include task complexity and uncertainty, team size, diversity, history and expertise, and geographic dispersion; these possibilities were not tested here.
