# Top Management Attention to Innovation: The Role of Search Selection and Intensity in New Product Introductions

**Abstract**
We develop and test an attention-based theory of search by top management teams and the influence on firm innovativeness. Using an in-depth field study of 61 publicly traded high-technology firms and their top executives, we find that the location selection and intensity of search independently and jointly influence new product introductions. We have three important findings. First, in contrast to the portrait of local managerial search, we find teams that select locations that contain novel, vivid, and salient information introduce more new products. Next, unlike information-gathering approaches that merely “satisfice,” persistent search intensity may lead to increases in new product introductions. Finally, level of search intensity must fit the selected location of search to maximize new product introductions.

**Research Question**
How do top management teams’ search selection and search intensity independently and jointly relate to their firms’ new product introductions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Unfamiliar TMT search selection is positively related to new product introductions.
- H1b: Distant TMT search selection is positively related to new product introductions.
- H1c: Diverse TMT search selection is positively related to new product introductions.
- H2a: Effortful TMT search intensity is positively related to new product introductions.
- H2b: Persistent TMT search intensity is positively related to new product introductions.
- H3a: Search effort enhances the positive unfamiliarity–new product relationship.
- H3b: Search effort enhances the positive distance–new product relationship.
- H3c: Search effort enhances the positive diversity–new product relationship.
- H3d: Search persistence enhances the positive unfamiliarity–new product relationship.
- H3e: Search persistence enhances the positive distance–new product relationship.
- H3f: Search persistence enhances the positive diversity–new product relationship.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Search selection through terrain unfamiliarity, terrain distance, and terrain source diversity; search intensity through effort and persistence.
- DV(s): Firm new product introductions.
- Mediators: No focal mediator specified; post hoc analysis found no mediation between search selection and intensity.
- Moderators: Search effort and search persistence are each hypothesized to strengthen the three search-selection relationships.

The theory proposes that unfamiliar, distant, and diverse search exposes managers to novel, salient, and vivid information that attracts attention and enables new combinations and replacement of obsolete knowledge. Effort and persistence are proposed to expand the cognitive capacity devoted to noticing, interpreting, and making sense of information during product detection, development, and deployment. The unexpected results lead the authors to suggest that search competes with other innovation activities for limited TMT attention; opportunity costs and delegation are possible explanations, not established mediating mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
The final sample comprises 61 public high-technology firms in the mid-Atlantic United States. Of 327 eligible CEOs contacted from an initial frame of 358 firms, 92 were interviewed; 61 firms supplied the CEO survey and at least one TMT survey. Four preliminary case studies outside the final sample informed instrument development. The 2005 search survey asked executives how they would respond to a standardized hypothetical competitive threat; it did not manipulate search behavior experimentally.

New product introductions were counted from Lexis-Nexis news articles, with duplicates removed. The principal outcome is the 2006 count; the alternative is the annual average for 2006–08. Unfamiliarity is the team-average allocation to unfamiliar information from a 100-point familiar/unfamiliar allocation. Distance is the team-average allocation to intraindustry and outside-industry sources from a 100-point allocation also including internal sources. Source diversity is one minus the sum, across 12 source categories, of squared team-average search shares: first average each category’s shares across TMT members, then square, sum across categories, and subtract from one. Effort and persistence each use four items on a 1–5 agreement scale (α = .81 and .75, respectively), aggregated by averaging individual responses within teams; effort captures search investment relative to other tasks, while persistence captures exhaustive continuation despite finding alternatives.

Negative binomial regressions control for team size, functional heterogeneity, firm size, R&D intensity, and slack. Table 2 reports robust standard errors, one-tailed tests for main effects, and two-tailed tests for interactions; predictors were mean-centered before creating interaction terms. The design estimates associations rather than identifying causal effects.

**Key Findings**
For the 2006 outcome, Table 2 Model 2 supports H1a (unfamiliarity: β = .03, p < .05) and H1c (diversity: β = 23.46, p < .01); H1b receives marginal support (distance: β = .04, p < .10). Persistence supports H2b (β = .64, p < .05), whereas effort is negative, contrary to H2a (β = −2.82, p < .001).

Model 3 reports the following interactions:

| Hypothesis | Interaction | Coefficient and qualification |
|---|---|---|
| H3a | Unfamiliarity × effort | −.10, p < .10; marginal and opposite to prediction |
| H3b | Distance × effort | −.23, p < .01; opposite to prediction |
| H3c | Diversity × effort | 144.34, p < .001; positive interaction, with the authors’ contrary-pattern qualification below |
| H3d | Unfamiliarity × persistence | .01; nonsignificant |
| H3e | Distance × persistence | .12, p < .10; marginal positive interaction |
| H3f | Diversity × persistence | −76.78, p < .01; opposite to prediction |

The text reports no significant findings for H3a, H3d, or H3e, although Table 2 marks H3a and H3e as marginal. It also describes the plots for H3b, H3c, and H3f as contrary to expectations. This qualification matters for H3c: its coefficient is positive, but Figure 2 shows effort negatively associated with innovation at both diversity levels, with a steeper decline under low diversity. Figures 3–4 show a steeper negative effort relationship under high terrain distance and a negative persistence relationship under high diversity, versus a positive persistence relationship under low diversity.

For the 2006–08 annual-average outcome, Model 5 retains positive selection coefficients, but unfamiliarity (.02) and diversity (9.13) are only marginal (p < .10); distance is positive (.04, p < .05). Effort remains negative (−2.05, p < .001) and persistence positive (.76, p < .05). In Model 6, distance × effort remains negative (−.19, p < .01) and diversity × effort positive (59.99, p < .05); the other four interactions, including diversity × persistence, are nonsignificant. The authors report broadly similar results with additional heterogeneity controls and Poisson models, but identify persistence as an exception in Poisson models incorporating presample product counts, lagged product counts, or patent counts. Post hoc analysis found no mediation between selection and intensity.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends managerial-search theory by distinguishing attention selection from attention intensity and by directly examining executive search processes rather than relying only on distal organizational proxies. It connects individual cognition operating at TMT level to firm innovation and highlights that effort and persistence have different associations and must be considered jointly with search location.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that TMTs in high-technology firms consider searching unfamiliar, distant, and diverse terrains while managing how much attention search takes from other innovation activities. Their recommendation emphasizes fit between terrain and intensity: persistent, less effortful search is a possibility to consider, subject to the reported interactions, rather than a uniformly beneficial combination.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on TMT search and treats search intensity as linear; relationships may differ for scientists and other R&D personnel. Its small high-technology sample and innovation outcome constrain generalization to other industries and outcomes. Sample firms had fewer assets, employees, and sales than the initial public-firm frame, so implications may apply more to smaller public firms.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining attention allocation across innovation activities, delegation of search to scientists and R&D employees, and the interrelationship between search selection and persistence. Further directions include executive search protocols, demographic and psychological characteristics, organizational antecedents and moderators of search, other industries and outcomes, and archival measures based on executives’ written accounts, documents, and public announcements.
