# Power Source Mismatch and the Effectiveness of Interorganizational Relations: The Case of Venture Capital Syndication

**Abstract**
This study explores the impact of the mismatch between two sources of power, ownership and status, on the effectiveness of interorganizational relations. We characterize three types of dyadic ownership-status relationships and examine their relative mixes at the group level: (1) power source match (A’s ownership and status are both higher than those of B); (2) ownership-dominated power source mismatch (A’s ownership advantage over B is greater than B’s status advantage over A); and (3) status-dominated power source mismatch (A’s ownership advantage over B is less than B’s status advantage over A). We found that power source match enhanced the effectiveness of venture capital (VC) syndication, and that ownership-dominated power source mismatch strengthened the positive effect of power source match because the syndicate could maintain legitimate ownership order and benefit from diverse inputs. By contrast, status-dominated power source mismatch weakened the positive effect of power source match, because it may have created disorderly interaction. Moreover, familiarity between participating VC firms diminished the negative moderating effect of status-dominated mismatch, because mutual trust helped organize group interaction, and entrepreneurial performance fulfilled a similar function, as the autonomy of entrepreneurs from the VC syndicate buffered turbulences caused by interaction problems among VC investors.

**Research Question**
How do different mixes of ownership-based and status-based power relations shape the effectiveness of VC syndication? When do familiarity among VC firms and entrepreneurial performance mitigate the adverse interaction between power source match and status-dominated mismatch?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Greater power source match increases the effectiveness of VC syndication.
- H2: Ownership-dominated power source mismatch strengthens the positive effect of power source match on syndication effectiveness.
- H3: Status-dominated power source mismatch diminishes the positive effect of power source match on syndication effectiveness.
- H4: Greater familiarity among syndicate members diminishes the negative interactive effect of power source match and status-dominated mismatch on syndication effectiveness.
- H5: Better entrepreneurial performance diminishes the negative interactive effect of power source match and status-dominated mismatch on syndication effectiveness.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Syndicate-level power source match, aggregating dyads in which the higher-ownership VC firm also has higher status.
- DV(s): Syndication effectiveness, proxied by the portfolio company’s rate of exit through IPO or acquisition.
- Mediators: No mediator is directly tested; ownership-based interaction order, diverse inputs, trust, and entrepreneurial autonomy are proposed explanatory processes.
- Moderators: Ownership-dominated mismatch strengthens, and status-dominated mismatch weakens, the match–effectiveness relationship; familiarity and entrepreneurial performance moderate the negative interaction involving status-dominated mismatch.

Ownership supplies legitimate power, while status supplies referent power. The authors argue that ownership-dominated mismatch permits diverse input while preserving ownership-based order because an ownership advantage exceeds the opposing status advantage; status-dominated mismatch instead creates conflicting interaction orders. Familiarity is proposed to help firms assert ownership rights within trusting relationships, while stronger entrepreneurial performance affords entrepreneurs autonomy to buffer conflicting directions from investors. These mechanisms motivate the hypotheses rather than constitute tested mediation.

**Data & Measures**
VentureXpert provides data on 1,503 VC firms investing in 1,096 U.S. biotechnology ventures during 1976–2005, covering 2,062 financing rounds. The survival models use company-quarter spells; Table 2 reports N=12,701, while Table 1’s descriptive statistics report n=12,118, without an explanation of the difference. By the end of 2005, 256 companies had IPOs and 74 were acquired; the remaining companies were right-censored on December 31, 2005. Eight professionals from eight VC firms were interviewed in November–December 2008 to inform the theorizing.

Ownership is a VC firm’s equity share based on cumulative investment, with other investors included in its computation; actual and estimated VentureXpert data provide 94% of equity information. Status is Bonacich centrality with its weighting parameter set to zero, computed from private-VC co-investment networks across all industries over the preceding five years and normalized to a 0–1 range. For each dyad, i denotes the higher-ownership firm. The printed match/mismatch formula subtracts the other firm’s status advantage at t−1 from i’s ownership advantage at t, sums over dyads in the relevant category, and divides by the number of all possible dyads in the round. The source contains a sign inconsistency: Exhibit 1 reports a positive status-dominated score of 0.03 although its displayed subtraction has a negative numerator; the printed formula contains no absolute-value operation.

Familiarity is the total number of prior dyadic ties among participating firms over five years, across industries. Entrepreneurial performance is proxied by patents held before an investment round, from NBER and Recombinant Capital; patent data are available for 236 companies, with missing counts replaced by zero and a replacement indicator included. Controls cover ownership/status levels and dispersion, dispersion of the match/mismatch measures, syndicate size and composition, investment stage and round sequence, calendar and industry conditions, cumulative investment, funds managed, and syndicate turnover. Average ownership and status are logged and orthogonalized. The design is associational: the authors address potential endogenous network formation with additional controls after finding instrumental-variable and conditional fixed-effect approaches infeasible.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: power source match is positively associated with the exit rate (Table 2, model 2: b=0.58, robust SE=0.29, p<.05); the authors report an exit-rate multiplier of 1.78 per additional unit of match.
- H2 and H3 are supported in model 3: the interaction with ownership-dominated mismatch is positive (b=21.48, SE=10.89, p<.05), whereas that with status-dominated mismatch is negative (b=−9.73, SE=4.76, p<.05). The mismatch main effects are nonsignificant.
- H4 is supported by the positive match × status-dominated mismatch × familiarity interaction (model 4: b=15.22, SE=6.96, p<.05). H5 is supported by the corresponding interaction with patent count (model 5: b=6.13, SE=2.79, p<.05). Both remain positive and significant in the full model 6 (b=15.80 and b=6.75, respectively; both p<.05).
- The authors report consistent results with Cox models, three-year familiarity, patent counts without zero replacement, and mean-centered interactions. However, substituting subsequent patent citations for patent count yields a nonsignificant three-way interaction. A separate multinomial-logit analysis finds no significant difference between the match/mismatch effects on IPO and acquisition.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a contingency account of resource dependence in which one power source can constrain another, linking ownership-based dependency with status-based dependency and social structural incoherence. It distinguishes beneficial ownership-dominated mismatch from potentially disruptive status-dominated mismatch and connects embeddedness and investor–entrepreneur power relations to the functioning of investor–investor relationships.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that VC syndicates may invite new members to alleviate problems caused by status-dominated mismatch. This interpretation follows their post hoc finding that greater status-dominated mismatch is associated with new-member entry without incumbent departures; it is not evidence that such entry improves exit outcomes.

**Limitations**
The data do not capture board membership or the individual characteristics of VC professionals that may shape power relations. Syndication effectiveness is proxied by exit timing because historical fund-return data are unavailable, and potential endogeneity is addressed through controls rather than an instrumental-variable or fixed-effect identification strategy. The formula/example sign inconsistency and differing observation counts remain unresolved in the source.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining board membership, professionals’ educational similarity and prestige, and the relationship between power source match/mismatch and group restructuring. They also suggest extending the analysis to securities underwriting and joint ventures, incorporating other power sources such as brokerage, and studying trust arising from sources other than familiarity, such as referrals.
