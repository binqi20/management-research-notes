# The Psychological Processes of Cultural Retooling

**Abstract**
An essential challenge of cultural adaptation is to smoothly and swiftly incorporate new behaviors into one’s cultural repertoire. I introduce the term “cultural retooling” to capture this process and provide a theory of its psychological dynamics. Using longitudinal data from 50 foreign-born master’s degree students in the United States, I find that individuals use two core methods for addressing the internal conflict that they experience during cultural retooling (integrative and instrumental) and that the retooling process itself unfolds over three distinct phases (conflict, ambivalence, and authenticity). Moreover, I find that individuals vary in their trajectories across these phases (regressing, stagnating, or transforming) and that these trajectories are shaped by how internal conflict is addressed.

**Research Question**
How do individuals manage internal conflict when learning culturally unfamiliar behaviors that contradict their ingrained values and routines? How does their experience of conflict change over time, and how do different approaches to managing it relate to those changes?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Value discrepancy produces experienced illegitimacy, whereas routine discrepancy produces experienced awkwardness; together these underpin feelings of inauthenticity.
- Practices: Integrative approaches comprise self-licensing, embracing the new culture’s interpretation of behavior and accepting its legitimacy, and personalizing, making small but meaningful behavioral adjustments. Instrumental approaches comprise necessitizing, emphasizing the necessity of performing despite discomfort, and suppressing, silencing discomfort while acting the part externally.
- Process / phases: Deep conflict involves high illegitimacy and awkwardness, ambivalence involves moderate levels, and authenticity involves low levels of both. Participants need not experience every phase or follow the same sequence.
- Emergent outcomes: Full transformation reaches authenticity; partial transformation reaches ambivalence; stagnation remains in deep conflict; regression returns to deep conflict after ambivalence.

The emergent process model distinguishes addressing the underlying sources of conflict from managing their immediate emotional manifestations. Self-licensing addresses illegitimacy and personalizing addresses awkwardness, whereas instrumental techniques reduce negative emotion without resolving these underlying psychological states. The approaches can coexist: participants progressing toward authenticity also used instrumental techniques temporarily while addressing underlying conflict through integration.

**Data & Measures**
The corpus comprises 150 switching-experience sheets from 50 MBA/MA students at an internationally focused U.S. business school. Participants came from 24 countries; although the abstract describes the sample as foreign-born, Methods specifies that all but one were foreign-born. The American-born participant interviewed in Japanese with Japanese companies locally. Participants selected real, personally and professionally meaningful situations with unfamiliar norms that conflicted with their cultural values, diagnosed those norms, and attempted three behavioral switches over eight weeks at two- to three-week intervals. They completed diary-style accounts as soon as possible afterward; calendar dates are not reported.

Open, in vivo coding and iteration between accounts and literature identified management techniques, psychological phases, and trajectories. Phase coding assessed experienced illegitimacy and awkwardness across each episode as a whole; trajectories compared each person’s episodes. Checks on 42 coded technique forms and 38 phase-coded episodes yielded 98% and 97% agreement, respectively. Supplementary coding of feelings before, during, and after switching distinguished 28 emotion terms, including 16 negative and 12 positive terms; two coders marked their presence or absence, with 96% agreement. These are inductive accounts and descriptive comparisons, without experimental assignment or control-variable models.

**Key Findings**
- All participants began in deep conflict. By the final switch, 22% remained in deep conflict, 64% were ambivalent, and 14% experienced authenticity (Table 2). The trajectory distribution was 14% full transformation, 64% partial transformation, 18% stagnation, and 4% regression (Table 4).
- Emotional patterns differed by phase: negative emotions accounted for 70% in deep conflict, 36% in ambivalence, and 9% in authenticity; corresponding positive-emotion shares were 30%, 64%, and 91% (Table 3). These are emotion distributions within phases, not percentages of participants achieving adaptation.
- Integrative techniques were associated with transformation, but their use did not guarantee it. Within the full-transformation, partial-transformation, stagnation, and regression groups, respectively, 100%, 84%, 44%, and 0% used at least one integrative technique; 29%, 78%, 89%, and 100% used at least one instrumental technique (Table 7). The overlapping use shows that instrumental coping was not confined to unsuccessful trajectories.
- Accounts and the emergent model link self-licensing to greater legitimacy and personalizing to greater comfort. Instrumental techniques instead managed immediate negative emotion while leaving underlying illegitimacy and awkwardness intact (Figures 1–2); these findings concern psychological experience, not experimentally established effects on performance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study develops a microprocess account of cultural retooling that complements research on individual cultural competence and broad stages of adjustment abroad. It differentiates value-based and routine-based conflict, explains distinct ways of addressing them, and shows how authenticity can emerge while performing initially value-incongruent behavior. It also connects coping with ambivalence to authenticity and highlights individual agency and heterogeneous adaptation trajectories.

**Practical Implication**
The author recommends that organizations help employees diagnose precisely what feels illegitimate or awkward, develop personalized self-licensing and behavioral adjustments within the new culture’s acceptable range, and rehearse in realistic situations. Instrumental techniques can provide temporary help managing disruptive negative emotion alongside integrative approaches. Business schools should supplement cultural exposure with helping students develop and customize their cultural toolkits.

**Limitations**
Graded reflections may encourage socially desirable self-presentation, even though grading emphasized thoroughness and thoughtfulness rather than successful adaptation. Questions may prime particular experiences, and the data come solely from participants’ own accounts. The concentration of native-English-speaking Indian men among those fully transforming complicates interpretation of cultural versus language influences; other language groups also progressed, and native English speakers did not uniformly achieve full transformation.

**Future Research**
The author suggests more open-ended questions and additional methods, such as retrospective interviews and observations, to enrich accounts and support triangulation. Future work should distinguish the effects of culture and language; the discussion also notes that longer observation could allow partially transformed participants to progress further and alter the demographic composition of the full-transformation group.
