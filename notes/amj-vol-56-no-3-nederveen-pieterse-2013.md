# Cultural Diversity and Team Performance: The Role of Team Member Goal Orientation

**Abstract**
As workforce diversity increases, knowledge of factors influencing whether cultural diversity results in team performance benefits is of growing importance. Complementing and extending earlier research, we develop and test theory about how achievement setting readily activates team member goal orientations that influence the diversity-performance relationship. In two studies, we identify goal orientation as a moderator of the performance benefits of cultural diversity and team information elaboration as the underlying process. Cultural diversity is more positive for team performance when team members’ learning approach orientation is high and performance avoidance orientation is low. This effect is exerted via team information elaboration.

**Research Question**
How do team members’ goal orientations moderate the relationship between cultural diversity and team performance, and does team information elaboration mediate these conditional relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Cultural diversity is more positively related to team performance for teams whose members have higher learning approach orientation.
- H2: Cultural diversity is more positively related to team performance for teams whose members have lower performance avoidance orientation.
- H3: Information elaboration mediates the interaction of cultural diversity and learning approach orientation on performance; a positive indirect relationship obtains only at higher learning approach orientation.
- H4: Information elaboration mediates the interaction of cultural diversity and performance avoidance orientation on performance; a positive indirect relationship obtains only at lower performance avoidance orientation.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team cultural diversity.
- DV(s): Team performance in a business simulation and associated group assignments.
- Mediators: Team information elaboration, measured in Study 2; intergroup biases are a proposed influence on elaboration but are not measured.
- Moderators: Mean team-member learning approach and performance avoidance orientations; learning avoidance and performance approach orientations are also examined, without predicted moderation.

The categorization-elaboration model treats diversity as a potential informational resource whose value depends on exchanging, discussing, and integrating task-relevant information and perspectives. Learning approach orientation is theorized to encourage deep information processing and openness, whereas performance avoidance can discourage exploration and increase reliance on stereotypes and defensive responses. These orientations may therefore shape whether diverse teams elaborate their information effectively; the studies test dispositional team composition rather than interventions that change goal orientations.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 surveyed Dutch business-school students before a three-week human resource management simulation in self-selected teams, usually of four members but sometimes three. There were 376 usable questionnaires; excluding 22 incompletely responding teams left 79 teams and 312 students, and excluding two multivariate-outlier teams left 77 teams for analysis. Study 2 used the same three-week simulation setting, obtaining 566 usable pre-simulation questionnaires and 549 end-of-simulation questionnaires; excluding 41 teams without complete participation in both surveys left 109 four-person teams.

Cultural diversity uses self-reported cultural backgrounds and Blau’s index, 1 − Σ(Pi)²: square each cultural category’s proportion of team members, sum those squares, and subtract from one. Goal orientation uses the adjusted 12-item Elliot–McGregor scale, with three items per dimension and team means; Study 1 specifies seven-point responses. Performance in both studies averages the separately standardized scores for four group assignments and the simulation; three experts graded the assignments using strict criteria, blind to team identity and research purpose in Study 1. Study 2 adds a three-item end-of-simulation measure of team information elaboration, including whether members discussed the rationales underlying their viewpoints.

Both studies control for member familiarity. Study 1 uses hierarchical regression to add the four diversity-by-orientation interactions; Study 2 models those interactions in the elaboration equation and predicts performance from familiarity, cultural diversity, and elaboration. Study 2 uses bootstrapping with one-sided tests and 90% bias-corrected confidence intervals. These survey designs are associational.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supports H1 and H2 (Table 2): the diversity × learning approach interaction is positive (b = 2.05, p < .01), and the diversity × performance avoidance interaction is negative (b = −1.43, p < .01). Diversity relates positively to performance at high learning approach (b = .96, p < .05) and negatively at low learning approach (b = −1.11, p < .01); it relates positively at low performance avoidance (b = .80, p < .05) and negatively at high performance avoidance (b = −.95, p < .05). Neither learning avoidance nor performance approach significantly moderates this relationship, and cultural diversity’s main effect is nonsignificant.
- Study 2 supports H3 and H4 under its one-sided testing convention (Table 4). The interaction indirect effects through elaboration are .02 for learning approach (90% BC CI [.0002, .0483]) and −.03 for performance avoidance (90% BC CI [−.0725, −.0051]). Conditional indirect effects are positive at high learning approach (.04, 90% BC CI [.0063, .0926]) and low performance avoidance (.05, 90% BC CI [.0054, .1191]), but nonsignificant at low learning approach and high performance avoidance. The learning-approach interaction predicting elaboration is only marginal (.07, p < .10); the performance-avoidance interaction is negative (−.12, p < .05), and elaboration positively predicts performance (.29, p < .01). The learning-avoidance and performance-approach interaction indirect effects are nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the categorization-elaboration model by specifying achievement-related motivational orientations and self-regulatory strategies as contingencies of cultural diversity’s relationship with performance, with process evidence for information elaboration. Its full four-factor treatment of mean team-member goal orientation distinguishes learning approach from learning avoidance and performance avoidance from performance approach, extending goal-orientation research to how teams respond to their social composition.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations may consider goal orientations in employee selection, particularly for culturally diverse teams. They propose encouraging learning approach and reducing performance avoidance by emphasizing team and personal development, de-emphasizing competition, and treating mistakes as learning opportunities rather than grounds for punishment. Training, feedback and compensation systems, and leader training are suggested ways to support these orientations; these interventions are not tested in the studies.

**Limitations**
Both studies use student teams, and cultural diversity is positively skewed, although fully diverse teams are represented and log-transformed diversity produces similar findings. Performance avoidance has relatively low reliability (α = .60 in Study 1 and .63 in Study 2), while intergroup biases are theorized but not directly measured. The survey designs do not permit causal conclusions, and Study 1 does not measure the proposed information-elaboration mediator.

**Future Research**
The authors call for replication in organizational settings and experimental research, and identify the value of measuring intergroup bias, for example through interpersonal liking. They propose examining contextual influences on goal orientation, including feedback, rewards, leadership, and error-management versus error-prevention cultures, and extending the analysis to other diversity attributes. Further directions include tasks requiring distributed information or intense cooperation and whether learning orientation’s relationship-quality benefits are stronger when leaders differ culturally from their teams.
