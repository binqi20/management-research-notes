# Signals across Multiple Networks: How Venture Capital and Alliance Networks Affect Interorganizational Collaboration

**Abstract**
In this article, we examine the contingent effects of signals generated by different types of networks on new ventures’ formation of future strategic alliances. We argue that the signaling value of a given tie in reducing adverse selection is more pronounced when another type of tie is lacking. In particular, we suggest that signals associated with (i) a new venture’s affiliations with venture capitalists (VCs) that have prominent positions in syndicate networks and (ii) a new venture’s prominent position in alliance networks resulting from previous alliances offer redundant benefits. As a result, the positive effect of VC prominence in determining a new venture’s future alliance formation diminishes as the new venture’s prominence in alliance networks increases. Evidence from biotech alliances between new ventures and established companies provides support for our theory.

**Research Question**
How do signals from a new venture’s affiliations with prominent VCs and its own prominence in alliance networks jointly relate to future alliance formation? Does alliance prominence diminish the signaling value of affiliations with prominent VCs?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: A new venture’s affiliations with prominent VCs positively affect its future alliance formation.
- H2: A new venture’s prominence in networks of previous alliances positively affects its future alliance formation.
- H3: The positive effect of affiliations with prominent VCs on future alliance formation diminishes as the new venture’s alliance prominence increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Prominence of the venture’s affiliated VCs in VC syndicate networks; the venture’s own prominence in alliance networks.
- DV(s): Formation of an R&D alliance between a new venture and an established company in a given year.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper. Reduced adverse-selection risk is a theorized explanation, not a measured mediation pathway.
- Moderators: The new venture’s alliance prominence negatively moderates the association between VC prominence and future alliance formation.

Prominent VCs can signal venture quality through their information access, selective investment, and reputation, while affiliation can require valuation concessions that lower-quality ventures find costly to bear. A prominent alliance position similarly signals valuable resources and prospects. Because either network can help distinguish higher-quality ventures, the authors argue that their signals offer redundant benefits: existing alliance prominence reduces the additional signaling value of prominent VC affiliations.

**Data & Measures**
- **Sample and sources:** The archival study covers U.S. R&D alliances during 1980–2003: 239 domestic private VC-backed biotech ventures and 156 public biotech/pharmaceutical companies. It includes 225 realized and 53,479 unrealized alliance-pair observations; Table 1 reports 53,704 observations, with dyad-year as the stated analysis unit. Firms that switched from the new-venture role to the established-company role were excluded. Alliance data come from Deloitte Recombinant LLC (Recap), VC investments from VentureXpert (1980–2004 coverage), and patent applications from NBER through 1999 and USPTO thereafter; Factiva/online searches and Corptech supplement missing information.
- **Outcome and estimation:** The dependent variable equals one if a particular dyad forms an R&D alliance in year t, zero otherwise. All independent and control variables enter with a one-year lag. Logit estimation uses robust standard errors clustered by dyad. The design estimates associations rather than experimentally identified causal effects.
- **Network prominence:** VC prominence is calculated by first obtaining each VC’s Bonacich centrality from co-investment ties during the preceding five years, then taking the maximum centrality among the VCs that invested in the venture by that time. In Equation 1, each tie count is multiplied by the sum of a scaling term and a weighted partner-centrality term, and these products are summed across partners; the weighting coefficient is three-quarters of the reciprocal of the relationship matrix’s largest eigenvalue, with scaling to make the annual maximum centrality one. Alliance prominence applies Bonacich centrality to the venture’s preceding-five-year alliance ties, likewise normalizing the annual maximum to one (Equation 2).
- **Controls:** IPO market conditions are biotech IPOs in the previous three months divided by private biotech companies. Prior dyadic ties are the log of alliances in the preceding five years. Controls also include both firms’ patent applications and alliance counts, established-company alliance prominence, venture age, VC portfolio-company count (the paper’s “VC deal size”), VC fund age, syndicate size, and funding rounds. Venture and VC fund ages are in months. Alliance royalty/milestone funding and equity invested through alliances cover the preceding five years and are measured in logged millions of dollars.

**Key Findings**
- **H1 and H2 supported:** VC prominence and alliance prominence are positively associated with future alliance formation. Table 2 Model 5 reports VC prominence b = 1.71 (SE = 0.35) and alliance prominence b = 8.49 (SE = 0.97). The table marks VC prominence with two stars (p < .01), although the results prose states p < .001 after adding the interaction; alliance prominence is marked p < .001. This table–prose discrepancy is retained.
- **H3 supported:** The interaction is negative, b = −27.77 (SE = 6.31, p < .001; Table 2, Model 5). The authors’ probability comparison holds other covariates at their means: increasing VC prominence one standard deviation from its mean increases future alliance likelihood by 60% at median alliance prominence, but by less than 1% at median-plus-one-standard-deviation alliance prominence (Figure 1 and accompanying text).
- **Supplemental checks:** The authors report similar results using rare-events logit and robustness to replacing maximum VC centrality with mean centrality. Added squared terms for VC prominence, alliance prominence, and accumulated VC funding rounds were all nonsignificant; estimates for these supplemental checks are not displayed.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling theory to contingent relationships between different networks, showing evidence consistent with redundant signaling benefits from prominent VC affiliations and alliance positions. It shifts attention from networks considered separately to how an organization’s position in one network conditions endorsements from another, and argues that network-efficiency considerations can span network types.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that managers should consider affiliations beyond a focal network when evaluating the benefits and costs of interorganizational relationships. Forming affiliations can be costly, and different networks may provide redundant signals about an organization’s resources and prospects.

**Limitations**
The study does not examine the coevolution of different network structures. Its empirical scope is R&D alliances between domestic private VC-backed biotech ventures and established biotech/pharmaceutical companies, a setting characterized by dispersed knowledge and long product-development cycles; the authors call for tests in other contexts and collaborative agreements. Their proposed extension to other networks is conditional on potential partners facing adverse-selection risk and companies being able to signal quality through network positions.

**Future Research**
Study the simultaneous or sequential evolution of multiple networks and their consequences for resource acquisition, financial performance, IPOs, and acquisitions. Examine other endorsers such as investment banks, other networks such as board interlocks and governmental ties in emerging markets, nonnetwork signals such as patents and financial choices, and other interorganizational exchanges. Alternative theoretical perspectives could identify when networks complement one another, including when rivals’ imitation costs are lower.
