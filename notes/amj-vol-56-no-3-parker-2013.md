# Making the Most of Structural Support: Moderating Influence of Employees' Clarity and Negative Affect

**Abstract**
We investigated structural support as a work design characteristic potentially enabling employee effectiveness in demanding contexts, proposing that structural support enhances job and role outcomes for employees but that effects depend on both the outcome under consideration (job vs. role) and the employees themselves. We tested hypotheses in a within-persons quasi-experiment in which 48 hospital doctors carried out their work with and without structural support. Structural support had positive effects on perceived core job performance, and these effects were stronger for individuals with higher clarity about others’ work roles, suggesting that individuals can better mobilize available support when clear about how to allocate it. Support was also associated with improved role outcomes although, consistently with conservation of resources theory, effects differed with affect. For individuals with higher negative work affect, structural support was associated with lowered perceived role overload (a resource protection mechanism). For individuals with lower negative work affect, support was associated with higher perceived skill utilization and proactive work behavior (a resource accumulation mechanism). We approach social support at work in a novel way, extend relational approaches to work design, and show the value of considering both job and role outcomes in work redesign research.

**Research Question**
Does making structural support available improve employees’ perceived core job performance and role outcomes in demanding work, and do clarity about others’ roles and negative work affect shape which benefits employees obtain?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Individuals report higher core job performance when structural support is available, with a greater within-person improvement among those with higher clarity about others’ roles.
- H2: Individuals report lower role overload when structural support is available, with a greater within-person reduction among those with higher negative work affect.
- H3: Individuals report higher skill utilization when structural support is available, with a greater within-person improvement among those with lower negative work affect.
- H4: Individuals report greater proactive work behavior when structural support is available, with a greater within-person improvement among those with lower negative work affect.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Structural support availability, operationalized by an advanced practice nurse being present versus absent on an overtime shift.
- DV(s): Perceived core job performance, role overload, skill utilization, and proactive work behavior.
- Mediators: None tested. Resource allocation, resource protection, and resource accumulation are proposed explanations; resource loss is inferred from negative work affect rather than measured directly.
- Moderators: Clarity about others’ roles for core performance; negative work affect for role outcomes. Both moderators enter every outcome regression.

Clarity about others’ roles is proposed to help employees allocate available support effectively toward prescribed core tasks. For role outcomes involving emergent and discretionary tasks, conservation of resources theory suggests that employees experiencing higher negative work affect use support to protect against further resource loss by reducing overload. Those experiencing lower negative work affect are expected to use support to accumulate resources through skill use and development and proactive behavior; these processes are interpretations of the outcome pattern, not demonstrated mediation.

**Data & Measures**
The main study followed junior doctors across overtime shifts in an Australian metropolitan training hospital. Of 86 participating doctors, 48 supplied surveys in both conditions; the remaining doctors supplied support-only or no-support-only surveys. One advanced practice nurse made instrumental and emotional assistance available on covered shifts. The study was introduced approximately three months after the intervention began; calendar dates are not reported. Conditions followed shift schedules rather than random assignment, and shifts were matched on weekday/weekend and wards covered.

End-of-shift self-reports assessed core performance with four items covering expected tasks and quality/timeliness of patient care; overload with four work-overload items plus one pressure item; skill utilization with five items assessing skill use and development; and proactivity with two voice and two taking-charge items. All used five-point response scales. Three clarity items concerned the advanced nurse, clinical nurse, and distinction between nurses’ and junior doctors’ roles. Six negative-affect items covered anxiety and depression. Both moderators used reports from the nonintervention condition. A separate 131-undergraduate sample assessed shortened-scale validity and the distinctiveness of the four outcomes; nurse ratings provided an additional proactivity-validity check among 39 doctors.

For each outcome, the regression dependent variable was the no-support score minus the support score. Condition order entered first, followed jointly by the two centered moderators. Follow-up paired tests compared support conditions within groups below or above half a standard deviation from each moderator’s mean. Positive affect was added in supplementary analyses, not the main specification. The design strengthens within-person comparison but does not eliminate the authors’ stated validity threats.

**Key Findings**
- Average condition differences: Support was associated with higher core performance, t(47) = 2.09, p < .05; marginally higher proactivity, t(47) = 1.92, p < .10; and marginally lower overload, t(47) = -1.75, p < .10. The average skill-utilization difference was nonsignificant (p. 881).
- H1: Clarity moderated the core-performance difference, standardized β = -.47, with p < .001 reported in the prose. Given the no-support-minus-support coding, this means a larger support-associated improvement at higher clarity. Follow-up tests found improvement in the higher-clarity group but no significant difference in the lower-clarity group (Table 3; Figure 2A).
- H2: Negative work affect moderated the overload difference, β = .31, p < .05. Support was associated with lower overload in the higher-negative-affect group, but not in the lower-negative-affect group (Table 3; Figure 2B).
- H3/H4: Negative work affect moderated skill utilization, β = .38, and proactivity, β = .39. Skill utilization and proactivity were higher under support in the lower-negative-affect group, but neither difference was significant in the higher-negative-affect group. These findings support the predicted moderation patterns. The prose reports p < .01 for both coefficients, whereas Table 3 marks them at p < .05 (Figures 2C–2D; p. 883).
- Additional tests: Condition order and its interactions with the moderators were nonsignificant. Controlling positive affect, alternative regressions, and hierarchical linear modeling retained the reported moderator pattern; positive affect itself did not moderate the outcomes. Anxiety moderated all three role outcomes. Depression moderated skill utilization, only marginally moderated proactivity (β = .26, p < .10), and did not moderate overload (β = .20, nonsignificant).
- Reporting qualification: The high-negative-affect overload subgroup is reported as n = 14 alongside t(23) = -2.81, p < .05; the sample count and degrees of freedom are internally inconsistent (p. 883). The note retains the reported direction and significance without repairing that inconsistency.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study shifts attention from perceived support to structurally available support and shows how individuals can obtain different benefits from the same support arrangement. It extends relational work design through internal interdependencies among coworkers, complementing approaches centered on employee–beneficiary contact and job enrichment. Distinguishing core job performance from role outcomes connects clarity-based resource allocation with conservation-of-resources explanations of protection and accumulation.

**Practical Implication**
For demanding hospital work, formally embedding interdisciplinary support can help employees obtain different benefits according to their clarity about roles and negative work affect. The authors report that hospital management subsequently institutionalized and expanded this support by creating two permanent positions; the study did not establish improved patient outcomes or persistence of employee benefits.

**Limitations**
The authors cannot rule out diffusion between conditions or the nurse responding differently to individual doctors, and the focal outcomes rely on self-reports. Resource loss and the proposed explanatory processes were not measured directly, and patient outcomes and longer-term employee effects were not assessed. The small sample limited power, while the distinctive junior-doctor/overtime context and use of one support provider limit generalizability.

**Future Research**
The authors call for other-rated outcomes, more direct measurement of resource loss, and tests distinguishing reduced demands from feeling supported, alongside assessment of patient outcomes and whether employee benefits persist, accumulate, or wane. Larger samples could test whether clarity further moderates the negative-affect interaction, and research should examine generalizability beyond this setting and provider. They also propose examining which features of structural support are most useful and whether formal structural support overcomes limitations of individually oriented or informal support.
