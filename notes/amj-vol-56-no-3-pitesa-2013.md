# Compliant Sinners, Obstinate Saints: How Power and Self-Focus Determine the Effectiveness of Social Influences in Ethical Decision Making

**Abstract**
In this research, we examine when and why organizational environments influence how employees respond to moral issues. Past research has proposed that social influences in organizations affect employees’ ethical decision making, but has not explained when and why some individuals are affected by an organizational environment and some disregard it. To address this problem, we drew on research on power to propose that power makes people more self-focused, which, in turn, makes them more likely to act upon their preferences and ignore (un)ethical social influences. Using both experimental and field methods, we tested our model across the three main paradigms of social influence: informational influence (Studies 1 and 2), normative influence (Study 3), and compliance (Study 4). Results offer converging evidence for our theory.

**Research Question**
When and why do employees follow organizational social influences in ethical decision making, and when do they instead follow their own preferences? The paper examines power as a moderator and private self-focus as its explanatory mechanism across informational influence, normative influence, and compliance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Power weakens the effect of social influences in ethical decision making.
- H2: Private self-focus mediates the moderating effect of power on the effect of social influences in ethical decision making.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Social influences: others’ responses to an ambiguous ethical dilemma in Studies 1–2, ethical climate in Study 3, and perceived organizational ethical culture in Study 4. Studies 3–4 also examine personal ethical preferences through Machiavellianism and moral identity, respectively.
- DV(s): Responses to the ethical dilemma in Studies 1–2; deception in a simulated negotiation in Study 3; self-reported frequency of unethical workplace behavior in Study 4.
- Mediators: Private self-focus, measured in Studies 1, 3, and 4 and experimentally manipulated in Study 2, explains power’s moderation of social influence.
- Moderators: Power, primed in Studies 1 and 3, structurally manipulated in Study 2, and measured as a subjective sense of power in Study 4; power is also expected to strengthen the relationship between personal preferences and decisions in Studies 3–4.

The situated focus theory of power suggests that reduced dependence on others directs powerful individuals’ attention toward their own thoughts, feelings, and preferences. Greater private self-focus should therefore reduce susceptibility to social influence while increasing reliance on personal preferences. The argument assumes that decision makers have preferences on the moral issue; it does not predict that power inherently makes behavior more ethical or more unethical.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: A randomized power prime (high, low, control) × social information (present, absent) experiment recruited 256 students; four who doubted the bogus responses were excluded, leaving 252. Participants recalled possessing power, lacking power, or visiting a grocery store, then completed a three-item private self-awareness scale. They rated whether to discourage a valuable employee from leaving, from 1 (definitely discourage) to 7 (definitely not discourage). The dilemma involved conflicting moral values, not a uniquely ethical answer. The influence condition displayed seven bogus prior responses averaging 6; the control displayed none. An independent pretest involved 51 participants.
- Study 2: Ninety-one students were randomly assigned to manager/subordinate roles and a self-focus/control word task. Participants wrote a story with self-related or neutral words, then completed the same dilemma with the same bogus answers as Study 1; all faced informational influence. This crossed manipulation tested the proposed psychological process experimentally.
- Study 3: A randomized high/low power prime × ethical-climate/no-climate-message experiment involved 260 students. Ethical preferences were measured first with the 20-item Mach IV scale, reversing designated items before averaging. Private self-focus used the Study 1 scale. Deception was the discrepancy between intended and announced numbers of tours in a simulated negotiation; the ethical-climate message emphasized honesty and peers’ ostracism of dishonest managers, and all participants were told they would discuss decisions with peers. Mood and negotiation experience were controls.
- Study 4: A survey recruited 312 managers at an international airport, with 30.38% of self-identified managers agreeing to participate; measure order was randomized. Measures were perceived ethical culture (14 items), moral-identity internalization (five), subjective power (eight), dispositional private self-consciousness (ten), and frequency of seven unethical workplace behaviors (never to daily). An independent pretest of 94 employed adults assessed the behaviors’ unethicality. Table 4 includes age and impression management as controls and mean-centers continuous variables. The design establishes associations, not experimental effects.

Studies 1, 3, and 4 used moderated path analysis: the power-to-self-focus coefficient was multiplied by the self-focus interaction coefficient, with indirect-effect confidence intervals obtained from 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Collection dates, study countries, and sampled industries are not reported.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported H1: with social information, high-power participants conformed less than controls (means 4.73 versus 5.19, p = .028), while low-power participants conformed more (5.62 versus 5.19, p = .037). Power had no significant effect without social information (p = .441). Table 1 and the mediated-moderation analysis supported H2: the indirect-interaction 95% confidence intervals were [0.023, 0.218] for low power and [−0.188, −0.021] for high power, each relative to control. Excluding suspicious participants did not change the reported results.
- Study 2 supported the proposed pattern: high-power participants conformed less overall than low-power participants (means 4.78 versus 5.39, p < .001). Power interacted with self-focus (p = .045): the power difference was significant with neutral words (p < .001), but not after self-focus was primed (p = .157). The self-focus prime reduced conformity among subordinates (p < .001), but its effect among managers was nonsignificant (p = .401).
- Study 3 supported H1 and the preference-consistency prediction (Table 2, Model 2): power × ethical climate b = .16 (p = .028), and power × Machiavellianism b = .20 (p = .036). Ethical climate reduced deception at both power levels, but less strongly at high power (b = −.28) than low power (b = −.59). Machiavellianism predicted deception at high power (b = .47, p < .001), but not low power (b = .07, p = .628). H2’s reported indirect-interaction 95% confidence intervals excluded zero for ethical climate [0.025, 0.160] and Machiavellianism [0.016, 0.193].
- Study 4 reported support for H1 (Table 4, Model 2): power × ethical culture b = .08 (p = .031), and power × moral identity b = −.10 (p = .017). Ethical culture was negatively associated with unethical behavior at low power (b = −.13, p = .021), but not high power (b = .04, p = .463); moral identity’s low-power slope was nonsignificant (p = .887). The authors report a negative high-power moral-identity relationship and H2 indirect-interaction 95% confidence intervals of [0.006, 0.042] for culture and [−0.053, −0.005] for identity, but the printed results contain unresolved inconsistencies: p. 650 gives the high-power moral-identity slope as b = .20 alongside t = −3.21 and a stated decrease. On p. 651, the prose assigns b = .18 to self-focus × culture and b = −.20 to self-focus × identity, whereas Table 4 labels those rows −.20 and .18, respectively. These discrepancies prevent treating the detailed Study 4 mechanism coefficients as unambiguous.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper identifies power and private self-focus as an explanation for differential susceptibility to organizational social influences in ethical decision making. It extends power research by testing private self-focus across informational, normative, and compliance settings, and argues that power can promote either ethical or unethical conduct by strengthening reliance on personal preferences rather than uniformly corrupting behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest attending to ethical preferences when selecting people for powerful positions and note that powerful employees may require stronger incentives to attend to organizational ethical systems. They also propose concentrating efforts against unethical social influence on relationships with salient power differences and training employees lacking power to recall personal ethical standards through if–then plans. These organizational interventions are proposed implications, not evaluated workplace programs.

**Limitations**
The parsimonious model does not examine other possible influences such as affiliation needs, characteristics of the influence source, or self-confidence. The authors did not systematically vary moral-issue characteristics or cross ethical versus unethical influence content with decision makers’ preferences, leaving those contingencies open. They also call for more objective behavioral indicators; Study 4’s printed coefficient conflicts are documented above.

**Future Research**
The authors propose testing public self-focus as a possible way to reduce powerful individuals’ disregard of ethical standards, and examining how moral-issue characteristics and combinations of social influence and personal preferences alter the relationships. They also recommend alternative, more objective behavioral measures and examination of downstream consequences, including whether powerful employees with unethical preferences harm their own organizational standing by disregarding enforced ethical policies.
