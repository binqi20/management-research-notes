# Looking Backward Instead of Forward: Aspiration-Driven Influences on the Efficiency of the Capital Allocation Process

**Abstract**
This study examines the influence of backward-looking, reference-dependent decisions on forward-looking capital allocation investment choices across business units within a firm. Specifically, we develop an integrated behavioral framework for predicting how aspirations for business unit performance affect the efficiency of the internal capital allocation process. Results suggest that, contingent on dispersion in business unit performance and on firm slack, performance aspirations have pervasive effects; current performance above and below aspirations influences the efficiency of the allocation process. Given that prior examinations of aspiration-driven behavior have generally focused on changes in strategic organizational actions (e.g., R&D investment) without further considering the quality or appropriateness of such actions, this study contributes by also focusing on the appropriateness of capital allocations with clear implications for allocation efficiency.

**Research Question**
How do backward-looking performance aspirations influence the efficiency of forward-looking capital allocations across business units, and how do performance dispersion, firm slack, and the source of aspirations shape these relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Current business unit performance below aspirations is positively associated with overinvestment.
- H2: Variance in performance across a firm's business units strengthens the positive association between performance shortfalls and overinvestment.
- H3: Firm slack weakens the positive association between performance shortfalls and overinvestment.
- H4: Current business unit performance above aspirations is positively associated with underinvestment.
- H5: Variance in performance across a firm's business units strengthens the positive association between performance above aspirations and underinvestment.
- H6: Firm slack weakens the positive association between performance above aspirations and underinvestment.
- H7: Performance relative to social aspirations is more likely to correspond to overinvestment and underinvestment than performance relative to historical aspirations.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Business unit performance relative to peer social, internal social, and historical aspirations, distinguishing shortfalls from performance above aspirations.
- DV(s): Overinvestment in units with relatively poor growth prospects; underinvestment in units with relatively strong growth prospects.
- Mediators: None tested; aspiration-driven search, escalation of commitment, satisfaction with performance, and protection against performance disruptions are theorized explanations.
- Moderators: Within-firm variance in business unit performance comparisons and firm slack.

The authors argue that backward-looking efforts to correct performance deficiencies can direct additional capital toward poorly performing units despite weak future prospects. Conversely, satisfaction and concern about protecting successful performance can limit investment in units with strong prospects. Greater performance dispersion is predicted to draw attention to discrepancies and amplify these responses, while slack-induced complacency and inertia are predicted to weaken them. Institutional arguments predict greater salience of social than historical aspirations in complex allocation decisions; the empirical results do not support that prediction.

**Data & Measures**
COMPUSTAT Business Segment, Industrial Annual, and Execucomp and Thomson Financial CDA/Spectrum S34 supply data for 1998–2006. Screening yields 8,266 initial business-unit–year observations; missing observations and exclusion of single-business firms reduce estimation samples. Table 3's overinvestment control model has n = 3,126; peer, internal, and historical aspiration models have n = 1,093, 1,051, and 683, respectively. Table 4's underinvestment control model has n = 1,955; corresponding aspiration models have n = 959, 669, and 415.

Investment is business unit capital expenditure divided by unit assets. Unit growth prospects are proxied by average Tobin's q of at least five single-business firms in the narrowest qualifying SIC industry. Units are classified above or below the annual median prospects across business units, excluding the focal firm. Overinvestment occurs when low-prospect units receive more scaled investment than the annual mean for high-prospect units; underinvestment occurs when high-prospect units receive less than the annual mean for low-prospect units. Both inefficiency measures are multiplied by 100 for estimation.

ROA is operating income divided by assets. Peer comparisons subtract average industry peer-unit ROA from focal-unit ROA, using at least five rivals and including single-business and diversified firms; internal comparisons subtract the average ROA of the firm's other units. Historical comparisons subtract the equally weighted average of the unit's prior two years of ROA. Below-aspiration comparisons are reverse-coded. Dispersion is the within-firm variance of each corresponding performance comparison, including the focal unit. Slack is firm operating earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization divided by total assets.

Multilevel mixed models use a one-year lag and separate stable time averages from transient variables. Controls include firm size, institutional ownership, CEO contingent compensation, diversification, environmental dynamism, firm capital expenditure, and CEO turnover; inverse Mills ratios address model-specific sample selection. The archival design estimates associations.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported for all three referents: peer, internal, and historical shortfalls are positively associated with overinvestment (Table 3, Models 2–4: b = 5.62, p < .001; b = 5.66, p < .001; b = 8.30, p < .01, respectively).
- H2 receives support for peer and internal comparisons: dispersion interactions are positive (Table 3, Models 5–6: b = 2.28 and .45, both p < .001). The historical interaction is nonsignificant (Model 7: b = −.17).
- H3 is supported for all three referents: slack weakens the shortfall–overinvestment association (Table 3, Models 5–7: peer b = −1.76, p < .05; internal b = −1.20, p < .001; historical b = −.46, p < .05).
- H4 is supported only for peer comparisons: performance above peer aspirations is positively associated with underinvestment (Table 4, Model 2: b = 3.41, p < .05). Internal and historical comparisons are nonsignificant (Models 3–4: b = .52 and 1.12). H5 and H6 are unsupported: neither dispersion nor slack significantly moderates underinvestment relationships in Models 5–7.
- H7 is unsupported. Historical aspirations have a marginally larger overinvestment association than peer aspirations (z = 1.69, p < .10), contrary to prediction; the historical–internal difference is nonsignificant (z = 1.47). Underinvestment comparisons were not calculated because only peer aspirations were significant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends the behavioral theory of the firm from predicting the occurrence or magnitude of strategic actions to examining their appropriateness: backward-looking performance correction can conflict with forward-looking allocation efficiency. It introduces performance dispersion alongside slack as contextual influences on aspiration-driven allocations and differentiates external peer, internal social, and historical referents. The authors also caution against treating backward-looking organizational routines and forward-looking prospect-theory choices as interchangeable explanations.

**Practical Implication**
The authors highlight that aspiration-related misallocations can involve substantial capital: their illustration estimates average overinvestment of $1.41 million, $1.42 million, and $2.08 million for each .01 change in the ROA shortfall relative to peer, internal, and historical aspirations, respectively. The practical concern is that allocations focused on correcting current problems can favor units with poor future prospects while neglecting stronger opportunities.

**Limitations**
The study assumes aspirations based on business unit ROA, although firms and units may pursue revenue growth, market share, or profit margins instead. Annual performance measures do not resolve whether aspirations adjust annually or only after longer, consistent performance trends. The authors do not examine the consequences of allocation inefficiency for overall firm performance.

**Future Research**
The authors call for examining how capital allocators respond to diverse performance criteria, including revenue, market share, and profit margins. They also propose studying whether aspirations adjust annually or in response to longer and more consistent trends, and how such adjustment affects allocation decisions.
