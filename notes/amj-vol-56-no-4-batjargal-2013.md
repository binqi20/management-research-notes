# Institutional Polycentrism, Entrepreneurs' Social Networks, and New Venture Growth

**Abstract**

What is the interrelationship among formal institutions, social networks, and new venture growth? Drawing on the theory of institutional polycentrism and social network theory, we examine this question using data on 637 entrepreneurs from four different countries. We find the confluence of weak and inefficient formal institutions to be associated with a larger number of structural holes in entrepreneurial social networks. While the effect of this institutional order on the revenue growth of new ventures is negative, a network’s structural holes have a positive effect on revenue growth. Furthermore, the positive effect of structural holes on revenue growth is stronger in an environment with a more adverse institutional order (i.e., weaker and more inefficient institutions). The contributions and implications of these findings are discussed.

**Research Question**

How does the confluence of weak and inefficient political, regulatory, and economic institutions relate to entrepreneurs’ network structural holes and new ventures’ revenue growth? Does this institutional confluence strengthen the relationship between structural holes and revenue growth?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1: The confluence of weak and inefficient institutions positively affects entrepreneurial networks’ structural holes.
- H2: The confluence of weak and inefficient institutions negatively affects new ventures’ revenue growth.
- H3: Entrepreneurial networks’ structural holes positively affect new ventures’ revenue growth.
- H4: The confluence of weak and inefficient institutions positively moderates the structural holes–revenue growth relationship; it is stronger under a more adverse institutional order.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Confluence of weak and inefficient national institutions; entrepreneurs’ network structural holes in the revenue-growth models.
- DV(s): Network structural holes; new-venture revenue growth.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper
- Moderators: Confluence of weak and inefficient institutions moderates the structural holes–revenue growth relationship.

Institutional multiplicity generates interdependent constraints: weak and inefficient institutions reinforce one another, create conflicting demands, and restrict entrepreneurs’ resource access. Institutional substitution explains why entrepreneurs cultivate disconnected contacts as alternative channels for resources and protection. Structural holes are theorized to provide nonredundant information, brokerage opportunities, tangible resources, autonomy, socioemotional support, and opportunities to combine contacts’ resources; these benefits become more valuable under adverse institutional conditions. These are proposed explanations rather than tested mediation pathways.

**Data & Measures**

The study interviewed 637 majority-owner entrepreneurs: 205 in China, 105 in France, 172 in Russia, and 155 in the United States. Ventures were independent, domestically owned, and at most eight years old; women were oversampled, and sampling emphasized metropolitan, growth-oriented ventures. Interviews occurred in summer–fall 2005 in China/Russia, winter 2006 and spring 2007 in the United States, and winter–spring 2007 in France. Methods reports excluding three outliers; Table 4 nevertheless specifies n = 637, without reconciling these statements. Missing revenue growth affected 21% of the sample and was replaced with each country’s mean; the authors report similar results without substitution.

Institutional confluence is a PLS composite based on 20 institutional indicators reduced to political democracy, regulatory control, capital availability, and market liquidity. Political democracy, capital availability, and market liquidity are reverse-coded before the factors are combined; higher composite scores indicate weaker and less efficient institutions. Name generators elicited contacts providing business advice, business resources, or emotional support during the preceding six months, with each contact named once. Structural holes equal one minus Burt’s network-constraint score, calculated with UCINET. Revenue growth uses reported sales for the four years before interview: the difference between consecutive years’ sales is divided by prior-year sales, and the annual growth percentages are then summed and divided by the number of revenue years.

Controls include firm age and full-time employees; entrepreneurs’ age, education, prior managerial experience, and gender; network size, density, women-tie share, and family-tie share; industry munificence/dynamism from 2000–04 sales; and a GLOBE-based culture composite. Network size is excluded from the revenue-growth models because of its high correlation with structural holes. PLS estimates cross-level associations. The sample-selection check estimates participation using Chinese/Russian data and reports a second-stage growth model for the full sample; longer-standing ties provide supplementary evidence concerning reverse causality, rather than experimental identification.

**Key Findings**

- H1 is supported: institutional adversity is positively associated with structural holes (Table 3, Model 2, standardized coefficient = .15, p < .05).
- H2 is supported: institutional adversity is negatively associated with revenue growth (Model 4, standardized coefficient = −.32, p < .001).
- H3 is supported: structural holes are positively associated with revenue growth (Model 5, standardized coefficient = .10, p < .05).
- H4 is supported: the institutions × structural holes interaction is positive (Model 6, standardized coefficient = .18, p < .01); the positive structural-holes association is stronger under more adverse institutions. Model 6 explains .27 of the variance in revenue growth.
- The Heckman-adjusted growth model retains negative institutional and positive structural-hole and interaction coefficients (Table 4: −.39, .16, and .23, respectively; p < .01, p < .001, and p < .01). This check concerns the growth relationships, not a separate replication of H1.
- Post hoc analyses find positive growth correlations for advice networks (r = .18, p < .01) and emotional-support networks (r = .12, p < .01), but no relationship for business-resource networks. These differences qualify a uniform account of network benefits.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper integrates institutional polycentrism and social network theory through institutional multiplicity and substitution, linking the confluence of formal institutions to entrepreneurial networks and venture outcomes. It advances institution-based comparative entrepreneurship research and explains why the value of structural holes is contingent on the institutional order, including networks’ potential to connect entrepreneurs with informal resource holders.

**Practical Implication**

For entrepreneurs, the study highlights social networks as sources of business resources, advice, and emotional support, especially when multiple weak and inefficient institutions create an adverse environment. The authors emphasize the potential of structural holes to substitute for deficient formal support and protect ventures against dysfunctional institutions, while recognizing their potential value in stronger institutional settings too.

**Limitations**

Differences across countries in sampling and data-validation procedures may introduce error variance, although the authors found no evidence of systematic effects. The sample may contain surviving ventures; the authors argue this concern is limited because the objective is financial performance rather than survival and the firm-age distribution is not skewed toward older firms. Revenue growth is only one success indicator, and employment growth or product development could yield different results.

**Future Research**

The authors call for examining national/local and formal/informal institutional relationships, dynamic configurations and context-specific rules, possible inverse U-shaped effects, and more comprehensive measures distinguishing polycentrism from complexity and pluralism. They also propose testing distinct network types and relational bases, and unpacking how culture and social-exchange processes shape entrepreneurs’ network benefits.
