# Lords of the Harvest: Third-Party Influence and Regulatory Approval of Genetically Modified Organisms

**Abstract**
Little is known about the factors that influence regulatory agency decision making. We posit that regulatory agencies are influenced by the firms they regulate but not exclusively via dyadic exchanges, as is traditionally argued in the regulatory capture and business-government literatures. Instead, regulatory decisions are indirectly shaped via third-party actors who shield agencies from legitimacy threats. Focusing empirically on the United States Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) approval of genetically modified organisms (GMOs), we find that product assessments by powerful stakeholders and peer agencies influence product approval and that their effects vary under different threats. We also discuss the implications of these findings for business-government relations, nonmarket strategy, and organization theory.

**Research Question**
How do powerful stakeholders and peer agencies influence regulatory agency decision making, and how does their influence vary with threats to pragmatic and procedural legitimacy? The study examines USDA approval of genetically modified plant products.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Positive assessments from powerful stakeholders increase the likelihood of product approval.
- H2: Positive assessments from peer agencies increase the likelihood of product approval.
- H3: Powerful stakeholders exert greater influence when political conditions, such as competitive congressional races, heighten threats to pragmatic legitimacy.
- H4: Powerful stakeholders exert greater influence when public controversies, such as collective protests, heighten threats to pragmatic legitimacy.
- H5: Peer agencies exert greater influence when limited regulatory supervision, as with products developed by non-US companies, heightens threats to procedural legitimacy.
- H6: Peer agencies exert greater influence when risky products, such as GMOs embodying novel technologies, heighten threats to procedural legitimacy.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Farm-association requests for product approval; positive FDA consultation memoranda.
- DV(s): Rate of USDA approval of GMO petitions.
- Mediators: None tested; protection of pragmatic and procedural legitimacy is the proposed mechanism.
- Moderators: Congressional electoral competitiveness and GMO protests for farm-association influence; foreign-company origin and technological novelty for FDA influence.

Regulatory agencies seek legitimacy to protect their autonomy, resources, and responsibilities. Powerful stakeholders can shield pragmatic legitimacy by supporting decisions and countering dissatisfied audiences; peer agencies can shield procedural legitimacy by supplying credible expertise and information for scientific review. The authors argue that the value of each shield increases with its corresponding legitimacy threat, creating opportunities for firms to influence regulators indirectly through third parties.

**Data & Measures**
The sample covers all 113 GMO approval petitions submitted by 29 companies from June 1992 through December 31, 2007: 73 were approved, 28 were not, and 12 remained pending. USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service records provide petition dates, outcomes, firm identities, and product characteristics. Cox models examine time to approval with firm fixed effects; the design is observational and does not establish an experimentally identified causal effect.

Powerful-stakeholder influence is the number of farm-association letters requesting approval, each representing an association. Peer-agency influence is a binary indicator of an FDA consultation memorandum received before the USDA decision; these memoranda declared nutritional labeling acceptable. Congressional electoral competitiveness averages Congressional Quarterly's ratings of House and Senate races, from 0 (safe seat) to 3 (no clear favorite), into an annual score. GMO protests count annual anti-GMO protests reported in US newspapers in Lexis-Nexis; Table 2 uses logged values. Foreign-company origin is binary, as is whether a GMO's genetic transformation differs fundamentally from that of a previously approved organism.

Controls cover firm size, patent-citation prestige, failed petitions, confidential information, field tests, testing duration, scientific-publication volume and tenor, firm-sponsored publications, phenotype variety, plant-incorporated protectants, submission density, congressional conservatism, consumer opposition letters, and firm press releases. Scientific-publication volume and press-release counts are also logged in Table 2. Campaign donations were examined for 1999–2007 but excluded from final models because they were nonsignificant and available for only part of the study period.

**Key Findings**
Table 2 reports positive associations for H1 (farm-association requests: model 2, b = 0.98, p < .05) and H2 (FDA memorandum: model 3, b = 1.64, p < .01). H3 is supported by the positive stakeholder-request × electoral-competitiveness interaction (model 4, b = 0.52, p < .05). H4's stakeholder-request × protest interaction is marginal in model 5 (b = 0.42, p < .10), becoming significant in the full model 8 (b = 1.29, p < .05).

H5's FDA-memorandum × foreign-company interaction is positive (model 6, b = 2.53, p < .05). H6's FDA-memorandum × novel-technology interaction is positive but marginal (model 7, b = 2.14, p < .10; model 8, b = 2.23, p < .10). In model 8, the H3 and H5 interactions remain positive and significant (b = 0.85 and 2.64, respectively; both p < .05). Although the authors describe all hypotheses as supported, the table distinguishes the marginal H4 result in model 5 and marginal H6 results from conventional significance.

The authors report that unshown models found stakeholder influence increasing with external but not internal risks, and peer-agency influence increasing with internal but not external risks. Excluding the 12 pending petitions produced no significant change in results. Direct influence through lobbying of elected officials and press-release advocacy did not show significant effects on agency decisions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study moves business-government analysis beyond dyadic regulatory capture toward agencies as social actors protecting legitimacy through third parties. It distinguishes powerful stakeholders' shielding of pragmatic legitimacy from peer agencies' shielding of procedural legitimacy, linking stakeholder influence to political contingencies. It also brings agency into institutional accounts of the state and identifies an indirect route for firms' nonmarket strategies.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that regulated firms can complement direct tactics with connections to influential third parties, illustrated by farm-association support and FDA consultations in agricultural biotechnology. They propose that such tactics may also be feasible in public utilities, oil and gas, and pharmaceuticals; these sectoral extensions were not tested in this study.

**Limitations**
The authors could not control for every possible direct influence tactic used by biotechnology firms, and the analysis covered genetically modified plants rather than animals. Controls for prevalent tactics and phenotype characteristics only partially address these limitations.

**Future Research**
The authors call for more comprehensive investigation of firm influence tactics, product-specific stakeholder pressures, and the relationships and relative importance of direct and indirect strategies. They also propose examining stakeholder interdependencies and how regulatory discretion and variation in policy implementation shape the emergence and adoption of organizational forms and technologies, as well as agencies' reciprocal influence with environmental actors.
