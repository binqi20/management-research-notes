# Support, Undermining, and Newcomer Socialization: Fitting in During the First 90 Days

**Abstract**
While much organizational socialization occurs through interpersonal interactions, evidence regarding how these processes unfold over time has not been forthcoming. Results from a 14-wave longitudinal study with a sample of 264 organizational newcomers show that support of newcomers from coworkers and supervisors declines within the first 90 days of employment. Early support and undermining had more significant relationships with work outcomes assessed after 90 days of employment than did increases or decreases in support and undermining over that time period, suggesting early support and undermining may lay a foundation for later work outcomes. Proactive behavior partially mediated the relationship between support and more distal work outcomes, including withdrawal behaviors. Supervisor undermining was uniquely associated with higher turnover (exit) hazard.

**Research Question**
How do initial levels and changes in supervisor and coworker support and undermining relate to newcomer adjustment during the first 90 days of employment? Do hedonic tone and proactive socialization mediate their relationships with subsequent work outcomes and voluntary turnover?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Initial supervisor and coworker support are positively related to initial newcomer hedonic tone.
- H2: Trends in supervisor and coworker support are positively related to trends in hedonic tone over the first 90 days.
- H3: Initial supervisor and coworker undermining are negatively related to initial hedonic tone.
- H4: Trends in supervisor and coworker undermining are negatively related to trends in hedonic tone over the first 90 days.
- H5: Initial supervisor and coworker support are positively related to initial proactive socialization behaviors.
- H6: Trends in supervisor and coworker support are positively related to trends in proactive socialization over the first 90 days.
- H7: Initial supervisor and coworker undermining are negatively related to initial proactive socialization behaviors.
- H8: Trends in supervisor and coworker undermining are negatively related to trends in proactive socialization over the first 90 days.
- H9: Hedonic tone mediates relationships between supervisor and coworker support and undermining and work proactivity, social integration, organizational commitment, withdrawal behaviors, and voluntary turnover.
- H10: Proactive socialization behaviors mediate those relationships with the same five outcomes.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Supervisor support, coworker support, supervisor undermining, and coworker undermining, distinguishing initial levels from trajectories over time.
- DV(s): Work proactivity, social integration, organizational commitment, withdrawal behaviors, and voluntary turnover.
- Mediators: Hedonic tone and proactive socialization behaviors, each represented by initial status and change.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper

Affective events theory treats supportive and undermining encounters as salient events that newcomers appraise while making sense of an unfamiliar workplace. Support signals acceptance and provides a safer setting for seeking feedback and building relationships; undermining actively obstructs progress and signals rejection, rather than merely representing absent support. Positive mood and proactive socialization are proposed pathways to adjustment, with early impressions and subsequent changes potentially having distinct relationships with outcomes. The design tests longitudinal associations and indirect paths, not experimentally identified causal effects.

**Data & Measures**
One university supplied hiring records for 906 potential participants; 316 completed an introductory survey, 264 entered the reported ongoing/model sample, and 255 were reported surveyed through all 14 rounds. The nonfaculty sample worked over 30 hours weekly and spanned 37 hiring cohorts across a 51-week sampling period. The introductory survey arrived within two weeks of employment, followed by weekly surveys over three months; terminal survey outcomes were measured at 90–100 days on a separate occasion from predictors and mediators. The source's reported exclusion counts do not reconcile with its recruitment and retention totals (p. 1111).

Support and undermining were measured separately for supervisors and coworkers, each with five past-week items from Vinokur et al. Hedonic tone used eight mood adjectives, with unpleasant items reverse-scored so higher values indicate more positive tone. Proactive socialization comprised four information/feedback-seeking items, three coworker-socializing items, and three boss-relationship-building items. Terminal work proactivity used nine items, social integration seven, organizational commitment nine, and withdrawal ten; the survey measures used five-point scales. Voluntary turnover and last workday came from organizational records collected one year after the final survey. Initial trait neuroticism was controlled in all analyses.

Nonlinear latent growth models separated initial status and change, allowing time loadings to vary within a range from 0 at the first round to 1 at the last after linear models fit poorly. Direct paths were retained when testing indirect effects. Turnover hazard models used repeated, time-varying predictors and job duration in days; these analyses were separate from the growth models.

**Key Findings**
- Average supervisor support, coworker support, and hedonic tone declined; average undermining and proactive socialization did not change significantly, although trajectories varied between people (Table 2).
- H1 was supported: initial supervisor and coworker support related positively to initial hedonic tone (respectively, γ = .15, p < .05; γ = .29, p < .01). H2 was partially supported: supervisor support change related positively to hedonic-tone change (γ = .51; Table 3 marks p < .01, while the prose reports p < .05); coworker support change did not. H3 was supported only for coworker undermining (γ = −.34, p < .01); H4 was unsupported (Table 3).
- H5 and H6 were supported: supervisor/coworker support related positively to proactive socialization both initially (γ = .50/.28) and in changes (γ = .42/.23; all p < .01). Neither initial undermining nor its changes predicted proactive socialization significantly: H7 and H8 were unsupported (Table 4).
- H9 and H10 received partial support. Table 6 reports significant initial-support indirect paths through initial hedonic tone only for commitment (supervisor .05, p < .05; coworker .10, p < .01). Through initial proactive socialization, supervisor/coworker support had positive indirect paths to work proactivity (.16/.09, both p < .01) and commitment (.07/.04, both p < .05), and negative paths to withdrawal (−.07, p < .05/−.04, p < .01). No indirect path involving slopes, undermining, or social integration is marked significant in Table 6.
- Direct paths differed by source (Table 5): initial supervisor undermining related positively to social integration (γ = .29, p < .01), whereas coworker undermining related negatively (γ = −.38, p < .01). Initial coworker undermining related positively to withdrawal (γ = .27, p < .01); initial supervisor support had a positive withdrawal path (γ = .15, p < .05), while coworker support had a negative one (γ = −.18, p < .05).
- Repeated supervisor undermining predicted higher voluntary-turnover hazard (hazard ratio = 3.01 without the mediators; 2.79 with them; both p < .01). Other focal predictors and the mediators were nonsignificant, providing no evidence of turnover mediation. An alternative model using only initial support and undermining was nonsignificant overall and for every coefficient (Table 7 and accompanying prose).

The source contains reporting inconsistencies: the results narrative claims mediation for all four survey outcomes and coworker undermining–commitment, beyond Table 6's significance pattern; the discussion discounts hedonic-tone mediation despite the table's significant commitment paths. The closing summary says coworker undermining decreased withdrawal, contrary to Table 5's positive coefficient. The detailed findings above retain the table-specific results and these discrepancies.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study brings supervisor and coworker behaviors into a common socialization model, distinguishing active undermining from absent support and initial experiences from subsequent trajectories. It frames newcomer proactivity as partly responsive to the social environment rather than solely self-initiated, with initial proactive socialization emerging as a mediator for several outcomes. The authors emphasize early impressions while also identifying continuing support and repeated supervisor undermining as relevant to adjustment and turnover.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend training supervisors to provide feedback and support throughout the initial 90-day adjustment period, and training coworkers to support newcomers as a regular in-role responsibility. They also recommend acknowledging and minimizing undermining. These are proposed management implications, not interventions tested by the study.

**Limitations**
The authors identify self-report measures and possible common-method variance, while noting neuroticism controls, temporal separation, and archival turnover as safeguards. Pre-entry organizational knowledge was not measured, and generalizability requires further examination despite participants' diverse work groups within the university. The observational design does not establish that support or undermining caused the measured outcomes.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining affective versus instrumental support, why support declines and its consequences, and whether newcomers seek information consistent with first impressions. They also suggest studying support and undermining as moderators of entry stressors, individual differences such as rejection sensitivity, and the role of pre-entry knowledge. Supervisor and coworker reports could be compared with newcomers' perceptions over time.
