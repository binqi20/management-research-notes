# Access Denied: Low Mentoring of Women and Minority First-Time Directors and Its Negative Effects on Appointments to Additional Boards

**Abstract**
This study contributes to the literature on women and minorities in corporate leadership by developing theory that can help to explain the persistent underrepresentation of women and minorities among those who are seen as members of the “corporate elite” because they hold multiple corporate board seats. Our conceptual framework suggests how disadvantages in the receipt of mentoring regarding prevailing norms in the corporate elite are negatively affecting the ability of women and minorities to secure multiple board appointments. Our theory explains why women and minority first-time directors receive comparatively less mentoring regarding a core norm in the corporate elite that outside directors should avoid exercising independent control over firm strategy. Our theory also explains why lower levels of mentoring result in women and racial minority first-time directors receiving relatively fewer appointments to other boards. This study also contributes to the corporate leadership literature by explaining how fundamental intergroup biases are negatively impacting the demographic diversity of the corporate elite. This article further highlights a specific social mechanism that undermines efforts to move toward more meritocratic outcomes in corporate leadership whereby those who are relatively qualified will have greater success in rising to the highest-level positions in the corporate world.

**Research Question**
Why do women and racial minority first-time directors receive less mentoring about accepted participation in board deliberations, and how does this disadvantage relate to obtaining additional board seats? Does the presence of incumbent directors from the corresponding demographic group mitigate the mentoring disadvantage?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Female first-time directors receive less participation process mentoring from incumbents than male first-time directors.
- H1b: Racial minority first-time directors receive less participation process mentoring from incumbents than white first-time directors.
- H2a: A female incumbent director's presence makes the negative relationship between female status and mentoring less negative.
- H2b: A racial minority incumbent director's presence makes the negative relationship between racial minority status and mentoring less negative.
- H3: First-time directors receiving less participation process mentoring receive fewer appointments to additional boards.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): First-time directors' female and racial minority status; participation process mentoring in models of subsequent appointments.
- DV(s): Participation process mentoring received; subsequent appointments to other corporate boards.
- Mediators: Participation process mentoring links demographic status to appointments; supplementary analyses test decision-control behavior as a further intervening variable between mentoring and appointments.
- Moderators: Presence of a female incumbent for female first-time directors, and presence of a racial minority incumbent for racial minority first-time directors.

The authors argue that predominantly white male incumbents show greater empathy and helping toward demographically similar newcomers, favoring their in-group without necessarily displaying overt hostility toward others. Mentoring communicates tacit expectations to obtain the CEO's approval before raising strategic concerns and to provide advice without challenging the CEO's primacy in strategy making. Less mentoring is expected to make newcomers less able to follow these norms, weakening incumbent support for additional appointments; corresponding minority incumbents can mitigate the mentoring disadvantage. The argument concerns normatively acceptable participation, rather than complete withdrawal from strategic discussion.

**Data & Measures**
The main sampling frame contained 3,079 first-time directors appointed during 1999–2006 at the 2,000 largest publicly held US companies with available archival data. Of 1,305 respondents (42%), 1,152 had at least one responding incumbent colleague; Tables 3–4 use this latter sample, while Table 5's decision-control paths use 1,124 directors. Initial surveys occurred six months after appointment, with follow-ups one and two years after appointment, extending through 2008. The study is observational; temporal sequencing and controls do not constitute experimental identification.

Participation process mentoring combines three continuous indicators of mentoring occasions and minutes, including amounts aggregated across incumbent mentors, with four binary indicators of advice about specific participation norms (Table 1; α = .92). Director gender/race and corresponding incumbent presence are binary indicators; interactions use their products. The principal outcome counts additional appointments at other firms in the population during the two years after the initial survey. Decision-control behavior is measured at the one-year follow-up for the preceding six months, using averaged incumbent-director reports (α = .90).

Controls differ by outcome and include social ties, elite social credentials, management experience, firm performance, and year indicators. Mentoring models additionally include board independence, log sales, and industry indicators; appointment models include perceived strategic knowledge, provision of advice/information, and incumbents' seats on other firms' nominating committees. Separate supplementary samples include 188 first-time directors in 2010 for mentoring-measure validation, and 159 respondents in 2009 and 147 in 2010 for analyses of participation-norm awareness.

**Key Findings**
- H1a and H1b were supported: Table 3, model 1 reports negative coefficients for woman status (b = −0.25) and racial minority status (b = −0.36), both p ≤ .001.
- H2a and H2b were supported: Table 3, model 2 reports positive interactions with corresponding incumbent presence for women (b = 0.39, p ≤ .01) and racial minorities (b = 1.13, p ≤ .001), indicating weaker mentoring disadvantages.
- H3 was supported: mentoring positively predicted subsequent appointment counts (Table 4: b = 0.20, p ≤ .001). Tables 3–4 use one-tailed tests for hypothesized effects and two-tailed tests for controls. Conditional on mentoring and the other controls, the residual coefficients for racial minority status (−0.05), woman status (−0.10), and their interactions with corresponding incumbent presence were nonsignificant in Table 4.
- Table 5 supports indirect paths through mentoring from racial minority status (z = −3.55, p ≤ .001) and woman status (z = −3.09, p ≤ .01) to appointments. Serial paths through mentoring and decision-control behavior were also significant for racial minority status (z = −3.16, p ≤ .01) and woman status (z = −2.68, p ≤ .01); both incumbent-presence interactions had significant positive indirect paths in both specifications. The pattern is consistent with less mentoring, greater decision-control behavior, and fewer appointments.
- Supplementary analyses associated mentoring with incumbent recommendations for additional seats and supported mediation through compliance with participation norms. Race and gender did not significantly predict seeking mentoring; controlling for advice seeking left the hypothesized results unchanged. In the separate norm-awareness surveys, prior top management experience did not significantly predict awareness after controlling for mentoring.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper explains how unequal access to mentoring can impede women's and racial minorities' movement from an initial directorship into the corporate elite of multiple-board holders. It extends explanations centered on selection bias or a shortage of qualified candidates by identifying intergroup favoritism in the transmission of tacit participation norms as a mechanism associated with unequal career opportunities. The findings also challenge a purely meritocratic account of advancement, since demographic minority directors showed stronger qualifications on the study's indicators while receiving less mentoring.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that unequal access to mentoring may compromise the quality of director contributions by disadvantaging relatively qualified women and minorities in obtaining additional seats. This implication for board effectiveness is conditional on prevailing board norms allowing qualified directors to make positive contributions; the study does not directly estimate an effect on board effectiveness or firm performance.

**Limitations**
The primary analyses do not fully address whether prior management experience provides knowledge of board participation norms, although supplementary surveys offer evidence against this explanation. Survey reports are partly subjective, and asking about mentoring could itself affect behavior; the authors report reliability, validity, and supplementary checks that reduce these concerns. Generalization beyond large publicly held US firms is uncertain, particularly where norms concerning independent director control differ.

**Future Research**
The authors propose further investigation of management experience, sponsorship, more objective measures, and other director populations. They also suggest extending the mentoring account to influential board-committee and executive positions, other minority dimensions such as nationality, other forms of help, and participation in group decision making at lower organizational levels. Further work should clarify the firm- and board-level consequences of gender and racial diversity.
