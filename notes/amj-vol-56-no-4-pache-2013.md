# Inside the Hybrid Organization: Selective Coupling as a Response to Competing Institutional Logics

**Abstract**
This article explores how hybrid organizations, which incorporate competing institutional logics, internally manage the logics that they embody. Relying on an inductive comparative case study of four work integration social enterprises embedded in competing social welfare and commercial logics, we show that, instead of adopting strategies of decoupling or compromising, as the literature typically suggests, these organizations selectively coupled intact elements prescribed by each logic. This strategy allowed them to project legitimacy to external stakeholders without having to engage in costly deceptions or negotiations. We further identify a specific hybridization pattern that we refer to as “Trojan horse,” whereby organizations that entered the work integration field with low legitimacy because of their embeddedness in the commercial logic strategically incorporated elements from the social welfare logic in an attempt to gain legitimacy and acceptance. Surprisingly, they did so more than comparable organizations originating from the social welfare logic. These findings suggest that, when lacking legitimacy in a given field, hybrids may manipulate the templates provided by the multiple logics in which they are embedded in an attempt to gain acceptance. Overall, our findings contribute to a better understanding of how organizations can survive and thrive when embedded in pluralistic institutional environments.

**Research Question**
How do hybrid organizations manage competing institutional logics at the intraorganizational level, and what determines the strategies through which they incorporate those logics?

**Mechanism Process**

- Key constructs / tensions: Persistent social welfare and commercial logics prescribe conflicting organizational means; the French state resolves conflict over goals by requiring WISEs to prioritize their social mission.
- Practices: Selective coupling combines intact prescriptions from both logics at the organizational level, alongside less frequent practice-level decoupling and compromise.
- Triggering conditions: Commercial origins threaten initial legitimacy in a field dominated by a social mission; social-sector origins confer greater initial acceptance.
- Emergent outcomes: The “Trojan horse” pattern involves substantive adoption of social welfare practices by commercial-origin entrants seeking acceptance; initially legitimate social-origin organizations have more freedom to combine both logics.

Selective coupling entails actual enactment of selected practices, rather than merely ceremonial compliance or modification of every conflicting prescription. The authors interpret founding origins as shaping legitimacy constraints and, through them, the combination of practices organizations adopt. The resulting combinations can satisfy different institutional referents while remaining constrained by the need for legitimacy.

**Data & Measures**
Four nationally scaled French WISEs were selected from a broader eight-organization project. The matched pairs were SOCYCLE/BUSITECH in recycling and TEMPORG/WORK&CO in temporary work; the first member of each pair had social-sector origins and the second commercial origins. Table 1 reports 17, 12, 12 and 7 case interviews for SOCYCLE, TEMPORG, BUSITECH and WORK&CO respectively. The corpus totals 48 case interviews, 14 field-expert interviews and more than 2,000 archival pages. Collection comprised exploratory interviews in 2005, field-level work in 2006–07, further case work in 2007–08 and follow-up conversations/e-mails in 2009.

Analysis first characterized the logics through goals, organizational form, control and professional legitimacy, triangulating field material with prior research and two field experts. Within-case and cross-case analysis then identified ten organizational elements: legal status, ownership, profit destination, site form, site governance, procedure localization, brand, monitoring, professional affiliation and volunteer mobilization. Table 3 specifies indicators such as incorporation status, surplus distribution, procedure enforcement, brand uniformity and royalties funding central monitoring. Enactments were compared with each logic’s prescriptions and classified as intact compliance or alternative behavior involving decoupling/compromise. Early scaling-up configurations were compared with 2007; Tables 4–6 report the latter. This is inductive comparative evidence, not an estimated causal effect.

**Key Findings**

- Selective coupling predominated, but decoupling and compromise were not absent. SOCYCLE used one decoupling and one compromise; TEMPORG used two compromises; BUSITECH and WORK&CO each used one decoupling and two compromises (Tables 4–5).
- Table 6 reports intact social welfare/commercial demands of 5/4 for SOCYCLE, 3/6 for TEMPORG, 6/1 for BUSITECH and 6/2 for WORK&CO. These counts concern ten organizational elements, with dual professional affiliations permitting compliance with both logics on the same element; the authors do not assume equivalence among elements.
- Commercial-origin organizations adopted more social welfare demands than their social-origin counterparts. Informants’ accounts connect this “Trojan horse” pattern to initial resistance and legitimacy deficits; the initially legitimate social-origin cases incorporated more commercial prescriptions. The comparison did not identify industry differences as shaping these patterns.
- The paper reports that 86 percent of demands enacted during early scaling-up remained in place in 2007. The authors interpret the configurations as lasting commitments; they explicitly caution that comparisons with the general population do not establish direct performance implications.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study explains the internal construction of hybrids through organizational-level selective coupling of intact elements, extending accounts centered on practice-level decoupling and compromise. Its “Trojan horse” pattern challenges a simple imprinting expectation: founding origins matter through legitimacy constraints, allowing commercial-origin hybrids to enact more social welfare prescriptions than socially founded counterparts. Hybrids’ access to multiple institutional templates affords agency, but their ability to use that repertoire remains bounded by legitimacy.

**Practical Implication**
For hybrid organizations, the authors suggest that avoiding conflict over goals and strategically combining intact elements can help lessen internal conflict over means. They also suggest distinguishing highly visible practices that signal goals and motivations from less symbolically loaded elements that can be combined more freely, while recognizing constraints imposed by legitimacy.

**Limitations**
When generalizing from the case setting, the authors suggest distinguishing organizations that voluntarily embrace competing logics from those forced into conflict, for example by regulation; the latter may be less willing or able to use selective coupling. The authors also caution against drawing direct performance implications from comparisons between their cases and the general population of organizations.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how hybrids configure bundles of elements, including differences in practice visibility, practices’ importance to different audiences and audience influence. The authors also call for comparative studies of survival and performance against single-logic, decoupling and compromising organizations, and investigation of leaders’ ability to combine competing logics.
