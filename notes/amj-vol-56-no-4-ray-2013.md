# Impact of Information Technology Capital on Firm Scope and Performance: The Role of Asset Characteristics

**Abstract**
This research draws on transaction cost and resource-based theory to examine how information technology (IT) capital moderates the relationship between different types of assets and firm scope—both vertical integration and diversification. The analysis suggests that IT capital enables firms with narrowly valuable assets to be less vertically integrated and less diversified, and enables firms with broadly valuable assets to be more vertically integrated and more diversified. The moderating influence of IT capital on the relationship between different types of assets and firms’ vertical and product-market scope is consistent with transaction cost as well as resource-based traditions.

**Research Question**
How does IT capital moderate the relationships between narrowly or broadly valuable assets and firms’ vertical integration and diversification, and how do these combinations relate to firm performance?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: In the presence of more IT capital, narrowly valuable assets are associated with lower vertical integration.
- H1b: In the presence of more IT capital, narrowly valuable assets are associated with lower diversification.
- H2a: In the presence of more IT capital, broadly valuable assets are associated with higher vertical integration.
- H2b: In the presence of more IT capital, broadly valuable assets are associated with higher diversification.
- H3a: In less vertically integrated firms, IT capital enhances the performance contribution of narrowly valuable assets to a greater extent.
- H3b: In less diversified firms, IT capital enhances the performance contribution of narrowly valuable assets to a greater extent.
- H4a: In more vertically integrated firms, IT capital enhances the performance contribution of broadly valuable assets to a greater extent.
- H4b: In more diversified firms, IT capital enhances the performance contribution of broadly valuable assets to a greater extent.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Narrowly valuable assets, proxied by physical and R&D capital; broadly valuable assets, proxied by relational and organizational capital. The simultaneous system also models scope as a predictor of IT capital.
- DV(s): Vertical integration, horizontal complementarity, related diversification, and unrelated diversification; return on assets in the performance analysis; IT capital in the reciprocal equation.
- Mediators: No separately estimated mediators; electronic brokerage and electronic integration are the proposed mechanisms.
- Moderators: IT capital moderates asset–scope relationships; vertical integration and diversification condition the performance contributions of IT–asset combinations. Supplemental models use SCM and ERP implementation as specific IT measures.

The electronic brokerage effect reduces the costs of identifying, contracting with, and monitoring external partners, allowing firms with narrowly valuable assets to specialize without relying as heavily on hierarchy to protect against opportunism. The electronic integration effect reduces internal coordination costs, allowing firms to deploy broadly valuable assets across vertically integrated and diversified activities. Physical and R&D capital are treated as relatively narrow on average, rather than inherently narrow in every application; relational and organizational capital are relatively flexible. The theorized performance benefits depend on matching IT, asset characteristics, and scope.

**Data & Measures**
The aggregate panel contains 4,440 observations on 959 Fortune 1000 firms during 2002–2009, linking Harte-Hanks’ CI Technology Database, COMPUSTAT and its segment database, and BEA input-output tables. The paper identifies CI coverage as United States and Canadian sites but does not report a sample-country breakdown.

- Aggregate IT capital: value hardware at implicit prices, deflate using BEA indices, add three times IT labor cost (IT employees multiplied by industry-average compensation), divide by total employees, then take the logarithm.
- Asset proxies: physical capital is plant, property, and equipment/sales; R&D and relational capital are, respectively, the past five years’ average R&D and advertising expenditures/sales. Organizational capital starts with the market–book value difference divided by book value of total assets, removes industry-average Tobin’s q through regression, then removes R&D, relational, and IT capital from that residual; the final residual represents organizational capital. Missing R&D and advertising expenditures are replaced with industry means.
- Scope: the primary segment has the highest sales. Vertical integration is the secondary-sales-weighted sum of primary–secondary industry relatedness, each pair’s relatedness averaging its two directional input-output coefficients. Horizontal complementarity uses the same weights after averaging each pair’s input- and output-distribution correlations across other industries. Weights divide each secondary segment’s sales by total secondary-segment sales. Entropy measures distinguish related diversification within two-digit NAICS groups from unrelated diversification across groups.
- Performance: pretax income/total assets. Firms are split above/below the median of their average scope across years, and IT–asset interaction coefficients are compared between low- and high-scope groups.

The observational design uses three-stage least squares to address joint determination of IT and scope, with lagged and industry IT, industry operating conditions, and industry-membership instruments. Interacted variables are mean-centered; Huber–White estimation addresses repeated observations within firms. Scope controls include capital investment, capital age, industry concentration, dynamism, munificence, capital intensity, employees, and year dummies. Performance controls include capital investment, debt/equity, market share, the four industry attributes, industry-average ROA, employees, and year dummies.

The SCM supplement uses 1,088 observations on 391 firms during 2000–2003 from InformationWeek 500, measuring the percentage of suppliers connected through electronic SCM systems. The ERP supplement uses 3,880 observations on 912 firms during 2003–2009 from CI, aggregating site installation indicators with revenue weights. These models examine SCM with vertical integration and ERP with horizontal complementarity, add aggregate IT as an instrument, and do not reestimate the performance equation.

**Key Findings**
- Aggregate scope models (Table 2): H1a receives support for physical capital (IT interaction b = −0.002, p < .01) and marginal support for R&D capital (b = −0.01, p < .10). For H1b, both interactions are negative for horizontal complementarity and related diversification (p < .01), but nonsignificant for unrelated diversification.
- H2a is supported for relational capital (b = 0.01, p < .01); the organizational-capital interaction is nonsignificant. For H2b, relational-capital interactions are positive for horizontal complementarity (b = 0.03, p < .01), related diversification (b = 0.02, p < .10), and unrelated diversification (b = 0.01, p < .10). Organizational-capital interactions are positive for related diversification (b = 0.001, p < .10) and unrelated diversification (b = 0.002, p < .05), but nonsignificant for horizontal complementarity.
- Performance models (Table 3): the authors report support for H3a and H3b, with larger IT–physical/R&D interaction coefficients in low-scope groups and between-group differences at p < .01 for vertical integration and each diversification measure. H4a is supported: IT–relational/organizational interaction coefficients are higher in the high-vertical-integration group (differences p < .01). H4b receives mixed support: relational capital supports each diversification comparison, and organizational capital supports horizontal complementarity, but its related-diversification comparison is reversed (low group b = 0.00; high group b = −0.01; difference p < .01); both organizational-capital coefficients for unrelated diversification are nonsignificant. The reported R&D comparison under unrelated diversification is significant even though both displayed interaction coefficients round to 0.02.
- The authors describe the fixed-effects scope results as substantively consistent (Table 4), but individual significance patterns differ: for example, IT × R&D for vertical integration, IT × physical capital for related diversification, and IT × relational capital for horizontal complementarity are nonsignificant in that specification.
- Supplements (Table 5): SCM × physical capital and SCM × R&D capital are negatively associated with vertical integration (b = −0.08 and −0.19, respectively; p < .01); the other SCM interactions are nonsignificant. ERP × relational capital and ERP × organizational capital are positively associated with horizontal complementarity (b = 0.95, p < .05; b = 0.07, p < .01); ERP interactions with physical and R&D capital are nonsignificant. These tests support aspects of H1/H2, without testing their performance implications.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconciles competing predictions about IT and firm scope by making the breadth of asset value central to the argument: electronic brokerage favors specialization with narrow assets, while electronic integration favors broader deployment of flexible assets. By considering production efficiencies alongside transaction and coordination costs, it develops common ground between transaction cost economics and the resource-based view.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend matching IT investments to asset characteristics: firms with narrowly valuable assets should use electronic brokerage technologies, such as open electronic data interchange and SCM systems, to facilitate specialization. Firms with broadly valuable assets should use proprietary intraorganizational information systems and ERP systems to facilitate integration and diversification and exploit economies of scale and scope.

**Limitations**
The asset proxies are admittedly coarse, although they permit a cross-industry analysis. Most tests use aggregate IT capital despite the variety of technologies supporting brokerage or integration; the specific SCM and ERP examples cover only parts of the hypotheses and do not test the performance model.

**Future Research**
The authors suggest that industry-specific measures of narrowly and broadly valuable assets may yield stronger results. They likewise call for more disaggregated measures of electronic-brokerage and electronic-integration IT capital.
