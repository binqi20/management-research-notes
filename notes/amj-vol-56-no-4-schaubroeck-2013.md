# Developing Trust with Peers and Leaders: Impacts on Organizational Identification and Performance during Entry

**Abstract**
This study extends existing research about how peers and leaders influence newcomers’ adjustment to an organization or profession by examining how specific trust perceptions evolve over time. We test a model of how affect-based trust in a leader and work unit peers develops from a basis of cognition-based trust and later influences organizational identification and role-related performance. U.S. Army soldiers were examined at the beginning, middle, and end of an intensive, 14-week residential entry program of training and collective socialization. Cross-lagged structural equation analyses supported a causal relationship of individuals’ cognition-based trust with affect-based trust directed toward their unit peers and, separately, their leaders. Individuals with high levels of chronic relational identity exhibited a stronger time-lagged relationship between cognition-based trust and affect-based trust for trust in peers but not for trust in a leader. Affect-based trust in the leader had lagged influences on organizational identification and role-related performance at time 3. Affect-based trust in peers was related over time to organizational identification but not to role-related performance. We discuss the implications of these findings for understanding the separate influences of social exchange and social identity processes on newcomer adjustment, with distinct roles played by peers and leaders.

**Research Question**
How does newcomers’ cognition-based trust develop into affect-based trust in peers and leaders, and how are these relationships linked over time to organizational identification and role-related performance? Does chronic relational identity strengthen the development of affect-based trust?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a–b: Time-lagged effects of cognition-based trust on affect-based trust are stronger than the reverse effects for trust in work unit peers (H1a) and a leader (H1b).
- H2a–b: Higher chronic relational identity strengthens the time-lagged relationship between cognition-based and affect-based trust in peers (H2a) and a leader (H2b).
- H3a–b: Affect-based trust in peers (H3a) and a leader (H3b) has positive time-lagged relationships with organizational identification.
- H4a–b: Affect-based trust in peers (H4a) and a leader (H4b) has positive time-lagged relationships with role-related performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cognition-based trust in work unit peers and the direct leader; affect-based trust in each referent when predicting entry outcomes.
- DV(s): Affect-based trust; organizational identification; role-related performance.
- Mediators: Affect-based trust is the proposed intervening link between cognition-based trust and entry outcomes; separate indirect-effect estimates are not reported.
- Moderators: Chronic relational identity, proposed to strengthen the cognition-based-to-affect-based trust relationship.

Cognition-based trust supplies diagnostic information about others’ competence, reliability and dependability, helping newcomers select partners for emotionally invested, reciprocal exchange relationships. Affect-based trust represents perceived mutual concern and emotional attachment. The authors propose that these relationships provide distinct psychosocial and instrumental resources through peers and leaders: positive affect and sense-making support identification, while learning, cooperation and felt obligations support performance. These resource processes explain the theory rather than constituting directly measured mediators.

**Data & Measures**
The study followed male U.S. Army infantry enlistees through a 14-week residential program. Of 682 enrolled trainees, 647 participated at time 1, 605 at time 2, and 519 completed all three surveys; complete usable samples were 512 for peer-only analyses and 267 for analyses including leader trust. Time 1 followed one day of familiarization, time 2 followed approximately seven weeks, and time 3 preceded the final training week. One company lacked leader-trust measures in the first two waves because of survey-length restrictions.

Cognition-based trust used five items and affect-based trust four items for each referent, adapted from McAllister and Yang and colleagues; some affect-based items differed between peers and leaders. Six Army-specific items measured organizational identification at times 1 and 3. Five general self-concept items measured relational identity at time 3. Drill instructors rated time-3 role-related performance using three seven-point items covering basic soldier skills, training tasks and Army ideals/values; ratings from two or three instructors were averaged per trainee. Other questionnaire measures used five-point response scales.

Cross-lagged SEM used trust scale means corrected for measurement error using alpha reliabilities, with item indicators for identification and performance; baseline identification and trust stability paths were included. HLM paired cognition-based trust at times 1 and 2 with affect-based trust at times 2 and 3, respectively. Table 2 reports lagged HLM samples of 510 individuals/1,020 observations for peers and 265/530 for leaders. Its contemporaneous peer footnote instead reports 530 individuals/1,530 observations, an unresolved inconsistency with the reported peer sample and three-wave structure. Age/education adjustments did not alter hypothesis findings and were omitted from reported models. The observational design supports temporal comparisons, not definitive causal identification.

**Key Findings**
- H1a was supported in both intervals (Figure 2): peer cognition-to-affect paths were β = .17, p < .05, and β = .34, p < .01; reverse paths were .00 and .08, both nonsignificant. H1b was only partially supported: the later leader cognition-to-affect path was .15, p < .05, versus a nonsignificant .06 reverse path. In the first interval, cognition-to-affect was −.07, nonsignificant, while the reverse path was −.17, p < .05.
- H2a was supported: relational identity strengthened the lagged peer cognition-to-affect association (γ = .15, p < .05). H2b was unsupported for leader trust (γ = .00, nonsignificant). Supplementary contemporaneous interactions were significant for both referents, but the peer estimate conflicts within the source: Table 2 reports .12, p < .05, whereas the p. 1161 prose reports .19, p < .01; the leader estimate is .30, p < .05. Supplementary models controlling squad membership retained the lagged moderation findings.
- H3a and H3b were supported: time-2 affect-based trust in peers and leaders predicted time-3 organizational identification, controlling baseline identification (β = .13 and .15, respectively; both p < .05). Direct cognition-based trust paths to the entry outcomes were nonsignificant and removed from the final model.
- H4b was supported for leader affect-based trust and subsequent performance (β = .16, p < .05); H4a was unsupported for peer trust (β = .01, nonsignificant). The authors caution that positive peer-performance results in the residual sample did not simultaneously account for leader trust.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper connects social exchange and social identity perspectives on newcomer adjustment by tracing how cognition-based trust precedes deeper affect-based exchange relationships and by identifying relational identity as a boundary condition for peer-trust development. It integrates instrumental and relational accounts of socialization while distinguishing peers’ and leaders’ associations with identification and performance. The leader findings qualify this account: the early trust-development interval and lagged relational-identity moderation did not support the corresponding predictions.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest helping organizational newcomers develop high-quality relationships with peers and leaders. Insiders can demonstrate benevolence and provide useful information and resources, while newcomers also need to establish reputations for trustworthiness. They additionally suggest encouraging newcomers to attend to others’ personal and professional qualities and strengthen relationships with those in whom they have high cognition-based trust.

**Limitations**
The intense, residential, all-male military setting, dangerous task context and identity-changing divestiture socialization limit generalizability; changing interpersonal conditions also discouraged interpreting mean trust changes over time. Missing early leader-trust data reduced the combined-analysis sample, and leader-trust measures exhibited only partial metric invariance. The authors acknowledge that covariance structure cannot establish causation; the table/prose discrepancies noted above also remain unresolved.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying established-insider peers, restricted choices of exchange partners, and shifts from dyadic reciprocal to generalized exchange using more granular longitudinal or network data. Future work could test resource acquisition and knowledge sharing as mechanisms linking trust to outcomes, examine different demands and socialization tactics, and investigate how newcomers gain others’ trust and access to insider opportunities.
