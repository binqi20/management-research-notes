# Can Surgical Teams Ever Learn? The Role of Coordination, Complexity, and Transitivity in Action Team Learning

**Abstract**
Recognizing that current theories of team learning do not apply to short-term action teams, we conceptualize how action teams may learn and test hypotheses regarding the performance-related effects of such learning, the mechanisms mediating such effects, and the conditions governing their magnitude. We operationalized the level of action team learning (ATL) in terms of the regularity and number of role-based, guided team reflexivity experiences of an action team’s members. Testing our hypotheses on 250 surgical teams, we find that higher levels of ATL are associated with shorter surgical duration, with this effect mediated by team helping and workload sharing, particularly under conditions of greater team task complexity. Additionally, we find higher levels of ATL to be directly associated with a reduced number of adverse events in low-complexity surgeries.

**Research Question**
Can compositionally unstable, short-lived action teams learn through members' accumulated guided reflexivity experiences across teams, and how do workload sharing, helping, and task complexity condition the relationship between this learning and team performance?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: ATL is inversely related to the number of adverse events during a team's mission.
- H1b: ATL is inversely related to mission completion time relative to the norm.
- H2a: Team workload sharing mediates the ATL–adverse-events relationship.
- H2b: Team workload sharing mediates the ATL–relative-duration relationship.
- H2c: Team helping mediates the ATL–adverse-events relationship.
- H2d: Team helping mediates the ATL–relative-duration relationship.
- H3a: Task complexity strengthens the beneficial relationship between workload sharing and fewer adverse events.
- H3b: Task complexity strengthens the beneficial relationship between helping and fewer adverse events.
- H3c: Task complexity strengthens the beneficial relationship between workload sharing and shorter relative duration.
- H3d: Task complexity strengthens the beneficial relationship between helping and shorter relative duration.
- H4a: Task complexity attenuates ATL's direct beneficial relationship with adverse events, making it stronger at lower complexity.
- H4b: Task complexity attenuates ATL's direct beneficial relationship with relative duration, making it stronger at lower complexity.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Action team learning, conceptualized as the team's accumulated, regular, guided, role-focused reflexivity experience brought by members across team assignments.
- DV(s): Number of adverse events during surgery; surgical duration relative to a ward- and procedure-specific baseline.
- Mediators: Team workload sharing and team helping, proposed manifestations of implicit coordination.
- Moderators: Team task complexity, hypothesized to amplify coordination–performance relationships while weakening ATL's direct performance relationships.

Guided reflection focused on roles rather than particular individuals is proposed to make shared insights transferable across changing team compositions. Numerous, regular reflection experiences should help members recognize patterns, anticipate other role holders' needs, and adjust their own behavior, enabling workload sharing and helping. These coordination processes are expected to be especially useful for complex tasks; domain knowledge transferred from earlier teams is proposed to be less applicable as complexity increases. Domain knowledge itself was not directly measured.

**Data & Measures**
The study covered 362 surgical teams in nine wards of one large public tertiary hospital in Israel over six months. One ward within each of three matched triplets was randomly assigned to the intervention. After observations of 112 baseline teams, staff in the intervention wards received guided briefing/debriefing training. During the four-month performance phase, observers blind to condition followed 135 intervention-ward and 115 control-ward surgeries. Among intervention teams, 59 conducted both briefing and debriefing, four debriefing only, 17 briefing only, and 55 neither. The main duration and adverse-event models used 205 and 199 observations, respectively; workload-sharing and helping analyses used 73 and 63 teams, with approximately 1.5 respondents per retained team. These are team/surgery counts, not counts of unique staff members.

ATL was calculated by first counting sampled surgeries and debriefed sampled surgeries involving team members, aggregating debriefing experience to the team, then multiplying that amount by its regularity: debriefed sampled surgeries involving members divided by all sampled surgeries involving them. ATL ranged from 0 to 24. Adverse events were observer-checklist counts of unusual surgical-management events posing potential or actual patient risk, ranging from 0 to 3. Relative duration was observed surgery time divided by the baseline mean for the same surgery type in the same ward. Workload sharing averaged members' four-item, seven-point ratings (α = .86); helping combined team aggregates of members' average help-giving and help-receiving ratings (seven-point responses; α = .92). Both were collected after surgery but before any debriefing. Complexity was the head surgeon's three-category rating relative to surgeries typical of the ward.

Controls were patient ASA physical status, preoperative briefing, team size, and the number of prior surgeries involving at least two members together, centered around the ward mean. Random-coefficient models accounted for nesting under head surgeons and wards, with surgeon-based complexity random effects in moderation models. Ward-level intervention assignment was randomized, but the analyses of observed ATL and its mediators estimate associations rather than randomized effects of the accumulated learning measure.

**Key Findings**
- H1b was supported: ATL was associated with shorter relative duration (b = −0.02, p < .05; Table 2, Model 2). H1a was unsupported: ATL's main association with adverse-event counts was nonsignificant (Table 3, Model 2).
- Adjusted ATL associations with workload sharing and helping were positive (b = 0.06, p < .01 and b = 0.06, p < .05, respectively; Table 4). H2b was supported: workload sharing predicted shorter duration (b = −0.22, p < .05), with indirect effect −0.01 and reported bootstrap interval [−0.03, −0.0002], accounting for 35% of the total ATL–duration effect. H2a, H2c, and H2d were unsupported: neither mediator explained adverse events, and helping did not mediate duration in the unmoderated model.
- H3c and H3d were supported for duration (Table 2, Models 4a–4b). Workload-sharing interactions were −0.44 for medium versus low complexity and −0.41 for high versus low complexity (both p < .05); helping's high-versus-low interaction was −0.42 (p < .05), while its medium-versus-low interaction was nonsignificant. At high complexity, both coordination variables were associated with shorter duration; at low complexity, helping was associated with longer duration. H3a and H3b, concerning adverse-event moderation, were unsupported.
- H4a received qualified support: ATL's medium-versus-low complexity interaction predicted adverse events (b = 0.08, p < .05), while the high-versus-low interaction was nonsignificant (Table 3, Model 3). The Results prose describes the low-complexity decline as marginal (p < .1) and medium/high slopes as nonsignificant. Figure 4 is inconsistent with that prose: it labels the low-complexity slope −0.05 and the medium-complexity slope 0.03 as significant at p < .05, and the high-complexity slope 0.01 as nonsignificant. H4b, complexity moderation of ATL's direct duration relationship, was unsupported.
- Using unweighted cumulative reflexivity experience with total surgical experience controlled left duration results unchanged but weakened adverse-event moderation to the .10 significance level. Replacing the complexity rating with generic procedure-complexity ratings and controlling for interdependence did not change the reported significance pattern.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends team-learning and multiteam-membership accounts by treating learning in temporally unstable teams as a team property accumulated across members' prior team experiences. It specifies guided, role-based, numerous, and regular reflexivity experiences as a basis for transitive learning when stable membership is absent. Workload sharing, helping, and task complexity provide a conditional account of how and when ATL relates to performance, while the proposed domain-knowledge pathway remains indirect interpretation.

**Practical Implication**
For managers of action teams, particularly in health care, the authors suggest that guided postaction debriefings may enhance team learning. They emphasize role-focused and consistent reflection as conditions supporting the transfer of shared insights across changing team compositions.

**Limitations**
Small mediation samples and infrequent adverse events may limit statistical power; workload sharing and helping may also reflect commitment or engagement rather than coordination alone. The single-item complexity measure may conflate uncertainty and interdependence, although supplementary analyses address this concern. Adverse-event counts disregard severity and corrective tradeoffs, and duration and event counts may imperfectly represent efficiency and quality; the inconsistent adverse-event significance reporting further warrants caution.

**Future Research**
The authors propose testing vigilance, attention to detail, commitment, and engagement as additional or alternative mediators. They also call for separating dimensions of complexity, examining a broader range of team outcomes, and assessing whether ATL and its conditional performance relationships generalize to action teams in other contexts.
