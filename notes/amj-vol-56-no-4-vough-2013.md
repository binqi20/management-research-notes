# What Clients Don't Get about My Profession: A Model of Perceived Role-Based Image Discrepancies

**Abstract**
While most work on image has focused on character traits, we argue that, with respect to professions, roles represent an important, underexplored source of image. Employing qualitative data from 85 members of four professions, we identify “role-based image discrepancies”—misalignments between what professionals perceive as the content of their professional work and what they believe others think constitutes the professionals’ professional work—as a salient and costly issue for professionals. Specifically, we find that professionals believe that clients’ perceptions of their roles lead clients not only to devalue professions but also to have misaligned expectations about the process and outcomes of the professional-client relationship. Further, professionals perceive that these client evaluations hinder the professional-client interaction and, consequentially, lead to productivity and emotional costs. In response to the recognition of these potential costs, professionals are motivated to manage role-based images and client evaluations through specific problem-focused image management tactics. We develop a model of role-based image discrepancy management that encapsulates these findings and identify mechanisms that link role-based image discrepancies with their outcomes and with professional responses. Our findings carry theoretical and practical implications pertinent to professions, professionals, and image management.

**Research Question**
What are the implications of perceived role-based image discrepancies for professionals, and how do professionals respond to those implications in their relationships with clients?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Role-based image discrepancies are misalignments between professionals' understandings of their work and what they believe outsiders think they do, particularly regarding scope and complexity/difficulty.
- Client evaluations: Perceived devaluation of the profession and misaligned expectations, including impracticable expectations about what professionals can accomplish and skeptical expectations about their intentions.
- Emergent outcomes: Perceived productivity costs—impaired collaboration, fee contestation and bypassing—and emotional costs, including frustration and annoyance.
- Practices: Informing clients about roles and processes; demonstrating work through behavior and cues; and relationship building through rapport and goodwill.
- Proposed mechanisms: Unmet client expectations and interpersonal trust connect client evaluations with productivity costs and help explain professionals' image-management responses.

The emergent process model links role misunderstandings to client evaluations that professionals believe obstruct their work and impose emotional costs; productivity difficulties can also be emotionally draining. Professionals describe problem-focused tactics intended to adjust client views or mitigate problematic evaluations. The discussion further proposes a self-reinforcing cycle in which unmet expectations and declining trust restrict clients' investment in the relationship, making expectations harder to fulfill. This is an interpretation of interview narratives, not a longitudinally tested causal sequence or mediation model.

**Data & Measures**
The pooled sample comprises 85 professionals: 24 architects in one firm, 13 advanced practice nurses (APNs) in one health-care organization, 17 litigation attorneys in various law firms, and 31 certified public accountants (CPAs) in Big Four accounting firms. Semistructured individual interviews lasted 45–120 minutes and were recorded and transcribed; the image inquiry formed part of larger studies in each profession. Architecture, nursing and law researchers developed a shared protocol during collection; independently collected accounting interviews were incorporated later after protocol and transcript comparisons. Observations, shadowing and archival materials supplied contextual understanding but were not formally analyzed because their systematic coverage varied.

Analysis iterated among open coding, higher-level categories and aggregate dimensions, profession-specific models, and a general cross-profession model; researchers exchanged datasets and recoded using agreed codes. For example, accounts of narrowly understood tasks were coded as scope discrepancies, while underestimated skills and difficulty informed complexity discrepancies. Client evaluations, productivity/emotional costs and coping tactics were likewise derived from professionals' accounts (Figure 1; Tables 1–5), rather than independently measured client beliefs or quantitative scales. The paper does not report the sample's country or calendar collection period.

**Key Findings**
- Across the four professions, interviewees described outsiders as understanding only a restricted scope of their work and underestimating its complexity. Such misunderstandings became particularly consequential when held by clients, whose perceived devaluation or expectations could obstruct professional service (Tables 1–3).
- Professionals associated client evaluations with impaired collaboration, fee contestation and bypassing, alongside frustration and anxiety (Table 4). Fee contestation particularly concerned the billable-hours architects and lawyers; architects and APNs working alongside allied professionals were more susceptible to bypassing. Skeptical expectations about intentions appeared in accounts from architects, CPAs and lawyers; APNs instead reported being viewed as caring and well intentioned.
- Informing, demonstrating and relationship building emerged as problem-focused responses (Table 5). Demonstrating was mentioned most frequently by APNs, while CPAs frequently described relationship building. The authors also identified emotion-focused coping with general-public perceptions but did not elaborate those tactics. They did not find professionals changing to meet outsiders' expectations or blaming externally imposed role constraints.
- The resulting model links discrepancies, client evaluations, productivity/emotional costs and professional responses (Figure 2). Unmet expectations and interpersonal trust are proposed explanatory mechanisms in the discussion (Figure 3); the study does not establish the tactics' effectiveness over time.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends image research beyond character traits to beliefs about what people do in professional roles, linking this distinction to identity theory and role enactment. It identifies specific productivity consequences and elaborates unmet expectations and interpersonal trust as relational explanations beyond cognitive-resource depletion and self-verification stress. Informing, demonstrating and relationship building extend understanding of how professionals manage discrepancies within interdependent client relationships.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend that professionals inform, demonstrate and build relationships from initial client contact, including managing expectations when clients appear to understand the profession. They suggest explaining the profession's scope, the individual's niche and credentialing, rather than attempting to teach detailed technical knowledge through potentially confusing jargon. These are recommendations derived from the qualitative model, rather than demonstrated intervention effects.

**Limitations**
Uneven access across professions limits strong comparisons, and pooling involved different interview protocols; the accounting data could not participate in the same shared collection–analysis iteration because they were incorporated after collection. The nonlongitudinal design bases process ordering on narrative causality, leaving temporal development and tactic effectiveness unresolved. The authors generalize to theory rather than claiming direct applicability to populations of other professions or occupations; client interdependence, visibility and work complexity are proposed boundaries.

**Future Research**
The authors call for longitudinal examination of process development and tactic effectiveness, cross-profession comparisons, and research on other occupations with differing client interdependence, visibility and complexity. They propose studying how role centrality shapes responses, how discrepancy types interact, and how professionals acquire image-management expertise. They also suggest connecting macro-level legitimacy threats and deprofessionalization with micro-level image experiences, explicitly acknowledging the absence of direct data for this proposed connection.
