# Power, Moral Clarity, and Punishment in the Workplace

**Abstract**
We propose that power increases how severely people punish transgressors. Further, we argue that this greater severity stems from an increased sense of moral clarity instilled by the psychological experience of power. We investigate the linkages among power, moral clarity, and punishment across multiple studies. Individuals with an increased sense of power advocated more severe punishments for transgressors than did those with a diminished sense of power. Further, moral clarity mediated the link between power and severity of punishment. We discuss the implications of these findings for managers in organizations and researchers interested in punitive reactions to moral transgressions.

**Research Question**
Does the psychological experience of power increase punishment severity by heightening moral clarity, understood as certainty when judging the ethicality of others' behavior?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: An increase in power leads to an increase in moral clarity.
- H2: People who possess a stronger sense of moral clarity are more severe in punishing others for perceived ethical transgressions.
- H3: Moral clarity mediates the relationship between power and severity of punishment.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Experienced power, manipulated through recall in Study 1 and manager versus builder roles in Study 4; measured moral clarity in Study 2 and clarity priming in Study 3 test the downstream relationship.
- DV(s): Moral clarity in Study 1; recommended punishment severity in Studies 2 and 3; assignment of less interesting tasks as behavioral punishment in Study 4.
- Mediators: Moral clarity, tested in the complete power–punishment model in Study 4. Extremity of immorality judgment is examined as an alternative mediator.
- Moderators: Perceived immorality in Study 2; transgression versus control behavior conditions the role-power effect on task assignment in Study 4.

The authors argue that power increases confidence in one's judgments and reduces attention to alternative viewpoints, making ethical issues seem less ambiguous. Greater certainty can embolden punishment when a behavior is perceived as a transgression; it does not necessarily mean seeing the act as more immoral or judging it more accurately. Moral clarity concerns certainty of judgment, whereas moral awareness concerns recognizing moral content. Only Study 4 tests the complete mediation model, and its measurement order does not establish mediator–outcome temporal precedence.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: Forty-nine working adults from a national participant pool administered by a US West Coast university were randomly assigned to recall a powerful experience or describe the previous day. They classified decisions in six workplace “right versus right” dilemmas as ethical, unethical, or “it depends.” The number of “it depends” responses, ranging from 0 to 6, measures uncertainty; a lower count indicates greater moral clarity.
- Study 2: Forty-eight Mechanical Turk adults completed a correlational vignette study. Five clarity questions for each of four initial vignettes formed a 20-item measure (α = .88); ambiguity was reverse-scored. Seven separate business-transgression vignettes measured perceived immorality and recommended punishment on seven-point scales (α = .85 and .83, respectively). Random-intercept models nested behavior ratings within people. Table 2's adjusted model includes moral-identity internalization and symbolization, moral attentiveness and awareness, utilitarian and formalist predispositions, and deontological orientation, alongside perceived immorality and its interaction with clarity.
- Study 3: One hundred forty-one online participants completed a clarity-priming or neutral scrambled-sentence task, then recommended how long to bar laboratory participants for five types of misconduct. Options ran from 0 to 12 months in two-month increments. A nine-item moral-clarity scale administered afterward served as the manipulation check (α = .73); analyses compared aggregated punishment ratings and used a random-intercept model of ratings within people.
- Study 4: The methods report 100 students at a private West Coast university and exclusion of two suspicious and two unusually fast respondents, but Table 3 still totals 100, comprising 49 transgression and 51 control participants; the paper does not reconcile these counts. Random manager/builder roles were crossed with a confederate retaining an overpayment versus receiving normal payment. Participants assigned the confederate a task described by its interest rating; lower interest represents harsher punishment. Five subsequent clarity items formed the mediator (α = .94), and four immorality-related judgments formed the alternative mediator (α = .72). Table 5's adjusted model includes judgment extremity, moral attentiveness and awareness, both moral-identity dimensions, and deontological orientation. Analyses were individual-level; the mediator was measured after task assignment.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supports H1: power-primed participants chose “it depends” less often than controls (.84 versus 1.54; Mann-Whitney U = 197, p = .03, d = .61). Neither “no” nor “yes” responses differed significantly (p = .47 and .60, respectively), indicating greater certainty without a significant shift toward either judgment (p. 1007).
- Study 2 gives qualified support for H2. The initial clarity main effect was nonsignificant (p = .26), but clarity positively moderated the immorality–punishment relationship (b = .27, p = .01). With controls, the prose reports a positive clarity coefficient of .39 (p = .03), whereas Table 2 reports .38*; this discrepancy is retained. The adjusted interaction remained positive (b = .17, p = .01), indicating a stronger clarity–punishment association when behavior was judged more immoral (Table 2; Figure 1).
- Study 3 supports the clarity–punishment link: the clarity-prime group recommended longer exclusions than controls (7.12 versus 6.19 months, p = .04); the random-intercept estimate was positive (b = .23, p = .05). The manipulation check was only marginal (clarity means 4.54 versus 4.28, p = .09), qualifying the evidence for the manipulation's effect on reported moral clarity (p. 1012).
- Study 4: The role × behavior interaction predicted task assignment (F[1, 96] = 5.61, p = .02), although neither main effect was significant. In the transgression condition, managers assigned less interesting tasks than builders (4.75 versus 5.70, p = .04); the control-condition role difference was nonsignificant (p = .25). Managers reported higher clarity about the transgression (5.28 versus 4.30, p = .05; H1), and clarity correlated with lower task interest (r = −.30, p = .04; H2), but not with task assignment in the control condition (p > .90). The authors report support for H3: indirect-effect 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals excluded zero without controls [.001, .641] and with controls [.005, .955], subject to the measurement-order limitation. Power did not significantly increase perceived immorality (p = .31), and the alternative indirect-effect interval included zero [−.080, .478]. Effects did not carry over to task assignment for the nonconfederate (Tables 3–5; pp. 1014–1016).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces moral clarity as a dimension of ethical judgment distinct from recognizing moral content or holding a strong moral identity. It identifies the punisher's experienced power as an antecedent of punitive responses and proposes an intrapsychic pathway through certainty, complementing accounts centered on transgressors, victims, and situations. The findings also motivate treating power differences as incremental rather than simply distinguishing people with and without the capacity to punish.

**Practical Implication**
Managers concerned about punishment appearing excessive could reduce its severity, solicit feedback from less powerful people, or consider alternative viewpoints before acting. The authors present perspective taking as a possible way to temper moral certainty, not as an intervention tested in these studies. They also acknowledge that powerful people's greater willingness to punish can help organizations enforce ethical standards when punishment is deserved.

**Limitations**
Study 1 examines conflicting moral values, while Study 2's correlational, hypothetical-vignette design cannot establish causality and offers equivocal support because its clarity main effect requires controls. Study 2 focuses on moderately immoral behaviors, limiting extension to acts seen as extremely immoral or entirely nonimmoral. Only Study 4 tests the full mediation model; its low-stakes laboratory punishment may not represent consequential organizational sanctions, and measuring clarity afterward leaves reverse ordering possible.

**Future Research**
The authors call for replication with harsher punishments and designs distinguishing whether clarity precedes punishment or follows it as a justification. They propose studying boundary conditions involving behavior perceived as innocent, benefits to the punisher, lenient organizational rules, and self-punishment, as well as interactions between the transgressor's and punisher's power. They also suggest examining whether interpersonal distance affects the link between power and judgments of immorality.
