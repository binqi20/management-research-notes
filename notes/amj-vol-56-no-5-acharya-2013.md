# Shoot for the Stars? Predicting the Recruitment of Prestigious Directors at Newly Public Firms

**Abstract**
This study explores how CEOs’ and outside directors’ desires for the benefits of signaling and “homophily” intertwine with their concerns over maintaining power and preserving local status hierarchies to affect the likelihood a firm recruits prestigious outside directors to its board. Using pooled cross-sectional data on the five years following the initial public offerings (IPOs) of 210 firms that went public between 2001 and 2004, we found that prestigious CEOs and directors viewed the recruitment of prestigious new directors differently and that these perceptions were moderated by factors that increase the salience of risk of potential losses to CEOs and existing board members.

**Research Question**
Why and under what conditions do newly public firms avoid recruiting prestigious outside directors even when they have the ability to do so? How do incumbent board prestige, CEO prestige, board tenure, and CEO power shape these recruitment decisions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Conditional on recruiting a new outside director, preexisting board prestige has a positive but diminishing relationship with the likelihood that the new director is prestigious.
- H2: Conditional on recruiting a new outside director, the presence of a prestigious CEO is positively related to the likelihood that the new director is prestigious.
- H3: Conditional on recruiting a new outside director, the positive but diminishing relationship between preexisting board prestige and prestigious recruitment is stronger the longer existing directors have worked together.
- H4: Conditional on recruiting a new outside director, the positive relationship between CEO prestige and prestigious recruitment is stronger the greater the CEO's structural and ownership power.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Preexisting board prestige and CEO prestige.
- DV(s): Recruitment of a prestigious outside director; alternatively, the number of prestige credentials a newly recruited director possesses.
- Mediators: No directly tested mediators; perceived signaling and homophily benefits versus threats to local status and discretion provide the theoretical explanation.
- Moderators: Average board tenure for board prestige; CEO ownership power (stock ownership and founder status) and structural power (CEO duality, outside director ratio, and staggered board elections) for CEO prestige.

Prestigious affiliations can signal quality and offer homophily benefits while threatening incumbent directors' local status and CEOs' discretion. The authors argue that diminishing benefits and increasing internal costs constrain the desire to add further prestigious directors. Longer shared tenure and additional sources of CEO power may reduce perceived threats, making loss aversion and psychological reactance less salient. These psychological mechanisms are interpretations of archival recruitment patterns, rather than directly measured mediating processes.

**Data & Measures**
Prospectuses and SEC annual proxy statements cover the five years following IPOs conducted in 2001–2004. Exclusions left 232 firms; missing data reduced the sample to 210 across 41 two-digit SIC industries. Seventy-seven firms were delisted and dropped after delisting; the resulting panel contains 826 firm-years. The negative binomial models use 451 observations in which recruitment occurred. The design is observational: selection corrections address whether recruitment occurs, without directly identifying the proposed psychological mechanism.

A director is prestigious if possessing at least one of three credentials: a degree from one of Finkelstein's 20 elite institutions, vice-president-or-higher executive experience at an S&P 500 company, or outside-director experience at an S&P 500 firm. Lagged-year S&P 500 lists determine qualifying employment and directorship affiliations. The binary outcome indicates recruiting a director with any credential; the alternative outcome counts credentials from zero to three. Board prestige counts continuing prestigious directors and enters both linearly and squared; CEO prestige uses the same any-credential rule. Average board tenure sums individual directors' service years and divides by the number of directors. Ownership power is measured by the CEO's year-end beneficial share ownership and founder status. Structural power uses CEO/chair duality, outside directors divided by total directors, and staggered-election status; outside directors have no current or past executive position in the firm or family relationship to current employees.

Controls include logged IPO offer size (shares offered multiplied by offer price), first-day underpricing, VC backing and prestige, underwriter prestige, log(1 + annual sales), logged firm age at IPO, lagged total stockholder return, board size, CEO tenure, multiple hires, industry, IPO year, and years since IPO. Total stockholder return is year-end price minus year-start price plus dividends, divided by year-start price. The first-stage recruitment equation uses departures, board size, VC backing, CEO prestige, industry, and post-IPO year dummies; departures are excluded from the second stage. Binary outcomes use Heckman probit selection models; count outcomes use negative binomial regression with robust standard errors and an inverse Mills ratio from a separate recruitment probit.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: board prestige is positively but diminishingly associated with prestigious recruitment. In Model 2, the linear/squared coefficients are 0.52/−0.03 in Table 2 and 0.53/−0.04 in Table 3 (all p < .001). The decline beyond the turning point is not significant for the binary outcome, but the count outcome shows a significant decline at high board prestige.
- H2 is unsupported in the main-effects models: CEO prestige is negative and nonsignificant for both the binary outcome (−0.18, Table 2, Model 2) and credential count (−0.09, Table 3, Model 2).
- The authors interpret H3 as supported: longer board tenure raises predicted prestigious recruitment and new-director prestige. In Model 3 of both tables, tenure interacts negatively with linear board prestige (−0.08, p < .05) and positively with its square (0.01). The prose calls both interactions significant, but Table 2 marks the squared interaction only p < .10; Table 3 marks it p < .05.
- H4 receives partial, outcome-specific support. For the binary outcome, CEO prestige × outside director ratio is negative (−4.53, p < .01; Table 2, Model 6), while the conditional CEO-prestige coefficient is positive (3.55, p < .05). The plotted CEO-prestige relationship is positive with fewer outsiders and negative with more outsiders; the other power interactions are nonsignificant. The outside-ratio interaction remains significant in the full binary model (−4.75, p < .01).
- For credential count, the CEO-prestige × founder interaction is positive in its separate model (0.35, p < .05; Table 3, Model 7), with a negative CEO-prestige coefficient (−0.21, p < .05). That interaction is nonsignificant in the full count model (0.31, Model 9); CEO duality, stock ownership, outside-ratio, and staggered-board interactions are also nonsignificant. Thus, support does not generalize across power measures or specifications.
- Alternative cumulative-prestige measures reproduce the reported patterns. Rarity-weighted prestige retains the board results, but CEO prestige is negative and significant except when the nonsignificant outside-ratio interaction is included, when its main effect is also nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study challenges the assumption that firms invariably desire prestigious affiliates when they can attract them, bringing nonfinancial internal costs into explanations of signaling and homophily. It links broader labor-market prestige to local board status hierarchies and CEO discretion, identifying contextual constraints on prestigious director recruitment. Different sources of CEO power have distinct consequences rather than interchangeable effects.

**Practical Implication**
The authors caution that governance prescriptions favoring very high outside-director representation or replacing founder CEOs may unintentionally reduce recruitment of prestigious directors who could bring greater human and social capital. They treat the findings as preliminary and emphasize considering contextual benefits and costs of board structure and founder leadership rather than applying uniform prescriptions.

**Limitations**
The study does not explicitly model departing directors' type, tenure, and stock ownership or resolve conflicting preferences between prestigious CEOs and directors. More than two prestigious directors were recruited in less than 1 percent of firm-years, limiting examination of multiple prestigious recruits. Expert power was not directly considered, and work- and education-based prestige measures cannot disentangle prestige from expertise.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on departing directors' characteristics, CEO–board sociopolitical interactions, and contexts with greater variation in the number of prestigious recruits. They also propose examining CEO expert power, separately measuring prestige and expertise, and investigating how different sources of CEO power combine to influence organizational outcomes.
