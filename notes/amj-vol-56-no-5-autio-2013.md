# Information Exposure, Opportunity Evaluation, and Entrepreneurial Action: An Investigation of an Online User Community

**Abstract**
We study how an individual’s exposure to external information regulates the evaluation of entrepreneurial opportunities and entrepreneurial action. Combining data from interviews, a survey, and a comprehensive web log of an online user community spanning eight years, we find that technical information shaped opportunity evaluation and that social information about user needs drove individuals to entrepreneurial action. Our empirical findings suggest that reducing demand uncertainty is a central factor regulating entrepreneurial action, an insight that received theories of entrepreneurial action have so far overlooked.

**Research Question**
How does individuals’ exposure to technological information and information about user needs differently relate to evaluating third-person entrepreneurial opportunities and undertaking entrepreneurial action?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Individuals with stronger lead user attributes are more likely to engage in opportunity evaluation.
- H2: Individuals engaging in more technological probing are more likely to engage in opportunity evaluation.
- H3: Individuals receiving more community attention are more likely to engage in entrepreneurial action.
- H4: Individuals engaging in more community spanning are more likely to engage in entrepreneurial action.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Lead user attributes and technological probing represent exposure to the technological domain; community attention and community spanning represent exposure to the social domain.
- DV(s): Opportunity evaluation and entrepreneurial action.
- Mediators: No formal mediation hypothesis or mediation test; reducing response and demand uncertainty is the proposed explanation for action.
- Moderators: None formally hypothesized.

Lead users’ sensitivity to technological bottlenecks and technological probers’ access to feedback and influence on development agendas facilitate recognition and evaluation of third-person opportunities. Community attention is proposed to reduce uncertainty about demand, a particular solution’s attractiveness, and acceptance of the individual as its supplier. Spanning related communities supplies analogies and alternative ways of framing problems, reducing perceived market uncertainty. These social informational advantages can help individuals regard an opportunity as feasible and desirable for themselves; observable evaluation need not always precede action.

**Data & Measures**
The study combines 275 valid survey responses from 280 respondents among 966 users who logged in during a one-month survey window (28.5% valid response rate), a web log of 151,092 messages by 8,640 users, and 42 interviews with 19 community members and company employees. Respondents represented 31 countries. Interviews informed questionnaire design, theory development and interpretation, deliberately oversampling users who had founded firms while also including nonentrepreneurs. Methods dates the web log from 2002 to May 2008 and interviews before the end of 2011; the abstract instead describes an eight-year web log. The survey’s calendar date is not specified; web-log exposure measures preceded survey distribution.

- Opportunity evaluation: Binary indicator of having made written business plans or sought start-up resources, together with reporting that community experience was important to that activity. It proxies recognition and evaluation through tangible planning activity, not opportunity awareness alone.
- Entrepreneurial action: Binary indicator of having sent company invoices, officially registered a company, or sold products/services through it, again requiring reported importance of community experience. An objective start-up check agreed with self-reports for the cases where the authors could obtain it; a handful lacked that check.
- Lead user attributes: Arithmetic mean of eight survey items on a 1–5 agreement scale covering early awareness, adoption, prototype involvement and related lead-user behaviors; α = .75.
- Technological probing: Number of new discussion threads started by a user divided by that user’s total postings.
- Community attention: Average number of responses received per posting by the user.
- Community spanning: Number of other technologically related online communities in which the respondent reported active participation during the preceding three years, identified through follow-up names and independently coded by two authors; general social networks were excluded.

Table 2 jointly estimates the two binary outcomes using bivariate probit with Huber–White standard errors clustered by country. Its controls are log(1 + coded answers to questions), skills and resource-mobilization self-efficacy, university degree, product experience, age-group indicators, and an inverse Mills ratio. The survey-response selection model uses answers, questions and community tenure; the number of questions supplies the exclusion restriction. This observational design estimates associations and does not eliminate reverse causality.

**Key Findings**
- All four formal hypotheses are supported in Table 2, Model 2 (unstandardized coefficients; two-tailed tests). Lead user attributes predict opportunity evaluation positively (b = 0.50, SE = 0.08, p < .01; H1), as does technological probing (b = 0.51, SE = 0.25, p < .05; H2).
- Community attention predicts entrepreneurial action positively (b = 0.03, SE = 0.01, p < .01; H3), as does community spanning (b = 0.11, SE = 0.02, p < .01; H4). Lead user attributes and technological probing are not significant direct predictors of action; community attention and spanning are not significant predictors of evaluation in this model.
- Evaluation and action are related but empirically distinct: 18 respondents reported evaluation without action, 13 action without observable evaluation, 20 both, and 224 neither (Figure 3). The Model 2 residual correlation is .79, with a significant test of related equations (p < .01).
- Table 3’s marginal effects, holding other variables at their means, distinguish the paths. Lead user attributes positively predict evaluation without action (0.05, p < .01) and planned action (0.03, p < .01), but negatively predict action without observable evaluation (−0.02, p < .05). Technological probing positively predicts planned action (0.05, p < .05); community attention positively predicts action without observable evaluation (0.003, p < .05). Spanning positively predicts both action without observable evaluation and planned action (0.01 each, p < .01).
- The authors report unchanged conclusions when using separate probits, Bonacich centrality as an alternative attention measure, and checking inclusion of the inverse Mills ratio; the joint model fits better than separate probits. Interviews contextualize technical agenda setting and social validation, rather than independently identifying causal mediation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends entrepreneurial action theory by distinguishing the external informational stimuli associated with volitional opportunity evaluation from those associated with action, emphasizing demand uncertainty alongside technological opportunity recognition. It connects individual opportunity beliefs to their social informational setting and moves user-entrepreneurship research toward testing how users’ different information exposures relate to commercialization.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that platform owners could monitor user communities to identify where technological innovation may emerge and what form it may take. For corporate venturing, participation in communities that connect technological and social domains may encourage commercially viable initiatives. Active participation may also help firms shape collective development efforts and technological complements, rather than only acquire information.

**Limitations**
One software community limits generalizability; the proposed theory particularly concerns gradually evolving knowledge frontiers with unequal access to signals about user needs. Action without measured evaluation need not mean an absence of opportunity awareness, because the evaluation proxy captures overt planning activities. Reverse causality cannot be ruled out, and the study does not closely examine changes in communication content over time.

**Future Research**
The authors call for fuller analysis of how users frame messages, choose words, discover opportunities and mobilize resources through community communication. Dynamic models could track individual network positions, community-network evolution and the exact timing of firm establishment or first sales to clarify the sequence of events. They also identify additional structural determinants of innovation within user communities as a promising research direction.
