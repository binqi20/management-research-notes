# From Bench to Board: Gender Differences in University Scientists' Participation in Corporate Scientific Advisory Boards

**Abstract**
This article examines the gender difference in the likelihood that male and female academic scientists will join corporate scientific advisory boards (SABs). We assess (i) demand-side theories that relate the gap in scientists’ rate of joining SABs to the opportunity structure of SAB invitations, and (ii) supply-side explanations that attribute that gap to scientists’ preferences for work of this type. We statistically examine the demand- and supply-side perspectives in a national sample of 6,000 life scientists whose careers span more than 30 years. Holding constant professional achievement, network ties, employer characteristics, and research foci, male scientists are almost twice as likely as females to serve on the SABs of biotechnology companies. We do not find evidence in our data supporting a choice-based explanation for the gender gap. Instead, demand-side theoretical perspectives focusing on gender-stereotyped perceptions and the unequal opportunities embedded in social networks appear to explain some of the gap.

**Research Question**
How large is the gender gap in university scientists’ participation on biotechnology companies’ scientific advisory boards, and how far do demand-side opportunity constraints versus supply-side preferences and career choices account for it?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Affiliation with a high-prestige institution has a greater positive effect on women’s likelihood of becoming SAB members than on men’s.
- H2: Evidence of scientific or management capabilities has a greater positive effect on women’s likelihood of becoming SAB members than on men’s.
- H3: Location in a strong, direct-tie network conducive to SAB opportunities has a greater positive effect on women’s likelihood of becoming SAB members than on men’s.
- H4: Relative to men, women become SAB members at greater professional ages, on average.
- H5: Controlling for the commercial orientation of research choices reduces the estimated male–female gap in the likelihood of becoming an SAB member.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Gender and differences in professional qualifications, social networks, employer prestige, and research commercializability.
- DV(s): Rate of first transition to SAB membership.
- Mediators: No formal mediation test; proposed processes involve gendered competence perceptions, role incongruity, trust, and access to commercial opportunities.
- Moderators: Employer prestige, the proportion of last-authored publications as a capability proxy, and direct coauthorship ties; the hypotheses predict stronger benefits for women.

Expectation states theory suggests prestigious affiliations can reduce reliance on gender as a competence cue. Role congruity and lack-of-fit theories predict that documented capabilities can counter stereotypes, while close professional ties can provide trust and referrals that offset male in-group favoritism. Competing supply-side explanations predict later participation by women because of family–career trade-offs, or a smaller gender gap after accounting for research choices that reveal commercial interest. These are proposed explanations tested through observable contingencies and proxies, not directly measured psychological mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
- The event set contains 720 unique Ph.D.-holding SAB scientists employed at US universities or research institutions, identified from filings for 511 of 533 US biotechnology firms that filed IPO prospectuses during 1972–January 2002. A comparison sample began with 13,000 doctorate holders stratified by discipline and Ph.D. year; about 2,000 had no post-Ph.D. Web of Science publications, and further exclusions removed corporate-only publishers, scientists with five consecutive publication-free years, and early academic exits. Methods describes 5,946 random-subcohort scientists augmented by 720 cases, whereas Table 3 reports 5,946 individuals in total, 720 events, and 110,461 scientist-years. The paper does not reconcile these sample descriptions.
- Individual career histories model first SAB appointment using a case-cohort-adjusted Cox estimator. Risk starts at the later of Ph.D. year or 1961; academics without appointments are right-censored in January 2002 or at assumed age 65. Professional age equals current calendar year minus Ph.D. year; time-changing covariates are updated annually and lagged one year. Gender was independently coded from names and web searches; 95% was confidently identified, and ambiguous names were treated as male.
- Employer prestige indicates a top-20 Gourman-ranked biochemistry department. Scientific/management capability is proxied by the proportion of papers on which the scientist is last author, conventionally the research-group leader; time-changing administrative appointments were unavailable. Direct networks are measured by coauthor counts and counts of coauthors who had previously founded or advised biotechnology firms.
- Research commercializability uses publication-title keywords: within each scientist-year, divide each keyword’s frequency by total keyword frequency, multiply by its patentability weight indexed to the preceding year, and sum across keywords. The weights contrast keyword use among patenting and nonpatenting scientists. This is a proxy for commercially relevant research choices, not a complete measure of willingness to undertake SAB work.
- Controls include cumulative publications and citations, an indicator switching on at the first patent application, Ph.D. year and field, an active employer technology transfer office (TTO), and a pre-1980 period indicator. Annual citations are imputed from totals gathered in 2001 using an exponential arrival distribution with rate 0.1. The design is observational; it does not directly identify discrimination or individual preferences.

**Key Findings**
- Only 49 SAB scientists were women, representing 6.8% of the event sample. In Table 3, the female coefficient is −0.80 (p < .01) in baseline Model 1 and −0.49 (p < .05) in Model 4 after research, network, and employer controls; the corresponding male-to-female hazard ratios are 2.23 and 1.63.
- H1 is not supported: the female × employer-prestige interaction is −0.60 (SE = .46), nonsignificant in Model 5. The null interaction persists under alternative prestige specifications.
- H2 is supported: the female × last-authored-publication proportion interaction is positive (b = 1.52, SE = .73, p < .05; Model 6).
- H3 receives support, with differing strength across its two network measures: female × coauthor count is positive (b = .003, SE = .001, p < .01; Model 7), while female × entrepreneurial-coauthor count is positive but only marginally significant (b = .29, SE = .16, p < .10; Model 8).
- H4 is not supported: among scientists who joined SABs, mean professional age at transition is 19.2 years for men and 19.0 for women; a two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test does not reject equal distributions (D = .12, p = .54). This comparison cannot exclude women deterred from joining altogether.
- H5 is not supported: adding research commercializability increases rather than reduces the male-to-female hazard ratio, from 1.63 in Model 4 to 1.82 in Model 9, and from 2.23 in Model 1 to 2.34 in Model 10. Commercializability itself is positively associated with SAB entry (b = 1.32, SE = .18, p < .01; Model 9). The overall evidence favors demand-side accounts, but the authors caution that these tests do not rule out supply-side differences.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper jointly assesses demand-side and supply-side explanations of gender inequality in senior corporate opportunities using an identifiable candidate pool and detailed career histories. Stronger associations of capability and direct-network proxies with women’s SAB participation are consistent with role incongruity, lack-of-fit, and in-group-favoritism accounts, whereas the predicted additional benefit of prestigious affiliations for women is not supported. The findings favor opportunity constraints over the particular preference-based explanations tested, without establishing that preferences are irrelevant.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that policy interventions should address how decision makers perceive women’s qualifications for senior corporate roles. They recommend university policies matching women who are willing to undertake senior administrative assignments to such positions, and active TTO promotion of women faculty’s research and brokerage of connections to influential entrepreneurship and investment communities.

**Limitations**
Demand-side mechanisms are inferred from theoretically predicted contingencies rather than directly observed selector evaluations; the last-authorship measure is a proxy for capabilities, while coauthorship captures only part of scientists’ networks. The transition-age test conditions on becoming an SAB member and cannot exclude women deterred from commercial participation altogether. Research commercializability omits other aspects of willingness to supply effort, including promoting discoveries to industry, and stated interest may itself reflect perceived selection biases; consequently, absence of support for the tested supply-side accounts is not conclusive.

**Future Research**
The authors call for supply-side indicators combining objective professional histories with career aspirations and self-reported motivation, potentially supplementing longitudinal scientist surveys. Such measures should account for perceived opportunity structures shaping expressions of interest and ideally be matched with direct evaluator assessments to better distinguish demand-side and supply-side explanations.
