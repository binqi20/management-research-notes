# Corporate Control and the Speed of Strategic Business Unit Decision Making

**Abstract**
Decision speed has long been recognized as a critical determinant of firm performance, particularly in dynamic environments. Extending prior studies, which have largely focused on firm-level decision speed in smalland medium-sized organizations, this study explores how control mechanisms set by corporate headquarters in multibusiness firms influence decision speed at the strategic business unit (SBU) level. Using a multimethod approach, we first inductively derive six types of corporate control, before deductively examining their effects on SBU-level decision speed in five international multibusiness organizations. Our results suggest that three corporate control types enhance decision speed (goal setting, extrinsic incentives, and decision process control); two have no effect (negative incentives and conflict resolution); and one has a negative effect (strategy imposition). By integrating results from our qualitative and quantitative analyses, we are also able to identify transparency/alignment, outcome orientation, participation, trust, and timely feedback as the key mechanisms accounting for these effects.

**Research Question**
How and why do different types of corporate control over the process, content, and outcomes of SBU strategy making influence the speed of SBU-level strategic decisions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The following propositions emerge from the two-stage analysis rather than constitute ex ante hypotheses.

- P1: Goal setting is positively related to SBU decision speed through increased transparency/alignment, stronger outcome orientation, increased SBU-manager participation, and more timely feedback.
- P2: Extrinsic incentives are positively related to SBU decision speed through increased transparency/alignment, stronger outcome orientation, increased participation, more timely feedback, and increased trust between SBU and corporate managers.
- P3: Negative incentives are not significantly related to SBU decision speed.
- P4: Decision process control is positively related to SBU decision speed through increased transparency/alignment, stronger outcome orientation, and increased trust between SBU and corporate managers.
- P5: Conflict resolution is not significantly related to SBU decision speed.
- P6: Strategy imposition is negatively related to SBU decision speed through decreased transparency/alignment, decreased SBU-manager participation, and decreased trust between SBU and corporate managers.

**Mechanism Process**
- Control dimensions: Outcome control, behavior control, and content control.
- Corporate control types: Interactively developed goals and budgets; financial and career incentives; negative sanctions; procedural norms and monitoring; conflict mediation or resolution; and top-down imposition of substantive strategy.
- Proposed mechanisms: Transparency/alignment, outcome orientation, participation, trust, and timely feedback, with different combinations specified for goal setting, extrinsic incentives, decision process control, and strategy imposition.
- Focal outcome: SBU decision speed, including the time required for corporate approval or disapproval.

The authors propose that clear goals, rewards, and procedural arrangements can align corporate and SBU interests while preserving discretion over how to achieve objectives, reducing ambiguity and second-guessing. In contrast, opaque, substantive intervention can undermine participation and trust and encourage SBU managers to defer decisions in anticipation of corporate intervention. These explanations derive from executives' cognitive maps and qualitative accounts; they are not independently estimated causal mediation effects.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 theoretically sampled five international multibusiness firms in dynamic industries. It used 14 semistructured face-to-face interviews averaging two and a half hours, 312 transcript pages, and public and internal documents. Informant concepts were iteratively aggregated into corporate control types using outcome, behavior, and content control as a conceptual guide; the types were refined until additional interviews yielded no new insights. The sampled firms were related diversifiers, with Aviation Co. also displaying vertical integration.

Study 2 conducted 30 computer-assisted interviews with different executives from the same firms: two corporate and four SBU executives per firm. Respondents compared randomly selected pairs from six control types plus high and low decision speed, supplied bipolar descriptive attributes, and assessed the remaining elements against those attributes. Aggregated assessments entered a singular-value decomposition, a principal-component variant; attributes correlated at least .70 with an element were designated typical. Overlap between control-type attributes and high/low-speed attributes represented perceived association, with full statistical significance requiring a cross-section dimension of 51% or less. The typical attributes were then examined alongside Study 1 interviews to elaborate mechanisms. These are perceptual associations rather than objective duration measures or causally identified effects; collection dates are not reported.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 identified six conceptually distinct but nonexclusive corporate control types, recognized across the five firms. Goal setting, extrinsic incentives, and negative incentives primarily reflected outcome control; decision process control and conflict resolution reflected behavior control; strategy imposition focused on content control. Goal setting and conflict resolution varied in their degree of content control.

In Study 2, extrinsic incentives were most closely associated with high decision speed, followed by goal setting and decision process control, with 15, seven, and five overlapping typical attributes, respectively. Negative incentives and conflict resolution had no overlapping typical attributes with decision speed. The authors interpret the former through relatively infrequent intervention and the latter through potentially offsetting effects of quick centralized resolution and time-consuming negotiation.

Strategy imposition was associated with low decision speed at a 53% cross-section dimension, short of the 51%-or-less criterion for full significance; the authors describe this as at least marginal/moderate and corroborate the negative relationship with interviews. They report no overlap with high decision speed. The source's attribute counts are inconsistent: it reports 32 typical attributes for low decision speed overall but 42 overlapping typical attributes for strategy imposition and low decision speed, without reconciling the two counts.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends decision-speed research from predominantly small, undiversified firms to the corporate–SBU relationship in multibusiness firms. Its taxonomy distinguishes control over outcomes, processes, and substantive strategy, helping explain why centralization and formalization need not have uniform implications for speed. The proposed mechanisms also shift attention from choosing between outcome and behavior control toward understanding how different controls can be combined while preserving SBU autonomy.

**Practical Implication**
For corporate headquarters in related multibusiness firms, the authors suggest that goal setting, extrinsic incentives, and decision process control can provide strategic consistency without imposing the substance of SBU strategy. Involving SBU managers in defining goals and incentives and maintaining transparent, trusting relations offers a way to balance control with autonomy and avoid the decision delays associated with strategy imposition.

**Limitations**
The findings rely on perceptual assessments despite multiple informants, companies, hierarchical levels, and two separate stages. All companies operate in dynamic industries, warranting caution in generalizing to stable environments. Emphasis on similarities across firms and levels leaves corporate–SBU perceptual differences and contingencies such as size and geographic distribution underexamined.

**Future Research**
The authors call for more objective measures of decision speed and comparisons between corporate intentions and SBU perceptions of controls' effects. They propose examining external contingencies such as dynamism, complexity, munificence, competitive environment, and industry membership. Internal contingencies include firm size, diversification, decision importance, and top-management-team nationality, average tenure, and international work experience.
