# Business Groups as Information Resource: An Investigation of Business Group Affiliation in the Indian Software Services Industry

**Abstract**
Although business groups benefit firms when markets fail, does group affiliation continue to be an advantage for firms in deregulated, globally competitive industries? I argue that affiliation allows firms to tap into the knowledge and connections of sister affiliates. This enables them to attract clients from more industries and foreign markets than can unaffiliated firms, and to attain higher international sales. Using data from the Indian software industry, I find support for these hypotheses, but only for 2000–02, when competition increased as a result of new deregulation. These results suggest business groups continue to provide benefits to group-affiliated firms, including information on market opportunities and “reputation signaling” to clients.

**Research Question**
Does business group affiliation provide information-based advantages to affiliates in a globally competitive industry where traditional capital, labor, and product-market failures are alleviated? How does additional deregulation and the resulting increase in competition condition the relationship between group characteristics and affiliate strategy?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: As a business group's diversification increases, an affiliated firm serves more industries than unaffiliated firms.
- H1b: After new deregulation, as a business group's diversification increases, an affiliated firm serves more industries than unaffiliated firms.
- H2a: As a business group's international sales increase, an affiliated firm serves more foreign markets than unaffiliated firms.
- H2b: After new deregulation, as a business group's international sales increase, an affiliated firm serves more foreign markets than unaffiliated firms.
- H3a: As a business group's international sales increase, an affiliated firm has higher international sales than unaffiliated firms.
- H3b: After new deregulation, as a business group's international sales increase, an affiliated firm has higher international sales than unaffiliated firms.
- H4: Business group characteristics positively affect firm performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Business group diversification and business group international sales, with unaffiliated firms as the comparison category.
- DV(s): Affiliate industry segments served, foreign markets served, international sales, and firm performance/productivity.
- Mediators: Information about market opportunities, local knowledge, referrals, and reputation-based reliability signaling are proposed explanations; they are not directly measured mediation tests.
- Moderators: Additional telecommunications deregulation and the associated increase in competition, examined through period splits and deregulation interactions.

Stable ties within family-controlled business groups are argued to transmit information about clients, industries, and foreign markets among sister affiliates. Referrals and favorable impressions from prior dealings with sister affiliates can also help prospective clients evaluate an affiliate's reliability and capabilities. As additional deregulation increases the number of competing suppliers, search and information-asymmetry problems can make these informational advantages more valuable, especially in the short term. The proposed role complements the traditional market-intermediary explanation; H4 separately examines whether group characteristics retain a direct association with performance.

**Data & Measures**
- Sources and sample: Firm entries in all seven Nasscom directories covering 1992–93 through 2001–02, supplemented by Prowess and internet/media searches for group characteristics and ownership. Government enterprises, foreign firms, joint ventures, missing or unrealistic records, and firms lacking at least two consecutive observations are excluded. The stated final unbalanced panel contains 1,085 observations across six outcome periods; Table 6 Models 1–2 include 452 firms, while Model 3 includes 1,020 observations from 417 firms. The analysis compares 1994–95, 1996–97, and 1998–99 with 1999–2000, 2000–01, and 2001–02.
- Outcomes: Industry scope counts client industries; foreign-market scope counts United Nations geographic regions rather than individual countries. Firm international sales are export sales in millions of rupees, rather than export intensity. Methods define productivity as total sales divided by total employees; Table 2 additionally marks productivity with the footnote “Logarithm” despite reporting raw-scale descriptive values, leaving an internal reporting inconsistency.
- Group characteristics: Diversification counts industries at the two-digit National Industrial Classification level. Group international sales sum the international sales of all firms in the group except the focal affiliate, in units of ten million rupees. International-sales information is unavailable for the entirely private small groups absent from Prowess.
- Controls and estimation: Controls include logged employee count, firm age, a hardware-affiliate indicator, stock-exchange listing, ISO 9000/CMM certification, lagged strategy outcomes and productivity in the strategy models, and year indicators. Explanatory variables are lagged one observation period. Count outcomes use negative-binomial GEE with AR1 correlation and robust variance; international sales use the Arellano-Bover/Blundell-Bond dynamic panel estimator; productivity uses firm fixed effects with firm-cluster robust standard errors. The paper's differences-in-differences robustness models interact group characteristics with a dummy equal to one after 1999. These are observational comparisons, with information transfer and signaling inferred rather than directly observed.
- Estimation samples: Industry-count models use 865 observations overall, 237 before and 519 after deregulation; foreign-market-count models use 891, 228, and 560; international-sales models use 662, 154, and 508. Tables 3–4 explain that their subperiod counts do not sum to the pooled counts because firms with only one observation within a subperiod are dropped from that subperiod regression.

**Key Findings**
- Industry scope: Group diversification is weakly positive in the pooled model (Table 3 Model 2, b = 0.008, p < .10), nonsignificant before deregulation (b = 0.003), and positive afterward (b = 0.013, p < .05). The author concludes that H1b is supported, while the unqualified H1a is not.
- Foreign-market scope: Group international sales are positively associated with foreign regions served in the pooled model (Table 4 Model 2, b = 3.68E-05, p < .05), nonsignificant before deregulation (b = 2.38E-05), and positive afterward (b = 4.52E-05, p < .05). The author concludes that H2b, but not the unqualified H2a, is supported.
- International sales: The corresponding group-sales coefficient is positive overall (Table 5 Model 2, b = 0.90, p < .01), nonsignificant before deregulation (b = 0.77), and positive afterward (b = 0.83, p < .01). Thus H3b, but not the unqualified H3a, is supported. The post-deregulation estimate corresponds to 830,000 rupees higher affiliate international sales per additional ten million rupees of group international sales.
- Performance: H4 is not supported: neither diversification nor group international sales is significantly associated with productivity in Table 6, and the author reports the same null pattern in separate pre/post-period analyses. Affiliate international sales are positively associated with productivity in all three Table 6 models (p < .05); industry scope is positive at p < .05 in Models 1–2 but only marginal at p < .10 in Model 3, qualifying the prose's broader significance statement. Foreign-market scope is nonsignificant in all three models.
- Robustness: Table 7's group-characteristic × deregulation interactions are positive for industry scope (b = 9.21E-03, p < .10), foreign-market scope (b = 4.22E-05, p < .05), and international sales (b = 0.53, p < .01). The first interaction is only weakly significant. Added quadratic group-characteristic terms are negative but nonsignificant in all models tested; these checks do not establish a downturn in affiliation benefits.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper refines institutional economics by proposing that business groups can remain useful as information resources when traditional market failures are alleviated. It connects information economics and economic sociology with signaling to explain how affiliation can shape affiliate strategies through opportunities, referrals, and perceived reliability. The findings suggest that this role depends on competitive conditions and can differ across industries within a country, rather than being a uniform national-level function of business groups.

**Practical Implication**
Business group affiliation can help firms in newly deregulated, crowded industries identify potential clients and provide prospective buyers with a signal of reliability. The demonstrated advantage is confined to the short term in this setting; group characteristics do not show a direct productivity advantage.

**Limitations**
The single software-services setting may have characteristics, including the sale of services rather than packaged products, that especially favor referrals and signaling. CEO education and work histories are unavailable, preventing direct assessment of returning expatriates' foreign contacts, and family ownership/control is an explicit boundary condition. Only three years after additional deregulation are observed, so persistence of the advantages over the longer term remains unresolved.

**Future Research**
The author proposes studying what knowledge affiliates share, how they transfer it, and how characteristics such as relevance shape flows, as well as whether affiliates' own industry and foreign-market strategies reshape group diversification and internationalization. Further work could follow longer-term benefits and new group functions as institutions develop, and examine information sharing and reliability signaling in widely held or state-owned groups. Group heterogeneity, whether smaller or more focused groups adjust better, and affiliate-level strategy and subnational industry differences are additional stated directions.
