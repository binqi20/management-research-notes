# Directive versus Empowering Leadership: A Field Experiment Comparing Impacts on Task Proficiency and Proactivity

**Abstract**
Using a field experiment in the United Arab Emirates, we compared the impacts of directive and empowering leadership on customer-rated core task proficiency and proactive behaviors. Results of tests for main effects demonstrated that both directive and empowering leadership increased work unit core task proficiency, but only empowering leadership increased proactive behaviors. Examination of boundary conditions revealed that directive leadership enhanced proactive behaviors for work units that were highly satisfied with their leaders, whereas empowering leadership had stronger effects on both core task proficiency and proactive behaviors for work units that were less satisfied with their leaders. We discuss implications for both theory and practice.

**Research Question**
How do directive and empowering leadership differentially affect work unit core task proficiency and proactive behaviors, and how does initial satisfaction with the leader condition these effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Directive leadership improves work unit core task proficiency.
- H2: Directive leadership decreases work unit proactive behaviors.
- H3: Empowering leadership increases work unit proactive behaviors.
- H4a: Empowering leadership improves work unit core task proficiency.
- H4b: Empowering leadership's positive effect on core task proficiency is equivalent to that of directive leadership.
- H5a: Directive leadership's positive effect on core task proficiency is stronger when work unit satisfaction with the leader is higher.
- H5b: Empowering leadership's positive effect on core task proficiency is stronger when work unit satisfaction with the leader is higher.
- H5c: Empowering leadership's positive effect on proactive behaviors is stronger when work unit satisfaction with the leader is higher.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Directive leadership (specific guidance, performance monitoring and feedback) and empowering leadership (sharing responsibility and decision authority, with support).
- DV(s): Work unit core task proficiency and proactive behaviors.
- Mediators: Not tested; proposed processes include role clarity and goal commitment for directive leadership, and intrinsic motivation, role-breadth self-efficacy and collective psychological empowerment for empowering leadership.
- Moderators: Initial work unit satisfaction with the leader, encompassing liking and perceived competence.

Directive leadership channels effort toward prescribed goals and procedures, theoretically benefiting core tasks while restricting self-directed change. Empowering leadership supplies autonomy and support that can strengthen collective empowerment and initiative while also sustaining core task effort. Drawing on consent theory of authority, the authors predict that satisfaction increases receptivity to both styles; the observed empowering-leadership interactions instead favor less-satisfied units. Their retrospective explanations, including greater appreciation of autonomy among dissatisfied followers, are proposed rather than directly tested mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
- A ten-week UAE field experiment initially randomized 95 leaders to directive, empowering and control conditions (32, 33 and 30); 86 remained (28, 30 and 28) after withdrawals citing time constraints. Leaders came from varied industries and occupations and had at least one direct report. Experimental leaders received initial training and biweekly coaching, and were instructed to practice the assigned behaviors for 15 minutes daily and maintain daily logs; controls were asked to continue their usual leadership.
- Employee surveys yielded 282 pretest and 272 posttest responses from 324 contacted direct reports, with 261 matched respondents. Customer surveys yielded 288 pretest and 280 posttest responses from 325 customers. The paper additionally reports 3.9 matched customer responses per unit without a matched total; that average does not reconcile with the reported 86 units and customer counts.
- Employees rated directive and empowering leadership with five items each and initial leader satisfaction with four items. Customers rated collective core task proficiency and proactivity with four items each, adapted from Griffin et al.; all used five-point response scales. Employee and customer ratings were aggregated to work units, supported by agreement and intraclass correlations.
- Main effects were assessed using MANCOVA, repeated-measures ANOVA and paired-sample tests; moderated OLS used grand-mean-centered initial satisfaction multiplied by condition indicators. Analyses accounted for initial performance and included recruitment source, employee/customer response modes, leader gender, tenure and ethnicity/nationality. Leadership conditions were randomized, whereas satisfaction was measured and followers were not randomly assigned to leaders.

**Key Findings**
- H1 and H4a were supported: proficiency increased by .81 under directive leadership and .86 under empowering leadership (both p < .001), versus a nonsignificant .04 increase in controls. For empowering proficiency, Table 4 reports t(29) = 5.80 while the prose reports 5.81. The authors interpret H4b as supported because the between-treatment difference was nonsignificant (time-by-condition F(1,48) = .01); this is their equivalence interpretation, rather than a formal equivalence test.
- H2 was not supported: directive proactivity changed by .09, nonsignificantly. H3 was supported: empowering proactivity increased by 1.02 (p < .001), compared with a nonsignificant −.06 change in controls. Table 4 posttest comparisons show empowering proactivity exceeded both other conditions (p < .001), while directive and control did not differ significantly.
- H5a was unsupported: the directive-by-satisfaction interaction for proficiency was nonsignificant (standardized β = −.12). H5b and H5c were contradicted by the observed direction: empowering leadership had stronger effects at lower satisfaction, with interactions of β = −.35 for proficiency (p < .01) and β = −.19 for proactivity (p < .05). Its proficiency slope was significant at low but not high satisfaction; its proactivity slopes were positive and significant at both levels, larger at low satisfaction.
- Post hoc, directive leadership's proactivity effect increased with satisfaction (β = .28, p < .05); the simple slope was positive and significant at high satisfaction but nonsignificant at low satisfaction. Table 5 reports standardized coefficients and one-tailed tests for these moderation results.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The experiment distinguishes leadership effects on core proficiency from effects on proactivity, identifying empowering leadership as a malleable contextual antecedent of proactive performance. It also shows the importance of followers' initial attitudes for leadership effectiveness, with the empowering-leadership moderation running opposite to the consent-based prediction. The UAE setting adds experimental evidence on empowering leadership in a high-power-distance culture.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend retaining directive behaviors in leaders' repertoires for core task performance while using empowering leadership to encourage proactivity, balancing meaningful autonomy with support and capability-building. They advise monitoring employees' perceptions: lower satisfaction may offer an opportunity for empowerment, whereas highly satisfied units may respond proactively even to directive leadership. Empowerment required more demanding coaching in this intervention; the authors suggest leaders are likely to need organizational support to balance autonomy and assistance.

**Limitations**
The heterogeneous sample leaves possible extraneous influences, and naturally nested followers leave preexisting employee and leader–employee differences uncontrolled by random assignment. The UAE context and Western-developed measures warrant cross-cultural examination, while separate leadership conditions simplify the mixed behaviors leaders may use in practice. Long-term effects beyond ten weeks and the proposed mediators remain untested; the authors consider control-group resentful demoralization but judge it unlikely given the design and performance pattern.

**Future Research**
The authors call for constructive replications in other cultural contexts and tests of blended or modified directive and empowering styles. Longitudinal field experiments should examine how collective intrinsic motivation, role-breadth self-efficacy and empowerment, or role clarity and goal commitment, mediate the respective styles' effects over time. They also propose examining how paternalistic caring might help directive leaders create conditions for proactivity.
