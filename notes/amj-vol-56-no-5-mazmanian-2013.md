# Avoiding the Trap of Constant Connectivity: When Congruent Frames Allow for Heterogeneous Practices

**Abstract**
A three-year qualitative study of the use of mobile e-mail devices in a footwear manufacturer focused on the experience of two occupational functions. Evidence suggests that congruent frames of heterogeneous communication practices enabled one group to develop communication norms that circumvented the trap of constant connectivity, while assumptions of homogeneous communication practices in the other group led to expanded accessibility and erosion of personal time. This study examines how such alternate trajectories of use emerged and discusses the key dimensions of difference between groups—identity, materiality, vulnerability, and visibility—that help account for these differences. In introducing the distinction between homogeneous and heterogeneous trajectories of use and explicating how such trajectories emerge, this study offers several theoretical insights: it suggests that there is a distinction between the congruence of technological frames of reference and the content of these frames; it provides an explanation for why groups might enact mobile communication technologies in a manner that does not lead to constant connectivity; and it highlights how shared assumptions of heterogeneity relate to systems of social control.

**Research Question**
How do occupational groups develop different trajectories of mobile e-mail use, and how can congruent technological frames permit heterogeneous communication practices rather than shared expectations of constant connectivity?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key distinction: congruence of technological frames means shared expectations, whose content can legitimate different individual practices rather than require uniform behavior.
- Process: initial framing, episodes of use, collective assumptions, and ongoing reframing link individual interpretations and practices with group expectations.
- Explanatory dimensions: occupational identity; physical, functional and symbolic materiality; vulnerability to social pressure; and visibility of communication practices.
- Contrasting trajectories: lawyers' client-service identity, perceived need to prove their value, and visible peer communication accompanied homogeneous availability expectations; sales representatives' autonomous identity, revenue-based evaluation and limited visibility of peers' customer correspondence supported accepted differences in use.

Identity and interpretations of the device shape initial enactment; experience and observations of others then inform collective assumptions and reframing. Shared expectations of heterogeneity can make varied practices normal rather than deviant, while homogeneous expectations can intensify social control. This is an inductively developed process account; the author does not claim that the observed trajectories are stable or irreversible.

**Data & Measures**
The field engagement ran from June 2005 to November 2008 at Linden, a pseudonymous, internationally operating US-headquartered footwear and apparel company. The focal workforce comprised seven in-house attorneys and approximately 100 US mobile salespeople. Semistructured interviews covered 50 sales team members and three spouses, all seven attorneys, and conversations with six attorneys' spouses; most participants were interviewed repeatedly. Structured follow-ups with 12 sales executives and seven attorneys reviewed at least four days of each person's mobile e-mail, recording messages and their contexts. Approximately 200 observation days, three annual open-ended e-mail surveys to company BlackBerry users (67–85% response), and organizational documents supplemented the interviews.

Grounded-theory analysis proceeded from provisional codes and e-mail patterns to aggregated theoretical categories, then interrogation of explanatory mechanisms, comparing accounts within and between groups. The four dimensions were interpretive categories, not experimentally manipulated variables. Tables 1–2 distinguish initial, emergent and ongoing phases: within one week, after one to two years, and after two to three years of receipt. Legal-team counts remain seven; sales counts are 47, 39 and 36. The narrative instead describes interviews with 47 sales representatives before or within two weeks of receipt; the paper gives these different timing descriptions without reconciling them.

**Key Findings**
- Lawyers moved from initial enthusiasm toward resentment as expectations of accessibility expanded. Approximately one year after receipt, each lawyer upheld availability expectations of 15–16 hours daily; a later discussion of boundaries did not prevent further perceived increases. Table 1 records six positive and one ambivalent lawyer initially, versus one positive, two ambivalent and four negative in the ongoing phase. The narrative's statement that six of seven resented the device is distinct from the table's four coded negative attitudes. All seven regularly used it during off hours in each phase.
- Sales representatives maintained heterogeneous practices while becoming more favorable toward the device: Table 2 records 19 positive, ten ambivalent and 18 negative initially (n = 47), versus 30 positive, four ambivalent and two negative in the ongoing phase (n = 36). Regular off-hours use was 43%, 54% and 50% across the three phases. Accepted variation, rather than universal avoidance of off-hours use, accompanied reports of greater efficiency and more personal time.
- The comparative account identifies identity, materiality, vulnerability and visibility as dimensions explaining these trajectories. Lawyers displayed the device as a professional symbol and saw peers' copied client communications; sales representatives valued its practical usefulness while distancing themselves from its symbolism and generally lacked visibility of peers' customer exchanges. Sales customers generally did not expect after-hours communication, qualifying the setting in which heterogeneous use persisted.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study separates the existence of congruent technological frames from the content of those frames: shared expectations can sustain heterogeneity rather than align behavior. It develops a longitudinal account of framing, use, collective assumptions and reframing that supports the situated enactment of technology. It also suggests a potential route of resistance to coercive cognitive and identity control through socially accepted differences in practice.

**Practical Implication**
For individuals and organizations struggling with unsustainable connectivity, the paper emphasizes collective expectations: simply switching off a device may be untenable when succeeding socially depends on accessibility. It proposes changing perceived vulnerability through rewards or censure, and visibility through superiors' modeling or communication rules and norms, as possible levers for shifting trajectories; these are proposed interventions, not evaluated remedies.

**Limitations**
The inductive case is not designed to support direct transfer of findings, and the four dimensions are neither exhaustive nor conventionally generalizable. Occupational communities emerged as relevant here but may not be the only social groupings in which shared trajectories develop. Although the lawyers were colocated and salespeople distributed, the study did not explicitly investigate that distinction as its explanatory focus.

**Future Research**
Examine other settings in which shared frames permit heterogeneous practices, their enabling conditions, and the individual and organizational costs and benefits of homogeneous versus heterogeneous connectivity. Establish whether ongoing communication alone can generate collective trajectories or whether shared identity, structural position or work practices must also align. Further formalize the process and shifts over time, including how distributed teams establish congruent frames and how distance shapes trajectories of use.
