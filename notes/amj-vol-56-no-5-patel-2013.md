# Walking the Tightrope: An Assessment of the Relationship between High-Performance Work Systems and Organizational Ambidexterity

**Abstract**
This study explores central questions related to the connections between a firm’s human resources (HR) system and its ability to exhibit “organizational ambidexterity.” We build from existing work on the behavioral view of ambidexterity to assess the extent to which the utilization of certain HR practices may be linked to a context marked by discipline, stretch, trust, and support. We further argue that these disparate practices may be combined into a single high-performance work system (HPWS), which allows the firm to achieve both the alignment and the adaptability necessary to produce ambidexterity. Thus, we examine HPWS as a systematic tool for enhancing organizational ambidexterity. We explore this link using data from 215 high-tech small to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and find that HPWS utilization is positively related to a measure of organizational ambidexterity. In turn, ambidexterity mediates the relationship between HPWS utilization and firm growth.

**Research Question**
Is high-performance work system (HPWS) utilization associated with organizational ambidexterity in high-technology SMEs, and does ambidexterity partially mediate the relationship between HPWS and firm growth?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: HPWS utilization is positively related to organizational ambidexterity.
- H2: Organizational ambidexterity partially mediates the relationship between HPWS utilization and firm growth.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): HPWS utilization, combining complementary employment practices.
- DV(s): Firm growth.
- Mediators: Organizational ambidexterity, operationalized as exploration-exploitation congruence.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The authors propose that alignment-oriented HR practices foster discipline and stretch, while adaptation-oriented practices foster support and trust. Together, these practices are expected to develop a workforce capable of both exploiting existing opportunities and exploring new ones; flexibility resides in employees’ behavioral choices. The practice sets are complementary rather than orthogonal and can have overlapping effects. Discipline, stretch, support, and trust provide the theoretical explanation but are not directly measured.

**Data & Measures**
- **Sample and timing:** The 2009 Corptech frame contained 1,526 high-technology manufacturing ventures in five midwestern US states, with 10-250 employees and ages of ten years or less. Surveys and reminders during October 2009-January 2010 yielded 219 CEO responses (14.35%); excluding four incomplete cases left 215 firms. Survey measures referred to 2007-2009, whereas archival performance covered 2008-2010.
- **HPWS:** A 27-item, five-point agreement scale was combined into an additive index covering participation, internal mobility, training, staffing, job description, appraisal, job security, and incentive rewards. Methods calls the job-description domain “broad job design”; Appendix A’s actual items concern clear, current, and accurate job descriptions.
- **Ambidexterity:** Six exploration and six exploitation items used five-point agreement scales (α = .87 and .86, respectively). Latent congruence modeling incorporates measurement error and item covariance. Appendix B describes level as the average of exploration and exploitation and congruence as their absolute difference, subsequently multiplied by −1 so that higher interpreted scores indicate closer balance. Its loading prose states +0.5 for both components, whereas Equation 2 uses −0.5 for exploitation; this source inconsistency is retained rather than reconciled.
- **Growth and estimation:** Firm growth averages sales and employee growth, using Corptech, Dun and Bradstreet, and Ward’s directories; all 215 firms appear in at least two sources, and 32 in all three. The printed sales CAGR formula is `(sales2010 / sales2008)^(1/3) − 1`; employee growth is also measured as a CAGR for 2008-2010. The analysis uses robust weighted least squares with standardized coefficients and 1,000 bootstrap samples for indirect-effect standard errors. Figure 1 includes controls for ambidexterity level, exploration, exploitation, firm size, age, R&D intensity, environmental dynamism, complexity and munificence, social desirability, and its interactions with HPWS and congruence. This is an associational design, with overlapping survey-reference and performance windows.
- **Post hoc outcomes:** Labor productivity builds on the log of sales per employee using a three-year prospective geometric average for 2008-2010; the paper does not clearly specify the order of logging and geometric aggregation. The innovation measure averages counts of incremental and radical new products introduced during 2008-2010, then divides by average employment over those years. Incremental products fall within product classes offered before 2007; radical products enter classes absent from the pre-2007 portfolio.

**Key Findings**
- **H1 supported:** HPWS is positively associated with ambidexterity congruence (β = .51; Results prose reports p < .001, while Figure 1 marks p < .01).
- **H2 supported as partial mediation:** The indirect HPWS-growth association through congruence is positive (β = .12, p < .05), alongside a positive direct association (β = .29, p < .05). Congruence is positively associated with growth (β = .24, p < .05); the partial-mediation model fits better than the full-mediation model.
- **Specification checks:** The multiplicative ambidexterity measure also supports H1 (β = .54, p < .01) and H2 (indirect effect .14, p < .01). Reported absolute-difference, difference-score, sum, and average specifications retain positive, significant H1 and indirect-effect results.
- **Post hoc associations:** HPWS relates positively to labor productivity (β = .27, p < .05) and employee-adjusted new-product introductions (β = .32, p < .05). These findings are consistent with the proposed efficiency-and-innovation account, without directly establishing the contextual mechanism.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study links the behavioral/contextual view of ambidexterity to a tangible organizational system: complementary HR practices that may develop resource flexibility within the same workforce. It also identifies ambidexterity as a partial statistical link between HPWS and growth in SMEs, extending strategic HR research beyond its usual emphasis on larger organizations. The proposed mapping from HR practices to discipline, stretch, support, and trust remains theoretical.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that attention to employee selection, training, compensation, and rewards may help firms pursue both efficiency and innovation. They expect such systems to be particularly valuable in high-technology settings marked by uncertainty and change. They also caution that HPWS can impose unaffordable time and costs on some small firms, may be unnecessary in more stable markets, and may be less suitable than alignment-focused practices for cost leaders.

**Limitations**
HPWS and ambidexterity were measured at a single point in time, and the four proposed contextual elements were not observed directly. The relatively small, high-technology SME sample and low response rate constrain generalization; nonsignificant response-bias tests cannot exclude systematic psychological differences between respondents and nonrespondents. HPWS measurement also did not distinguish which employee groups were covered by particular practices.

**Future Research**
The authors call for longitudinal tests, direct measures of discipline, stretch, support, and trust, and finer comparisons of participation, selection, and compensation programs. Larger samples spanning more industries and finer analyses of employee groups, including in larger organizations, would clarify coverage and generalizability. They also identify environmental uncertainty, stable demand and product life cycles, and organizational strategies such as cost leadership as possible boundaries on the value of HPWS and ambidexterity.
