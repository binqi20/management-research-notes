# Finding Meaning through Volunteering: Why Do Employees Volunteer and What Does It Mean for Their Jobs?

**Abstract**
Volunteering is prevalent and on the rise in the United States, but little research has examined the connection between individuals’ volunteering and their jobs. In the absence of that research, it remains unclear whether employees volunteer to build on meaningful work experiences or to compensate for the lack of them. Similarly, it remains unclear whether volunteering is beneficial to jobs in some way or if it is a distraction, akin to moonlighting. In this research, several theoretical perspectives from the multiple domain literature—particularly, compensation, enhancement, and resource drain—were employed in two studies to examine the intersection between volunteering and work domains. Results suggested that volunteering was associated with both volunteer and job meaningfulness, and that the pull of meaningful volunteer work was even stronger when employees had less meaning in their jobs. The results further revealed benefits of volunteering for employers. Volunteering was related to job absorption but not job interference, and it was therefore associated with better job performance. Implications of these findings for future theorizing on volunteering are discussed.

**Research Question**
Do employees volunteer to extend the meaningfulness experienced in their jobs or to compensate for its absence, and how is volunteering related to their job performance through absorption and interference?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1 (Study 1): Job meaningfulness has a negative indirect effect on volunteering through wanderlust.
- H2 (Study 1): Job meaningfulness has a positive indirect effect on volunteering through voracity.
- H3 (Study 2): Volunteering meaningfulness is positively related to volunteering when job meaningfulness is controlled.
- H4 (Study 2): Job meaningfulness is positively related to volunteering when volunteering meaningfulness is controlled.
- H5 (Study 2): Job meaningfulness moderates the volunteering meaningfulness–volunteering relationship, which is more positive when job meaningfulness is low.
- H6 (Study 2): Through job interference, volunteering has negative indirect relationships with task performance and citizenship behavior and a positive indirect relationship with counterproductive behavior.
- H7 (Study 2): Through job absorption, volunteering has positive indirect relationships with task performance and citizenship behavior and a negative indirect relationship with counterproductive behavior.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Job meaningfulness in both studies; volunteering meaningfulness and volunteering intensity in Study 2.
- DV(s): Volunteering intensity in both studies; task performance, organization-directed citizenship behavior, and organization-directed counterproductive behavior in Study 2.
- Mediators: Study 1 wanderlust and voracity between job meaningfulness and volunteering; Study 2 job interference and job absorption between volunteering and job performance.
- Moderators: Study 2 job meaningfulness moderates the association between volunteering meaningfulness and volunteering intensity.

Compensation theorizing proposes that employees seek volunteering to replace something missing from their jobs, captured in Study 1 as wanderlust; enhancement proposes that valued job experiences stimulate appetite for more, captured as voracity. Study 2 treats compensation as a stronger pull from meaningful volunteering when job meaningfulness is low, alongside a positive enhancement association with job meaningfulness. For job performance, resource drain predicts that volunteering consumes finite psychological resources and creates interference, whereas enhancement predicts that it supplies attention and energy that support job absorption. These are theoretical mechanisms tested through observational relationships, not experimentally established causal effects.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 recruited employed students from introductory business courses in the US Southeast. Of 232 registered individuals who had volunteered in the preceding year, 208 completed the second survey (89.7%). Surveys were approximately four weeks apart: the first measured job meaningfulness, prosocial identity and demographics; the second measured wanderlust, voracity and volunteering. Calendar dates are not reported.
- Volunteering is the intensity of active giving of time or skills in planned activities for volunteer organizations, measured with five frequency items rather than hours alone. Study 1 used five items each for wanderlust and voracity, three items for job meaningfulness, and controlled for three-item prosocial identity, age and gender. Volunteering responses ranged from almost never to very often; the motive and meaningfulness scales used agreement responses, all on five-point scales. Separate volunteering-scale validation samples comprised 782 students for content validity and 81 working students for factor/convergent validity; separate wanderlust/voracity validation samples comprised 593 and 80 students, respectively. These are distinct from the main sample.
- Study 2 recruited employed volunteers through the United Way and Junior League in one southeastern US county. Of 300 approached, 226 agreed; the paper prints a 76.5% response rate, inconsistent with those counts. Methods reports a final sample of 172 with complete self-reports and at least one coworker report, whereas Tables 3 and 4 use 171. The coworker paragraph reports 173 participants but lists 115 with two coworker reports and 59 with one, another unresolved inconsistency. Two coworker ratings were averaged where available; no collection dates or survey interval are reported.
- Study 2 participants rated volunteering with the same five-item scale, volunteering meaningfulness with three adapted Spreitzer items, interference with five adapted family–work-conflict items, and absorption with six items. Coworkers rated job meaningfulness with three items and task performance with five items; eight citizenship and twelve counterproductive-behavior items concerned actions toward the organization. Most scales used five-point responses, while citizenship and counterproductive behavior used seven-point frequency scales. Prosocial identity, age and gender controlled volunteering; a three-item coworker opportunity-to-observe measure controlled the performance regressions.
- Both studies used structural equation modeling and RMediation indirect-effect tests. In Study 2, job and volunteer meaningfulness scale scores were single indicators with error variance set to (1 − alpha) × variance; scores were mean-centered before multiplication to form the interaction indicator. Product reliability was [(reliability of X × reliability of Z) + squared X–Z correlation] / [1 + squared X–Z correlation], and the same error-variance formula was applied to the product. Other constructs were modeled as fully latent. Temporal/source separation mitigates some common-method concerns but does not establish causal direction.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: H1 was unsupported: the indirect relationship via wanderlust was nonsignificant (job meaningfulness → wanderlust b = −.10; wanderlust → volunteering b = .17). H2 was supported, with a reported positive indirect effect of .03 through voracity. The prose gives the voracity → volunteering path as .17, whereas Table 2 gives .19; both give job meaningfulness → voracity as .19. The prose also calls age and gender nonsignificant, while Table 2 marks their coefficients significant; these discrepancies remain unresolved.
- Study 2: H3 and H4 were supported: volunteering meaningfulness and job meaningfulness were positively related to volunteering when the other was controlled (b = .47 and .15, respectively). H5 was supported: meaningful volunteering had a stronger positive association with volunteering intensity when job meaningfulness was low; the interaction is b = −.13 in PDF Figure 2. Thus compensation in Study 2 is an interaction finding, distinct from the unsupported wanderlust-mediated prediction in Study 1.
- Study 2: H6 was unsupported; the indirect relationships through job interference were nonsignificant. H7 was supported: volunteering was positively related to absorption (b = .21), which was positively related to task performance and citizenship behavior (b = .14 each) and negatively related to counterproductive behavior (b = −.24). Table 4 reports corresponding indirect effects of .03, .03 and −.05, and total effects of .19, .20 and −.20. The cited significant Study 2 paths and effects use p < .05, one-tailed; Figure 2 identifies its path coefficients as unstandardized. These findings favor the proposed enhancement pathway over resource drain in this sample, without showing that volunteering cannot be harmful in other circumstances.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends multiple-domain research beyond the work–family relationship by examining volunteering as a distinct domain and operationalizing compensation and enhancement through wanderlust and voracity. It shows how positive job-meaningfulness associations can coexist with a compensation interaction involving volunteer meaningfulness, and contrasts absorption and interference as potential links to job performance. It also supplies initial validity evidence for a behavioral measure of volunteering intensity.

**Practical Implication**
The author suggests employers need not assume community volunteering distracts employees from their jobs and may consider its potential benefits in selection and formal support for volunteering. Managers may also encourage meaningful volunteering when employees feel their jobs lack meaning. Suggested benefits for motivation and retention are practical proposals, rather than outcomes tested in these studies.

**Limitations**
Common-method bias and reverse causality cannot be excluded despite temporal separation in Study 1 and source separation in Study 2; hypothesized orderings fit better than or equivalently to reversed alternatives, which does not resolve causal direction. The volunteering measure focuses on intensity rather than the initial decision to volunteer or commitment over time, and corporate volunteering may blur the assumed boundary between volunteer and work domains. Study 2 relies on coworker performance ratings despite instructions to select knowledgeable raters, agreement checks and an opportunity-to-observe control.

**Future Research**
Use cross-lagged panels to examine causal direction and reciprocal relationships, and investigate volunteering choices and longevity separately from intensity. Examine skills and contacts as workplace-related drivers, their interaction with volunteer characteristics, and corporate volunteering’s blurring of domain boundaries and personal preferences for segmentation or integration. Obtain supervisor performance reports and extend the outcomes to coworker-directed behaviors.
