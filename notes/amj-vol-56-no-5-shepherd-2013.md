# “I Care about Nature, but …”: Disengaging Values in Assessing Opportunities that Cause Harm

**Abstract**
Some managers and entrepreneurs decide to act in ways that result in harm to the natural environment, despite the fact that such actions violate their own values. Building on moral self-regulation theory (Bandura, 1991), we propose that entrepreneurs’ assessments of the attractiveness of opportunities that harm the natural environment depend on the simultaneous impact of values and personal agency. By cognitively disengaging their pro-environmental values, decision makers (i.e., entrepreneurs) can (under certain circumstances) perceive opportunities that harm the environment as highly attractive and thus suitable for exploitation. The results of a judgment task that generated 1,264 opportunity assessments nested within 83 business founders offered support for this general prediction and indicated that the extent of founders’ disengagement of their pro-environmental values was stronger when they had high, rather than low, entrepreneurial self-efficacy, and stronger when industry munificence was perceived as low rather than high. We discuss our new measure of moral disengagement in a decision-making context and the implications of the study’s findings for extant literatures on moral disengagement and sustainable entrepreneurship.

**Research Question**
Under what conditions do entrepreneurs disengage their pro-environmental values when assessing opportunities that would harm the natural environment? How do entrepreneurial self-efficacy and perceived industry munificence condition the relationship between values and the weight assigned to specified environmental harm?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Stronger pro-environmental values increase the weight entrepreneurs assign to specified environmental harm when assessing opportunity attractiveness.
- H2: The impact of pro-environmental values on the weight assigned to harm is reduced more when entrepreneurial self-efficacy is high than when it is low.
- H3: The impact of pro-environmental values on the weight assigned to harm is reduced more when perceived industry munificence is low than when it is high.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Pro-environmental values; manipulated information about specified environmental harm, relative to a neutral environmental-impact context.
- DV(s): Opportunity-attractiveness judgments and the negative weight assigned to specified harm in those judgments; industry energy inefficiency serves as an alternative outcome in the actual-entry robustness analysis.
- Mediators: No separately estimated mediator. Moral disengagement is inferred from attenuation of the relationship between values and the weight assigned to harm.
- Moderators: Entrepreneurial self-efficacy and perceived industry munificence moderate the values–harm-weight relationship; objective munificence is used in robustness analyses.

Values ordinarily constrain opportunities inconsistent with respect for nature. High entrepreneurial self-efficacy makes exploiting opportunities potentially satisfying, while low perceived munificence makes forgoing scarce opportunities costly; the resulting conflict with moral values is theorized to encourage disengagement. Rather than changing values or the specified harm, founders can reduce the influence of values on how strongly they penalize that harm. Values are assumed relatively stable, and the study does not directly measure the cognitive tactics or felt conflict proposed to explain this pattern.

**Data & Measures**
The sampling frame comprised 534 ventures with 597 founders in 26 German incubators across three states. Of 350 contacted founders from different ventures, 93 responded (26.6%); four incomplete responses and six with test–retest correlations below .30 were excluded, leaving 83 founders (mean retest correlation .87). Recruitment lasted four months, with no calendar dates reported. Methods report 16 neutral/harm assessments per founder and 1,328 observations; the abstract and introduction instead state 1,264, an unresolved source discrepancy.

Three binary strategic attributes—value, rarity, and inimitability/nonsubstitutability—were combined into four original profiles and four replications per condition. Founders completed 25 profiles: nine neutral, including one excluded practice profile, eight environmentally harmful, and eight environmentally beneficial profiles used to examine order effects. Neutral always came first; beneficial and harmful conditions were counterbalanced, and participants were randomly assigned among eight forms varying attribute, profile and environmental-information orders. Reported order-effect tests were nonsignificant. Attractiveness of exploitation for the current business was rated from 1 (not at all attractive) to 11 (very attractive); this is a judgment outcome, not observed exploitation.

The postexperimental questionnaire measured pro-environmental values using four paired-statement respect-for-nature items on seven-point scales (α = .79), entrepreneurial self-efficacy using 22 task-confidence items on five-point scales (α = .93), and perceived munificence using a reverse-coded three-item scale (higher scores indicate greater munificence; α = .60). Controls included founder age, perceived dynamism (four items, α = .75), and the three profile attributes. Independent variables were mean-centered. HLM modeled assessments within manipulated contexts within founders; measured founder characteristics and their interactions remain associational even though profile information was experimentally varied.

Objective munificence used a five-year industry shipment-regression slope divided by mean shipments; the paper explicitly describes the regression as time on shipment value. Objective dynamism divided the slope's standard error by the same mean, using three-digit German industries. The actual-entry robustness outcome was industry energy intensity—energy required per €1 of value creation across ten broad sectors—with an additional dummy for ventures explicitly dedicated to environmental protection. This proxy does not measure the focal firm's actual emissions.

**Key Findings**
- Specified harm reduced attractiveness judgments (harm intercept = −2.68, p < .001). H1 was unsupported: in Table 2's main-effects model, the values coefficient on harm weight was −.10 (SE = .20, p > .10). The authors caution against interpreting this main effect without the interactions.
- H2 was supported in the full model: values × entrepreneurial self-efficacy = .19 (SE = .08, p < .05), indicating less negative harm weighting associated with stronger values when self-efficacy was high. Among founders with high pro-environmental values, the illustrated harm weights were −3.87 for low versus −1.95 for high self-efficacy.
- H3 was supported: values × perceived munificence = −.18 (SE = .09, p < .05). Among high-values founders, illustrated harm weights were −3.89 for high versus −1.87 for low munificence. Thus, greater disengagement means a weaker negative weight on harm, not a positive valuation of harm.
- Strategic attributes positively predicted attractiveness. Table 2 reports value = 2.62 in the base model but 2.17 in the main/full models, whereas the prose states 2.62 throughout; rarity = .53 and inimitability = 1.68 throughout (all p < .001).
- Munificence moderation persisted with objective industry measures (interaction = −14.93, p < .05) and the actual-industry-entry proxy (−1.34, p < .05). Self-efficacy moderation was nonsignificant in both robustness analyses, so its support does not extend to those specifications. Retaining the unreliable respondents reportedly left the main results substantially unchanged.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends moral disengagement theory by examining how personal agency conditions the connection between values and the weight assigned to a specified harm, complementing explanations based on cognitively restructuring harm or responsibility. Its conjoint approach captures this values–judgment relationship as judgments are made, while the voluntary opportunity context helps explain why high self-efficacy may accompany greater disengagement, unlike findings from required tasks. It also brings environmentally harmful opportunity choices into sustainable entrepreneurship research that has often emphasized environmentally beneficial opportunities.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that entrepreneurs' awareness of these assessment patterns may increase attention to environmental and possibly long-term consequences. They argue that environmental-protection legal frameworks may be particularly important in low-munificence industries. Efforts to increase students' entrepreneurial self-efficacy may have an unintended disengagement downside, but this educational implication remains a proposal for further investigation.

**Limitations**
Hypothetical profiles simplify real decisions and omit emotion and other contextual features; the measured values scale may overlap political or economic views. Self-efficacy moderation failed the robustness checks, with coarse industry-entry measurement and reduced independence from industry nesting offered as possible explanations, not demonstrated causes. German incubator founders limit generalization to other entrepreneurs and cultures, and measured personal characteristics do not establish causal effects of values or self-efficacy.

**Future Research**
The authors propose manipulating or theoretically sampling industry munificence, examining judgments outside experiments, and distinguishing moral values from political and economic views. They call for studies of team heterogeneity in values, self-efficacy and perceived resources, nonincubator entrepreneurs, and additional cultures. Further work should investigate why stronger values sometimes accompany greater disengagement under high personal agency and whether self-efficacy-enhancing education produces the proposed unintended effect.
