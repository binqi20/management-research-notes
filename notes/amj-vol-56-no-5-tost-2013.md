# When Power Makes Others Speechless: The Negative Impact of Leader Power on Team Performance

**Abstract**
We examine the impact of the subjective experience of power on leadership dynamics and team performance and find that the psychological effect of power on formal leaders spills over to affect team performance. We argue that a formal leader’s experience of heightened power produces verbal dominance, which reduces team communication and consequently diminishes performance. Importantly, because these dynamics rely on the acquiescence of other team members to the leader’s dominant behavior, the effects only emerge when the leader holds a formal leadership position. Three studies offer consistent support for this argument. The implications for theory and practice are discussed.

**Research Question**
How does a leader’s subjective experience of power affect team communication and performance, and how do formal authority and awareness of other members’ instrumentality condition these effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Formal leaders with high, versus neutral, subjective power spend more time talking in team meetings.
- H2: Teams with high-power formal leaders report lower authority openness and open communication than teams with neutral-power formal leaders.
- H3: Teams with high-power formal leaders perform worse than teams with neutral-power formal leaders.
- H4: Leader talking and then communication openness sequentially mediate the effect of formal-leader power on team performance.
- H5: Power’s effects on leader talking, team open communication and team performance emerge only when the leader holds formal authority.
- H6: Formal authority moderates the first stage of the sequential indirect effect, which is significant only for formal leaders.
- H7: Reminding formal leaders of other members’ instrumentality eliminates power’s effects on talking, open communication and team performance.
- H8: Instrumentality awareness moderates the first stage of the sequential indirect effect, which is significant only without the reminder.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader’s subjective experience of power, manipulated as high versus neutral power; distinct from holding formal authority.
- DV(s): Team performance, operationalized through goal achievement, correct decisions and, in Study 2, learning.
- Mediators: Leader’s amount of talking followed by communication openness: perceived authority openness in Study 1 and team open communication in Studies 2–3.
- Moderators: Formal leadership role in Study 2 and the formal leader’s awareness of team members’ instrumentality in Study 3, each proposed to moderate the power-to-talking path.

The authors argue that subjective power encourages expressing one’s own opinions and devaluing others’ contributions, prompting attempted verbal dominance. Formal authority legitimizes that behavior and encourages followers to yield the floor; the resulting dominance restricts communication and information sharing. Assuming leaders value team performance, reminders of members’ useful contributions should encourage listening instead. Follower deference is a proposed, unmodeled proximal moderator, while the talking–openness sequence is examined through measured mediators.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 involved 106 undergraduates and MBA students at a southeastern US university: 20 five-person teams, six with an additional observer. Participants were randomly assigned to teams and simulation roles. Formal leaders received a high-power autobiographical writing exercise or no power exercise before reviewing role materials the day before an Everest simulation; the simulation comprised six rounds lasting about 80 minutes. Nonleaders’ post-simulation estimates of leader talking as a percentage of the time allotted for the simulation were aggregated, as were five seven-point authority-openness items, including two reverse-scored items. Performance was the simulation’s percentage of team goals achieved. All analyses controlled for team size.
- Study 2 involved 144 people, including 125 students, in a northeastern US city, assigned to 48 three-person teams. A two-by-two design crossed high-power versus neutral grocery-shopping recall for one randomly chosen member with a formal LEADER name tag versus no formal designation for that member. A hidden-profile murder mystery supplied 45 shared and eight unique clues; the focal member had two unique clues and each other member three. Following 20 minutes of individual reading and 30 minutes of discussion, members reported talking percentages and four seven-point open-communication items, aggregated to team scores. Decision performance was identifying the correct suspect; learning was the team mean of members’ reported number of unique clues learned, out of eight. Other members also rated the focal member’s autocratic style.
- Study 3 involved 152 people, including 130 students, in a northeastern US city, assigned to 38 four-person teams. All teams had formal leaders; high-power writing versus no writing was crossed with a reminder that each member could uniquely contribute versus no reminder. A hidden-profile CFO selection task allowed 20 minutes for individual reading and 20 minutes for discussion. Talking and open communication used the Study 2 measures; performance was choosing the best candidate. The instrumentality check averaged a leader’s four item ratings for each of three members and then averaged across those members. ANOVA and logistic models tested effects; exact logistic regression tested Study 3 decision performance because one cell had no correct answers. Sequential mediation was estimated from measured talking and openness, so the mediator links were not separately randomized.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported H1–H4. High-power versus neutral-power formal leaders talked more (32.73% versus 18.70%, p < .001), were perceived as less open (4.84 versus 5.37, p < .05), and led teams attaining fewer goals (59.00% versus 76.20%, p < .01). The sequential indirect effect through talking and openness had a 95% bias-corrected bootstrap interval of [−22.49, −0.156], based on 1,000 resamples.
- Study 2’s formal-leader contrasts supported H1–H3: high versus neutral power increased talking (42.39% versus 33.26%, p = .001), reduced open communication (5.68 versus 6.30, p = .035), reduced correct decisions (25% versus 75%, p = .014) and reduced learning (4.17 versus 5.38 clues, p = .023). Corresponding contrasts without formal authority were nonsignificant. Moderation evidence for H5 differed by outcome: the interaction was significant for decision performance (b = −0.62, p = .048), at p = .05 for communication, and marginal for talking (p = .061) and learning (p = .056). Serial indirect effects supported H4/H6 for formal leaders: decision-performance CI [−2.32, −0.03] and learning CI [−0.82, −0.01]; the respective no-formal-leader intervals [−0.90, 0.14] and [−0.30, 0.04] included zero. Supplementary analysis found greater autocratic style under high power (1.66 versus 1.35, p = .032), without formal-authority moderation; both means were low on the seven-point scale.
- Study 3: Without instrumentality reminders, high-power versus neutral-power leaders talked more (35.37% versus 23.89%; unequal-variance test p = .004), teams communicated less openly (5.78 versus 6.44, p = .016), and fewer teams selected the correct candidate (0% versus 55.6%, p = .009). These power contrasts were nonsignificant with reminders. Interactions supported H7 for talking (p = .03) and communication (p = .042), but the decision-performance interaction was marginal (b = −2.32, p = .08). The authors interpret serial mediation as supporting H4/H8, calling the no-reminder indirect effect significant at p = .05 while printing its 95% interval as [−80.01, 0.00]; the displayed interval reaches zero. The following nonsignificant interval, [−25.55, 0.74], is labeled as a no-formal-authority condition despite all Study 3 leaders being formal leaders, an unresolved internal reporting inconsistency.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper identifies microprocesses through which hierarchy can undermine collaborative team performance: subjective leader power changes behavior, with effects extending from the individual to the team. It distinguishes subjective power from formal authority and highlights followers’ responses to legitimate authority as a condition for dominance to affect collective outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations may reduce these risks through flatter structures and egalitarian cultures, training leaders to encourage openness, and practices that remind leaders of members’ useful contributions. They also propose encouraging members to question the legitimacy of leaders who dominate interactions, potentially reducing deference to that behavior. These are broader organizational proposals, while the experiments directly test a brief instrumentality reminder in collaborative tasks.

**Limitations**
Classroom and laboratory settings may limit external validity, and the tasks required collaborative information sharing and integration. Dominating leadership may operate differently in coordination tasks with identifiable differentiated expertise, under uncertainty or crisis, or when the leader has substantially greater expertise. Conversations were not recorded, leaving talking measures based primarily on participant reports rather than observed speech.

**Future Research**
The authors call for field studies, direct tests of follower deference and recordings of team conversations to examine information content. They propose studying success–promotion–power cycles, leaders’ calibration of participation to members’ expertise, and teams with multiple members experiencing high power. Additional directions concern personality and follower tendencies to defer, organizational antecedents of subjective power, and ways of modifying those antecedents to reduce adverse effects.
