# Is This How I Will Be Treated? Reducing Uncertainty through Recruitment Interactions

**Abstract**
While a great deal of research has investigated strategies for increasing job seekers’ initial attraction to organizations, far less is known about how job seekers respond to recruitment activities after application submission. We draw from signaling, uncertainty reduction, and uncertainty management theories to develop a conceptual model of the relationship between recruitment interactions (contact episodes) after application submission and organizational attraction. We test this model in three independent studies with data collected at multiple time periods. Study 1 employed a time-lagged research design with actual job seekers. Findings showed that justice perceptions associated with recruitment interactions influence attraction to an organization indirectly and directly via positive relational certainty (i.e., reduced uncertainty regarding how organizational relations might be upon entering the organization). Study 2 used a controlled experimental design to provide additional evidence of the relational certainty mechanism through which justice signals influence attraction. Finally, Study 3 incorporated a longitudinal (repeated-measures) design to examine reactions to recruitment interactions over ten weeks. Results indicated that the relationship between justice signals and organizational attraction via positive relational certainty is dynamic, suggesting that organizations should carefully manage their communications throughout the recruitment process.

**Research Question**
How do perceptions of interpersonal and informational justice in recruitment interactions after application submission relate to organizational attraction through positive relational certainty? How does this process depend on prior employee-relations information and change across successive contact episodes?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a/b: Interpersonal and informational justice positively relate to positive relational certainty.
- H2: Positive relational certainty positively relates to organizational attraction.
- H3a/b: Positive relational certainty mediates the positive relationships of interpersonal and informational justice with organizational attraction.
- H4a/b: These justice–attraction relationships are weaker when participants have preexisting knowledge of superior organizational employee-relations practices.
- H5a/b: Increases or decreases in interpersonal and informational justice correspond to increases or decreases in positive relational certainty.
- H6: Positive relational certainty change positively relates to organizational attraction change.
- H7a/b: Positive relational certainty change mediates the positive relationships between interpersonal/informational justice change and organizational attraction change.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived interpersonal and informational justice in recruitment contact episodes; within-person changes in these perceptions in Study 3.
- DV(s): Organizational attraction; within-person attraction change in Study 3.
- Mediators: Positive relational certainty, meaning confidence that organizational relationships will be good; its change in Study 3.
- Moderators: Preexisting information about superior employee-relations practices, manipulated through a recruitment website in Study 2.

During the post-application maintenance phase, applicants interpret respectful treatment and informative communications as signals of their prospective relationships with the employer. The authors propose that these signals reduce uncertainty about positive relationships and thereby sustain attraction; certainty about negative relationships is not predicted to increase attraction. Successive contacts supply reference points for reevaluating justice, so fairness heuristics can remain malleable rather than becoming fixed after the first exchange.

**Data & Measures**
- **Study 1:** Upper-level management students at two southeastern US universities were actively seeking work and intended to apply online. Of 233 eligible job seekers, 164 completed the first survey, 155 the second and 147 all three; excluding 19 applicants rejected by their chosen organization left 128. Initial attraction was measured at the first wave; one week later, four interpersonal-justice and five informational-justice items assessed intervening phone/email contact; another week later, three positive-relational-certainty items and five attraction items were administered. Justice and attraction used five-point agreement scales; certainty used five-point confidence ratings concerning good management relations, few employee complaints and few grievances. Mediation regressions controlled initial attraction and application status (97 still waiting; 31 invited to continue), with 2,000 bootstrap resamples and bias-corrected and accelerated confidence intervals. This is an observational design, with mediator and outcome measured together at the final wave.
- **Study 2:** Of 129 students solicited at a southwestern university, 94 provided complete data. In a randomized between-person experiment, participants role-played applicants to hypothetical HBA Corporation: the website either displayed superior employee-relations information or omitted it, and a subsequent email jointly conveyed high or low interpersonal and informational justice. The email followed application by approximately two days; the survey followed the email by one week. The same justice and attraction scales were used. Hierarchical moderated regressions tested measured justice perceptions interacting with website condition; the two justice dimensions were not independently manipulated. This moderation-of-process design tested the proposed mechanism without directly measuring relational certainty as a mediator in this study.
- **Study 3:** Of 228 students targeted at two southeastern universities, 119 job seekers completed the initial survey; excluding 47 with missing data left 72, supplying 237 contact observations over ten weeks. Participants were asked to report immediately after each interaction with one prospective employer, using the same focal scales adapted to the most recent episode. The paper reports three to five episodes per person and a mean of 3.43; that mean does not reconcile with its stated observation and participant totals. Random-coefficient models centered time-varying predictors and the mediator on each person's mean, separating within-person effects from between-person variation. Controls included time, prior justice expectations, initial certainty/attraction as applicable, and the other justice dimension; the models accounted for heteroskedasticity. Indirect effects used 2,000 bootstrap resamples. These longitudinal relationships are observational.

**Key Findings**
- **Study 1 (Tables 2–3; unstandardized coefficients):** H1a/b were supported: interpersonal and informational justice related positively to certainty (b = .46 and .44, both p < .01). Certainty related positively to attraction (b = .21, p < .01), supporting H2. Indirect effects were .10 (95% CI [.05, .17]) and .09 (95% CI [.04, .18]), respectively. Interpersonal justice's remaining direct association was nonsignificant (b = .06), whereas informational justice retained a positive direct association (b = .14, p < .05). The authors therefore support H3a but describe H3b as mixed evidence consistent with partial mediation.
- **Study 2 (Table 5; unstandardized coefficients):** Both H4 interactions were negative: interpersonal justice × website information, b = −.39, and informational justice × website information, b = −.32 (both p < .05; additional R² = .11, p < .01). Justice related positively to attraction when prior employee-relations information was absent; both slopes were nonsignificant when that information was present. This supports the predicted attenuation, rather than showing that justice is invariably irrelevant when applicants have prior information.
- **Study 3 (Tables 7–8):** Within-person changes in interpersonal and informational justice positively related to certainty change (b = .37, p < .01; b = .19, p < .05), supporting H5a/b. Certainty change positively related to attraction change in both models (b = .19, p < .01), supporting H6. Indirect effects supported H7a/b: interpersonal justice, .07 (95% CI [.02, .13]); informational justice, .04 (95% CI [.003, .08]). The latter interval approached but excluded zero. These results support dynamic covariation across episodes rather than treating applicant reactions as stable.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates signaling, uncertainty reduction and uncertainty management theories to explain how post-application treatment relates to attraction through positive relational certainty. Its dynamic model extends recruitment research beyond initial attraction and static between-person comparisons, suggesting that fairness heuristics remain open to revision during the maintenance phase as applicants compare successive interactions.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise recruiting organizations to plan the quality of correspondence after application submission, including automatic acknowledgments, because even early contact can shape applicants' attitudes. Carefully planned correspondence is presented as a relatively low-cost, potentially effective strategy; organizations should attend to formal and informal cues throughout recruitment.

**Limitations**
The studies focus on justice signals, although other environmental cues and more concrete job or organizational information may matter; Study 1 could not rule out omitted situational influences and emphasized the first post-application contact. Samples largely comprised soon-to-graduate students who were not currently employed, and Studies 1 and 3 preceded the economic downturn, limiting extrapolation to different applicants and labor markets. The authors also note lab–field differences; in Study 3, small samples at the fourth and fifth contact episodes prevented confirmatory factor analyses at those occasions.

**Future Research**
Examine the combined effects of early treatment and objective job/organizational attributes on actual job choice, including the relative weights of fairness, recruiter behavior, job attitudes and organizational attitudes. Extend the research beyond soon-to-graduate applicants and test whether fairness matters less when job prospects are scarce. Triangulate with behavioral measures in laboratory and field studies using the same participants, and develop theory that captures recruitment over time.
