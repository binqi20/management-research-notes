# Organizational Sponsorship and Founding Environments: A Contingency View on the Survival of Business-Incubated Firms, 1994–2007

**Abstract**

Organizational sponsorship mediates the relationship between new organizations and their environments by creating a resource-munificent context intended to increase survival rates among those new organizations. Existing theories are prone to treat such resource munificence as the inverse of resource dependence, indicating that the application of new resources in an entrepreneurial context should always benefit new firms. These existing theories, however, often overlook heterogeneity in both types of applied resources as well as founding environmental conditions. By attending to these nuances, we reveal that resource munificence is not necessarily predictive of organizational survival. We find that resource munificence related to sponsorship can potentially decrease or increase survival rates among new organizations and that these effects are contingent on fit of resource type with its respective geographic-based founding density. These findings confirm the need for a more-nuanced theory of sponsorship that attends to the mechanisms and conditions by which resource munificence is likely to alter new organization survival rates.

**Research Question**

How do different sponsorship activities affect new organizations' survival, and how does geographic founding density condition their effectiveness? The paper asks when resource munificence created through sponsorship may increase rather than decrease exit rates.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1: Networking sponsorship efforts reduce sponsored organizations' exit rates more effectively where market founding density is higher.
- H2: Field-building sponsorship efforts reduce sponsored organizations' exit rates more effectively where market founding density is lower.
- H3: Direct support sponsorship efforts reduce sponsored organizations' exit rates more effectively where market founding density is higher.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Sponsorship activities: networking, field building and direct support.
- DV(s): Organizational exit.
- Mediators: No statistical mediation tests; buffering and bridging are theorized sponsorship mechanisms.
- Moderators: Geographic founding density, measured within the firm's county and industry at founding.

Buffering protects new organizations from external resource dependencies while they develop internal resources; bridging connects them to external resources, social capital and legitimacy. Networking bridges to established organizations and resource providers and is predicted to become more useful as founding density increases. Field building connects similar new organizations, supporting collective learning and legitimacy in less dense, emerging markets. Direct support can buffer or bridge; scarce resources and more established practices in dense markets are expected to make resource provision and knowledge transfer more effective.

**Data & Measures**

The study combines incubator rosters and archived websites, NETS annual establishment records derived from Dun & Bradstreet, and US Census County Business Patterns. Address matching identified businesses in 178 university-affiliated incubators; firms with more than 100 employees or older than five years when entering incubation were excluded. The sample prose reports 2,110 firms, but Tables 3–4 report 2,100 firms, 8,991 observations/time-varying spells and, in Table 4, 695 exits; the discrepancy is unexplained. Businesses were followed during 1994–2007, including after leaving incubation.

Exit equals one in the last year of activity if that year falls on or before 2007; ownership change alone is not exit, although a subsequent closure is. Founding density counts organizations in the same county and three-digit NAICS industry in the firm's founding year; the tables scale density by 1,000. Networking is a binary indicator of reported networking activities connecting tenants to external resource providers; direct support indicates scheduled management training workshops, courses or certifications. Field building proxies incubator specialization from annual tenant industry shares: the stated procedure takes one minus the Blau index, whose displayed formula is 1 − Σpₖ², where pₖ is the tenant share in industry k. The subsequent sentence calls this a diversity index while assigning high specialization to one, an internal wording inconsistency; the measure does not directly capture intentional field-building activity.

The observational design estimates associations using exponential survival models, censoring firms without an exit by 2007 and clustering standard errors by incubator. Controls include contemporaneous density, NBIA membership, selection policies, firm age, ownership change, founding before sponsorship, postincubation status, lagged logged sales indexed to the 2008 CPI, and the annual difference in logged employment lagged one year; selection policies sum two binary requirements for published admissions criteria and a business plan. Methods describe year, industry and state controls, although Table 4 leaves Model 4's corresponding entries blank. Results prose calls the exponentiated coefficients hazard ratios, whereas Table 4 labels them odds ratios.

**Key Findings**

- Separate interaction models support H1–H3: founding density × networking HR = 0.67 (Model 2), × field building HR = 2.97 (Model 3), and × direct support HR = 0.67 (Model 4), each p < .05 using the table's one-tailed tests.
- Joint Model 5 weakens this support: networking's interaction is marginal (HR = 0.73, p < .10); field building (HR = 1.59) and direct support (HR = 0.76) are nonsignificant. Thus, the three interactions do not retain conventional significance when entered together.
- Without interactions, Model 1 associates networking with higher exit (HR = 1.38, p < .05), field building with lower exit (HR = 0.44, p < .05), and direct support with no significant difference (HR = 0.89). The conditional comparisons are between firms with and without the respective service, not between sponsored and nonsponsored firms.
- Table 5 indicates that networking is associated with higher exit at low and mean founding density, with the comparison reversing near two standard deviations above the mean; field building's advantage at low density reverses at high density. Direct support is associated with higher exit one standard deviation below the mean and lower exit at the mean and above. The printed multiplier equation has ambiguous exponential grouping and repeats a coefficient label; some prose ratio inputs also differ from Table 5, including the networking baseline at mean density (0.95 in prose versus 0.96 in the table).
- The authors report similar coefficient effects and interaction significance under Weibull and piecewise exponential specifications, and consistent results when excluding ownership-change firms. These checks do not remove the weakened joint-model support.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper develops a contingency view of organizational sponsorship, distinguishing buffering and bridging and linking the effectiveness of different services to geographic founding conditions. It extends resource dependence arguments by showing that collective structures intended to create munificence need not uniformly improve survival, and refines density delay theory by incorporating geographically bounded intermediaries that can alter founding-density relationships.

**Practical Implication**

For sponsorship aimed at increasing survival, the authors advise administrators to match training and external networking to high-density settings and specialized peer collaboration to emerging, low-density settings. Sponsors may use limited resources more effectively by supporting young businesses with an established record of viability, while new ventures should assess whether sponsorship benefits fit their founding environment. Scholars and sponsors should also examine whether support introduces new dependencies.

**Limitations**

The study does not compare sponsored with nonsponsored organizations or examine configurations of sponsorship activities, and it cannot directly control for initial capital availability despite including firm size. Coarse sponsorship measures, especially specialization as a proxy for field-building activity, cannot distinguish intentional from emergent processes; the authors acknowledge diminished interaction significance in the joint model. Unobserved differences among firms receiving networking services may help explain their higher general exit risk, but this explanation remains speculative.

**Future Research**

Future work should examine new dependencies created by sponsorship, their consequences for long-term survival, and whether sponsors' legitimacy transfers to firms differently across founding environments. The authors propose studying service configurations, sponsored versus nonsponsored trajectories, other sponsorship forms over longer periods, and larger established organizations where local versus national or international sponsorship may matter differently. Larger samples, direct controls for initial resources, richer service measures and qualitative investigation could clarify sponsorship practices and distinguish intentional from emergent field building.
