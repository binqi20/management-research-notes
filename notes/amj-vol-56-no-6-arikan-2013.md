# National Animosity and Cross-Border Alliances

**Abstract**
We extend the cross-border strategic alliance knowledge base by introducing dyadspecific antagonism (animosity between nation pairs) and hold that the formation and the type of firm-level cross-border alliances are nontrivially impacted by conflicting relations and animosity between their home nations. We examine the formation of alliances between firms among nation-dyads with and without a history of conflicts. The frequency and magnitude of conflicts increase the perception of likelihood of opportunism, and dyad-specific risks materially affect the context in which firms make alliance decisions. As animosity between two nations increases, the number and the probability of forming alliances within the dyad the nations form decreases. Conditional on the expected number of alliances, increased antagonistic actions of nations outside the dyad and dissimilarity in the historical conflicts that each nation has engaged in outside the dyad (i) increase the number of equity alliances and (ii) decrease the number of nonequity alliances as a proportion of total alliances. We find positive main effects of learning to contract through prior experience only for equity (vs. nonequity) alliances. The reputation effects of antagonism based on relationships with nations outside of the dyad negatively moderate the positive learning effects of prior equity alliance experience.

**Research Question**
How does animosity between nations shape the formation and governance type of cross-border firm alliances? How do nations’ external conflict histories and reputations condition alliance composition and the effects of prior alliance experience?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: As animosity between two nations increases, the number of cross-border firm-level alliances within their dyad decreases.
- H2: Greater dissimilarity in the two nations’ prior conflicting relationships with other nations increases the proportion of equity alliances relative to other alliance types.
- H3: More one-sided conflicts initiated by the two nations with other nations in the previous year increase the proportion of equity alliances relative to other alliance types.
- H4: Learning-to-contract effects through experience are stronger for equity than nonequity alliances.
- H5: A stronger one-sided antagonistic reputation of the nations in a dyad negatively moderates the positive learning effects of prior equity alliance experience.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Within-dyad national animosity; dissimilarity in historical conflicts with outside nations; recent one-sided external antagonism; prior same-type alliance experience.
- DV(s): Annual cross-border alliance count; equity and nonequity alliances as proportions of total alliances.
- Mediators: Perceived opportunism, mistrust, collective memory and stereotyping are theorized mechanisms, not directly tested mediators.
- Moderators: One-sided external antagonism weakens the association between prior equity alliance experience and equity alliance share.

Historical conflict is argued to heighten perceived opportunism and mistrust, restricting partner selection and discouraging alliance formation. Conditional on forming alliances, external conflict reputations and dissimilar histories increase demand for credible commitments and mutual hostages, favoring equity governance. Prior contracting experience can facilitate cooperation, but antagonistic reputations undermine the conditions in which that learning operates; the empirical tests use dyad-level alliance histories rather than directly observed managerial learning.

**Data & Measures**
SDC Platinum supplies consummated cross-border alliances between two partners of different nationalities, with activities in one country; third-country activities are included, multicountry activities excluded, and ultimate parents cannot share nationality. These are merged with International Crisis Behavior conflict data, supplemented by rivalry data, dyadic trade-GDP data, CEPII geography and COW material capabilities. The reported 1988–2000 panel has 2,480 nation-dyads and 23,346 observations; Table 2 Models 5–6 report 20,906 observations. The separate descriptive networks cover 197 nations, conflicts in 1918–87 and alliances in 1988–2003.

H1 uses the annual number of firm alliances in a nation-dyad, with a binary any-alliance robustness check. Animosity proxies are lagged cumulative within-dyad conflicts, relative economic animosity, and an estimated dyadic conflict-equation error representing unobserved conflict drivers. Relative economic animosity subtracts Imports_ji/TotalTrade_j from Imports_ij/TotalTrade_i, using previous-year values; the first import subscript denotes exporter and the second importer. Historical external-conflict dissimilarity uses the source’s “Euclidian distance”: square the difference between each nation’s conflict-tie strength to a third nation, then sum across nations outside the dyad. The methods and Table 3 specify 1918–87; Table 4 instead labels this measure 1918–88. External antagonism sums conflicts initiated by either dyad nation against outside nations, excluding within-dyad conflicts; the tables specify the previous year, while the methods call it cumulative antagonism. Experience is the previous-year count of equity or nonequity alliances; H5 multiplies prior equity count by external antagonism.

H2–H5 use equity/total-alliance and nonequity/total-alliance shares, conditioning on predicted alliance counts from Table 2 Model 6. Their Tobit models contain 262 observations in 74 dyad clusters after outlier exclusion; the text specifies equity/nonequity ratios greater than 4, whereas Table 3’s footnote specifies at least 4. Count models use three-way clustered errors except the one-way-clustered baseline; Tobit errors cluster by dyad. Separate and simultaneous equations for trade and conflict address their endogeneity; controls include trade predictions/residuals, material capabilities, geography, shared borders/language, bilateral agreements and time terms, with specifications varying by model. These observational models do not directly identify the proposed psychological mechanisms.

**Key Findings**
- H1 receives qualified support. Table 2 Model 6 associates fewer alliances with cumulative within-dyad conflicts (b = −0.09, p < .001) and the conflict-error proxy (b = −1.09, p < .001), but economic animosity is nonsignificant (b = 0.01). Cumulative conflicts are nonsignificant when entered separately in Model 3; economic animosity is weakly negative in Model 4 (b = −0.054, p < .10).
- H2–H3: Historical external-conflict dissimilarity predicts a higher equity share in Table 3 Model 2 (b = 0.105, p < .05), but is only marginal in Model 6 (b = 0.066, p < .10); recent external antagonism remains positive there (b = 0.011, p < .001). Table 4 Model 6 shows corresponding negative associations for nonequity share: dissimilarity b = −0.086, p < .05; external antagonism b = −0.006, p < .10.
- H4: Prior equity-alliance count positively predicts equity share (Table 3 Model 6: b = 0.030, p < .01), whereas prior nonequity-alliance count does not significantly predict nonequity share (Table 4 Model 6: b = −0.004). The authors interpret this contrast as stronger learning in equity alliances. Their H4 results prose also describes the predictor as a proportion, although the operationalization and tables specify a count.
- H5: External antagonism negatively moderates the prior-equity-experience association (Table 3 Model 6 interaction b = −0.001, p < .05). The analogous nonequity interaction is nonsignificant (Table 4 Model 6: b = −0.001). The authors report alternative count/probability specifications and trade/conflict modeling checks, but the displayed models retain the null and marginal results above.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends country-specific political-hazard explanations by treating national history and conflict networks as dyad-specific contexts for firm alliances. It connects transaction-cost governance and learning to contract with historical reputations, arguing that higher-order nation-state relations constrain firm-level governance choices. The distinction between alliance formation and conditional alliance composition explains how animosity can discourage cooperation while increasing the relative appeal of equity commitments.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that lack of trust and a history of conflicts and tensions require firms to protect themselves against opportunism in alliances. They identify both formal governance that incorporates transaction risks and informal governance emphasizing trust as relevant.

**Limitations**
The study examines alliance formation rather than financial outcomes, so it cannot establish whether the partnerships succeed or distinguish financial objectives from strategic foothold motives. It includes consummated deals but not aborted transactions. Industry exposure to national-security concerns and firms’ status as national symbols may create heterogeneity that the broad industry coverage does not resolve.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining the duration of conflict effects and economic stakes, domination connotations across governance modes, and direct survey measures of animosity and third-party mediation. They also propose studying firm size/age differences, conflicts arising after alliance formation, contract negotiation/framing, and generational and educational influences on managers. Further work could examine transitive trust, herding, and relational contracting capabilities in adversarial contexts.
