# Building Positive Resources: Effects of Positive Events and Positive Reflection on Work Stress and Health

**Abstract**
This three-week longitudinal field study with an experimental intervention examines the association between daily events and employee stress and health, with a specific focus on positive events. Results suggest that both naturally occurring positive work events and a positive reflection intervention are associated with reduced stress and improved health, though effects vary across momentary, lagged, daily, and day-toevening spillover analyses. Findings are consistent with theory-based predictions: positive events, negative events, and family-to-work conflict independently contribute to perceived stress, blood pressure, physical symptoms, mental health, and work detachment, suggesting that organizations should focus not only on reducing negative events, but also on increasing positive events. These findings show that a brief, end-of-workday positive reflection led to decreased stress and improved health in the evening.

**Research Question**
How are naturally occurring positive events, negative events, and family-to-work conflict independently associated with employees’ stress, health, and work detachment across different time windows? Can an assigned end-of-workday positive reflection improve evening outcomes, buffer negative experiences, or enhance positive events’ benefits?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Positive workplace events are negatively associated with (a) stress, (b) blood pressure, and (c) health complaints, and positively associated with (d) work detachment.
- H2: Reflecting on positive events at the end of a workday is negatively associated with (a) stress, (b) blood pressure, and (c) health complaints, and positively associated with (d) work detachment.
- H3: Negative workplace events are positively associated with (a) stress, (b) blood pressure, and (c) health complaints, and negatively associated with (d) work detachment.
- H4: Family-to-work conflict is positively associated with (a) stress, (b) blood pressure, and (c) health complaints, and negatively associated with (d) work detachment.

Buffering and enhancing interactions were exploratory; the authors offered no formal moderation hypotheses.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Positive workplace events, negative workplace events, family-to-work conflict, and assigned positive reflection.
- DV(s): Perceived stress, systolic blood pressure, physical and mental health complaints, and evening work detachment.
- Mediators: Resource building and depletion are proposed mechanisms, not tested mediators.
- Moderators: Exploratory tests of positive reflection interacting with each event type, and positive events interacting with negative events or family-to-work conflict.

Positive events are theorized to build psychological resources, including mastery, purpose, interpersonal connections, and positive self-regard; negative events threaten or deplete resources. Reflection may counter negative attentional biases and hedonic adaptation, allow positive events to be relived, and make resources more accessible by explaining why good things happened. Family demands may deplete work resources while building family resources whose benefits emerge later; the formal hypothesis nevertheless predicts adverse associations. These mechanisms motivate the tests but were not directly established through mediation analysis.

**Data & Measures**
The reported final sample comprises 61 women with patient contact, working at least 32 hours weekly in nine outpatient clinics run by two organizations in a metropolitan area. The listed recruitment exclusions do not transparently reconcile with this final count. The protocol called for four daily PDA surveys across 15 weekdays, use of an A&D TM-2430 monitor taking blood pressure every 30 minutes during two-hour morning, afternoon, and evening windows, and nightly telephone interviews; participants completed 86% of daily PDA surveys. Collection across clinics spanned ten months, with calendar dates unspecified.

Event items were initially rated 0–3, dichotomized into absent/present, and summed separately across four positive, five negative, and two family-interference event types. Two of the original 13 items were dropped: rare regret and ambiguously worded family contact. The methods describe workday scores as averages across four PDA signals, but Table 1 reports a positive-event workday mean of 7.40, incompatible with the maximum of a four-item averaged count; the paper does not reconcile this aggregation discrepancy. Work stress was a single five-point agreement item; evening stress used a single five-point severity item. Physical complaints averaged four symptoms (α = .72); mental complaints averaged fatigue, concentration difficulty, and decision difficulty (α = .63). Detachment combined forgetting work, difficulty switching off, and time spent thinking/preparing/doing work: the latter two were reverse-scored, then all three were standardized and averaged (α = .74).

On days 8–15, participants wrote about three good things, personal or work-related, and why they happened. HLM analyses used person-mean centering and simultaneous event predictors; baseline event analyses used days 1–7. Table 2’s lagged models predict afternoon outcomes from morning events while controlling morning stress and afternoon events; analyses with trait positive/negative affectivity controls were unchanged. The intervention compares periods and actual completion days within people, without a separate control group; these comparisons do not establish causality. Analysis-specific observation counts were 738–741 momentary, 351–353 lagged, 332 workday, 327–329 spillover, 773–838 intervention, and 631–701 moderation (Tables 2–5).

**Key Findings**
Support for H1–H4 varies by outcome and time window. Coefficients below are unstandardized γ; * denotes p < .05 and ** p < .01, following the paper’s reported significance.

- Baseline work outcomes (Table 2): Positive events predict lower stress concurrently (−.14**), from morning to afternoon (−.10*), and across workdays (−.06**), supporting H1a at each window; their blood-pressure associations are nonsignificant. Negative events and family-to-work conflict predict higher momentary stress (.29** and .16*) and workday stress (.09** and .08**), plus higher workday blood pressure (.35* and .57*). Neither predicts lagged stress; no event type significantly predicts momentary or lagged blood pressure. H3a/H4a and H3b/H4b therefore receive time-specific support.
- Baseline evening spillover (Table 3): Positive events predict lower blood pressure (−.55*) and greater detachment (.03*), supporting H1b/H1d for evening outcomes, but not lower stress or health complaints. Negative events predict greater stress (.06**) and lower detachment (−.06**), supporting H3a/H3d; their blood-pressure and complaint coefficients are nonsignificant. No family-to-work-conflict spillover coefficient is significant. Thus, baseline complaint predictions H1c/H3c/H4c and evening H4 predictions are unsupported in these tests.
- Reflection (Table 4): Comparing intervention with baseline periods, evening stress (−.16**), physical complaints (−.07**), and mental complaints (−.07*) are lower, but detachment (.06) and blood pressure (.99) do not significantly change. Comparing completed with noncompleted days yields lower stress (−.07**), physical complaints (−.11**), and mental complaints (−.04**), and greater detachment (.06*); blood pressure (.36) remains nonsignificant. H2a/H2c are supported, H2d only in the completion comparison, and H2b is unsupported; the authors characterize effects as generally small.
- Exploratory interactions (Table 5): Reflection moderates family-to-work conflict’s associations with evening blood pressure (−1.56*) and mental complaints (−.07*), interpreted as buffering, despite the nonsignificant baseline spillover associations. Reflection neither buffers negative events nor enhances positive-event associations. Naturally occurring positive events buffer negative-event associations with evening stress (−.01*) and physical complaints (−.004**, reported as p < .05 in the prose), but do not buffer family-to-work conflict; the remaining interactions are nonsignificant.
- Post hoc family content: Family appeared in 44% of 397 daily reflection exercises. However, participants’ proportion of family-related entries did not significantly strengthen the intervention’s buffering effects; family-content explanations remain tentative.

The source contains reporting discrepancies: the discussion attributes physical-complaint spillover to negative events, whereas Table 3’s coefficient is nonsignificant; several coefficient/t-statistic signs in Tables 3–5 are inconsistent. Figure 3’s legend compares completed/noncompleted days, although the accompanying prose calls it a before/during comparison. The results above retain the tables’ explicit comparisons, coefficients, and significance rather than reconstructing unreported corrections.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study integrates conservation of resources and job demands-resources perspectives with broaden and build theory, treating positive experiences as dynamic resource-building events with direct associations with stress and health. It challenges simple affect symmetry by linking positive events to reduced negative outcomes and extends positive-reflection research to an assigned workplace exercise and outcomes beyond happiness or depression. Its temporal and selective buffering patterns motivate a more differentiated account of resource accumulation, without directly testing the proposed mediators.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest organizations, managers, and employees give positive events more prominence alongside addressing negative experiences, through positive feedback, sharing accomplishments, celebrating success, and expressing gratitude. They propose adapting the three-good-things exercise by opening staff meetings with employees’ positive experiences from the preceding week; this adaptation is a recommendation, not a tested intervention in the study.

**Limitations**
The all-female health-care sample limits generalization to men and other settings, especially work with fewer interpersonal interactions; coarse event counts lose information about specific events, frequency within intervals, and intensity. Blood-pressure estimates omit caffeine and activity controls and cover episodic windows, while concurrent self-reports may share transient-affect influences. Most importantly, the absence of a no-intervention control group limits internal validity, including possible anticipation of study completion; resource-building mediators were not directly tested, and end-of-day demands may constrain reflection.

**Future Research**
The authors call for replication with a no-intervention control group, men and other occupations, finer event measures and theory about event intensity and the timing of event effects, and fuller physiological assessment such as 24-hour blood-pressure monitoring alongside cortisol. They propose testing work-only reflection, alternative timing for employees with less distinct work-life boundaries, individual differences in responsiveness, and recovery activities. Direct tests should examine whether positive events and reflection build core psychological resources and explain their distinct immediate, cumulative, and evening associations.
