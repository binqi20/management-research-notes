# Coerced Practice Implementation in Cases of Low Cultural Fit: Cultural Change and Practice Adaptation During the Implementation of Six Sigma at 3M

**Abstract**
In this article, we present the findings of a longitudinal study of coerced implementation of a practice in the face of a low degree of fit between the practice and an organization’s culture. Contrary to current predictions stating that a lack of cultural fit will eventually be resolved through adaptation of new practices, our findings portray the implementation of culturally dissonant practices as an ongoing process involving the mutual adaptation of organizational practices and culture. Our emerging model describes the cultural changes induced by the coercive implementation of new practices as involving a partial change in shared beliefs and behavioral patterns and a more general enrichment of the cultural repertoire of organization members.

**Research Question**
When organization members are coerced to implement a practice with low cultural fit, how is the new practice adapted, and how is organizational culture affected by the coerced implementation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Practice implementation: fidelity to the original practice and extensiveness across organizational units.
- Interpretive processes: leaders’ sensegiving through symbolic tactics, discursive tactics, and language; members’ collective sensemaking of cultural dissonance and practical implications.
- Cultural layers: a broad cultural repertoire, more internalized beliefs and behavioral norms, and deeply internalized, emotionally laden core values.
- Adaptation processes: reconfiguration removes or alters practice elements; reframing reinterprets retained elements as compatible with traditional values; coercive learning can change beliefs and enrich cultural repertoires.

The emergent model portrays implementation as a recursive relationship between practice and culture. Positive practical consequences can make members tolerate moderate dissonance and internalize new behavioral patterns, whereas perceived threats to core values intensify resistance and may induce leaders to relax coercion or alter implementation. Prolonged engagement can leave concepts and behavioral patterns in place after coercion ends, while retained practices are reinterpreted to fit established values.

**Data & Measures**
Fieldwork at a European subsidiary and Minnesota headquarters combined retrospective and real-time evidence. Table 1 reports 42 subsidiary interviews in June–September 2005, 16 headquarters interviews in June–September 2006, and 17 subsidiary follow-ups in February–March 2008; Methods instead reports 93 interviews with 59 informants overall, and Table 2 lists 17 headquarters informants despite the reported 16 in that phase. The paper does not reconcile these counts. The first interview round spanned seven business units and five staff functions; interviews lasted 60–90 minutes and were taped and transcribed. Follow-ups purposefully revisited senior managers who had expressed more articulated views.

Table 1 reports approximately 400 observation hours at the subsidiary in June–September 2005 and 250 at headquarters in June–September 2006. Intranet documents, annual reports, company magazines, corporate histories, business press, and prior studies supported reconstruction and triangulation. Coding tracked fidelity/extensiveness, cultural fit, practical implications, and leaders’ sensegiving; political and technical fit were initially examined as rival explanations but featured sporadically or not at all, respectively, in informants’ accounts. Two researchers check-coded and compared interpretations, used informant feedback, and temporally decomposed the process. Cultural change was inferred from reported persistence after coercion, changed attitudes, and spontaneous concept use; this is an interpretive process study rather than a causal-effect estimate.

**Key Findings**
- **2001–2003:** Faithful, extensive implementation of DMAIC and DFSS disrupted established practices. Informants’ recognition of efficiency and financial benefits weakened initial resistance. Manufacturing members experienced less dissonance than colleagues in sales, marketing, and R&D because earlier quality programs had already encouraged a data-driven approach.
- **2004–2005:** “Hyperfidelity” and “overextension” made compliance targets increasingly displace business goals. Informants reported that rigid application hindered growth and innovation, threatened creativity, initiative, and tolerance for mistakes, and widened the gap between leaders’ positive narratives and everyday experience.
- **2006–2008:** Relaxed coercion allowed selective application and customization: DMAIC was concentrated in operations, logistics, and quality control; DFSS retained stage gates while allowing discretion over tools. Members reframed retained language and structures as supporting collaboration, innovation replication, and coordinated initiative. Figure 1 and the findings narrative date McNerney’s departure to June 2005, whereas Methods says 2006.
- **Cultural legacy:** Informants described persistent attention to calculated risk, measurement, monitoring, accountability, and frank performance evaluation, alongside spontaneous use of Six Sigma concepts after particular tools or obligations were dropped. The resulting model distinguishes partial change in beliefs and behavioral norms from broader repertoire enrichment, with core values bounding acceptable change.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study challenges the assumption that low cultural fit is resolved solely by adapting or abandoning a practice: prolonged coerced engagement can also change culture, producing mutual adaptation. It connects culture and practice-based perspectives through recursive sensemaking and coercive learning, and reconciles culture as constraint with culture as tool kit through three layers differentiated by internalization rather than tacitness.

**Practical Implication**
The authors identify the potential usefulness of forced implementation for exposing members to alternative practices and encouraging reconsideration of cultural beliefs. They further suggest, explicitly as speculation, that resistance may be lower when leaders provide narratives making new practices compatible with core values and/or encourage revisions of foundational goals and values consistent with the practice’s expected outcomes.

**Limitations**
Late entry makes the reconstruction of the early implementation stage tentative despite archival triangulation, while allowing closer examination of longer-term effects. McNerney’s sudden departure may be idiosyncratic, and the counterfactual is unknowable; the authors argue that the board’s support for adaptation and the opportunity to observe persistence after coercion help interpret the case.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research with an open time frame examining how leaders introduce practices and induce organizational acceptance, and how these choices shape the subsequent process. They also encourage investigation of the microprocesses through which practices change as they diffuse and the organizational changes their implementation triggers.
