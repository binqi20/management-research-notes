# Alumni Effects and Relational Advantage: The Impact on Outsourcing When a Buyer Hires Employees from a Supplier's Competitors

**Abstract**
Research examining the impacts of employee mobility on interfirm relationships suggests that firms earn positive “relational spillovers” when their former employees, or alumni, depart to join other organizations. Drawing on the theory of relational advantage, we extend this line of work by examining how a supplier firm is affected when a buyer hires alumni from the supplier’s competitors. Using detailed data on mobility involving patent law firms and their Fortune 500 clients, we find that supplier firms receive less outsourced business when buyers hire employees from the focal supplier’s competitors. Further, this negative effect decreases when the focal supplier has its own alumni already working for the buyer firm and increases when the buyer firm has higher turnover or hires locally from competing suppliers. The article thus underscores the importance of firm alumni in the competition for valuable business relationships and highlights a form of “talent war” waged through the placement of and relationships with former employees.

**Research Question**
How does a buyer’s hiring of employees from a focal supplier’s competitors affect its outsourcing to that supplier, and how do existing focal-supplier alumni, buyer turnover, and geographic proximity condition this relationship?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Hiring employees from a supplier’s competitors decreases the amount of business a buyer outsources to the focal supplier.
- H2: A greater presence of focal-supplier alumni in the buyer weakens this negative relationship.
- H3: Buyer turnover strengthens this negative relationship.
- H4: This negative relationship is stronger for local than for nonlocal hiring from competing suppliers.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Buyer hiring of alumni from the focal supplier’s competitors.
- DV(s): Patent prosecution business outsourced by the buyer to the focal supplier.
- Mediators: Relational assets and relative relational advantage are theorized mechanisms, not directly tested mediators.
- Moderators: Focal-supplier alumni already employed by the buyer attenuate the negative relationship; buyer turnover and local competitor hiring strengthen it.

Competitor alumni may strengthen their former employer’s relational advantage while disrupting the focal supplier’s relationship-specific investments, interfirm routines, resource complementarities, and informal governance. Existing focal-supplier alumni can conserve these assets and resist competing suppliers’ influence, whereas turnover removes employees who sustain existing relationships. Geographic proximity facilitates contact with former colleagues and the transmission of knowledge, routines, and beliefs. These mechanisms are proposed explanations for the observed outsourcing patterns.

**Data & Measures**
The archival sample covers 1991–1995 and pairs 109 US Fortune 500 buyers with 123 patent law firms selected from 1990 frames; the main models contain 417 dyads and 1,631 dyad-years after excluding dyads without outcome variation. USPTO patent records identify the annual count prosecuted by each supplier for each buyer, a proxy for outsourced business; subsidiaries, divisions, and affiliates are tracked through the Directory of Corporate Affiliates. USPTO attorney rosters supply career histories and workplace addresses. Competitor hiring counts attorneys arriving from all other sample law firms. Focal-supplier alumni presence is the percentage of buyer attorneys whose last job was with that supplier. Turnover equals exiting attorneys multiplied by 100, divided by buyer-employed attorneys including departures but excluding new hires in the primary denominator. Local versus nonlocal hiring uses actual attorney moves within versus across metropolitan statistical areas.

The associational models use dyadic fixed effects, Poisson QML estimation, robust standard errors, and year dummies. Independent and control variables are lagged one year; nondummy variables are logged, adding one where zero values are possible. Controls cover mobility within and beyond the dyad, competing-supplier alumni stocks, buyer–supplier knowledge fit, prior outsourcing trends, supplier size/experience/rank, and buyer size, R&D, patent-attorney staffing, and legal top-management turnover. Knowledge fit is the cosine similarity of patent-class vectors based on the preceding three years’ patents.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported directionally: Table 2, Model 2 reports competitor hiring at b = −0.33 (SE = 0.15, p < .05, two-tailed). The prose instead states significance at 1% and reports a 10.5% decline for one hire versus none; its effect-size footnote uses a different coefficient, −0.37. These source inconsistencies are retained without reconciliation. Focal-supplier hiring has the opposite association (b = 0.46, p < .05).
- H2 is supported: the interaction with focal-supplier alumni is positive (Model 3: b = 21.65, p < .05; likelihood-ratio comparison with Model 2: p = .045), attenuating the negative association.
- H3 is supported: the interaction with buyer turnover is negative (Model 4: b = −5.50, p < .01), with model-fit improvement significant at p < .001.
- H4 receives qualified support: local hiring is negative and significant (Model 5: b = −0.57, p < .01), whereas distant hiring is negative but nonsignificant (b = −0.26). The coefficient-difference test gives p = .07; the authors interpret this as support at the 5% level using a one-tailed test.
- Supplementary experience-weighted mobility models show no significant improvement in fit, and comparisons across attorney-experience groups reveal no discernible pattern.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends alumni-effects research beyond employee movement within a focal dyad to competition originating in other supplier relationships. It develops the employee-level foundations of relational advantage by explaining how mobile individuals may build, preserve, or impair interfirm relational assets. It also extends strategic human-capital competition to customers’ hiring decisions and firms’ relationships with former employees.

**Practical Implication**
The authors highlight nurturing and leveraging partner ties through former employees as part of managing strategic human capital in high-skill, knowledge-intensive businesses. Their managerial implication extends attention beyond a firm’s own hiring and retention to personnel changes at its business partners.

**Limitations**
Findings from patent legal services and large Fortune 500 buyers may not generalize to other settings; the framework may apply primarily to similar knowledge-based services. Detailed employee attributes, managerial strategies, and organizational structures are unavailable. Dyadic fixed effects and extensive controls address stable differences and selected time-varying alternatives, but unobserved factors jointly affecting hiring and outsourcing can still limit identification.

**Future Research**
The authors call for finer-grained investigation of alumni status, expertise, formal roles, network positions, job satisfaction, departure conditions, and firms’ HR and alumni-outreach strategies as potential moderators. Research should examine interindustry variation and distinguish alumni hired into roles maintaining interfirm relationships from those hired into production-oriented roles. Further work is needed on how firms develop and use alumni relationships.
