# The Costs of Ambient Cultural Disharmony: Indirect Intercultural Conflicts in Social Environment Undermine Creativity

**Abstract**

Intercultural tensions and conflicts are inevitable in the global workplace. This article introduces the concept of ambient cultural disharmony—indirect experience of intercultural tensions and conflicts in individuals’ immediate social environment—and demonstrates how it undermines creative thinking in tasks that draw on knowledge from multiple cultures. Three studies (a network survey and two experiments) showed that ambient cultural disharmony decreased individuals’ effectiveness at connecting ideas from disparate cultures. Beliefs that ideas from different cultures are incompatible mediated the relationship between ambient cultural disharmony and creativity. Alternative mechanisms such as negative affect and cognitive disruption were not viable mediators. Although ambient cultural disharmony disrupted creativity, ambient cultural harmony did not promote creativity. These findings have theoretical and practical implications for research in workplace diversity and creativity.

**Research Question**

Does indirectly experiencing intercultural tensions and conflicts in one's immediate social environment undermine multicultural creativity, and do beliefs that ideas from disparate cultures are incompatible explain this effect?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1: Ambient cultural disharmony decreases individuals' ability to associate ideas from different cultures and hence decreases multicultural creativity.
- H2: Beliefs that ideas from disparate cultures are incompatible mediate this negative relationship.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Ambient cultural disharmony—awareness of intercultural tensions or conflicts without personal involvement.
- DV(s): Multicultural creativity, assessed through remote association and creative idea generation; traditional remote association provides a comparison outcome in Studies 1 and 2.
- Mediators: Beliefs about cultural incompatibility, tested in Studies 2 and 3; discomfort in Study 2 and negative affect and cognitive disruption in Study 3 are alternative mechanisms.
- Moderators: Cultural essentialism, explored in Study 1; whether observed interactions involve people of the same or different cultures in the experiments.

Observing intercultural disharmony is proposed to activate or increase beliefs that ideas and values from different cultures cannot be combined, hindering access to and connections among disparate cultural knowledge. Cultural essentialism concerns how fixed or malleable culture is and is distinct from beliefs about cultural incompatibility. The paper explores whether incompatibility beliefs are activated or shaped; it does not formally predict a creativity benefit from ambient cultural harmony.

**Data & Measures**

- Study 1: The 163 participants completed a network survey and a remote associates test (RAT), with order counterbalanced and a filler task between them. Ambient cultural disharmony equals the number of negative ties between contacts differing in ethnicity or nationality, divided by all possible pairs among listed contacts, N(N−1)/2. Twelve traditional and twelve multicultural RAT items were randomly combined into one test; scores capture correct associations. Cultural essentialism used eight items (α = .92). OLS controls included the other RAT score, direct intercultural negative ties, other tie densities, network size and cultural heterogeneity, openness, English proficiency, global identity, and prior multicultural experience/contact.
- Study 2: The 188 participants were randomly assigned to recall conflict or harmony between contacts of the same or different cultures. They completed the same RATs and generated fashion ideas for Shanghai Tang combining Chinese and Western elements. Two design experts rated creativity, defined by novelty and usefulness, on a seven-point scale, yielding average and most-creative-idea ratings. Cultural incompatibility used three items (α = .64); discomfort was also assessed. Mediation analyses used 5,000 bootstrap iterations.
- Study 3: The 264 students were randomly assigned to silent videos of same- or different-culture male dyads interacting in conflict, harmony, or a neutral manner. Participants generated business ideas meeting unmet needs in both Ewenki and Jivaro cultures. Two entrepreneurship experts rated average and most-creative-idea creativity; nonredundant idea count measured fluency. For originality, similar ideas across the full sample were grouped, each idea received its category's frequency, and these frequencies were summed and divided by the participant's number of ideas: higher average frequency means lower originality. Cultural incompatibility used the same three items (α = .79); negative affect used PANAS (α = .84), and cognitive disruption used recall of fifteen word pairs, with higher recall indicating less disruption. Bootstrapped indirect effects used 5,000 iterations.

**Key Findings**

- Study 1 supported H1 associationally: ambient cultural disharmony predicted lower multicultural RAT scores (Table 3, Model 2: b = −2.45, p < .05). The cultural-essentialism interaction was positive (b = 2.01, p < .05): the negative slope was significant for low essentialists (b = −6.24, p < .01), but not high essentialists (b = −1.85, nonsignificant). Traditional RAT performance was unaffected. The cross-national-only specification was weaker: the main association was marginal (p = .09), and the essentialism interaction was nonsignificant. Study 1 did not directly test mediation.
- Study 2 supported H1: the intercultural-conflict condition scored lowest on multicultural RAT and both design-creativity ratings. Multicultural RAT means were 1.74 for intercultural conflict, 2.93 for same-culture conflict, 2.74 for intercultural harmony, and 2.68 for same-culture harmony; the interaction was significant (F[1, 184] = 6.65, p = .01). H2 received qualified support: moderated indirect effects through incompatibility were significant for multicultural RAT and most-creative-idea ratings, but not average creativity (95% CI [−0.35, 0.02]). Within the intercultural conditions, indirect effects were significant for all three outcomes, with nonsignificant residual direct effects. Discomfort did not mediate. Unlike Study 1, traditional RAT showed significant culture and conflict main effects, although their interaction was nonsignificant (p = .21).
- Study 3 supported H1: compared with intercultural-neutral interaction, intercultural conflict reduced average creativity (b = −1.22), best-idea creativity (b = −1.57), and idea count (b = −1.07), while increasing average idea frequency (b = 17.26), indicating lower originality (Table 8; all p < .01). Supporting H2, conditional indirect effects through incompatibility were significant for all four outcomes in different-culture conditions and nonsignificant in same-culture conditions. Adding the mediator attenuated the first three direct effects and rendered the frequency effect nonsignificant. Negative affect and cognitive disruption did not mediate, although Table 7's conflict-by-culture interaction for recall was marginal (p < .10). Intercultural harmony showed no systematic creativity advantage; same-culture harmony increased incompatibility beliefs relative to same-culture neutral interaction.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper introduces ambient cultural disharmony to extend intercultural-conflict research from directly involved parties to uninvolved observers. It identifies cultural incompatibility beliefs as a mechanism through which the surrounding pattern of intercultural relationships can undermine multicultural creativity, extending attention beyond cultural composition alone. It also develops a multicultural RAT for studying connections among ideas from different cultures.

**Practical Implication**

Individuals cultivating multicultural networks should attend to relationships among their contacts and, where possible, help culturally different associates get along. Companies employing culturally diverse workforces should actively manage perceptions of events affecting intercultural relations. The author also proposes encouraging cultural metacognition—awareness and monitoring of cultural assumptions—as a possible way to dampen incompatibility beliefs, rather than as a tested intervention.

**Limitations**

The samples were not drawn from organizational contexts; Study 1 was cross-sectional and did not directly test prior cultural knowledge, limiting causal interpretation and separation of knowledge from associative ability. The multicultural RAT remains nascent, and the null comparison between culture-related and other conflict content in Study 2 may reflect limited observations of noncultural issues. Source reporting is internally inconsistent: Study 2's pooled traditional-RAT conflict mean is printed as 2.63, below Table 4's conflict-cell means of 4.50 and 2.89; Study 3's frequency interaction is marked p < .05 in Table 7 but p < .01 in the prose; and its Procedure and Measures sections disagree on whether recall preceded or followed mediator surveys.

**Future Research**

Replicate the findings in organizational field settings and expand and validate multicultural RAT items across cultures and countries. Investigate alternative mechanisms, including changes in relationships and idea flows, the salience of ambient disharmony and the duration of its effects, and why low versus high cultural essentialists respond differently. Extend investigation to conflicts between workgroups, organizations, and nations and their implications for individual creativity.
