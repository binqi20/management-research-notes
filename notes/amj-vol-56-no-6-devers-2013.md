# Do They Walk the Talk? Gauging Acquiring CEO and Director Confidence in the Value Creation Potential of Announced Acquisitions

**Abstract**

We explore whether acquiring CEOs and directors act consistently with the idea that their newly announced acquisitions will increase long-term firm value. Specifically, we examine postannouncement adjustments to CEOs’ equity-based holdings and find acquiring CEOs tend to exercise options and sell firm stock following acquisition announcements. Moreover, positive short-term market performance exacerbates this effect. Further, we find directors tend to grant their acquiring CEOs stock options, after acquisition announcement, presumably to more tightly align CEO-shareholder interests. These findings suggest that when CEOs and directors manage acquiring CEOs’ equity-based holdings, they do not appear to anticipate long-term value creation from their acquisitions.

**Research Question**

Do CEOs and directors adjust acquiring CEOs’ equity-based holdings as though they are confident in the long-term value creation potential of newly announced acquisitions? How do market reactions to those announcements relate to subsequent adjustments?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1a: CEOs are less likely to exercise stock options after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H1b: CEOs are more likely to exercise stock options after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H2a: CEOs are less likely to sell firm stock after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H2b: CEOs are more likely to sell firm stock after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H3a: Directors are less likely to grant CEO stock options after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H3b: Directors are more likely to grant CEO stock options after acquisition-announcement periods than after nonannouncement periods.
- H4a: Among announced acquisitions, market reaction is negatively associated with subsequent CEO option exercise.
- H4b: Among announced acquisitions, market reaction is positively associated with subsequent CEO option exercise.
- H5a: Among announced acquisitions, market reaction is negatively associated with subsequent CEO stock sales.
- H5b: Among announced acquisitions, market reaction is positively associated with subsequent CEO stock sales.
- H6: Among announced acquisitions, market reaction is negatively associated with subsequent director grants of CEO stock options.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Acquisition announcements; acquisition-announcement cumulative abnormal returns (CARs) in announcement-only analyses.
- DV(s): Subsequent CEO stock option exercise, firm stock sales, and receipt of stock option grants.
- Mediators: None tested; acquisition-specific confidence and perceived downside wealth risk are theorized explanations.
- Moderators: None tested; CARs enter separate announcement-only models rather than an interaction model.

The value enhancement perspective predicts that confident CEOs retain accessible equity to benefit from future appreciation, while directors perceive less need for additional alignment incentives. The private interest perspective predicts that CEOs who lack confidence in long-term value creation reduce personal downside exposure through option exercises and stock sales; this does not imply confidence that acquisitions will destroy value. Positive announcement returns can reinforce value-enhancement confidence or, under private-interest motives, increase accumulated wealth exposed to loss and encourage cashing out. Additional option grants are interpreted as directors’ attempts to strengthen interest alignment, rather than direct evidence of directors’ intentions.

**Data & Measures**

One archival study combines Thomson Reuters Insider Filing Data (daily insider transactions, aggregated quarterly), Execucomp, Compustat, CRSP, and SDC acquisition data for 1996–2007. From a frame of 2,804 publicly traded firms, the largest binary models use 48,911 firm-quarter observations from 2,069 firms; announcement-only binary models use 8,817 observations from 1,574 firms. Continuous-outcome samples are 45,423 for option exercise, 45,916 for stock sales, and 45,610 for option grants; corresponding announcement-only samples are 8,333, 8,342, and 8,354.

Each outcome is measured both as a quarterly transaction indicator and a ratio: options exercised divided by total options held at the start of the same year; shares sold divided by shares owned at the start of that year; or options granted divided by options held at the start of that year. Stock sales resulting from option exercise are excluded from the stock-sales outcome. Acquisition announcement equals one when a quarter contains at least one majority acquisition (51% or greater), worth at least $1 million if value is reported, that is subsequently completed; announcements are lagged one quarter. CARs cover three days before and three days after announcements and are averaged across multiple acquisitions in a quarter.

Random-effects logit and zero-censored tobit models cluster on firms. Controls cover compensation and exercisable-option value, CEO characteristics and turnover, firm size and income, diversification and stock risk, industry conditions, preannouncement holdings changes, the disclosure-rule change, and year effects; announcement-only models additionally control acquisition relatedness and value. The panel design establishes associations; confidence and acquisition motives are not directly measured.

**Key Findings**

- Tables 2–3 support H1b, H2b, and H3b over their value-enhancement alternatives. Announcement coefficients are positive for option exercise (logit b = .1720; tobit b = .0379; both p ≤ .001), stock sales (.1369 and .0522; both p ≤ .001), and option grants (.0826, p ≤ .05; .9413, p ≤ .001).
- Table 4 supports H4b and H5b over H4a and H5a: CARs positively predict option exercise (logit b = 1.337; tobit b = .2075) and stock sales (1.242 and .4327), with all four coefficients marked p ≤ .01. The prose instead reports p < .001 for the binary option-exercise result, an unresolved table–prose discrepancy.
- H6 is unsupported: CARs do not significantly predict option grants (Table 5: logit b = .2172; tobit b = 1.46).
- Patterns agree across binary and continuous outcomes. The authors also report consistent monthly-panel results, alternative CAR windows, and a two-stage instrumental-variable tobit check; the latter’s detailed results are available only on request. Holdings changes are consistent with the private-interest interpretation, but do not directly establish CEOs’ motives.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper organizes acquisition explanations into value enhancement and private interest perspectives and tests their competing predictions about CEO and director behavior. It challenges the conventional assumption that acquiring CEOs act confidently about long-term value creation, distinguishing acquisition-specific confidence from generalized CEO overconfidence. It also questions whether equity-based compensation necessarily aligns CEOs’ risk exposure and interests with shareholders’.

**Practical Implication**

Not reported in paper

**Limitations**

Holdings adjustments proxy for acquisition confidence, so the study cannot directly test acquisition motives. It cannot completely exclude the alternative that CEOs treat acquisitions as liquidity events and rebalance portfolios to diversify wealth. Ex post confidence may differ from ex ante confidence, and board–CEO power dynamics and effective acquisition vetting fall outside the study’s scope.

**Future Research**

Directly measure expressed acquisition confidence in real decisions, surveys, or laboratory studies; examine ex ante versus ex post confidence, confident CEOs’ attention, and the liquidity-event explanation. Investigate possible differences associated with family ownership, CEO humility and risk tolerance, competitive institutional pressures, and the joint pursuit of value enhancement and private benefits. Examine CEO–director dynamics and how other governance mechanisms and compensation arrangements affect acquisition behavior and equity-holdings adjustments.
