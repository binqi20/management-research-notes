# Rocking the Boat but Keeping It Steady: The Role of Emotion Regulation in Employee Voice

**Abstract**
Intense emotions such as frustration, anger, and dissatisfaction often drive employees to speak up. Yet the very emotions that spur employees to express voice may compromise their ability to do so constructively, preventing managers from reacting favorably. I propose that to speak up frequently and constructively, employees need knowledge about effective strategies for managing emotions. Building on theories of emotion regulation, I develop a theoretical model that explains the role of managing emotions in the incidence and outcomes of voice. In a field study at a health care company, emotion regulation knowledge (1) predicted more frequent voice, (2) mediated by the emotional labor strategies of deep acting and surface acting, and (3) enhanced the contributions of voice to performance evaluations. These results did not generalize to helping behaviors, demonstrating that emotion regulation uniquely affects challenging but not affiliative interpersonal citizenship behaviors. This research introduces emotion regulation as a novel influence on voice and its consequences.

**Research Question**
How does employees' emotion regulation knowledge relate to the frequency of voice and to managers' performance evaluations of employees who speak up? Do deep acting and surface acting mediate the knowledge–voice relationship, and are these relationships more relevant to voice than to helping?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Emotion regulation knowledge is positively related to voice.
- H2a–b: Emotion regulation knowledge is positively related to (a) deep acting and (b) surface acting.
- H3a–b: (a) Deep acting and (b) surface acting are positively related to voice.
- H4a–b: The positive relationship between emotion regulation knowledge and voice is partially mediated by (a) deep acting and (b) surface acting.
- H5: Emotion regulation knowledge moderates the voice–performance evaluation relationship: stronger knowledge makes the relationship more positive.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Emotion regulation knowledge, meaning awareness of effective strategies for managing emotions; voice in the performance-evaluation model.
- DV(s): Voice, meaning speaking up with suggestions for improvement; managers' performance evaluations. Helping is a comparison citizenship behavior.
- Mediators: Deep acting, which changes felt emotions, and surface acting, which changes displayed emotions, between emotion regulation knowledge and voice.
- Moderators: Emotion regulation knowledge in the relationship between voice and performance evaluations.

The theory proposes that emotion regulation knowledge facilitates deep and surface acting, making voice feel safer and more worthwhile by helping employees manage fear and other emotions. Managing emotional experience and expression may also enable constructive delivery and strategic timing, eliciting more favorable managerial evaluations. These explanations concern challenging voice rather than the less emotionally demanding, affiliative behavior of helping; the specific confidence, emotional, and timing mechanisms were not directly measured.

**Data & Measures**
The single field study began in spring 2009 at an optometry company headquartered in the southeastern United States. Of 209 employees invited, 123 completed the initial emotional-labor/personality survey and 101 completed the knowledge assessment, on average three weeks later; three HR managers rated 100 of these employees two months after the initial survey, excluding one employee too newly hired to evaluate.

Emotion regulation knowledge was measured with MacCann and Roberts's 44-item Situational Test of Emotion Management (STEM), applying mean expert ratings to response options (α = .73). Deep acting and surface acting used five- and four-item self-report scales from Brotheridge and Lee and Grandey and colleagues, concerning reactions to disappointing or frustrating work interactions (α = .65 and .72). Each HR manager provided single-item, seven-point ratings of constructive voice frequency, overall effectiveness in meeting expectations, and helping coworkers and patients; reported ICC2 values were .70, .61, and .69, respectively. Table 2 regressions controlled helping and self-reported extraversion; the SEM included extraversion paths to both emotional-labor strategies. For moderation, knowledge and voice were standardized separately before multiplication; partially latent SEM used item means as indicators and fixed error variance to (1 − reliability) × indicator variance. The design is associational, and the proposed mediators were measured before emotion regulation knowledge.

**Key Findings**
- H1–H3 were supported in Table 2: knowledge predicted voice before including the mediators (β = .19, p < .05), deep acting (β = .21, p < .05), and surface acting (β = .27, p < .01). Deep acting and surface acting independently predicted voice (β = .22 and .20, respectively; both p < .05).
- H4a–b were supported: each indirect effect was .01, with bias-corrected 95% bootstrap intervals excluding zero, based on 1,000 resamples. The author describes each strategy as a partial mediator; the direct knowledge coefficient became nonsignificant when both were included (β = .10). The partially latent SEM also supported the hypothesized paths.
- H5 was supported in Table 3: the knowledge × voice interaction predicting performance evaluations was positive (b = .17, β = .24, p < .05). Voice was positively related to evaluations at both high knowledge (one standard deviation above the mean: b = .53, p < .001) and low knowledge (one standard deviation below: b = .21, p = .02), with the stronger relationship at high knowledge.
- Knowledge, deep acting, and surface acting were not significantly related to helping, and the knowledge × helping interaction was nonsignificant (b = −.05). Direct comparisons showed significantly stronger knowledge and deep-acting relationships with voice than helping (p = .04 and .02), but the surface-acting difference was marginal (p < .09). The moderation effect was also significantly stronger for voice than helping (difference b = .22, p = .02).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study introduces emotion regulation knowledge and emotional-labor strategies as antecedents of voice, adding a “can do” knowledge perspective to explanations emphasizing personality and “will do” motivation. It identifies emotion regulation knowledge as a contingency in the performance-evaluation consequences of voice and distinguishes challenging voice from affiliative helping. It also links an ability-based situational judgment measure to both emotional-labor strategies and proposes that emotional knowledge interacts with behavior to shape evaluations.

**Practical Implication**
Leaders and managers may need to grant “idiosyncrasy credits” to employees with weak emotion regulation knowledge so their ideas are not suppressed over time. Employees may benefit from developing emotion regulation knowledge; those with weaker knowledge may separate idea generation from communication by seeking help from more emotionally knowledgeable peers, while those with stronger knowledge may use it to challenge the status quo more often.

**Limitations**
Correlational data cannot establish the proposed causal order, particularly because emotion regulation knowledge was measured after emotional-labor strategies; cognitive ability remains an alternative explanation. HR ratings may reflect the visibility rather than frequency of voice, some ratings had marginal reliability, and generalization beyond this sample and improvement-oriented voice remains untested. The study did not measure relevant contextual factors, other emotional-intelligence dimensions, audience emotion regulation knowledge, or the proposed micromediators.

**Future Research**
Future studies should vary measurement order, use experiments or longitudinal data with additional controls, assess behavioral emotion-regulation skills, triangulate HR ratings with other observers, and test more diverse samples and other voice forms such as whistle-blowing and complaints. The author proposes examining emotional-labor requirements, display rules, psychological safety, other emotional skills, and managers' emotion regulation knowledge. Further work should develop and test how knowledge shapes voice timing and tactics, including setting, scripts, audiences, and improvisation.
