# Leviathan as a Minority Shareholder: Firm-Level Implications of State Equity Purchases

**Abstract**
In many countries, firms face institutional “voids” that raise the costs of doing business and thwart entrepreneurial activity. We examine a particular mechanism that may address those voids: minority state ownership. Minority stakes are less affected by the “agency distortions” commonly found for full-fledged state ownership. Using panel data from publicly traded firms in Brazil, where the government holds minority stakes through its development bank, we find a positive effect of those stakes on firms’ returns on assets and on the capital expenditures of financially constrained firms with investment opportunities. However, these positive effects are substantially reduced when minority stakes are allocated to business group affiliates and as local institutions develop. Therefore, we shed light on the firm-level implications of minority state ownership, a topic that has received scant attention in the strategy literature.

**Research Question**
When can minority state ownership improve firm performance and investment by alleviating institutional voids, and how do business-group affiliation and institutional development condition these benefits?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: In less developed institutional settings, minority state ownership positively affects firm-level economic performance.
- H2: In less developed institutional settings, minority state ownership promotes capital expenditures by firms with constrained opportunity.
- H3: In less developed institutional settings, the positive performance effect is attenuated when a firm belongs to a pyramidal business group.
- H4: In less developed institutional settings, the positive capital-expenditure effect for firms with constrained opportunity is reduced by pyramidal business-group membership.
- H5: The positive capital-expenditure effect for firms with constrained opportunity is reduced as local institutions develop.
- H6: The attenuation in H5 is greater for firms outside business groups.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Minority state equity, supplied by the Brazilian National Development Bank (BNDES) through BNDESPAR.
- DV(s): Return on assets, market-to-book ratio, and fixed investment.
- Mediators: No formal mediation test; relief of investment constraints is the proposed mechanism.
- Moderators: Constrained opportunity, pyramidal business-group membership, and institutional development.

Minority state capital may finance valuable long-term projects while leaving control with profit-oriented owners, reducing the agency distortions associated with full state ownership; equity also offers greater flexibility than debt, and state investors may supply patient capital. Group internal markets can make external state capital unnecessary, while tunneling can divert it from the recipient firm's projects; these are proposed explanations rather than separately established mechanisms. As institutions develop, alternative resources become more available, particularly reducing stand-alone firms' dependence on state capital; residual political interference remains a competing possibility.

**Data & Measures**
- The unbalanced panel covers 367 Brazilian listed firms during 1995–2009, using CVM filings and Economática, Interinvest, and Valor Grandes Grupos. Financial firms and listed holding corporations were excluded, inconsistent records removed, and highly variable key measures winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Samples differ by analysis: Table 3 Model 1 has 2,920 observations/367 firms, its matched direct-stake Model 4 has 1,194/130, and Table 4's matched direct-stake investment Model 4 has 878/124; Table 5 uses 878 observations for time and 660 for the other institutional measures.
- ROA is annual net profits divided by total assets; market-to-book is stock market value divided by book equity. Fixed investment is annual capital expenditures divided by fixed capital at the previous year's end. The authors use this last measure as a proxy for long-maturity investment, without reliable R&D or finer nonredeployability measures.
- Minority ownership is measured as the direct BNDESPAR equity percentage or a combined direct/indirect ownership dummy, tracing at most two ownership layers; additional direct-stake indicators examine residual control. Group membership identifies firms whose controlling owner or owners control other sample firms, using shareholder agreements; multinationals with only one Brazilian subsidiary are excluded from this group definition. Constrained opportunity equals one only when both conditions hold: Tobin's Q—stock market value plus book debt, divided by book total assets—exceeds one, and net profits divided by initial fixed capital falls below the sample median.
- Institutional measures are time, counted from one in 1995; World Competitiveness Report measures of ease of credit, competition legislation, and skilled-labor availability; Institutional Investor country credit ratings; and World Bank stock-market capitalization/GDP. Except time, the measures use annual percentage variations with three-year moving-average windows; models also include time and its interactions.
- This is an observational design. Methods specifies firm, year, and industry-by-year fixed effects, combined in additional models with kernel propensity-score weights from probits using 1995 characteristics and a common-support restriction; the authors describe this comparison as analogous to differences-in-differences. Controls include log revenues, leverage, fixed assets/total assets, ownership and merger indicators, and relevant lower-order interaction terms. Some tables instead label the effects “firm-industry,” and Table 7 has ambiguous duplicate control-row labels; the control-definition prose also reverses the state/foreign ordering before “respectively.” These source inconsistencies are not resolved here.

**Key Findings**
- Using the formal hypothesis numbering, support for H1/H3 is strongest for direct stakes and ROA. Table 3 Model 4 reports a direct-stake coefficient of 0.004 (p < .01) and a group interaction of −0.007 (p < .05): per percentage-point increase in direct equity, the baseline association is a 0.4-percentage-point ROA increase, attenuated by 0.7 percentage points for group affiliates. The combined direct/indirect dummy is significant without matching but not with matching. Market-to-book results are largely nonsignificant; Model 12 has a marginal positive stake coefficient (0.901, p < .10) and negative group interaction (−1.555, p < .05). Its threshold is stated as >10% in prose but ≥10% in the printed table heading.
- H2/H4 receive support for direct stakes, with and without matching, but not the combined direct/indirect measure. Table 4 Model 4 reports constrained opportunity × direct stake = 3.767 and its additional group interaction = −3.710 (both p < .05), while the baseline stake coefficient is nonsignificant. The authors interpret this as investment benefits concentrated in stand-alone firms with constrained opportunity.
- H5/H6 receive measure-specific support: Table 5 finds attenuation with time, ease of credit, stock-market capitalization/GDP, and skilled-labor availability, and corresponding positive group interactions indicating weaker attenuation for affiliates. The country-credit-rating and competition-legislation interactions are nonsignificant. For time, the focal three-way interaction is −1.361 and the group four-way interaction is 1.401 (both p < .01); the paper also reports no significant performance or investment effects in models restricted to years after 2002.
- Selection tests find no significant lagged-ROA association with stakes, but the early-period combined-stake model has positive market-to-book and negative constrained-opportunity associations, and some controls are marginally significant (Table 6). Thus, the prose claim that all whole-period variables are insignificant overlooks marginal controls in Model 1. Table 7 finds no significant stake–leverage association; a negative financial-expense association for combined stakes without matching disappears with matching, providing no consistent support for the debt-access explanation.

The results prose misnumbers the performance/group predictions as H1/H2 and the investment predictions as H3/H4; the formal hypotheses above retain their original numbering. Table 3 Model 5 also prints 2,919 observations and 130 firms despite identifying the model as matched; no explanation is supplied.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper explains minority state equity as a conditional response to institutional voids, integrating institution-based strategy with agency and transaction-cost reasoning. It distinguishes state capital under decentralized private control from full state ownership and identifies business-group affiliation and institutional development as contingencies shaping firm-level benefits. State and group capital may substitute within affiliates yet complement one another economy-wide when state capital targets independent firms.

**Practical Implication**
Policymakers considering minority equity as an industrial-policy tool should avoid pyramidal groups with poor governance and instead target stand-alone, well-run firms with a clear need for productive capital expenditures. The authors recommend allocating equity directly to target firms and progressively exiting as local institutions develop.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that the results may be specific to Brazil and its channel of minority state participation; their financing-void argument cannot predict performance benefits in developed economies with active, liquid capital markets. Indirect equity magnitudes are incompletely observed, requiring a dummy rather than a comparable continuous stake measure. Fixed investment is only a proxy for long-maturity, nonredeployable projects because reliable finer measures, including R&D expenditures, were unavailable.

**Future Research**
The authors call for examining other institutional contexts, state-capital channels and outcomes, and the conditions under which development-bank loans improve firm development. They propose studying innovation, R&D expenditure and interfirm knowledge spillovers, as well as differences among state investment vehicles such as pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds and holding companies. More detailed governance research should examine state–private shareholder coalitions, residual state influence, and the performance consequences of their corporate networks.
