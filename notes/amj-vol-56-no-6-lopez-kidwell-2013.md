# What Matters When: A Multistage Model and Empirical Examination of Job Search Effort

**Abstract**
We develop a multistage self-regulatory perspective on job search effort assuming active job seekers conducting job searches within a job search goal life span. Specifically, we propose that time pressure increases as the goal of finding employment becomes more proximal, while job search uncertainty decreases. Drawing on these premises, we integrate social comparison theory, control theory, and the attentional focus model of time pressure to hypothesize how various intrapersonal (i.e., prior effort, job search progress) and sociocontextual (i.e., effort put forth by peers in a social network) factors relate to job seekers’ self-regulation of effort at different stages (i.e., preparatory, active-extensive, and active-intensive) of a job search process. In two studies of job seekers, we found that (1) prior job seeker effort is positively related to current effort across stages, (2) average peer job search effort is more strongly and positively related to job seeker effort earlier in job search, and (3) job search progress (i.e., the ratio of interviews to applications in Study 1 and perceived progress in Study 2) is negatively related to job seeker effort later in job search. Theoretical implications and future research directions are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do prior effort, peer effort, and job search progress relate differently to active job seekers’ self-regulation of effort across preparatory, active-extensive, and active-intensive stages of a job search goal life span?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Prior job search effort is positively related to current effort throughout a job search goal life span.
- H2: Peer job search effort is positively related to individual effort, with a stronger relationship earlier in the goal life span.
- H3: Job search progress is negatively related to individual effort, with a stronger relationship later in the goal life span.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Prior job search effort; peer job search effort; job search progress.
- DV(s): Current job search effort.
- Mediators: None tested in the focal model; increasing time pressure and decreasing job search uncertainty are unmeasured theoretical explanations.
- Moderators: Job search stage, examined through separate stage models in Study 1 and early/late stage interactions in Study 2.

The job search goal life span extends from forming an employment goal to the desired employment deadline. Earlier, uncertainty encourages social comparison with peers, while low time pressure permits acting on that information; later, reduced uncertainty and greater time pressure are proposed to concentrate attention on personal progress. Control theory predicts reducing effort when progress is good and increasing it when progress is poor, particularly later in search. Prior effort supplies an initial reference and subsequently reflects habituation.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: Of 78 MBA students, 61 participated in every wave; excluding 12 not actively seeking employment left 49 initial active seekers. The three stage regressions used 48, 40, and 37 observations, respectively. Following July background data and a September effort baseline, surveys were administered near the midpoints of stages typically beginning three to four, approximately six, and approximately nine months after program entry. Effort used Blau’s adapted four-item general job search effort scale, with five-point agreement responses and α = .93–.96. Methods specify prior-stage effort as the lagged predictor, although Table 2 labels its row “Job search effort, time 1.” Peer effort averages nominated MBA job-search discussion partners’ own reported effort. Progress divides cumulative interviews obtained by cumulative applications submitted; higher ratios indicate greater progress. Controls were core self-evaluation, GMAT score, age, engineering major, and network size (outdegree). Stage regressions report standardized coefficients and two-tailed tests; cross-stage coefficient comparisons use one-tailed tests.
- Study 2: There were 514 first-wave responses and 188 responses to both surveys, six weeks apart; 97 had not accepted an offer, and 22 incomplete cases were excluded, yielding 75. Time 1 measured baseline effort with the same four-item scale and perceived progress with two success items following Kulik and Rowland. Time 2 measured current effort and self-classified stage: 20 early, 36 intermediate, and 19 late. Respondents also rated up to five named job-seeking peers’ effort over the preceding month using the adapted four-item scale; these ratings were averaged. Controls were core self-evaluation, conscientiousness, GPA, business major, and network size. Standardized moderated regressions used intermediate stage as the comparison group; this design compares stage groups rather than following every respondent through all three stages. Both studies are observational.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: H1 was partially supported: prior-effort βs were .59 (p < .01), .28 (p < .10), and .58 (p < .01) across the three stages (Table 2, Models 1b–3b). H2 was also partially supported: peer-effort βs were .34 and .31 (both p < .05), then .01 (nonsignificant); the first two coefficients exceeded the last only marginally (z = 1.47 and 1.38, both p < .10), with no difference between the first two. H3 was supported: progress βs were .06 (nonsignificant), −.43 and −.34 (both p < .05); the later negative relationships were stronger than the early relationship (z = 2.41, p < .01; z = 2.09, p < .05), but did not differ from each other. Weighting peer effort by tie strength or peers’ indegree centrality did not change the pattern or significance of Study 1 results.
- Study 2: H1 was supported (β = .43, p < .01; Table 4, Model 2). H2 was supported: the late-stage × peer-effort interaction was negative (β = −.36, p < .01), while the early-stage interaction was nonsignificant. Peer-effort slopes were .42 (p < .05), .49 (p < .01), and −.10 (nonsignificant) for early, middle, and late stages. H3 was supported: the late-stage × progress interaction was negative (β = −.26, p < .05), while the early-stage interaction was nonsignificant; progress slopes were .22 and .10 (both nonsignificant), then −.35 (p < .05).

Study 2 significance levels above reproduce the authors’ reporting: the methods specify one-tailed tests, but Table 4’s footnote says two-tailed tests. This source discrepancy remains unresolved.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends self-regulatory accounts of job search by showing that predictors’ relative importance varies across a goal life span. It integrates intrapersonal and sociocontextual sources of effort regulation, connecting social comparison earlier in search and control-theory feedback later, while treating time pressure and uncertainty as proposed explanations.

**Practical Implication**
Job seekers and employment counselors should consider search stage when interpreting effort; MBA career centers could make early search efforts visible, and administrators might consider communication-network-based student grouping to facilitate useful information exchange. Later, administrators should encourage students who perceive good progress to keep striving, for example by seeking additional offers. Companies might tailor recruitment to search stages and make positive overtures near midcycle, when the authors suggest such feedback could reduce attractive candidates’ search effort toward competing companies.

**Limitations**
Time pressure and uncertainty were not measured, and alternative explanations remain—for example, later-stage seekers may reduce effort while awaiting interview outcomes. The small, early-career samples constrain power and generalizability; Study 1 additionally used a cohesive MBA cohort, researcher-assigned stages, restricted peer nominations, a progress ratio that omits opportunity quality, and relatively homogeneous cognitive ability. Deadlines are not strictly binding, and the model does not fully capture changing employment subgoals or all deadline-resetting possibilities.

**Future Research**
The authors call for direct measurement of time pressure and uncertainty, tests with more experienced and unemployed job seekers, and examination of whether social cognitive theory predicts early effort while control theory better predicts later effort. Future work could examine effort directed at particular firms, industries, or jobs, and tie strength in larger, less cohesive, or nascent networks. Research should also test how extending the active-intensive stage beyond an unmet deadline affects the continued influence of prior effort and progress, how restarting searches changes self-regulation, and how search strategies relate to subsequent job satisfaction.
