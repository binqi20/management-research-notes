# The Benefits of Climate for Inclusion for Gender-Diverse Groups

**Abstract**
I introduce the construct of climate for inclusion, which involves eliminating relational sources of bias by ensuring that identity group status is unrelated to access to resources, creating expectations and opportunities for heterogeneous individuals to establish personalized cross-cutting ties, and integrating ideas across boundaries in joint problem solving. I show that within inclusive climates, interpersonal bias is reduced in such a way that gender diversity is associated with lower levels of conflict. In turn, the negative effect that group conflict typically has on unit-level satisfaction disappears. This has important implications, as unit-level satisfaction is negatively associated with turnover in groups.

**Research Question**
Does climate for inclusion moderate the relationships between gender diversity and group conflict, and between conflict and unit-level satisfaction? Is unit-level satisfaction, in turn, negatively associated with voluntary turnover?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1: Climate for inclusion moderates the gender diversity–relationship conflict relationship: gender-diverse groups with highly inclusive climates experience less relationship conflict than diverse groups with less inclusive climates.
- H2: Climate for inclusion similarly moderates the gender diversity–task conflict relationship: gender-diverse groups with highly inclusive climates experience less task conflict.
- H3: The negative relationship between relationship conflict and satisfaction is attenuated or reversed in units with highly inclusive climates.
- H4: The negative relationship between task conflict and satisfaction is attenuated or reversed in units with highly inclusive climates.
- H5: Unit-level satisfaction is negatively associated with unit-level turnover.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Gender diversity; relationship and task conflict in the satisfaction equations; unit satisfaction in the turnover equation.
- DV(s): Relationship conflict, task conflict, unit-level satisfaction, and voluntary turnover.
- Mediators: Relationship and task conflict are modeled as conditional pathways from gender diversity to satisfaction; satisfaction links the proposed model to turnover.
- Moderators: Climate for inclusion, encompassing fair employment practices, integration of differences, and inclusion in decision making.

The author argues that inclusive climates weaken the association between gender and favored status, enable authentic self-expression and cross-cutting ties, and integrate diverse perspectives into decisions. These conditions should reduce categorization-based conflict and help members interpret and address relationship disagreements without damaging satisfaction. Status equalization, personalized ties, and conflict-resolution styles are proposed explanations rather than directly tested mechanisms; the reported conditional indirect tests concern satisfaction, not the entire pathway to turnover.

**Data & Measures**
Scale development administered 47 five-point items to 633 university employees; exploratory factor analysis retained 31 items across three dimensions. Cross-validation used 701 working adults, with criterion data collected three weeks after scale administration. The main study used 1,324 employees in 100 departments at a regional site of a large biomedical company; the average within-unit response rate was 72%. Country and calendar collection dates are not reported.

Climate for inclusion used a shortened 15-item scale and the average of its three dimensions (α = .93; mean rwg = .94; ICC1 = .13; ICC2 = .64); confirmatory factor analysis accounted for respondents' clustering within departments. Gender diversity used Blau's index, 1 − Σ pₖ²: square each gender-category proportion, sum the squares, then subtract from one. Relationship and task conflict each used three five-point Jehn items (Jehn & Mannix, 2001). Satisfaction used the 2006 General Social Survey single item, rated from 1 to 7 and averaged within units. Approximately six months after the survey, the organization supplied monthly voluntary-departure counts; the reported turnover rate divides departures by unit employment at survey administration, but the text does not explicitly describe how monthly counts were combined.

All hypotheses were tested jointly in a bootstrapped unit-level SEM. Controls were racial diversity (Blau index), age and tenure diversity (standard deviations), educational disparity (coefficient of variation), department size, and six-item distributive justice climate. The design is associational: all data except administrative turnover were cross-sectional.

**Key Findings**

- H1 and H2 were supported: gender diversity × inclusion climate predicted lower relationship conflict (β = −.22, p < .01) and task conflict (β = −.23, p < .01). For relationship conflict, the gender-diversity slope was positive under low inclusion (b = 1.61, p < .01) and negative under high inclusion (b = −2.18, p < .01); task conflict showed a similar slope pattern. Inclusion climate also had negative direct associations with relationship and task conflict (β = −.56 and −.51, respectively; both p < .01).
- H3 was supported: relationship conflict × inclusion climate predicted satisfaction (β = .45, p < .01). The relationship-conflict slope was negative under low inclusion (β = −.27, p = .05) but nonsignificant under high inclusion (β = .04, p > .10). H4 was unsupported: neither the task-conflict direct path (β = .05) nor its interaction with inclusion (β = −.31) was significant (both p > .10).
- H5 was supported: satisfaction was negatively associated with turnover (β = −.32, p < .05). The reported conditional indirect effect of gender diversity on satisfaction through relationship conflict was significant (β = −2.55, p < .05); the corresponding task-conflict effect was not (β = 1.34, p > .10).
- Adding mean unit tenure did not change the results pattern. However, replacing gender heterogeneity with percentage female produced no significant percentage-female × inclusion interaction for either conflict outcome. Additional tests of racial, age, tenure, and educational diversity found no significant main or inclusion-moderated relationships with study outcomes.

Table 3 and the results prose agree on the hypothesis coefficients. Source-reporting anomalies remain unresolved: Table 3 prints negative values in several turnover standard-error cells and reports χ²(2) = 20.20 together with RMSEA = .01.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study operationalizes climate for inclusion as a construct that extends beyond fair employment practices to interpersonal and decision-making integration. It shows that organizational social context qualifies the association between gender diversity and conflict, and identifies a condition under which relationship conflict is not significantly associated with lower satisfaction. The argument emphasizes whether gender predicts social status in the local context, beyond its perceptual salience.

**Practical Implication**
The author recommends that managers begin by monitoring HR outcomes and employees' perceptions of the fairness of employment practices, to identify biases that may obstruct improvements in interpersonal integration and inclusive decision making. More broadly, minimizing structural inequalities, assimilation norms, and exclusionary decision processes is proposed as a starting point for reducing divisive conflict.

**Limitations**
Except for turnover, the data are cross-sectional; the proposed interpersonal mechanisms remain untested. The abbreviated inclusion scale may limit construct coverage, existing diversity-climate measures were absent, and only distributive justice climate—not procedural or interactional justice climate—was controlled in the main study. Incomplete employee participation may distort unit diversity measures.

**Future Research**
The author calls for tests in organizations where racial and other demographic disparities are salient, and for direct comparisons with diversity-climate and additional justice-climate measures using the full inclusion scale. Further work should examine conflict experiences, construals and resolution styles—including possible recursive effects on climate—and whether cross-cutting ties in inclusive climates reduce dependence on a single in-group and whether employees in those climates develop concentric loyalties to their multiple identities and the inclusive collective. Other directions include organizational and individual antecedents of inclusive unit leadership, diversity–performance relationships, and mediating processes such as information elaboration.
