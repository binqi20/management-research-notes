# Between You and Me: Setting Work-Nonwork Boundaries in the Context of Workplace Relationships

**Abstract**
This study examined how individuals do “boundary work,” the process of negotiating, setting, moving, and adjusting boundaries between work and life outside of work. In an inductive qualitative study of 70 attorneys in a large US law firm, I found that boundary work cannot be fully understood without considering the relationships within which it happens. Attorneys engaged in different types of boundary work in different types of relationships that activated either approach or avoidance motivation to pursue substantive (i.e., boundary-related) and relational goals. Boundary work led to measurable and predictable outcomes—success in boundary setting and impact on relationships—that depended on the nature of relationships and the type of boundary work used. I develop a theoretical model of boundary work and a set of propositions, and discuss theoretical and practical importance of examining boundary work in the context of interpersonal relationships.

**Research Question**
How does relational context affect boundary work, how do relationships shape its outcomes, and how does boundary work affect relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The study develops emergent propositions rather than testing a priori hypotheses.

- Proposition 1: Boundary work pursues substantive goals of setting desired boundaries and relational goals of managing relationships through impression management and self-management.
- Proposition 2: Relationships shape approach-avoidance motivation through regulatory focus and regulatory anticipation. Boundary setters use approach boundary work when they trust counterparts regarding boundary-work outcomes or want to develop their relationships, and avoidance boundary work when lack of trust or active distrust leads them to expect negative consequences from involving counterparts.
- Proposition 3(a): In close relationships, positive substantive and relational outcomes can be obtained simultaneously, although substantive goals are sometimes compromised to achieve relational goals.
- Proposition 3(b): Established relationships usually permit positive substantive outcomes, while neutral relational impact is satisfactory and improvement need not be pursued.
- Proposition 3(c): New relationships often entail trade-offs: complete boundary success usually comes at a relational cost, while positive relational impact usually requires compromising boundary success.
- Proposition 3(d): In leery relationships, further relational harm is practically inevitable: boundary violators usually react negatively when setters succeed, whereas setters usually react negatively when violators prevent desired boundaries.

**Mechanism Process**

- Relational context: Close relationships involve trust about professional and personal issues; established relationships involve professional trust without comparable personal intimacy; new relationships involve limited familiarity and variable trust; leery relationships involve suspicion and distrust relevant to boundary work.
- Boundary setting: Approach practices enlist counterparts through information sharing, advance notice, exchanges and alternatives; avoidance practices pursue boundaries while excluding counterparts from decisions.
- Impression management: Approach practices seek favorable impressions through explanation and selective accommodation; avoidance practices conceal or obscure nonwork commitments to prevent unfavorable impressions.
- Self-management: Approach practices make benevolent attributions about counterparts and reframe competing demands; avoidance practices make negative attributions to avoid guilt and anticipated blame.

In the author's process model, relationship nature shapes approach or avoidance motivation and thus the selection of boundary-work practices; relationship nature and practice type jointly shape boundary success and relational impact. Boundary work can then reinforce or change relationships, affecting subsequent episodes, while preparatory relational work helps establish the context before a particular conflict occurs. The nature and urgency of competing work and nonwork demands also shape tactic selection, although these influences are outside the study's central focus.

**Data & Measures**
The researcher interviewed 70 attorneys at Mack & Clark, a pseudonymous large US firm, theoretically sampled from 130 volunteers among 361 attorneys approached in two offices. Interviews lasted 30–110 minutes, averaging 73, and generated almost 2,000 transcript pages, 176 specific boundary episodes and 319 general descriptions. The sample included 32 junior associates, 17 senior associates, 3 of counsel and 18 partners; 43 worked in litigation and 27 in corporate practice. Collection dates are not reported.

Open, axial and selective coding identified boundary-work types, relationship nature and outcomes; tactics began as in vivo codes and were grouped by goals and approach-avoidance motivation. Relationship categories reflected closeness, trust/distrust and duration. Boundary success compared desired with actual boundaries; relational impact captured immediate positive, neutral, negative or unknown effects as perceived by interviewees. For Figure 3, complete/partial/no success and positive/neutral/negative relational impact were each coded 1/0/−1, averaged over examples coded for all necessary aspects, then rounded to the nearest integer. These are descriptive example averages, not causal estimates or participant-level success rates.

The Methods reports 76 descriptions/examples coded for relationship nature, but Table 1 and Figure 3 list relationship-category counts of 14, 40, 14 and 15; Figure 3 also gives an overall total of 96. The paper does not reconcile these counts.

**Key Findings**

- Close relationships used approach boundary work exclusively, usually without active impression or self-management. The rounded overall outcome was complete boundary success with positive relational impact, consistent with a reinforcing process of trust, disclosure and help.
- Established relationships predominantly used approach boundary setting, supplemented more often by impression management than close relationships. Boundary success was very high, but relational impact was more often neutral than positive, with only one negative case reported in the prose; unusually generous help could shift an established relationship toward closeness.
- New relationships emphasized impression management and mixed approach with avoidance. Overall success was partial and relational impact neutral, concealing substantial variation: avoidance boundary setting could secure boundaries while damaging relationships, whereas approach boundary setting generally preserved relationships and had a positive average relational impact in Figure 3. Approach versus avoidance impression management made little average difference to either outcome.
- Leery relationships encouraged concealment and negative attributions, although sufficiently important nonwork demands or minimal disruption to work could elicit approach boundary setting. Figure 3 places approach boundary setting at partial success and negative relational impact, versus complete success and negative impact for avoidance boundary setting. Although the prose characterizes relational impact as consistently negative, Figure 3 shows neutral average relational impact for avoidance impression management in leery relationships.

Attorneys also described cumulative positive experiences and unusually cooperative or uncooperative encounters that changed relationships over the longer term; these accounts are distinct from the immediate relational-impact coding.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study recasts boundary work as a relational process, extending accounts centered on individual differences and job structures to ongoing relationships and preparatory relational work. It connects boundary theory with interpersonal-relationship research and approach-avoidance motivation to explain why substantive and relational goals generate different practices. Its model makes boundary success and relational impact joint outcomes whose combination depends on both practice type and relationship nature.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should support a culture that treats life outside work as legitimate, enabling individuals to discuss nonwork commitments with less reluctance. The author also recommends supporting longer-lasting working relationships rather than continually requiring new counterparts, and facilitating the dissolution of leery relationships when possible.

**Limitations**
The inductive interviews did not ask about relationships directly, leaving many descriptions without sufficient information to classify relationships or outcomes. Outcome coding reflects boundary setters' perceptions and captures immediate rather than long-term relational impact; some episodes remained unresolved at interview. Iterative reconciliation of relationship and boundary-work coding did not permit a traditional interrater-reliability assessment, although independent outcome coding was described as having high agreement and disagreements were reconciled.

**Future Research**
Future boundary theory should examine ongoing relationships and preparatory relational work beyond isolated interactions or responses to boundary violations. Research should assess boundary success alongside relational consequences and investigate dynamics over time, including how previous interactions and anticipated future interactions shape boundary work and how reinforcing relationship spirals change its challenges.
