# Within or Without? How Firms Combine Internal and External Labor Markets to Fill Jobs

**Abstract**
We examine which jobs are more likely to be filled by internal mobility (specifically, promotions and lateral transfers) than by hiring. Building on the assumptions of transaction cost accounts of employment, we develop a new theory that focuses on the interaction between the problems of evaluating and integrating external hires, on the one hand, and the incentive costs of failing to promote eligible workers, on the other. These arguments lead us to predict how three specific characteristics of jobs—demands for firm-specific skills, performance variability, and supply of internal candidates—affect how those jobs are staffed. Using seven years of personnel data spanning all jobs from the U.S. offices of a large investment bank, we find that jobs with higher performance variability and a larger grade ratio of junior to senior workers are more likely to be filled by internal mobility. We also find evidence that the effects of performance variability are contingent on the grade ratio, affecting staffing decisions only when the firm does not face strong pressures to promote junior workers in order to maintain incentives. Contrary to expectations, we find no effect for firm-specific skills.

**Research Question**
Which job characteristics explain whether firms fill jobs through external hiring, promotion, or lateral transfer? How do difficulties evaluating and integrating hires interact with the incentive pressures to promote eligible workers?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The likelihood of filling a job through hiring rather than internal mobility (promotions and transfers) decreases as the firm-specific skills required for the job rise.
- H2: The likelihood of filling a job through hiring rather than internal mobility decreases as performance variability in the job rises.
- H3a: The likelihood of entering a job through hiring rather than promotion decreases as the grade ratio increases.
- H3b: The likelihood of entering a job through transfer rather than promotion decreases as the grade ratio increases.
- H4a: The positive relationship between the need for firm-specific skills and internal mobility is stronger when the grade ratio is lower.
- H4b: The positive relationship between performance variability and internal mobility is stronger when the grade ratio is lower.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Job sensitivity to firm-specific skills; performance variability; grade ratio, representing the structural supply of workers on the next-lowest rung of the job ladder.
- DV(s): Entry into a job by promotion, lateral transfer, or external hiring.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper
- Moderators: Grade ratio moderates the hypothesized relationships of firm-specific skills and performance variability with internal versus external staffing.

The theory concerns ex ante worker–job matching and promotion incentives: hired and internally moved workers subsequently operate under the same organizational governance. Internal candidates possess firm-specific skills and are better known to the firm; better screening should be especially valuable where poor matches impose greater costs because performance varies widely across incumbents. A high grade ratio creates pressure to promote because passing over many eligible junior workers threatens their motivation. Such pressure may preempt consideration of task characteristics, making those characteristics more consequential when the grade ratio is low; these are proposed mechanisms, not measured mediation effects.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses annual personnel records for all employees in the U.S. operations of one investment bank, pseudonymously called FinServ, during 2003–2009. Jobs combine O*NET occupation, hierarchical rank, and division; the main analysis excludes the bottom, entry-level rank. Its 7,749 mobility events involve 5,773 workers in 236 jobs and comprise 4,222 promotions, 2,132 external hires, and 1,395 internal transfers. Promotion means moving from a lower rank; transfer means moving from another division or occupation at the same rank; hiring means entering from outside the firm. Most promotions occur through an annual process at the beginning of the year.

Sensitivity to firm-specific skills is operationalized as the mean subjective performance rating of promotees minus that of hires within each job and year, averaged across years to give a fixed job characteristic; ratings range from 1 to 5. This performance-gap proxy is distinct from observing skills directly. For 23 jobs entered exclusively through promotion or hiring, the authors impute the measure from regressions on observable job and incumbent characteristics; excluding these jobs did not substantively change results. Contribution variability is the within-job/year standard deviation of ratings among promotees; bonus variability uses the standard deviation of their natural-log bonuses, substituting US$1 where no annual bonus was received. Each variability measure is then averaged across years. Restricting these measures to promotees reduces contamination by differences between hires and promotees. Grade ratio divides the number of workers one rank below the focal job, in the same division and O*NET occupation, by the number in the focal job, then averages this ratio across years.

Multinomial logit models compare promotion and transfer with hiring, clustering standard errors by job. Controls include job size, growth, average bonus and performance rating, promotion/transfer/turnover exits, reorganization, occupational unemployment, location, rank, and year. Hausman and Small–Hsiao tests did not reject independence of irrelevant alternatives. The design is associational: suitable instruments were unavailable. Supplementary models include workers remaining in the same job and use that “no change” outcome as the base, with 23,114 observations and 244 jobs. The source calls the main observations position–years/mobility events, but Table 1's footnote calls the unit job–year; it also reports 236 jobs in Table 2 versus 244 in Table 3 without explaining the increase.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is unsupported: firm-specific-skill sensitivity does not significantly predict promotion or transfer relative to hiring (Table 2, Model 2: b = .36 and −.21, respectively).
- H2 receives support for promotions, with weaker and measure-dependent transfer results. Bonus variability positively predicts promotion relative to hiring (Model 3: b = .22, p < .01), while its transfer coefficient is marginal (b = .09, p < .10). Contribution variability positively predicts promotion (Model 4: b = .95, p < .01) but not transfer (b = .36, nonsignificant). In the saturated Model 9, bonus variability is positive for both promotion (b = .13, p < .01) and transfer (b = .10, p < .05), while contribution variability remains significant for promotion and nonsignificant for transfer. The Discussion's statement that performance variability has no effect on transfers versus hiring is broader than these Table 2 results; the source discrepancy is retained here.
- H3a and H3b are supported: a higher grade ratio predicts promotion relative to hiring (Model 5: b = .23, p < .01), and the promotion-versus-transfer coefficient difference is significant (p < .01).
- H4a is unsupported: the grade-ratio × firm-specific-skills interaction is nonsignificant for promotion (Model 6: b = −.32) and positive for transfer (b = .60, p < .05), opposite to the predicted attenuation of internal mobility.
- H4b receives partial support. Grade ratio weakens the positive bonus-variability association with promotion and transfer relative to hiring (Model 7 interactions: b = −.11, p < .01, and b = −.12, p < .05, respectively). Neither interaction with contribution variability is significant (Model 8). Supplementary analyses find bonus-variability effects insignificant at high grade ratios, consistent with task characteristics mattering when structural promotion pressures are weaker.
- Effect-size calculations compare one standard deviation below with one standard deviation above each predictor's mean. For bonus variability, contribution variability, and grade ratio, respectively, the reported promotion-rate increases are 11%, 11%, and 28%, and hiring-rate decreases are 20%, 12%, and 8%; corresponding transfer-rate changes are +3%, −11%, and −22%.
- With “no change” as the comparator (Table 3, Model 1), grade ratio predicts more promotion (b = .16, p < .01) but not less hiring (b = .01, nonsignificant), consistent with incentive pressure to promote rather than simply substituting a better internal candidate for a hire. The supplementary models do not reproduce every significance pattern: contribution variability is nonsignificant for promotion in Model 1 and marginal in Model 2; firm-specific-skill sensitivity is marginally negative for transfers in both models. In Model 2, its interaction with the ratio is negative for promotion and positive for transfer, while the bonus-variability interaction is positive for hiring and nonsignificant for promotion and transfer. These comparisons concern remaining in the same job, not hiring as the base outcome.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends transaction cost reasoning to staffing by shifting attention from ex post governance to ex ante evaluation and the motivational spillovers of promotion decisions. It links job-level performance variability and career structure to the balance between internal and external labor markets, showing how structural promotion pressures can condition the relevance of task characteristics. The weaker and uneven transfer findings also question treating promotion and lateral transfer as equivalent forms of internal mobility.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations could benefit from developing and evaluating workers in jobs with variable performance or many junior workers, where internal mobility is most likely, while attraction and selection capabilities may be more valuable for other jobs. They also suggest that workers may face more difficulty moving across firms into jobs with high grade ratios or highly variable performance, whereas promotion opportunities into jobs with more variable performance may benefit from reduced competition from external hiring.

**Limitations**
The single financial-services firm limits generalizability, and detailed controls do not eliminate omitted-variable concerns in the absence of suitable instruments. Performance ratings are subjective, rater identities are unavailable, and the authors acknowledge that shortcomings of the firm-specific-skills measure could explain its null results, while also proposing managerial underestimation of transfer difficulties as an alternative explanation. The data do not identify individual vacancy reasons, detailed applicant quantity and quality, or several possible influences favoring external hiring.

**Future Research**
The authors call for studies in other organizations, of lateral transfers, of promotions involving substantially different tasks, and of settings such as academia where performance is more externally observable. They propose examining the number and quality of applicants, internal versus external candidate supply, reasons for individual vacancies, and quasi-experimental or instrumental-variable designs. Further work could examine external knowledge needs, prior poor performance, the relative influence of functional areas, and available funds as influences on hiring.
