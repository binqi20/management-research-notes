# Why Do Cooperatives Emerge in a World Dominated by Corporations? The Diffusion of Cooperatives in the U.S. Bio-Ethanol Industry, 1978–2013

**Abstract**
Given the strong economic disincentives that exist, why do cooperatives continue to emerge? And why is it that, in some communities, these cooperatives are collocated with their commercial counterparts, yet, in others, territorial partitioning occurs? In this paper, we develop a community ecology approach that integrates economic and sociological accounts of cooperatives, in an attempt to reconcile these contradictory observations. Using a detailed panel data set for the county-level founding process of cooperatives in the U.S. ethanol industry from 1978 to 2013, consistent with rational economic arguments, we find that the founding rate of cooperatives decreases in the presence of high, local, corporate ethanol production capacity. However, this negative competitive interdependence is attenuated in local communities where: (1) corporations represent a potential threat to the autonomy of local farmers, (2) there is a generally anti-corporate climate, and (3) there is a well-established organizational infrastructure supporting a cooperative ideology. Consistent with sociological theories that emphasize the mobilizing force of ideology, these local conditions spur and facilitate collective action among farmers to establish cooperatives in response to the local diffusion of corporations. We show further that the diffusion of plants owned by big business (oil and agribusiness) in communities characterized by a general anticorporate climate especially promotes greater ideological contestation and the mobilization of resources to form cooperatives.

**Research Question**
Why do cooperatives emerge despite economic disincentives, and why do they coexist locally with corporations in some communities while the two forms are territorially partitioned in others? How do local ideological conditions and corporate ownership identities condition the relationship between corporate ethanol production capacity and cooperative founding?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Lower local corporate ethanol production capacity increases the likelihood of ethanol cooperative founding.
- H2: The negative relationship between local corporate ethanol production capacity and cooperative founding is weaker where corporate ethanol producers can vertically integrate into farming.
- H3: This negative relationship is weaker in counties with an anti-corporate climate.
- H4: This negative relationship is weaker in counties with more established organizational infrastructures supporting cooperative ideology.
- H5a: The attenuating effect of vertical-integration threat in H2 is stronger the more corporate ethanol production is controlled by oil and agribusiness companies.
- H5b: The attenuating effect of anti-corporate climate in H3 is stronger the more corporate ethanol production is controlled by oil and agribusiness companies.
- H5c: The attenuating effect of supportive infrastructure in H4 is stronger the more corporate ethanol production is controlled by oil and agribusiness companies.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Local corporate ethanol production capacity, including ownership-specific capacity of oil and agribusiness companies versus other corporations.
- DV(s): County-level rate of cooperative ethanol plant founding.
- Mediators: No directly tested mediators; the proposed explanation invokes ideological rivalry, affectively laden “hot cognition,” collective mobilization, and resource assembly.
- Moderators: Vertical-integration threat, general anti-corporate climate, and organizational infrastructure supporting cooperative ideology; corporate ownership identity is hypothesized to condition these moderating effects.

Economic accounts predict that corporate ethanol plants provide outlets for farmers’ corn, reducing the need to incur cooperatives’ governance, financing, and collective-action costs. The community ecology argument proposes that threats to farmer autonomy, anti-corporate sentiments, and supportive organizational infrastructure can instead mobilize farmers to establish cooperatives against corporate expansion. Oil and agribusiness owners are proposed to make the opposing corporate identity particularly salient. The formal moderation hypotheses predict attenuation of negative interdependence, without requiring its complete reversal; cognition and mobilization are theoretical explanations rather than directly measured mediation processes.

**Data & Measures**
The archival plant population comprises 365 plants in 36 states entering between January 1978 and May 2013, including 85 cooperative-owned plants in 17 states: 78 incorporated under traditional cooperative statutes and seven under limited cooperative association acts. Sources include Renewable Fuels Association reports, industry analyses, government sources, trade magazines, newspapers, and press releases. The analytical risk set covers 3,127 counties and 444,034 quarterly spells from January 1978 to June 2013, excluding Hawaii and the District of Columbia because key data are unavailable. These plant-population and county-risk-set endpoints are reported separately in the paper. The dependent indicator equals one when a cooperative plant officially begins production in a county-quarter; the county clock resets after each founding. Explanatory variables are lagged six quarters to reflect the reported average 18-month interval from first equity drive to production. Cox proportional hazards models estimate founding hazards with state-clustered Huber–White standard errors; these are observational associations.

Local corporate capacity is calculated by summing the production capacity of operating corporate plants in the focal county and adjacent counties, then taking the logarithm; local cooperative capacity uses the same construction. Vertical-integration threat is a dummy for whether corporate farming is permitted. General anti-corporate climate is proxied by absence of a state right-to-work law, rather than directly measured county sentiment. Supportive organizational infrastructure is proxied by the logarithm of county Farm Bureau tenure in quarters, using founding dates from state business filings. H5 splits local corporate capacity into oil/agribusiness-owned and other corporate capacity; the former category contains 46 plants. Controls include corn price and county corn production, average farm size, livestock, corn processors, county racial diversity (one minus the Herfindahl index of Whites, Blacks, and Others), state ethanol consumption and excise-tax exemptions, governor membership in the Governors’ Ethanol Coalition, gasoline price, minimum efficiency scale (log of mean plant capacity in a quarter), state farmer-cooperative counts, local cooperative capacity, and region dummies.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: corporate capacity has a negative coefficient in Table 2, Model 2 (b = −0.13, p < .05). The H2, H3, and H4 interactions are positive when entered separately (b = 0.26, 0.18, and 0.05, respectively; all p < .01). With all interactions included in Model 6, the vertical-integration interaction is marginal (b = 0.19, p = .055, two-sided), while the climate and infrastructure interactions remain significant (b = 0.10 and 0.04, respectively; both p < .05). Thus, the authors’ support for H2 retains this full-model qualification. Their joint Wald test is significant (χ² = 13.35, df = 3, p = .0039). Farm Bureau tenure has no significant direct association with founding.
- H5b is supported with a qualified ownership contrast: in Table 3, Model 3, the climate interaction is positive for oil/agribusiness capacity (b = 0.24, p < .05) but nonsignificant for other corporate capacity (b = 0.04); the difference between the two interaction coefficients reaches only the 10% significance level. Figure 2D depicts a positive capacity–founding relationship for oil/agribusiness plants in anti-corporate communities. H5a and H5c are not supported because their ownership-specific interactions do not differ significantly. For H5a, the vertical-integration interactions are 0.19 for oil/agribusiness (p < .10) and 0.28 for others (p < .01); for H5c, the infrastructure interactions are 0.08 for oil/agribusiness (nonsignificant) and 0.05 for others (p < .01). A significant component coefficient does not establish the hypothesized between-owner difference.
- In Table 3, Model 1, oil/agribusiness capacity is negatively associated with founding (b = −0.13, p < .01), whereas other corporate capacity is nonsignificant (b = −0.02); their difference is marginal (p = .06). An alternative three-way specification combining total corporate capacity, each context moderator, and the oil/agribusiness share again yields a significant positive interaction only for anti-corporate climate (p < .05). The authors report little change with eight-quarter rather than six-quarter lags, nearly identical results after mean-centering capacity and Farm Bureau tenure, and substantively similar results with additional economic, institutional, and period controls.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study integrates economic necessity and social-movement explanations of cooperative emergence by showing how local ideological conditions condition competitive interdependence between organizational forms. It restores the spatial community to community ecology, emphasizing direct rivalry over both resources and ideology, and identifies corporate identity as a contingent rather than universal source of mobilization. It also argues that regulation shapes organizational diversity by conditioning interdependence between forms, beyond its direct effects on organizational vital rates.

**Practical Implication**
Not reported in paper

**Limitations**
The state-level right-to-work proxy misses potential within-state differences in county anti-corporate sentiment, and Farm Bureau tenure is an imperfect proxy for supportive infrastructure; the authors suggest cumulative cooperative establishments by local bureaus might measure it better. The analysis models cooperative entry in response to corporate prevalence, leaving effects of incumbent cooperatives on corporate vital rates unexamined. It concerns a single industry and cooperative founding rather than a complete account incorporating survival and reversion to corporate ownership.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying how local-context features interact with corporate identity in mobilization and developing finer measures using local anti-corporate activist events, county referendums, or cumulative cooperative establishments by Farm Bureaus. They call for theories of corporate responses to cooperative emergence and research on the findings’ generalizability. They also propose examining whether material versus ideological founding incentives affect cooperative longevity, linking founding and survival to the form’s relative scarcity.
