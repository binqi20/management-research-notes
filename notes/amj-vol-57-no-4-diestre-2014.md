# Toward an Input-Based Perspective on Categorization: Investor Reactions to Chemical Accidents

**Abstract**
We build on the literature on categorization to develop and test a model of input-level spillover effects. Our model predicts that, when one firm suffers an accident with a particular input, investors will punish other users of that input by discounting their stocks, and that the magnitude of this negative spillover can be predicted by the nonresponsible firm’s level of input usage. We also hypothesize that the magnitude of the punishment will be moderated by intermediaries’ assessments of the input: Ex ante regulatory sanctions on the input will amplify negative spillovers, while the presence of input-level associations will weaken these effects. Finally, we predict that similarity between the responsible and nonresponsible firm in terms of two peripheral attributes—input portfolio and geographic location—will amplify the negative effect of input usage. We find strong support for our predictions in an event study that examines the stock market valuations of 270 nonresponsible manufacturing firms triggered by 78 industrial accidents involving a toxic chemical. We highlight our study’s theoretical and empirical contributions to the categorization and spillover literatures.

**Research Question**
Why do accidents with a critical input lead investors to discount nonresponsible firms across industry boundaries, and how do intermediaries’ assessments of that input and similarity to the responsible firm condition these spillovers?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: After an accident with a given input, a nonresponsible firm’s decline in market value will be greater the greater its usage of that input.
- H2: The market discount predicted in H1 will increase with the magnitude of ex ante input-level regulatory sanctions.
- H3: The market discount predicted in H1 will decrease with the presence of an input-level association.
- H4: The market discount predicted in H1 will increase with input-portfolio similarity between the responsible and nonresponsible firms.
- H5: The market discount predicted in H1 will increase with geographic proximity between the responsible and nonresponsible firms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Nonresponsible firm’s usage of the input involved in another firm’s accident.
- DV(s): Nonresponsible firm’s cumulative abnormal stock returns following the accident.
- Mediators: Proposed causal attribution to the input, membership in the category of firms at risk of suffering accidents, and investors’ increased perceptions of accident risk; these processes are not directly measured as mediators.
- Moderators: Ex ante input-level regulatory sanctions, input-level association, input-portfolio similarity, and geographic proximity to the responsible firm.

The responsible firm becomes the prototype of an accident-risk category, and the input perceived to have caused the accident becomes its core attribute; greater usage makes other firms stronger candidates for membership and market discounts. Regulatory sanctions suggest widespread input-related liabilities, whereas associations’ information and services encourage attribution to weaknesses specific to the responsible firm. Similar input portfolios and geographic proximity are theorized to signal, respectively, similar underlying technologies and security practices, reinforcing the perceived relevance of the accident to other input users. These are proposed explanations for observed stock-price reactions, rather than direct observations of investors’ categorization.

**Data & Measures**
The study identified 426 potential accidents through Factiva searches for 1997–2005 and retained 78 industrial accidents involving identifiable regulated toxic chemicals, locations and responsible facilities, with human, material and/or environmental damage. Its nonresponsible sample comprises 270 U.S. public manufacturing firms reporting regulated chemical emissions. Of 21,060 possible firm–accident observations, 13,414 remained after unavailable observations were removed; excluding pairs with any nonresponsible facility in the responsible facility’s four-digit SIC and pairs with specified confounding corporate announcements within five days on either side of the accident left 11,858 observations. Data combine EPA Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) facility records, Dun & Bradstreet parent-company links, CRSP stock returns and Compustat financial information.

DV: CAR is the sum of daily abnormal returns from trading day 0 through +4, where AR = actual stock return − (estimated intercept + estimated market beta × market return). Market-model parameters are estimated by OLS over days −250 through −50 using the value-weighted CRSP index; an accident outside trading hours starts the window on the next trading day. IV: input usage is proxied by prior-year TRI generation of the accident chemical in pounds, weighted by chemical toxicity using reportable-quantity data. Regulatory sanctions equal the total US$ millions of EPA fines associated with the chemical across manufacturing firms during the preceding five years. The ILA measure counts services, from 0 to 4: risk information, dissemination of technical knowledge and best practice, member risk-management support, and nonmember emergency support. Sanctions and ILA scores are averaged across chemicals in multi-chemical accidents.

Input-portfolio similarity first represents each facility by a 347-dimensional vector of chemical emissions and sets coordinates for accident chemicals to zero. Similarity between the responsible facility f and a nonresponsible facility n is C_f C_n′ / sqrt[(C_f C_f′)(C_n C_n′)]; facility similarities are then averaged across the nonresponsible firm using facility-size weights. Geographic proximity is the negative of the facility-size-weighted mean distance, in thousands of miles, between the responsible facility and the nonresponsible firm’s facilities. These measures proxy underlying technology and security-practice similarity; they do not observe either directly.

The archival event-study regressions relate CARs to lagged explanatory variables, with responsible-industry, year and toxicity fixed effects and two-way clustered standard errors at year and nonresponsible-firm levels. Controls cover accident injury and responsible-facility visibility, nonresponsible-firm size and fines, competition and industry chemical usage, shared-state exposure and regulatory stringency, chemical regulatory age and the number of industries using the chemical. Interactions use centered variables. The ILA endogeneity analysis instruments its service score with PubChem molecular complexity, alongside chemical-specific controls; the authors posit that complexity reduces knowledge transfer and association formation without directly affecting CARs. The observed associations and IV specification do not directly test the proposed cognitive mediation.

**Key Findings**
H1 is supported: input usage is negatively associated with nonresponsible firms’ CARs (Model 2 coefficient = −0.03, p < .05). The four moderator variables have no significant main effects in Model 3. H2–H4 are supported in separate interaction models: regulatory sanctions strengthen the negative association (Model 4: −0.09, p < .001), ILA services weaken it (Model 5: +0.42, p < .001), and portfolio similarity strengthens it (Model 6: −1.36, p < .001). Corresponding Model 8 interactions are −0.05 (p < .01), +2.92 (p < .001), and −1.66 (p < .001).

H5 receives unstable support: the proximity interaction is negative but nonsignificant in Model 7 (−0.23), and negative and significant in the full Model 8 (−3.71, p < .001). The authors explicitly decline to treat this as strong evidence for H5, despite the abstract’s broad claim of strong support. The ILA interaction remains positive in the two-stage IV estimation (+2.74, p < .001). The paper also reports similar support using only single-chemical accidents and robustness to three- and seven-day windows; replacing fine values with fine counts supports H2.

Source-internal reporting discrepancies remain: Figure 2’s caption and footnote describe portfolio similarity while its legend names ILA, and Figure 3 reverses those labels. Separately, the responsible-firm diagnostic describes a 2.82% market-value loss among the 43 publicly traded responsible firms, whereas footnote 21 prints average CAR = 2.82% without a minus sign; both give p < .001. These discrepancies are retained without an inferred correction.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends socio-cognitive categorization from output salience toward an audience-specific account in which an attribute’s perceived causal power determines category membership, explaining spillovers across industry and product boundaries. It integrates intermediary assessments and peripheral attributes into that account and suggests that weaker category fit can protect firms when the category is associated with deviant behavior. The evidence supports input-related market spillovers while leaving geographic-proximity moderation less secure.

**Practical Implication**
Managers of firms using potentially hazardous inputs may benefit collectively from encouraging input-level associations that influence investors’ assessments after accidents, even when the benefits accrue to all input users. The authors also emphasize a trade-off between protecting proprietary knowledge and sharing input-management best practices: shared regulatory liabilities can expose even firms with potentially superior practices to spillovers, while knowledge sharing can risk competitive advantage.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on input-based categorization even though heterogeneous investors may simultaneously apply different categorical attributes and logics. It examines immediate market reactions, leaving proactive category manipulation, input removal and the associated redesign costs unexamined. Its outside-intermediary analysis focuses on regulators rather than the wider range of actors that may shape investors’ perceptions.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining multiple categorization attributes and their individual and combined effects, as well as how firms manage investor expectations over longer periods, including the benefits and costs of removing dangerous inputs. They call for research on other outside intermediaries, including nongovernmental organizations and media, and on different kinds of inputs to develop a more general input-based perspective.
