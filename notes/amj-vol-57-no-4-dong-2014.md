# No Pain, No Gain: An Affect-Based Model of Developmental Job Experience and the Buffering Effects of Emotional Intelligence

**Abstract**
Drawing on an overarching framework of transactional stress theory, this study develops and tests an affect-based model of developmental job experience (DJE) that explicates the affective mechanisms through which DJE is associated with both positive and negative individual outcomes—advancement potential and turnover intention— and the buffering role of emotional intelligence (EI) in the affective processes. In a sample of 214 early-career managers, we found DJE to be related to increased advancement potential by boosting employees’ pleasant feelings, but also that it can fail in this regard by increasing their unpleasant feelings. Moreover, whereas it is not surprising that there was a negative relationship between DJE and turnover intention mediated by pleasant feelings, our results also demonstrated a positive relationship via unpleasant feelings, depending on employees’ levels of EI. Specifically, they suggested that DJE was positively related to turnover intention for only low-EI employees, but not for high-EI employees.

**Research Question**
How is developmental job experience (DJE) associated with advancement potential and turnover intention through employees’ pleasant and unpleasant feelings, and does emotional intelligence (EI) buffer the relationships involving unpleasant feelings?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: DJE is positively related to pleasant feelings.
- H2: DJE is positively related to unpleasant feelings.
- H3a: Pleasant feelings are positively related to advancement potential.
- H3b: Pleasant feelings mediate a positive indirect relationship between DJE and advancement potential.
- H4a: Unpleasant feelings are negatively related to advancement potential.
- H4b: Unpleasant feelings mediate a negative indirect relationship between DJE and advancement potential.
- H5a: Pleasant feelings are negatively related to turnover intention.
- H5b: Pleasant feelings mediate a negative indirect relationship between DJE and turnover intention.
- H6a: Unpleasant feelings are positively related to turnover intention.
- H6b: Unpleasant feelings mediate a positive indirect relationship between DJE and turnover intention.
- H7: EI weakens the positive relationship between DJE and unpleasant feelings.
- H8: EI weakens the negative relationship between unpleasant feelings and advancement potential.
- H9: EI weakens the positive relationship between unpleasant feelings and turnover intention.
- H10a: Higher EI weakens the negative indirect relationship between DJE and advancement potential through unpleasant feelings.
- H10b: Higher EI weakens the positive indirect relationship between DJE and turnover intention through unpleasant feelings.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): DJE, defined as the individual’s experience of demanding work assignments offering opportunities for learning and broadening leadership competencies; the focal construct is subjective developmental experience, distinct from organizationally intended job design.
- DV(s): Advancement potential, meaning prospects for effective performance at higher job levels; turnover intention.
- Mediators: Pleasant and unpleasant feelings experienced at work.
- Moderators: Ability-based EI, hypothesized to attenuate the DJE–unpleasant feelings relationship and both unpleasant feelings–outcome relationships, thereby weakening the corresponding indirect relationships.

Transactional stress theory suggests that developmental demands present both opportunities for growth and risks of loss, generating pleasant and unpleasant feelings across situations and time. Pleasant feelings are proposed to facilitate active coping, cognitive flexibility, and engagement, while unpleasant feelings encourage avoidance and constrain these processes, with opposite implications for advancement potential. Mood maintenance/repair and feelings-as-information arguments link pleasant feelings to staying and unpleasant feelings to withdrawal intentions. EI is proposed to help employees perceive, understand, and regulate negative affect and its consequences; the supported buffering relationship concerns turnover intention, while the other proposed EI interactions are unsupported.

**Data & Measures**
The study recruited 357 full-time early-career managers from a part-time MBA program at a large university in the mid-Atlantic United States, during a seven-week management course; 478 nominated supervisors were invited to assess advancement potential. The final matched sample comprised 214 managers who completed the affect, work, and EI surveys and 316 supervisors. Included supervisors reported supervisory responsibility and high familiarity with the manager’s work; multiple supervisor ratings were averaged per manager. Participants averaged 28 years of age and 2.94 years of organizational tenure; 68% were male, with diverse industry backgrounds.

Affect surveys were sent once daily for 27 consecutive business days at randomly selected times between 9 a.m. and 3 p.m.; responses outside a two-hour window were excluded, as were participants with fewer than five affect reports. There were 2,685 retained reports, 5–21 per manager (mean 12.55, SD 3.82). Work and MSCEIT surveys were sent approximately three weeks (15–18 business days) after affect sampling began, with advancement potential obtained through a separate supervisor survey. The course schedule meant that some affect reports preceded DJE measurement and others followed the outcomes; repeated sampling therefore did not establish the hypothesized temporal sequence. Calendar dates are Not reported in paper.

DJE used a shortened Developmental Challenge Profile: unfamiliar responsibilities (five items), creating change (five), high levels of responsibilities (nine), working across boundaries (five), and managing diversity (eight). Managers rated how well each statement described their current job on a 1–5 scale; overall α=.91. Following second-order CFA, items were averaged within each dimension and the five dimension scores served as indicators of latent DJE. Daily affect used six pleasant and six unpleasant adjectives rated 0–4: first, items were averaged separately within each valence for each day; then daily scores were averaged across days within each manager. Pleasant and unpleasant affect reliabilities were .84 and .75, calculated on raw items before aggregation; these state-based measures were distinguished from trait affectivity.

EI used the 141-item MSCEIT Version 2.0, with the publisher’s expert-consensus scoring of perceiving, using, understanding, and managing emotions; the overall score ranged from 0 to 180 (α=.88). Advancement potential used three supervisor-rated promotability items adapted from Thacker and Wayne on a 1–5 agreement scale (α=.76); it measured perceived future potential, not realized promotion. Turnover intention used three self-reported items derived from Cammann and colleagues on a 1–5 agreement scale (α=.84), not observed departures. Controls were age, gender, organizational tenure, positive and negative trait affectivity measured with the 20-item PANAS, and within-person standard deviations of pleasant and unpleasant feelings.

Two-step SEM in Mplus 6.11 tested the measurement and structural models. Pleasant feelings, unpleasant feelings, and EI were single-indicator latent constructs, with loadings fixed to the square root of reliability and residual variances set to (1−reliability)×variable variance. Each DJE dimension was multiplied by EI to create five indicators of the DJE×EI interaction. Indirect effects used 1,000 bootstrap resamples; conditional indirect effects were assessed at EI one standard deviation above and below its mean. Auxiliary analyses examined measurement timing, daily affect nested within managers, and additional EI moderation involving pleasant feelings; the authors characterize the principal design as essentially cross-sectional and unable to verify causality.

**Key Findings**
- The hypothesized model fit was χ²(271)=404.42, p<.001, CFI=.92, TLI=.91, RMSEA=.05, SRMR=.07. Adding direct DJE paths to advancement potential and turnover intention did not significantly improve fit (Δχ²(2)=1.36, p>.05), and both direct paths were nonsignificant; the authors retained the more parsimonious hypothesized model.
- H1 and H2 were supported: DJE was positively related to pleasant feelings (standardized β=.22, p<.01) and unpleasant feelings (β=.17, p<.05). H3a and H4a were supported: pleasant feelings were positively related to advancement potential (β=.21, p<.05), and unpleasant feelings negatively related (β=−.27, p<.01). H5a was supported for pleasant feelings and turnover intention (β=−.21, p<.05); H6a was unsupported for unpleasant feelings and turnover intention (β=.04, p>.05).
- Bootstrapped indirect effects supported H3b (DJE→pleasant feelings→advancement potential: b=.06, 95% CI [.004,.175]), H4b (DJE→unpleasant feelings→advancement potential: b=−.07, CI [−.195,−.011]), and H5b (DJE→pleasant feelings→turnover intention: b=−.17, CI [−.410,−.010]). H6b was unsupported for the overall unpleasant-feelings indirect pathway to turnover intention (b=.03, CI [−.111,.216]).
- H7 and H8 were unsupported: the DJE×EI interaction predicting unpleasant feelings (β=.05) and the unpleasant feelings×EI interaction predicting advancement potential (β=.01) were nonsignificant. H9 was supported: EI attenuated the unpleasant feelings–turnover intention relationship (interaction β=−.15, p<.05). Simple slopes were positive at low EI (β=.24, p<.05) and nonsignificant at high EI (β=−.04, p>.05).
- H10a was unsupported: the unpleasant-feelings indirect effect on advancement potential did not differ significantly between high EI (b=−.06, p<.05) and low EI (b=−.07, p<.05). H10b was supported: the unpleasant-feelings indirect effect on turnover intention was positive at low EI (b=.13, p<.05), whereas the high-EI estimate was b=−.07, with a significant difference between conditions (p<.05). The paper contains a reporting inconsistency: p.1069 calls the high-EI effect nonsignificant and Table 2 leaves it unstarred, but the adjacent prose prints p<.05. Table 2 reports nonsignificant direct and total DJE effects for both outcomes at both EI levels; the positive low-EI result concerns the unpleasant-feelings indirect pathway, not a positive total relationship.
- Auxiliary timing checks found no significant mean differences between affect averaged over the first 15 versus last 12 days; the authors report unchanged model relationships using the last 12 days. Hierarchical models of daily affect also showed positive DJE associations with pleasant feelings (γ=.15, p<.05) and unpleasant feelings (γ=.19, p<.01), and no DJE×EI association with unpleasant feelings (γ=.00, p>.05). All three added EI interactions involving pleasant feelings were nonsignificant, and that extended model had less ideal fit. These checks do not overcome the acknowledged causal limitations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper complements cognitive explanations of developmental experience with an affect-based account in which pleasant and unpleasant feelings carry opposing associations with advancement potential and turnover intention. It develops a balanced view of DJE’s intended benefits and possible costs, identifying EI as a selective buffer of the unpleasant-feelings pathway to turnover intention rather than a buffer of every negative affective process. It also positions DJE as a source of both positive and negative workplace affect and connects EI to coping with workplace stressors.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest supporting employees undertaking developmental assignments through preparatory training, openness and learning orientation, tolerance for errors and risk-taking, and professional, social, and psychological support from supervisors and peers. Organizations may find it valuable to identify high-EI individuals in recruitment and selection, assign them developmental challenges, and develop employees’ emotional awareness, understanding, and regulation through training. They also suggest monitoring assignment allocation and challenge levels over time to avoid developmental overload and its potentially counterproductive affective consequences.

**Limitations**
The sample focuses on early-career managers, who may handle developmental challenges differently from more senior managers. Measuring the key variables within the same time frame and averaging affect reports makes the design essentially cross-sectional, leaving reverse directions possible: positive affect may encourage taking on developmental assignments, and high-potential employees may receive more challenging jobs. Subjective DJE may incompletely reflect intentionally designed developmental jobs, the time frame may be insufficient for skill development, and aggregating affect loses within-person variation.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research across career stages and managerial levels, and experiments or longitudinal studies examining the sequence from intended developmental job design through subjective DJE and affect to outcomes. They propose distinguishing DJE dimensions, using within-person or multilevel designs for daily affect and outcomes, examining discrete emotions, and directly testing the assumed links between affect and proximal psychological processes or distal learning and leadership-skill development. Further work should explain EI’s selective moderation across outcomes and examine other individual and situational moderators, including self-efficacy, of both positive and negative affective processes.
