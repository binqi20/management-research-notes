# Managing to Stay in the Dark: Managerial Self-Efficacy, Ego Defensiveness, and the Aversion to Employee Voice

**Abstract**
Soliciting and incorporating employee voice is essential to organizational performance, yet some managers display a strong aversion to improvement-oriented input from subordinates. To help to explain this maladaptive tendency, we tested the hypothesis that managers with low managerial self-efficacy (that is, low perceived ability to meet the elevated competence expectations associated with managerial roles) seek to minimize voice as a way of compensating for a threatened ego. The results of two studies support this idea. In a field study (Study 1), managers with low managerial self-efficacy were less likely than others to solicit input, leading to lower levels of employee voice. A follow-up experimental study (Study 2) showed that: (a) manipulating low managerial self-efficacy led to voice aversion (that is, decreased voice solicitation, negative evaluations of an employee who spoke up, and reduced implementation of voice); and (b) the observed voice aversion associated with low managerial self-efficacy was driven by ego defensiveness. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings, as well as highlight directions for future research on voice, management, and leadership.

**Research Question**
Why do some managers avoid soliciting and responding positively to improvement-oriented employee voice? The paper examines whether low managerial self-efficacy produces voice aversion through ego defensiveness and whether reduced managerial solicitation helps explain lower employee voice.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Managers with lower managerial self-efficacy are less likely to solicit voice from subordinates than managers with higher managerial self-efficacy.
- H2: Subordinates are less likely to speak up to managers with lower managerial self-efficacy than to managers with higher managerial self-efficacy.
- H3: Managerial solicitation of voice mediates the relationship between managerial self-efficacy and subordinate voice behavior.
- H4: Ego defensiveness explains the relationship between managerial self-efficacy and voice solicitation: lower efficacy evokes defensiveness and reduced solicitation.
- H5a: Managers with lower managerial self-efficacy are less likely to assign positive ratings to subordinates who speak up than managers with higher managerial self-efficacy.
- H5b: Ego defensiveness explains the relationship between managerial self-efficacy and employee denigration: lower efficacy evokes defensiveness and greater denigration.
- H6a: Managers with lower managerial self-efficacy are less likely to implement subordinates' suggestions than managers with higher managerial self-efficacy.
- H6b: Ego defensiveness explains the relationship between managerial self-efficacy and willingness to implement employee voice: lower efficacy evokes defensiveness and reduced willingness.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Managerial self-efficacy, defined as perceived capacity to be effective and influential in one's managerial domain; it is distinct from objectively assessed competence.
- DV(s): Study 1, perceived managerial solicitation and employee voice behavior; Study 2, intended solicitation, denigration of the employee who speaks up, and willingness to implement that employee's suggestions.
- Mediators: Managerial solicitation links self-efficacy to employee voice in Study 1. Ego defensiveness is the proposed psychological mechanism behind managerial voice aversion, tested indirectly through an ego-security intervention in Study 2 rather than through a measured mediator.
- Moderators: Study 2 experimentally varies self-affirmation, expected to buffer the ego threat associated with low managerial self-efficacy.

Managerial roles carry elevated competence expectations that managers internalize. Low perceived managerial efficacy creates a discrepancy between the actual self and these ought-based standards, fostering ego defensiveness. Improvement-oriented voice can then imply criticism of the manager's ability, motivating less solicitation, denigration of the speaker, and reduced willingness to use the suggestion. Reduced solicitation also signals that employee input is unwelcome, discouraging employees from speaking up; affirming self-worth is proposed to interrupt the defensive response.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 uses web surveys of employees and managers, matched with internal HR records from four divisions of a multinational oil and gas exploration and refinery organization. Usable responses came from 54 of 93 managers and 304 of 544 employees; matching yielded 148 employees under 41 managers, an overall response rate of 27.2%. Managers completed Chen, Gully, and Eden's eight-item self-efficacy measure with instructions to answer for their own job and work domain (five-point agreement scale; α = .90). Employees rated managerial solicitation on a new four-item frequency scale (five points; α = .91), covering requests for improvement ideas, prior-job knowledge, task-related knowledge, and potentially useful skills, and reported their own improvement-oriented voice using three Detert and Burris items (five points; α = .81). Controls comprise gender and organizational tenure from HR records, employee pay grade, and employee-rated job satisfaction, transformational leadership, futility of speaking up, turnover intention, and manager effectiveness. Multilevel models account for employees nested under managers, and mediation is assessed using 1,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected confidence intervals; the design is associational. Separate scale-development exercises involved 201 working professionals, 30 faculty/doctoral content raters, and 205 employees of a financial-services organization.

Study 2 recruits 131 adults from varied professions through an online national database maintained by a large West Coast university; 39% held supervisory roles. Participants adopt the role of a commuter-airline manager whose maintenance chief challenges a proposed plan. A randomized 2 × 2 between-subjects design varies managerial self-efficacy through information about prior organizational success or failure and supervisors' assessments of the manager (65 high-efficacy; 66 low-efficacy), and ego security through a writing task (66 self-affirmation; 65 no self-affirmation). Affirmation participants explain why their most important value matters to them; comparison participants explain why their least important value might matter to someone else. A pretest supported the efficacy manipulation, and a separate pretest indicated that affirmation did not alter managerial self-efficacy. Ego threat/defensiveness is not directly measured: the authors infer its role from whether affirmation eliminates the low-efficacy response. Solicitation uses two seven-point items about seeking further advice and encouraging similar voice (r = .56); denigration uses four seven-point competence ratings, reverse-scored (α = .89); willingness to implement uses four seven-point items concerning revising the plan, reconsidering it, taking comments to supervisors, and valuing the comments (α = .80). These outcomes measure scenario responses and intentions. A separate follow-up factor analysis of 201 working adults linked the two solicitation items with Study 1's four items. Neither study reports collection dates or waves, and participant countries are not explicitly identified.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supports H1–H3 in the controlled multilevel models (Table 2; unstandardized coefficients). Managerial self-efficacy positively predicts solicitation in Model 2 (b = .35, SE = .16, p < .05) and employee voice in Model 4 (b = .35, SE = .14, p < .05). In Model 5, solicitation positively predicts voice (b = .31, SE = .08, p < .01), while the efficacy coefficient falls to .23 (SE = .13) and is nonsignificant; the indirect-effect 95% confidence interval is [.02, .26]. However, the zero-order efficacy–solicitation correlation is nonsignificant (r = .08), and the authors explicitly raise statistical suppression as a possible explanation for the stronger controlled relationship.

Study 2 supports the predicted low-efficacy pattern among participants without self-affirmation. Compared with high efficacy, low efficacy reduces solicitation (M = 5.07 versus 5.89; t(64) = 2.61, p = .01), increases denigration (M = 2.99 versus 2.36; t(64) = 2.39, p = .02), and reduces willingness to implement voice (M = 4.88 versus 5.57; t(64) = 2.75, p = .01), supporting H1, H5a, and H6a in this condition. With affirmation, low versus high efficacy differences are nonsignificant for solicitation (5.52 versus 5.48; p = .92), denigration (2.41 versus 2.79; p = .12), and implementation willingness (5.34 versus 5.14; p = .45). The reported efficacy × affirmation interactions are F(1, 130) = 3.80, p = .05 for solicitation; F(1, 130) = 7.99, p = .01 for denigration; and F(1, 130) = 6.06, p = .02 for implementation willingness. The authors interpret this pattern as support for ego defensiveness underlying voice aversion (H4, H5b, H6b); the solicitation interaction lies at the reported .05 boundary. Planned contrasts comparing low efficacy/no affirmation with the other three conditions also support the predicted pattern (p = .03, .02, and .02, respectively). Results retain the same pattern with gender controlled, and supervisory status did not moderate the pattern.

The paper contains unresolved reporting discrepancies. Although the method assigns 66 participants to affirmation and 65 to no affirmation, the corresponding simple contrasts report degrees of freedom of 63 and 64, respectively; the affirmed implementation contrast is printed as t(63) = 0.42, p = .45. Table 3 prints a positive denigration–solicitation correlation (r = .68, p < .01), and negative correlations of high-efficacy coding with solicitation (r = -.16) and implementation willingness (r = -.12), alongside the conditional means reported above. These printed statistics are preserved without correction or an invented reconciliation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts attention from managerial behaviors as predictors of employee voice to the psychological antecedents of those behaviors, linking role-based competence expectations and self-discrepancy to voice aversion. It identifies ego defensiveness as an explanation for why managers who feel less efficacious may avoid potentially helpful input, with experimental self-affirmation evidence supporting that interpretation. It also extends leadership research by treating leaders' feelings about their roles as determinants of leadership behavior rather than examining only leadership's consequences.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that employees can reduce managerial threat by speaking up privately, offering affirming feedback or compliments, and framing improvement ideas to reduce perceived managerial blame or liability. They suggest that executives foster psychological safety and norms of learning from mistakes and seeking feedback, supported by performance evaluations and promotion reviews that incorporate employee participation and support for voice. Organizations should also help managers develop their sense of competence; the specific workplace strategies are proposed implications rather than interventions directly tested in these studies.

**Limitations**
Study 1 is correlational, so reverse causality remains possible: limited employee input might reduce managers' efficacy rather than simply result from it. The authors also flag the high mean and restricted variation in self-efficacy, possible statistical suppression, reliance on employees' perceptions of solicitation and reports of their own voice, and the absence of a field test of ego defensiveness or reactions to already-offered voice. Study 2 provides experimental control but uses a vignette, compromising external validity; its dependent measures concern scenario judgments and willingness rather than observed workplace implementation.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining supportive forms of voice, identifying managers prone to low efficacy, and testing contextual conditions that amplify or mitigate feelings of low managerial self-efficacy, such as unfamiliar organizational/national cultures. They separately propose feedback orientation, leader–subordinate relationship quality, and destructive workplace politics as possible moderators of the relationship between efficacy and voice solicitation. They also call for separating efficacy-related aversion from constraints imposed by organizational policies, participation and change norms, power distance, or a manager's own superior. Further work could investigate alternative social processes, such as low efficacy producing poor decisions that elicit employee criticism and thereby reduce managers' perceived need for additional voice, and how relationships with subordinates alter defensive reactions.
