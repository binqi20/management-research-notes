# Job Titles as Identity Badges: How Self-Reflective Titles Can Reduce Emotional Exhaustion

**Abstract**
Job titles help organizations manage their human capital and have far-reaching implications for employees’ identities. Because titles do not always reflect the unique value that employees bring to their jobs, some organizations have recently experimented with encouraging employees to create their own job titles. To explore the psychological implications of self-reflective job titles, we conducted field research combining inductive qualitative and deductive experimental methods. In Study 1, a qualitative study at the Make-A-Wish Foundation, we were surprised to learn that employees experienced self-reflective job titles as reducing their emotional exhaustion. We triangulated interviews, observations, and archival documents to identify three explanatory mechanisms through which self-reflective job titles may operate: self-verification, psychological safety, and external rapport. In Study 2, a field quasi-experiment within a health care system, we found that employees who created self-reflective job titles experienced less emotional exhaustion five weeks later, whereas employees in two control groups did not. These effects were mediated by increases in self-verification and psychological safety, but not external rapport. Our research suggests that self-reflective job titles can be important vehicles for identity expression and stress reduction, offering meaningful implications for research on job titles, identity, and emotional exhaustion.

**Research Question**
What are the implications of self-reflective job titles for employees’ experiences of work? How might titles that express employees’ unique contributions reduce emotional exhaustion, and through which identity-related mechanisms?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Proposition 1: Self-reflective job titles reduce emotional exhaustion.
- Proposition 2a: Self-verification partially mediates the effect of self-reflective job titles on emotional exhaustion.
- Proposition 2b: Psychological safety partially mediates the effect of self-reflective job titles on emotional exhaustion.
- Proposition 2c: External rapport partially mediates the effect of self-reflective job titles on emotional exhaustion.

**Mechanism Process**
- Self-reflective job titles: self-generated, personalized designations capturing how employees add unique value, used alongside formal titles.
- Identity affirmation: self-verification through having valued aspects of oneself understood and affirmed by others.
- Identity accommodation: psychological safety to express oneself and take interpersonal risks as status barriers diminish.
- Identity utilization: external rapport through smoother, more enjoyable initial interactions with outsiders.
- Proposed outcome: reduced emotional exhaustion, the feeling of lacking energy and being depleted.

The qualitative account proposes that personalized titles help employees reappraise stressful work by directing attention toward its meaning and their distinctive contributions. Identity affirmation can buffer stress; greater psychological safety can ease help-seeking and support; rapport can make external interactions more authentic and less draining. The quasi-experiment supports the self-verification and psychological-safety pathways, but not the external-rapport pathway. The broader argument treats titles as jointly constructed artifacts: organizations legitimize their use while employees customize their content.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 examined one Midwestern U.S. Make-A-Wish chapter with 31 paid staff. Over two years following initial exposure, the researchers collected semistructured interviews lasting 35–115 minutes, 23 hours of nonparticipant observation, and over 100 archival documents; the titles initiative had begun approximately a year and a half before the study. The paper reports 22 interviewed Midwest employees and also describes interviews with the president/CEO, board chair, and affiliated outsiders, without separately specifying the overall interviewee total. Iterative analysis moved from change-initiative themes to open coding of accounts linking titles and exhaustion, six aggregate codes, and three identity-related mechanisms; two authors independently recoded the data, achieving 87% agreement before resolving differences through discussion.

Study 2 was a field quasi-experiment at nine family-practice sites in a southeastern U.S. health-care system. Of 224 potential participants, 169 completed the baseline survey; sites were allocated to titles (three sites, 55 respondents), pure control (four sites, 64), or alternative-workshop control (two sites, 50). Methods describes stratified random site allocation constrained by site size and proximity, with no contact across conditions; individuals were not randomly assigned. Both workshops took place on the same day, lasted one hour over a meal, and provided equivalent contact with managers and researchers: the titles workshop introduced and developed self-reflective titles, whereas the alternative workshop used the Carolina Blue Tree negotiation exercise and discussed shared interests. Pure controls attended no workshop. The follow-up survey was sent five weeks later: 121 responded, but 37 incomplete and eight unmatched surveys left 76 matched employees (31/18/27 by condition); 89% were female.

At both waves, employees self-reported emotional exhaustion using the Maslach Burnout Inventory, self-verification using Wiesenfeld et al.’s scale, psychological safety using Edmondson’s scale, and rapport with patients using adapted Bernieri et al. items. Confirmatory factor analysis supported four distinct factors. Repeated-measures ANOVA and paired tests examined change; hierarchical regressions and 1,000-resample bias-corrected bootstrap intervals tested mediation using change scores, with a robustness analysis predicting posttest exhaustion while controlling pretest exhaustion and entering posttest mediators. Table 4 reports residual degrees of freedom of 64 and 63 in its two model-comparison tests without separately stating regression sample sizes. Open-ended posttest questions examined title use and reactions; these passages describe use after at least six weeks, although the survey procedure specifies five weeks.

Six years after the original MAW study, the authors interviewed four employees—two present since the initiative began and two newer employees—to explore persistence after the original CEO had departed two and a half years earlier. These were qualitative follow-up accounts rather than repeated quantitative outcome measurements.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 generated the three proposed mechanisms rather than testing causal effects. The paper reports that 85% of participants mentioned titles helping them cope with emotional exhaustion; self-verification was discussed by 69% of interviewees; psychological safety was described for 77% of employees; and 85% of participants mentioned external rapport. These percentages retain the paper’s participant/interviewee framing; its overall denominator is not specified separately. Accounts linked titles to personal affirmation, reduced hierarchical barriers, and more enjoyable external interactions. The four later interviews suggested continued use and perceived benefits after the original champion’s departure.

Study 2 supported Proposition 1: the titles group’s mean exhaustion declined from 3.02 to 2.70, t(30) = 2.14, p < .05; neither control group changed significantly. Self-verification increased from 5.38 to 5.69, t(30) = 2.50, p < .05, and psychological safety from 4.71 to 4.97, t(30) = 2.04, p < .05, in the titles group, with no significant changes in either control. External rapport showed no significant change in any condition, and its change was not significantly associated with exhaustion change (β = −.14), leaving Proposition 2c unsupported.

Propositions 2a and 2b received support: increases in self-verification (b = −.21, p < .05) and psychological safety (b = −.46, p < .001) predicted decreases in exhaustion, and the titles-condition coefficient became nonsignificant when both mediators entered. The authors describe partial mediation and report nonzero indirect-effect 95% confidence intervals of [−.01, −.16] and [−.01, −.20], respectively, with endpoint order preserved as printed. The alternative posttest model controlling pretest exhaustion also supported both mediators, with printed intervals [−.01, −.17] and [−.01, −.14]. Reported nonresponse and survey-completion-time checks found no significant differences or associations on the focal variables. Only 61% of titles-group employees reported using their titles; 39% did not.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reframes job titles as potential vehicles for agency, creativity, identity expression, and coping, while retaining formal titles’ coordination and human-resource functions. It proposes an avenue toward optimal distinctiveness through personalized titles that combine differentiation with acceptance, rather than requiring changes in group membership. Its co-construction account connects top-down organizational legitimation with bottom-up identity work, extending approaches to emotional exhaustion that primarily change either individuals or their organizational context.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that leaders can encourage employees to reflect on their distinctive value and express it through personalized titles alongside existing titles, potentially fostering affirmation and psychological safety and reducing emotional exhaustion. They suggest particular usefulness in service encounters requiring rapid relationship-building and memorable, authentic first impressions, while noting that employees selectively disclose titles and that inappropriate titles or unreceptive audiences can undermine professionalism.

**Limitations**
Study 1 relies on participants’ accounts that may reflect lay theories rather than actual causal processes; both studies rely heavily on self-reports, involve human-service organizations and predominantly female samples, and do not establish behavioral or service-recipient outcomes. Study 2 does not randomize individuals, uses a single-blind design, and has lower posttest participation in the pure-control group, leaving potential site differences, researcher influence, and nonresponse concerns despite the reported checks. Positive affect and autonomy were unmeasured alternative mechanisms, leader support and colleagues’ participation may condition the effects, external-rapport nulls remain open to several explanations, and the small qualitative follow-up cannot establish general long-term durability.

**Future Research**
The authors call for behavioral and service-recipient evidence, including patient ratings or video-coded interactions to investigate rapport, and tests in other industries with attention to gender and national-culture differences. They propose comparing serious versus playful titles and other self-expressive artifacts, using double-blind designs and stronger participation incentives, comparing other structured interventions, and examining leader support, organizational climate, and individual versus collective adoption. They also encourage research on reputational harm, manipulative uses and cynical reactions, as well as temporal dynamics and whether creating a new title restores benefits if earlier effects fade.
