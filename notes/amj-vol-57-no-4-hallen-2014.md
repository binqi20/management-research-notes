# How Do Social Defenses Work? A Resource-Dependence Lens on Technology Ventures, Venture Capital Investors, and Corporate Relationships

**Abstract**
Interorganizational relationships offer many potential benefits, but they also expose firms to dangers, such as misappropriation, which pull partners apart. This tension between collaboration and competition is central to tie formation, especially for young technology firms that have both a high need for resources and high appropriability of their own resources. Prior work has examined legal and timing defenses that enable interorganizational ties by such low-power firms; we focus here on social defenses. In a longitudinal study of equity tie formation between young firms and established corporate “sharks,” spanning five technology-based industries and 25 years, we unpack the effects of social defenses and find, intriguingly, that centrally positioned third parties are a particularly powerful social defense and that third-party social defenses are especially significant when more traditional defenses are unavailable. We thus offer the insight that third-party chaperones (central venture capital investors) play a key role in helping young firms to mitigate and navigate vulnerabilities while mobilizing resources.

**Research Question**
How do social defenses supplied by existing venture capital (VC) investors influence young firms’ formation of investment relationships with corporate “sharks”? How do VC centrality and geographical proximity interact with each other and with secrecy and timing defenses?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Entrepreneurs with more central VC investors will form more investment relationships with corporations.
- H2: Entrepreneurs with more geographically local VC investors will form more investment relationships with corporations.
- H3: The more local its VC investors, the stronger the relationship between the centrality of a young firm’s VC investors and the number of corporate investment relationships it forms.
- H4a: Weaker secrecy defenses strengthen the relationship between VC centrality and the number of corporate investment relationships a young firm forms.
- H4b: Weaker timing defenses strengthen the relationship between VC centrality and the number of corporate investment relationships a young firm forms.
- H4c: Weaker secrecy defenses strengthen the relationship between VC proximity and the number of corporate investment relationships a young firm forms.
- H4d: Weaker timing defenses strengthen the relationship between VC proximity and the number of corporate investment relationships a young firm forms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Centrality of existing VC investors in the co-investment network; geographical proximity of existing VC investors to the venture.
- DV(s): Number of corporate venture capital (CVC) investors participating in a venture’s funding round; supplementary analysis examines formation of a particular venture–corporate-investor tie.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper
- Moderators: VC proximity moderates the centrality relationship; secrecy and timing defenses moderate both social-defense relationships. Supplementary dyad analyses examine corporate R&D and same-industry relatedness.

The proposed disciplining mechanism allows low-power ventures to borrow central VC investors’ power: corporations risk losing future syndication opportunities and suffering reputational damage if they misappropriate venture resources. The proposed aligning mechanism relies on nearby VCs’ familiarity and influence to identify mutually suitable partners. Contrary to the original prediction that proximity facilitates corporate ties, the findings prompt a revised account in which nearby VCs steer ventures away from corporate partners and toward other VCs, reducing dependence on corporate resources. Centrality and proximity are proxies for these processes; the analyses do not estimate mediated effects.

**Data & Measures**
The stratified random sample comprises 700 U.S. high-technology ventures, approximately 140 each in biotechnology, electronics, communications, software, and medical products, with initial venture funding during 1979–1995. Funding rounds were tracked until exit or 2003, yielding 4,073 rounds and 18,168 investments, including more than 1,200 corporate investments. VentureXpert records were triangulated with VentureOne and LexisNexis; investor-network data cover 3,622 U.S.-based investors and 163,736 investments during 1974–2003. Contextual evidence includes background interviews, whose total number is not reported, and prior fieldwork following the fundraising of nine ventures.

The round-level DV counts nonfinancial corporations purchasing private equity, including U.S. and foreign corporations but excluding bank/insurance subsidiaries, loans, and public offerings. VC centrality is the maximum eigenvector centrality among all VCs that invested in the venture in any previous round. Centrality is computed from a binary co-investment adjacency matrix over the five-year window ending in the year before the round, scaled annually between 0 and 1; ventures without prior VC investors or whose investors are outside the primary network component receive 0. VC proximity is the inverse of the shortest headquarters distance in miles between the venture and its prior VC investors, calculated from zip-code coordinates; ventures without prior VCs receive 0. Secrecy defense is the percentage of product inventions for which industry R&D managers considered trade secrets effective against appropriation, drawn from the 1994 Carnegie Mellon Survey of Industrial R&D and matched at the three-digit SIC level. Timing defense is the logged investment-round number; an alternative development-stage measure, available for about half the rounds, yielded similar results.

GEE negative-binomial regressions address count overdispersion and repeated rounds within ventures using an exchangeable correlation structure; interaction variables are centered. Controls include inflation-adjusted logged round size, logged venture age, industry manufacturing and marketing intensity, corporate-spinoff background, prior CVC investment, citation-weighted patent applications, industry patent protection, VC funding availability, regional VC richness, and industry/year indicators. Regional VC richness is ln(Σⱼ[1/(dᵢⱼ + 1)] + 1), summing over active U.S. VC investors; this density control is distinct from proximity to an existing investor. The supplementary conditional-logit analysis uses 11,132 dyad observations pairing sampled ventures with public U.S. corporate investors active in the focal industries, including formed and potential unformed ties. Venture-round fixed effects condition this analysis on round-level factors and accepting CVC; centrality and proximity main effects cannot be included because they do not vary within a venture-round. Corporate R&D expenditures proxy technical appropriation ability, while same-industry relatedness proxies market incentives. These observational analyses test associations rather than establishing causal effects of social defenses.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported in Table 2: VC centrality is positively associated with corporate-investor counts in Model 2 (b = .28, p < .01) and the full Model 7 (b = .12, p < .05), although its coefficient is nonsignificant in Model 3. H2 is not supported: proximity is negative but nonsignificant in Model 3 and negative and significant in Model 7 (b = −.41, p < .01). Using Model 7, the authors report that a one-standard-deviation increase in centrality corresponds to 4.2% more corporate investors, whereas an equivalent increase in proximity corresponds to 9.9% fewer.
- H3 is contrary to prediction: centrality × proximity is negative in Model 4 (b = −.72, p < .10) and Model 7 (b = −.71, p < .01). The authors’ plotted comparison indicates that moving from low to high centrality increases expected CVC investors by 16% with very distant VCs but decreases them by 34% with a nearby VC. Thus proximity does not complement centrality in promoting corporate ties.
- H4a–b receive support in the full model: centrality × secrecy is −.05 and centrality × timing is −.38 (both p < .01), indicating greater benefits of centrality when traditional defenses are weak. In Model 5, the secrecy interaction is nonsignificant and the timing interaction is negative (−.25, p < .05). H4c–d are not supported: proximity × secrecy is positive (.05, p < .01), and proximity × timing is positive but marginal (.16, p < .10), in Model 7; both are nonsignificant in Model 6. Proximity’s negative relationship with corporate ties is strongest when secrecy and timing defenses are weak. Figure 2 also shows a negative centrality relationship at very high secrecy or timing defenses, rather than the anticipated flat or slightly positive relationship.
- The dyad analysis qualifies the broad social-defense account. In Table 3 Model 4, corporate R&D and same-industry relatedness are positively associated with tie formation (.08, p < .05; .56, p < .01). Centrality × same-industry relatedness is positive (.83, p < .05), while proximity × same-industry relatedness is negative but marginal (−.93, p < .10). Neither interaction with corporate R&D is significant: the moderation evidence concerns market incentives, not technical appropriation ability.
- Reported checks include Poisson GEE, alternative centrality and proximity measures, and controls addressing regional location and overlap between central and proximate investors. An exploratory analysis of adding another VC investor yields a positive centrality–proximity interaction reported at p < .10. Together with contextual interviews, this is consistent with the revised aligning interpretation that nearby VCs direct ventures toward VC rather than CVC partners; it is not a direct mediation test.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends resource-dependence theory to the point of tie formation, showing how low-power ventures can draw on third parties to manage anticipated power imbalances before entering relationships. It distinguishes disciplining through central investors’ reputational reach from aligning through nearby investors’ influence, with the latter potentially redirecting ventures away from corporate resources. It also connects social defenses with secrecy and timing defenses, suggesting that central third-party ties can be especially valuable to ventures lacking traditional protection rather than simply amplifying advantages held by already well-protected firms.

**Practical Implication**
For young technology firms, the authors’ implication is that existing VC ties may help them engage corporate partners even when secrecy and timing defenses are weak. Nearby VC investors may instead reinforce preferences for other VC partners, constraining corporate engagement; the two forms of social defense therefore have different implications for resource mobilization.

**Limitations**
The study examines VC ties as one type of social defense, although these investments represent only part of a young firm’s possible interorganizational relationships. It examines relationship formation rather than the comparative efficacy of defenses in producing high-performing relationships.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining other relationships, including strategic alliances and industry consortia, whose different network structures may provide other forms of defense. They also call for comparison of defenses in enabling high-performing ties, including whether relatively exploratory corporate ties contribute to breakthrough innovation. Further work could examine when disciplining or aligning dominates, including uncertainty about repeated interaction, and investigate these tactics in other low-power settings such as small suppliers dealing with Walmart or Amazon and nonprofits interacting with major donors.
