# The Stigma of Affirmative Action: A Stereotyping-Based Theory and Meta-Analytic Test of the Consequences for Performance

**Abstract**
Affirmative action plans (AAPs) are designed to facilitate workplace success for members of the groups they target (e.g., women, ethnic minorities), yet may have the ironic effect of stigmatizing AAP targets and, in turn, decreasing their performance outcomes. Prior work has focused on the stigma of incompetence as the primary mechanism that links AAPs to performance; however, the broader social psychological literature suggests that additional mechanisms may also play a role. We use stereotyping theories to develop a more comprehensive model of the pathways through which AAPs limit targets’ performance outcomes. Drawing from the stereotype content model, we propose that the negative effect of AAPs on others’ evaluations of targets’ performance is driven by perceptions of incompetence and low warmth. Drawing from stereotype threat theory, we propose that the negative effect of AAPs on targets’ self-evaluated and objective performance is driven by perceptions of low self-competence, negative state affect, and perceived stereotyping by others. Meta-analytic path analyses support our hypotheses. Our theory and findings demonstrate that multiple mechanisms explain the negative consequences of AAPs for targets’ performance outcomes, highlight differences in reactions to AAP targets by others versus the self, and provide insight into preventing the unintended negative effects of AAPs.

**Research Question**
Through which mechanisms do affirmative action plans negatively affect others’ evaluations of targets’ performance and targets’ self-evaluated and objective performance? Do stereotyping theories explain these consequences more comprehensively than accounts centered on incompetence, discounting, and self-doubt?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on others’ evaluations of targets’ performance through lower perceived competence.
- H2: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on others’ evaluations of targets’ performance through lower perceived warmth.
- H3a–b: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on targets’ (a) self-evaluated and (b) objective performance through lower perceived self-competence.
- H4a–b: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on targets’ (a) self-evaluated and (b) objective performance through more negative state affect.
- H5a–b: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on targets’ (a) self-evaluated and (b) objective performance through increased perceived stereotyping by others and, subsequently, lower perceived self-competence.
- H6a–b: AAP presence has an indirect negative effect on targets’ (a) self-evaluated and (b) objective performance through increased perceived stereotyping by others and, subsequently, more negative state affect.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Extent of AAP presence, including experimental comparisons of presence versus absence or stronger versus weaker AAPs.
- DV(s): Others’ performance evaluations of AAP targets; targets’ self-evaluated and objective performance.
- Mediators: In the other-driven model, perceived competence and warmth; in the self-driven model, perceived self-competence, state affect, and perceived stereotyping by others, with stereotyping preceding competence and affect in the serial pathways.
- Moderators: No formal moderator hypotheses; post-hoc competence comparisons examine target-group chronic stereotyping, AAP type, and laboratory versus field setting.

The stereotype content model suggests that AAPs signal targets’ low status and increase their ability to compete for resources, prompting others to perceive them as less competent and less warm and consequently evaluate their performance less favorably. Stereotype threat theory suggests that AAPs generate doubts about self-competence and negative affect both directly and through targets’ expectations of being stereotyped by others. The proposed self-regulatory response to negative cognitions and affect consumes cognitive resources and may impair performance. These are theorized pathways: the meta-analysis supports several indirect relationships but does not definitively establish causal ordering among the endogenous variables.

**Data & Measures**
The search used research databases, conference programs, manual journal searches, and requests for unpublished work. It included papers available through the end of 2011, with no database-search start date; manual searches covered 1980–2011. Of 85 potentially relevant manuscripts, 35 met the inclusion criteria and were reported to contain 45 independent samples and 6,432 individuals. The paper subsequently reports excluding one outlier study that asked White participants to imagine they were Black, without restating corpus totals; a second potential outlier was retained, and excluding it did not alter statistical conclusions. These counts describe studies and participants, not a common longitudinal observation window.

For both models, AAP presence was assessed through participants’ reports about their organizations in field studies or manipulated in laboratory studies. The other-driven model pools competence ratings covering ability and agency, warmth ratings such as sincerity and likability, and evaluations of actual or expected task/career performance. Table 4 reports k=20/N=2,791 for competence, k=5/N=686 for warmth, and k=13/N=1,995 for performance evaluations. The self-driven model includes anticipated judgments of incompetence by others, perceived self-competence, general state affect, self-evaluations of actual or expected performance, and objective task performance; corresponding k/N values are 6/1,060, 13/2,009, 9/1,204, 7/1,017, and 6/1,122. State affect is treated as unidimensional, with higher scores indicating positive affect and lower scores negative affect.

Effect sizes were converted to correlations and corrected for unreliability; missing reliabilities were replaced by the average for that variable, and AAP manipulations were assigned perfect reliability. When one sample contributed multiple effects for the same outcome, correlations were transformed to z-scores, averaged, and back-transformed to preserve sample independence. Hunter–Schmidt random-effects estimates and corrected intercorrelations were used in separate path models, with harmonic sample sizes of 589 for others’ reactions and 301 for self-reactions. Indirect effects were calculated by multiplying their component paths and tested with delta-method standard errors; individual observations were unavailable for bootstrapping. Competence and warmth were allowed to covary in the other-driven models; self-competence and affect, and the two performance outcomes, were allowed to covary in the self-driven models. Table 4 defines its 95% confidence intervals around uncorrected r and its 80% credibility intervals around corrected ρ; the results prose places the confidence intervals beside ρ.

**Key Findings**
- Overall estimates: Table 4 reports negative corrected correlations between AAPs and others’ ratings of competence (ρ=−.27), warmth (ρ=−.28), and performance (ρ=−.23); the prose on p. 975 prints the last value as “−23.” For targets’ self-reactions, AAPs correlate positively with perceived stereotyping (ρ=.26) and negatively with self-competence (ρ=−.22), state affect coded toward positivity (ρ=−.28), self-evaluated performance (ρ=−.32), and objective performance (ρ=−.19). All corresponding overall confidence intervals in Table 4 exclude zero, although significant Q statistics indicate between-study heterogeneity.
- Others’ reactions: H1 and H2 are supported. The standardized indirect effects on performance evaluations through competence and warmth are β=−.10 and β=−.12, respectively (both p<.01), with total indirect β=−.21 (p<.01). The hypothesized model fits well (CFI=1.00, RMSEA=.00, SRMR=.01); direct-effects and competence-only alternatives fit poorly. In the partial-mediation regression, the remaining direct AAP association is nonsignificant (β=−.02), supporting adoption of the full-mediation model.
- Self-reactions, hypothesized model: H3a/b are supported through self-competence (self-evaluated β=−.06; objective β=−.04; both p<.05). H4a is supported through state affect (β=−.06, p<.01), but H4b is not (objective β=.00, nonsignificant). H5a/b are supported through stereotyping and self-competence (β=−.04/−.02, both p<.01). H6a is supported through stereotyping and affect (β=−.02, p<.01), but H6b is not (objective β=.00, nonsignificant). The state-affect path is significant for self-evaluated performance (β=.30, p<.01), but not objective performance (β=.02). Thus, the affect pathways receive outcome-specific support.
- Self-reactions, final model: The hypothesized model’s RMSEA=.09 exceeds the stated .06 threshold. Adding direct AAP paths to both performance outcomes improves fit (CFI=1.00, RMSEA=.00, SRMR=.01; Δχ²(2)=13.28, p<.01), whereas the direct-effects-only and competence-only alternatives fit poorly. The adopted partial-mediation model retains negative direct associations with self-evaluated performance (β=−.16) and objective performance (β=−.14), both p<.01; directions and significance of the remaining paths and indirect effects are unchanged. Final total indirect effects are −.16 and −.05, respectively, and the authors’ confidence-interval comparisons find no significant difference between direct and total indirect effect magnitudes for either outcome.
- Post-hoc boundaries: Moderator comparisons concern competence only. Among self-reaction studies, effects do not differ significantly between chronically negatively stereotyped groups and other target groups. Effects also do not differ significantly across the tested AAP types—strong preferential, weak preferential, and generic for others’ reactions; strong preferential and generic for self-reactions—or between laboratory and field settings in either model. These null comparisons do not establish that every subgroup effect is significant: the generic-AAP self-competence interval includes zero. There are insufficient studies for opportunity-enhancement AAPs or self-reactions to weak preferential treatment, and insufficient data to test these moderators for other outcomes.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends incompetence-centered explanations by integrating the stereotype content model and stereotype threat theory into models of other-driven and self-driven AAP stigmatization, identifying warmth, affect, and perceived stereotyping as additional pathways. It presents AAP targets as a situationally defined group that can be stereotyped as low in both competence and warmth, and extends stereotype-threat reasoning to workplace policies and groups with permeable membership boundaries. Differences between other-driven and self-driven mechanisms remain suggestive because their distinctive mechanisms could not be tested across both models.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should address both competence stereotypes and perceptions that AAP targets threaten non-targets’ interests, potentially by explaining how increased diversity can benefit the organization and its members. Targets need to know both that they are qualified and that others know their qualifications, addressing self-doubt and anticipated stereotyping. The authors favor implementing AAPs while improving their effectiveness over eliminating them, emphasizing that performance is only one outcome and that representation and longer-term reductions in discrimination also matter.

**Limitations**
Although many included studies manipulated AAPs, the causal order among mediators and performance outcomes remains uncertain, and existing studies prevented simultaneous testing of the other-driven and self-driven models. Some estimates rely on few studies; publication bias could inflate estimates, although the authors consider their generally large fail-safe counts reassuring. Residual direct associations in the self-driven model indicate omitted mechanisms, and the nonsignificant affect–objective-performance relationship limits support for the proposed affect pathways.

**Future Research**
The authors propose testing physiological anxiety and other omitted mechanisms linking AAPs to performance, and examining whether affect has nonlinear effects or matters particularly for challenging tasks. They also suggest testing whether organizational learning climates can prevent detrimental performance effects. Integrating identity-management research could examine whether AAP-related stigma prompts targets to disassociate from their organizational identity and thereby affects workplace outcomes.
