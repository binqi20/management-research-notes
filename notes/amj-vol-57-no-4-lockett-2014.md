# The Influence of Social Position on Sensemaking about Organizational Change

**Abstract**

Traditionally, scholars have examined the influence of actors’ sensemaking on context; in this paper, we explore the reverse. Employing Bourdieu’s theory of practice we explore how actors’ unique contexts, as encapsulated by their social positions, provide the important “raw materials” for their sensemaking about organizational change. Drawing on a case study of three focal actors, located in different social positions in the National Health Service in England, but tasked with enacting a common organizational change, we explore how actors’ capital endowments and dispositions shape their sensemaking about organizational change. We conclude by developing a theoretical model of the influence of social position on sensemaking about organizational change and discuss the practical implications of paying closer attention to the social positions of actors engaged as change agents.

**Research Question**

How do individual actors’ social positions, encompassing their capital endowments and dispositions, shape their sensemaking about a common organizational change? Why do actors construct different opportunities, identify different problems, and develop different visions of change?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

The following hypotheses emerge from the qualitative analysis and summarize the authors’ theorizing; they are not reported as statistically tested predictions.

- H1a: Actors’ cultural capital, beyond economic and social capital, shapes their sensemaking about opportunity construction.
- H1b: Cultural capital operates through profession-centrism in shaping sensemaking about opportunity construction.
- H2: The more an actor’s cultural capital is privileged by existing organizational schemata, the more likely profession-centrism is to promote sensemaking about change that does not disrupt those schemata.
- H3: Greater heterogeneity of social capital makes allocentrism more likely, in the sense of awareness and understanding of others’ interests and perspectives.
- H4: The more cultural capital is privileged by existing organizational schemata, the less likely allocentrism is, in the sense of recognizing that enacting change depends on others’ thoughts and actions.
- H5a: Greater allocentrism predicts higher engagement in sensemaking about opportunity problematizing.
- H5b: Higher levels of sensemaking about opportunity problematizing lead to lower levels of conflict in sensemaking about organizational change.

**Mechanism Process**

- Social position: Configurations of economic capital, cultural capital, and social capital, including professional knowledge/status and historical relationships within and across professional groups.
- Dispositions: Profession-centrism orients actors toward their own inter- and intraprofessional group’s interests; allocentrism encompasses awareness of others’ perspectives and recognition that action depends on others. These dispositions are not mutually exclusive.
- Sensemaking process: Opportunity construction and opportunity problematizing shape the vision of change, which may reproduce or reconfigure existing organizational schemata.

In the authors’ model, cultural capital shapes profession-centrism through socialization and professional status competition, orienting how actors construct opportunities. Heterogeneous social capital promotes awareness of other groups, while the privilege associated with cultural capital shapes perceived autonomy and dependence on others; together they shape allocentrism. Allocentrism influences engagement in opportunity problematizing, helping explain why an initial opportunity can translate into a vision directly or be scaled back. These are theoretically interpreted processes from the cases, rather than estimated causal effects.

**Data & Measures**

The study follows NHS England pilots intended to distribute genetics knowledge beyond tertiary care and integrate referrals across primary, secondary, and tertiary care. Stage 1 (2004–05) interviewed 21 stakeholders across seven pilots; Stage 2 (2005–06) theoretically selected three cases differing in social position and conducted 38 interviews with stakeholders including clinicians, nurses, managers, and commissioners. Interviews were semi-structured, lasted one to two hours, and were recorded and fully transcribed. Government policy documents, pilot bids, strategy papers, meeting minutes, and over 70 hours of site-specific and program-wide observations supplemented the interviews. The focal actors were Ruth, a research-active tertiary-care doctor; Mark, a practice-facing secondary-care doctor; and Florence, a senior primary-care nurse who had moved into a commissioning role. The interview counts do not establish a total number of unique participants across stages.

Capital categories were predetermined from Bourdieu’s theory: economic capital concerned control of commissioning decisions and revenue streams; cultural capital concerned professional knowledge and inter- and intraprofessional status; social capital concerned relationships spanning professional boundaries. Other first-order codes were induced, consolidated across cases, and organized through axial coding into theoretical categories and aggregate dimensions. Profession-centrism captured the concerns of the actor’s professional group; allocentrism captured awareness of others’ interests and perceived independence from their influence. Opportunity construction concerned reasons for engaging and perceived opportunities; opportunity problematizing concerned anticipated difficulties; visions were assessed against integration of care pathways and distribution of genetics knowledge. Iteration between data and theory and within- and cross-case comparisons developed the process model; source and analyst triangulation challenged interpretations.

**Key Findings**

- Ruth combined high-status, knowledge-producing cultural capital with predominantly homogeneous professional ties. Her research-oriented profession-centrism and low allocentrism framed the pilot as a test of a telephone service, with little attention to organizational engagement problems. Her vision connected primary and tertiary care more efficiently but retained genetics knowledge in tertiary care and largely reproduced existing schemata.
- Mark combined practice-oriented medical capital with heterogeneous inter- and intraprofessional ties. He recognized others’ interests and the need for their support, while believing he had sufficient standing to promote change. His vision integrated primary, secondary, and tertiary care and distributed genetics knowledge across boundaries; negotiation and networking helped address implementation problems.
- Florence’s primary-care nursing and commissioning background oriented her toward public health. Her heterogeneous ties were concentrated within primary care; she recognized others’ perspectives but perceived limited power over doctors. She scaled back her ambition to a primary-care family-history service with traditional referrals to tertiary care, largely reproducing existing schemata rather than establishing an integrated pathway or distributing genetics knowledge.

The postscript reports that Mark’s pilot had the most referrals and was deemed most cost-effective; Florence’s had under one fifth of Mark’s referrals and was least cost-effective. Both used 45-minute face-to-face consultations; Ruth’s 15-minute telephone service was initially less cost-effective than Mark’s but more than Florence’s. As referrals increased, Ruth’s service became 40% less costly than face-to-face consultations at a neighboring genetics center, enabling continued funding. Its applicability beyond cancer-genetics risk assessment led to extension into other clinical areas. Mark secured regional continuation funding, and a nurse succeeded him as lead; Florence obtained a further 12 months of funding, with longer-term uncertainty. These outcomes accompany the qualitative model rather than statistical tests of the emergent hypotheses.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper treats actors’ individual contexts as antecedents of sensemaking, explaining how multidimensional social positions supply its raw materials rather than considering only how sensemaking changes context. It distinguishes cultural capital’s role in opportunity construction through profession-centrism from the joint role of cultural and social capital in opportunity problematizing through allocentrism. This account highlights differences within as well as between professions and explains why awareness of others can support negotiated change or a more conservative vision, depending on perceived capacity to act.

**Practical Implication**

Senior management and policymakers should attend to change agents’ social positions and look beyond actors at the apex of the medical hierarchy when seeking new organizational schemata; the authors identify practice-facing doctors below that apex as promising change agents. For elite actors, they suggest encouraging allocentrism through exposure to other organizational and professional perspectives, including work in multidisciplinary teams. Such exposure may improve understanding of implementation problems and encourage more consensual sensemaking.

**Limitations**

The healthcare setting raises questions about generalizability across contexts; the authors identify other professionalized organizations and fields with significant power differentials as especially relevant settings for assessing transferability. They acknowledge that their case accounts are one of many possible interpretations and use triangulation across data types and analysts to challenge those interpretations.

**Future Research**

Examine the recursive relationship between actors’ contexts and sensemaking, with dispositions both structured by context and structuring it. The authors propose using Bourdieu’s concept of position taking to examine strategies and actions that adjust a field’s balance of power, and encourage further empirical studies to assess generalizability beyond health care, particularly in professionalized organizations and fields with substantial power differences.
