# Front-Stage and Backstage Convening: The Transition from Opposition to Mutualistic Coexistence in Organizational Philanthropy

**Abstract**
Actors who support dissimilar institutional models can overcome conflict and move toward mutually beneficial coexistence. To see how, we studied the emergence of venture philanthropy, a rationalized approach to organizational philanthropy in Europe. Our analysis leverages multiple sources of data and focuses on field-configuring events as settings for interactions. We show how convening—bringing together dissimilar actors—in different types of events creates relational spaces for negotiation over institutional models, their practices, and their underlying assumptions. Front-stage interactions in public spaces are important in making models accessible to a broad audience, whereas backstage interactions in protected spaces allow models to be deconstructed. Our findings show that the interplay between front stage and backstage enables the reframing of institutional models by refining the constituent practices, which neutralizes opposition and facilitates joint courses of action. Our results contrast with popular accounts of competing institutional logics, advance organizational research on the role of events in field trajectories, and expose the collective rationalization of giving.

**Research Question**
How can opposition between dissimilar institutional models evolve into mutualistic coexistence, in which each model persists and benefits from interaction with the other? The study examines the sources of conflict, the transition process, and the foundations of mutualism between venture philanthropy (VP) and traditional philanthropy (TP) in Europe.

**Mechanism Process**
- Institutional models: VP initially prescribed an openly rationalized approach emphasizing investment, organizational capacity, engagement, and explicit returns; TP emphasized grant-making and comparatively implicit reciprocity.
- Initial opposition: Separate professional strongholds, alienating financial terminology, direct transposition of venture-capital practices, and professional hubris reinforced conflict.
- Front-stage convening: Public annual conferences brought dissimilar actors together and made VP accessible, while participants generally retained the scripts of their professional backgrounds and institutional models.
- Backstage convening: Formally organized, restricted-access workshops provided protected spaces to question and deconstruct VP into specific practices, share subjective experiences, and experiment across institutional divides.
- Recursive transition: Overlapping participants and communication channels connected backstage refinement of practices with front-stage reframing of VP, enabling joint projects, common methods, and shared aspirations.

Convening creates relational spaces in which actors negotiate models, practices, and underlying assumptions. Workshops shifted discussion from the ideological merits of VP to practical questions about how to give; confidentiality helped participants disclose difficulties that they were reluctant to expose publicly. Practice refinements informed public reframing of VP as an additional tool in philanthropy, gradually neutralizing opposition. The authors regard front-stage accessibility as necessary but insufficient for mutualistic coexistence; backstage deconstruction and the recursive interplay between the two settings were critical in this case.

**Data & Measures**
The single case traces European VP from the early 2000s through 2012. The authors observed seven EVPA annual conferences in 2006–2012 and fourteen two-day workshops in 2007–2012, recording field notes and verbatim workshop notes. Initial semi-structured interviews involved 12 people and were audiotaped and transcribed; 124 additional formal and informal interviews occurred during primary fieldwork, followed by conversations with approximately 300 informants during immersion. These counts describe different forms of participation and are not a total of unique respondents. Quarterly EVPA newsletters from 2004–2012 served as a proxy for discourse about VP; archival materials included strategic plans, member profiles, news articles, event programs, reports, minutes, and participant lists. One author subsequently worked for EVPA; the authors state that core data collection and analysis preceded this insider role.

Analysis used preliminary observation-based codes to guide discourse analysis of newsletters covering 2004–2008. Four random newsletter sections were coded in Atlas.ti, the coding system was revised, and the entire newsletter text was then coded. Axial coding grouped codes into actors, practices, and reasons for giving, supporting historical sequences of action. Event-level analysis compared who convened and attended events, motives, locations, topics, and interactions; emerging mechanisms were cross-checked against first-order codes and documentary discourse. Front-stage and backstage interactions are qualitative process categories, and mutualism is evidenced through forms of collaboration rather than a numerical outcome scale.

The paper's reporting differs across passages: prose dates primary fieldwork to 2006–2010, whereas Table 2 labels it 2006–2009 and also dates workshop observations to 2006–2009. Prose lists overlapping roles among the 12 initial interviewees as four EVPA founders, eight VP directors, two TP directors, and one expert; Table 2 instead lists three founders, six VP directors, two TP directors, and one expert. Workshops are described as typically involving 20–40 people, whereas Table 3b records attendance from 21 to 80. These differences are retained without inferring a reconciliation.

**Key Findings**
- Early professional exclusivity and mutual claims of superiority reinforced opposition. Open conferences broadened participation and made VP intelligible across groups, but public discussion generally remained aligned with the institutional models participants represented.
- Protected workshops allowed VP advocates to acknowledge limitations and TP advocates to explore unfamiliar practices without publicly committing to the model. Discussions shifted toward how to give, facilitating experimentation and refinement rather than wholesale transplantation of venture-capital methods.
- Refinements included reconsidering board seats and burdens imposed on investees, and recognizing that monetizing social outcomes was not always desirable or necessary. Communication between workshops and conferences supported reframing VP as a complementary tool rather than a replacement for TP.
- Three forms of mutualistic collaboration emerged: joint projects combining complementary practices through a division of labor; new/common methods, including socially contingent remuneration and mutual due diligence; and shared aspirations around regions, sectors, and social-enterprise development. Both models persisted and benefited, while rationalized practices became more widely accepted within TP.
- Figure 1 summarizes a gradual transition from opposition through neutralizing differences to mutualistic coexistence, driven by recurrent front-stage and backstage interactions. This is a qualitative process account of one trajectory, not evidence that convening inevitably produces mutualism; the authors explicitly identify mutualistic relationships as a potential rather than inevitable outcome.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study advances an interactionist account of institutional field trajectories by showing how situated negotiation can loosen the association between practices and particular professional groups, permitting mutualistic coexistence rather than replacement or sustained contestation. It treats convening as a relational mechanism and events as endogenously generated settings for recursive, gradual change, distinguishing the roles of public and protected interactions. For organizational philanthropy, it identifies collective rationalization through interaction among dissimilar resource providers rather than explaining rationalization solely through professionalization or the spread of business practices.

**Limitations**
The case does not encompass all actors in European organizational philanthropy: it centers on VP-promoting organizations and EVPA, together with TP organizations that joined in. It focuses on horizontal relationships, and the authors acknowledge that Europe's cultural and social context may have affected the observed trajectory. Their backstage construct concerns formally convened protected spaces; informal behind-the-scenes interactions such as rumors and gossip are outside the analysis.

**Future Research**
Comparative work in the United States and Asia could examine cultural context and differences such as the absence of an EVPA-like central field actor in the United States. Including grantees and investees could deepen analysis of reciprocity and competitive dynamics, while studying legislators and the public sector could clarify power in vertical relationships. The authors also speculate about applicability to virtual settings and expect ongoing research on field emergence to enable direct comparisons across time and settings, expecting greater contestation and a larger role for institutional entrepreneurship where practices and purposes are less settled.
