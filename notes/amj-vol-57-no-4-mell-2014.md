# The Catalyst Effect: The Impact of Transactive Memory System Structure on Team Performance

**Abstract**
Research on transactive memory systems (TMSs) implicitly assumes that metaknowledge (i.e., the “knowledge of who knows what”) is uniformly distributed among team members. Relaxing this assumption results in a more realistic notion of team cognition in which the distribution of metaknowledge can take different forms. Demonstrating the importance of this conceptual shift, we compare teams in which metaknowledge is concentrated within one central member (a centralized TMS structure) with teams in which metaknowledge is distributed evenly among the members (a decentralized TMS structure). We predict that centralized metaknowledge can give teams a performance advantage over decentralized metaknowledge, because centralized metaknowledge can allow the central member to function as a catalyst for information exchange and integration. We propose this catalyst effect to be contingent on the extent to which the distribution of task information among members poses high coordination demands to effectively integrate members’ knowledge. In a laboratory team decision-making experiment (n ⴝ 112), we found the predicted interaction effect between TMS structure and the distribution of task information. Furthermore, the experiment supported our hypotheses about the mediating role of the transactive retrieval process and the ensuing team information elaboration.

**Research Question**
How does the distribution of metaknowledge among team members affect team information processing and performance when average metaknowledge is held constant? Does concentrating metaknowledge in a central member improve performance through transactive retrieval and information elaboration, particularly when interdependent task information is distributed across different members?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Teams with a centralized TMS structure perform better than teams with a decentralized TMS structure when interdependent task information has a disconnected distribution, but not when it has a connected distribution.
- H2: The interaction between TMS structure and the distribution of interdependent task information affects team performance sequentially through (a) transactive retrieval and (b) information elaboration.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): TMS centralization—the disparity in members’ metaknowledge levels, comparing concentration in one member with a more even distribution at an equal team total.
- DV(s): Team performance, operationalized as the quality of collective product-ranking decisions.
- Mediators: Transactive retrieval (communication seeking specific information from teammates), followed by information elaboration (exchange, discussion, and integration of task-relevant information).
- Moderators: Connected versus disconnected distribution of interdependent task information; disconnected information requires integration across members and imposes greater expertise-coordination demands.

A central member’s relatively complete view of the team’s knowledge resources should encourage targeted information seeking and provide an example that other members can follow. Such retrieval makes relevant information available and promotes its integration. The catalyst role is particularly valuable under disconnected information, because members cannot readily recognize the meaning of their own information or integrate it independently; under connected information, members can more readily integrate their own complete information sets. The experiment tests the team-level consequences of these configurations, with observed retrieval and elaboration as sequential mediators.

**Data & Measures**
The single laboratory experiment recruited 372 students at a Dutch university; 93% studied business or economics. Groups with a majority of non-business students were excluded, while 21 groups containing one such student were retained; three groups were dropped for insufficient English and two for procedural errors. The final sample comprised 112 three-person teams, with 26–29 teams per experimental cell. Groups were randomly assigned to conditions and members to roles. Calendar dates are not reported. Participants had up to 25 minutes to read individually, followed by team discussion averaging 15.2 minutes (range 11.2–18).

Teams acted as consultants ranking five food-product innovations for each of two clients. Each choice set contained 25 critical information items, cross-classified by five products and five functional categories. In the connected condition, each member received all functional information for one or two products; in the disconnected condition, each received one or two functional categories spanning all products. Written instructions and a seating sketch allocated five metaknowledge units per team: all five to one member in the centralized condition, versus one or two to each member in the decentralized condition. Team-average metaknowledge and metaknowledge sharedness were held constant.

Performance was scored by summing the deviations of the ten product rankings from their correct positions and subtracting that total from 24; higher scores indicate better decisions. Transactive retrieval was the frequency of questions seeking information in a specified knowledge domain or information complementary to the asker’s own knowledge; general invitations to participate and preference questions were excluded. Information elaboration assigned each of the 50 critical items its highest observed rating: 0 if unmentioned, 1 if mentioned, 2 if acknowledged or supplied in response to a question without further discussion, 3 if responded to with a clarifying question, 4 if used to draw a conclusion without explicit integration, and 5 if integrated with another item. Scores were summed (possible range 0–250); integration by one member and across members both earned 5, while silently integrated items not recounted received 0. A blinded coder rated all 112 recordings and an independent second coder rated 24; ICC1/ICC2 were .74/.85 for retrieval and .93/.97 for elaboration.

Factorial ANOVA and planned contrasts tested performance differences. Serial mediated-moderation regressions used the interaction as the focal predictor and contrast-coded conditions as covariates (centralized and disconnected coded 1). The indirect effect was the product of the interaction-to-retrieval, retrieval-to-elaboration, and elaboration-to-performance paths, tested with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and a bias-corrected confidence interval.

**Key Findings**
H1 was supported: TMS structure interacted with information distribution in predicting performance (F(1,108) = 4.48, p < .05, η² = .04). Under disconnected information, centralized teams performed better than decentralized teams (M = 11.00 versus 8.62; planned contrast t(108) = 1.76, p < .05, one-tailed, η² = .03). Under connected information, the corresponding difference was nonsignificant (M = 12.00 versus 13.69; t(108) = 1.23). There was no overall TMS-structure main effect (F(1,108) = .13, n.s.); connected information yielded better performance overall than disconnected information (M = 12.85 versus 9.81; F(1,108) = 9.95, p < .01, η² = .08).

H2 was supported by the positive serial indirect effect (b = .15, bias-corrected 95% CI [.03, .37]). Centralization increased transactive retrieval under disconnected information (M = 8.11 versus 5.03; F(1,55) = 6.49, p < .05), but not under connected information (M = 5.86 versus 5.69; F(1,53) = .05, n.s.). Table 3 reports a positive interaction-to-retrieval path (b = .73, p < .05), a positive retrieval-to-elaboration path (b = 4.56, p < .001), and a positive elaboration-to-performance path (b = .04, reported significant). In the full performance regression, neither the residual interaction (b = .70, t = 1.53) nor retrieval (b = .14, t = 1.02) was significant.

Connected-information teams had lower observed team elaboration despite better performance: members more often presented conclusions after individually integrating information, reducing the need for integration during discussion. Coding elaboration to require integration by a different person yielded a measure correlated .93 with the original and identical analytical conclusions; accounting for reward choice or one non-business student also left conclusions unchanged. Table 2 contains an unresolved descriptive inconsistency: its full-sample elaboration mean is 101.83, while its four cell means are 64.24, 73.00, 117.64, and 108.55 with 26–29 teams per cell; the article supplies no reconciliation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper identifies TMS centralization as a performance-relevant dimension that is obscured by focusing solely on team-average metaknowledge or assuming that all members share the same expertise map. It connects differences in individual metaknowledge to team retrieval, elaboration, and performance while identifying information distribution as a boundary condition. This distribution perspective also opens questions about TMS development, individual and dyadic consequences, and the configurations of other forms of team cognition.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that metaknowledge may itself be an area of specialization when many expertise domains and actors, fluctuating membership, and changing knowledge make complete shared metaknowledge costly to maintain. Concentrating development resources on selected individuals who serve as information hubs and process catalysts may be fruitful, particularly in cross-functional business teams or interdisciplinary research teams.

**Limitations**
The experiment examines very small student teams under cooperative conditions, and the authors call for field studies to assess generalization. Written instructions also reminded all decentralized-team members, but only one centralized-team member, of information differences a second time; the authors argue that any resulting encouragement of elaboration would make their catalyst test conservative. Sharedness and average metaknowledge were held constant, leaving their moderating roles untested.

**Future Research**
The authors propose field research using network representations and consideration of disjunctive rather than additive aggregation, alongside tests of communication accessibility, metaknowledge sharedness, and average metaknowledge as boundary conditions; they expect centralization to matter most at intermediate average levels. They also propose studying TMS development, individual and dyadic consequences, central members’ personality, cognitive ability and motivation, and competitive goals or incentives that could encourage strategic information use or metaknowledge hoarding. Further directions include overlap between formal leadership and TMS centrality, larger teams, and configurations of other forms of team cognition.
