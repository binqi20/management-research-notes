# Should I Quit My Day Job?: A Hybrid Path to Entrepreneurship

**Abstract**
Research suggests that the risk and uncertainty associated with entrepreneurial activity deters entry and contributes to the high rates of new business failure. In this study, we examine how the ability to reduce these factors by means of hybrid entrepreneurship—the process of starting a business while retaining a “day job” in an existing organization—influences entrepreneurial entry and survival. Integrating insights from real options theory with logic from the individual differences literature, we hypothesize and find that individuals who are risk averse and have low core self-evaluation are more likely to enter hybrid entrepreneurship relative to full-time self-employment. In turn, we argue and find that hybrid entrepreneurs who subsequently enter full-time self-employment (i.e., quit their day job) have much higher rates of survival relative to individuals who enter full-time self-employment directly from paid employment. Adding support to our theory that the survival advantage is driven by a learning effect that takes place during hybrid entrepreneurship, we find that the decrease in exit hazard is stronger for individuals with prior entrepreneurial experience. Taken together, our findings suggest that individual characteristics may play a greater role in determining the process of how (rather than if) entrepreneurial entry occurs, and that the process of how entrepreneurial entry transpires has important implications for new business survival.

**Research Question**
How do risk aversion and core self-evaluation influence entry into hybrid entrepreneurship relative to direct full-time self-employment, and how does entering full-time self-employment through a hybrid stage relate to subsequent survival? Do cognitive ability and prior entrepreneurial experience moderate the staged-entry–survival relationship?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Individuals with higher risk aversion are more likely to enter hybrid entrepreneurship than full-time self-employment.
- H2: Individuals with low core self-evaluation are more likely to enter hybrid entrepreneurship than full-time self-employment.
- H3: Individuals entering full-time self-employment in stages through hybrid entrepreneurship survive longer than those entering directly from paid employment.
- H4: Cognitive ability strengthens the positive relationship between staged entry and full-time self-employment survival.
- H5: Entrepreneurial experience strengthens the positive relationship between staged entry and full-time self-employment survival.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Risk aversion and core self-evaluation for entry; staged entry through hybrid entrepreneurship, measured as a transition dummy or preceding hybrid duration, for survival.
- DV(s): Entry into hybrid entrepreneurship versus direct full-time self-employment; exit from full-time self-employment.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper. Learning about the business idea and the entrepreneur’s capabilities/fit is the proposed explanation, without a direct mediation test.
- Moderators: Cognitive ability and prior entrepreneurial experience, the latter distinguished by full-time versus hybrid experience and by counts versus durations.

Hybrid entrepreneurship reduces the opportunity cost and downside risk of starting a business by retaining paid employment, making incremental entry relatively attractive to risk-averse and less confident individuals. The hybrid stage functions as a real option: entrepreneurs can learn about venture viability and their own capabilities and fit before choosing whether to commit full time. The authors argue that this learning enables better selection of ventures for full-time pursuit; cognitive ability and prior experience are proposed to improve the interpretation of information and the decision to exercise the option. The study tests associations consistent with parts of this argument rather than directly identifying the learning mechanism.

**Data & Measures**
The National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 cohort originally included 12,686 men and women aged 14–22, interviewed annually through 1994 and biennially thereafter. The main analyses cover 1994–2008 and exclude nonrespondents to key questions, people working fewer than 30 hours weekly, those below the tenth percentile of cognitive ability, and job spells beginning before 1994. The entry sample contains 5,299 participants contributing 31,919 paid-job spells in Methods and Table 1; Table 2 instead reports 31,191, without explaining the difference. The survival sample contains 1,093 participants contributing 2,198 full-time self-employment spells. These are two analytical samples from one longitudinal dataset, not independent studies.

Employment dates identify simultaneous paid and self-employed jobs, avoiding the assumption that both statuses recorded in one year necessarily overlap. Self-employment means reporting work in one’s own business; all other job categories are treated as paid employment. Direct full-time entry includes starting a business within three months of leaving paid work. Entry models follow paid-job spells until hybrid or direct full-time entry, censoring continued paid work, moves to another paid job, or unemployment. Survival models follow full-time self-employed spells, exclude time previously spent as a hybrid, count taking a secondary paid job as exit, and censor continued full-time self-employment at the window’s end; individuals can re-enter the relevant risk set with another qualifying spell.

Risk aversion is an index from hypothetical occupational-income gambles, coded from weak (1) to strong (4): respondents choose between certain income and a 50–50 chance of doubling income or losing one-third, one-fifth, or one-half, with follow-up questions conditional on the first choice. The 1993 responses are used from 1994 to 2002, the 2002 responses until 2004, the 2004 responses until 2006, and the 2006 responses until 2008. CSE is a time-invariant proxy reconstructed from 12 NLSY79 questions reflecting the established CSE scale, rather than a directly administered CSE instrument. Cognitive ability is the time-invariant Armed Forces Qualifications Test measure of verbal and quantitative skills. Staged entry is either a dummy for transition from hybrid to full-time self-employment or the immediately preceding hybrid spell’s duration in years, with direct entrants coded zero. Prior experience comprises cumulative counts of full-time and hybrid businesses and the duration of the most recent prior spell of each type.

Cox models use participant-clustered robust standard errors and Efron handling of ties. Controls include gender, age, schooling, logged family income, urban/rural region, logged hourly pay, logged industry tenure, prior-job count, logged firm size, and three-digit industry, one-digit occupation and year effects. Entry coefficients are compared using Wald tests with bootstrapped standard errors; Table 2 uses two-tailed significance tests, whereas Tables 3a–3b specify one-tailed tests for hypothesized effects and two-tailed tests for controls. A shared-frailty selection check is nonsignificant (p > .5), and the reported main models omit frailty. This adjustment does not convert the observational design into causal identification.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported comparatively: risk aversion predicts lower direct full-time entry hazard (b = −.178, p < .001; reported decrease 16.3% per unit), while its hybrid-entry coefficient is nonsignificant (b = −.005); the coefficient difference is significant at p < .05 (Table 2, M2 versus M4). Thus, support does not mean that risk aversion significantly increases hybrid entry.
- H2 is supported: CSE predicts direct full-time entry positively (b = .398, p < .05) and hybrid entry negatively (b = −.473, p < .05), with a significant difference (p < .01). The text describes the .398 coefficient as a 32.8% increase per unit, which is inconsistent with the paper’s stated conversion, 100[exp(b) − 1]; this reporting discrepancy is unresolved. Pooling the two entry types yields a nonsignificant CSE coefficient and only a marginal risk-aversion coefficient (p < .10).
- H3 is supported: the staged-entry dummy predicts lower exit hazard (b = −.405, p < .001; reported reduction 33.3%), and staged-entry duration is also negative (b = −.025, p < .001; reported reduction approximately 2.5% per additional year), relative to direct entry coded zero (Tables 3a–3b, M2).
- H4 is unsupported: the staged-entry dummy × cognitive ability interaction is nonsignificant (b = .153), while the duration × cognitive ability interaction is positive (b = .053, p < .01), opposite to prediction. Longer hybrid spells are associated with a larger survival benefit among those with lower cognitive ability.
- H5 receives qualified support. For the staged-entry dummy, interactions with prior full-time business count (b = −.035) and prior hybrid duration (b = −.144) are marginal at p < .10; interactions with hybrid business count (b = −.130, p < .001) and prior full-time duration (b = −.199, p < .05) are significant. For staged-entry duration, only the prior full-time-duration interaction is significant (b = −.022, p < .01); interactions with full-time count (b = −.001), hybrid count (b = −.005), and prior hybrid duration (b = −.010) are nonsignificant. All eight interaction estimates are negative, but support varies across operationalizations (Tables 3a–3b, M3–M6). Prior experience’s main effects in these models are positive for exit hazard.

Using narrower definitions based on employing businesses or incorporated businesses supports H1–H3; moderator signs remain the same but typically exhibit less statistical significance, with survival samples reduced to 877 and 1,028 spells, respectively. The authors also report unchanged results under gender-specific samples, inclusion of people working fewer than 30 hours, and alternative hazard specifications. NLSY79-based replications of Petrova and Folta use 4,106 and 20,059 spells, respectively, and generally support the entry argument; however, Table 4 does not mark the Petrova-replication CSE coefficient contrast significant. These supplementary analyses do not establish uniform support for every moderator or coefficient comparison.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the treatment of entrepreneurial entry as a mutually exclusive choice between paid work and self-employment: individual traits may help explain how entry occurs, rather than simply whether it occurs. It connects hybrid entry to subsequent full-time self-employment survival and extends real options reasoning by examining individual risk preferences, confidence and capacities to evaluate information. The results support a contingent account of staged entry, while the contrary cognitive-ability interaction qualifies the proposed learning argument.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that entrepreneurs make small initial commitments to assess whether a business idea warrants full-time commitment. Retaining a day job while learning about the venture can preserve the option to commit fully after assessing its prospects; the findings warrant caution in capital-intensive industries where hybrid entry may be unrealistic.

**Limitations**
The measures do not distinguish venture novelty, fully capture venture-specific characteristics or uncertainty, or equate continued entrepreneurial effort with superior financial performance; CSE is time invariant. The data cannot separate learning about the idea from learning about personal capabilities/fit, identify whether prior ventures succeeded or failed, or definitively resolve the alternative that hybrid entrants selected better ideas beforehand, although the authors interpret the nonsignificant frailty test as evidence against that alternative. Generalizability requires caution in capital-intensive industries; the entry-spell counts and CSE percentage conversion also contain the source discrepancies reported above.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining venture-specific risk alongside individual characteristics, changes in CSE following entrepreneurial experiences, and outcomes beyond survival, including financial performance, job creation, resource acquisition and favorable acquisition. They call for separating the two learning dimensions and studying how prior failures or sequences of failures affect entrepreneurial entry and outcomes. They also identify hybrid entrepreneurs’ specific motivations and individual characteristics’ roles in other real-options decisions, such as speed to option adjudication, as further research directions.
