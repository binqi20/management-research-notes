# The Relational Ecology of Identification: How Organizational Identification Emerges When Individuals Hold Divergent Values

**Abstract**
This research builds on theory about how identification develops when members differ in which organizational values they hold to be important. It is relatively well established that conflict and dis-identification arise under such conditions. In the socially responsible retail company I studied, in contrast, I found identification as well as dis-identification. Both outcomes emerged from members’ interactions with others whose values and behaviors differed from their own. Identification arose when managers interpreted and enacted organizational values for frontline employees by developing integrative solutions, removing ideology, and routinizing ideology. Dis-identification developed in the absence of these practices. The resulting process model suggests a relational ecology of identification, in which identification emerges from the combination of bottom-up interactive processes among organizational members and top-down interpretations and enactments by managers. This model advances understanding of the relational dynamics of identification, offers new insight into how organizations can benefit from multiple identities, and illuminates the double-edged sword of ideology in organizations.

**Research Question**
How does organizational identification emerge when members differ in which organizational values they hold to be important? The study examines how interactions among such members and managers' interpretations and enactments of organizational values contribute to identification or dis-identification.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key tension: Natural Foods combines societal values of health, environmental sustainability, and community welfare with economic values of profit and growth; members differ in which values are personally important and enact them differently in store operations and customer interactions.
- Member configurations: Idealists place high importance on societal values and low to moderate importance on economic values; capitalists show the reverse pattern; pluralists place high importance on both; indifferent members place low to moderate importance on both.
- Identification management practices: Pluralist managers develop integrative solutions that advance both missions, remove ideology by emphasizing choice and service instead of ideological prescriptions, and routinize ideology through formal policies and work procedures that enact the social mission.
- Identification processes and outcomes: Identity confirmation means perceiving cherished values to be upheld; identity conversion means incorporating previously less important societal values into one's identity; identity violation means perceiving those values to be morally violated. Confirmation and conversion foster identification, while violation fosters dis-identification in the emergent model.

Pluralist managers combine their acceptance of divergent values with managerial authority to shape how members encounter and interpret one another's behavior. Integrative solutions confirm members' values; removing ideology makes room for members who do not embrace ideological principles; routinization both preserves societal values and involves members in enacting them. These practices connect top-down enactment with bottom-up interaction, confirming or expanding identities despite continuing value differences. In their absence, divergent behavior can be interpreted as a moral violation, generating disillusionment and distance from the organization.

**Data & Measures**
The main study comprises 92 semi-structured interviews and 174 hours of nonparticipant observation across eight northeast U.S. stores over 15 months; a prior pilot involved 21 interviews at two other stores. The eight main sites were purposively selected to include three former cooperative stores, two former conventional stores, and three newly built stores; interviews and observations occurred concurrently within each site, with collection proceeding sequentially across sites until theoretical saturation. Interviews lasted 30–80 minutes and were recorded and transcribed; archival sources covered company history, operations, mission, policies, and evaluation criteria. The source contains a sample-count discrepancy: Methods reports 47 managers and 44 non-managers, whereas Table 2 reports 10 store managers, 38 department managers, and 44 non-managers, totaling 92; the table's manager categories include assistant managers.

Analysis iterated between data and emerging theory, moving from empirical themes to conceptual categories through axial coding and constant comparison, then to a process model. High value importance required positive statements linking the value to personal identity or values; low to moderate importance included negative statements or acceptance without such centrality. Behavior was coded primarily from observations, especially of managers, supplemented by self-reports; identification was captured as valuing membership, and dis-identification as distancing oneself and describing the company in opposition to one's values. A research assistant independently recoded previously selected passages with the author's theme labels removed, disagreements informed revisions, and member checks involved store and regional managers and the CEO. This is inductive qualitative evidence, not a causal test of the proposed processes.

**Key Findings**
Table 2 identifies 32 idealists, 16 capitalists, 24 pluralists, and 20 indifferent members. Most idealists and indifferent members were non-managers, whereas most capitalists and pluralists occupied management positions; the types had blurry boundaries and were not static. Pluralist managers' local enactment of the three practices fostered identification: integrative solutions confirmed idealists', capitalists', and pluralists' values; removing ideology made capitalists and pluralists feel welcome; and routinization confirmed idealists' and pluralists' values while fostering conversion among initially capitalist or indifferent members.

Identity violation was observed only among idealists, who interpreted neglected or sacrificed societal values as morally wrong; capitalists and pluralists accepted value trade-offs instead. Violation was associated with disillusionment, reduced effort, and, in some cases, departure. The conversion pattern was asymmetric: the data offered little support for idealists coming to embrace economic values, and several idealist managers stepped down or were asked to do so, with one leaving the company. The suggestion that integrative solutions could themselves foster identity conversion is a theoretical extension for which the author found no evidence at Natural Foods.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The relational ecology of identification explains how identification and dis-identification can arise from interactions among members who hold divergent values, connecting bottom-up relational processes with top-down managerial enactment. It shifts attention from practices that affect individuals directly to practices that shape their interactions, and identifies frontline pluralists' combination of integrating values and preserving their distinctions as a basis for benefiting from multiple identities. The paired removal and routinization of ideology explains how organizations may retain ideological commitments while making participation accessible to members who do not initially share them.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations with divergent values may hire people committed to only one set of values, provided they also develop managers who support both sets and enact the associated identification management practices. The author cautions against hiring and promoting only pluralists: when organizations want or need to uphold divergent values, hiring both people who embrace them together and distinct representatives of each may better prevent one value from dominating.

**Limitations**
The study develops theory from a single extreme case, and its qualitative, cross-sectional data do not establish the relative importance of socialization and attraction–selection–attrition in producing pluralists. Evidence that direct contact increases the potential for dis-identification comes partly from retrospective accounts of a formerly isolated store and is explicitly tentative.

**Future Research**
Ethnographic and longitudinal studies should examine how interactions unfold over time and whether the relational ecology of identification depends on direct contact among members holding divergent values. Larger samples and longitudinal designs are needed to investigate how pluralists emerge and distinguish socialization from attraction, selection, and attrition, including whether idealists can come to embrace economic values.
