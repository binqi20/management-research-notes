# Nothing Succeeds Like Moderation: A Social Self-Regulation Perspective on Cultural Dissimilarity and Performance

**Abstract**
Addressing inconsistencies in relational demography research, we examine the relationship between cultural dissimilarity and individual performance through the lens of social self-regulation theory, which extends the social identity perspective in relational demography with the analysis of social self-regulation. We propose that social self-regulation in culturally diverse teams manifests itself as performance monitoring (i.e., individuals’ actions to meet team performance standards and peer expectations). Contingent on the status associated with individuals’ cultural background, performance monitoring is proposed to have a curvilinear relationship with individual performance and to mediate between cultural dissimilarity and performance. Multilevel moderated mediation analyses of time-lagged data from 316 members of 69 teams confirmed these hypotheses. Cultural dissimilarity had a negative relationship with performance monitoring for high cultural-status members, and a positive relationship for low cultural-status members. Performance monitoring had a curvilinear relationship with individual performance that became decreasingly positive. Cultural dissimilarity thus was increasingly negatively associated with performance for high culturalstatus members, and decreasingly positively for low cultural-status members. These findings suggest that cultural dissimilarity to the team is not unconditionally negative for the individual but, in moderation, may in fact have positive motivational effects.

**Research Question**
How and when is cultural dissimilarity to teammates associated with individual performance? The paper examines whether performance monitoring mediates this relationship in a curvilinear manner contingent on the status associated with an individual's cultural background.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Cultural status moderates the cultural dissimilarity–performance monitoring relationship: greater dissimilarity predicts less monitoring for high cultural-status team members and more monitoring for low cultural-status members.
- H2: Performance monitoring has a decreasingly positive curvilinear relationship with individual performance.
- H3: Performance monitoring mediates an increasingly negative curvilinear cultural dissimilarity–performance relationship for high cultural-status individuals and a decreasingly positive curvilinear relationship for low cultural-status individuals.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cultural dissimilarity, the extent to which an individual differs from teammates in cultural background, operationalized through the Anglo/non-Anglo distinction.
- DV(s): Individual performance in the main study; individual team-member effectiveness in the separate scale-validation study.
- Mediators: Performance monitoring, an individual process of tracking progress toward goals with reference to team standards and others' expectations.
- Moderators: Cultural status moderates the dissimilarity–monitoring relationship; the monitoring–performance relationship is curvilinear, with diminishing positive returns.

The authors propose that greater dissimilarity creates instrumental uncertainty for high-status individuals about whether the team can achieve valued outcomes, discouraging monitoring, whereas norm uncertainty about meeting peer expectations motivates monitoring among low-status individuals. Monitoring can clarify expectations, mobilize effort, and facilitate adjustment, but its cognitive demands imply diminishing returns. Team-based accountability and reduced exclusion are proposed to buffer depletion, so the prediction is a leveling-off of benefits rather than a detrimental high-monitoring effect. These uncertainty and depletion processes provide the theoretical explanation; the empirical mediation test concerns performance monitoring.

**Data & Measures**
The main study began with 316 upper-level business undergraduates in 69 four- to five-member teams at a UK international business school; the final questionnaire sample was 261 students in 68 teams (82% response), with 21 tutorials in the multilevel analysis. Students participated in a two-semester EUROCAR business simulation. The university assigned students to teams on a random basis subject to including a high-scoring student and balancing gender; the resulting time-lagged analysis is associational and does not establish causality. Monitoring was measured in weeks 12 and 22, and the individual performance report was submitted in week 24; calendar dates are not reported.

Cultural background and team size came from university records covering all 316 students, including nonrespondents. Cultural dissimilarity equals the number of opposite-category members divided by full team size: non-Anglo members for an Anglo student, and Anglo members for a non-Anglo student; scores ranged from 0 to 0.8. Cultural status was coded 1 for Anglo/White British and 0 for non-Anglo/non-White British. To check this status proxy, the authors averaged peers' task-competence ratings within each of four competency areas and then averaged across areas, separately in weeks 12 and 22. Performance monitoring used four self-report items on a seven-point agreement scale, averaged across the two waves after finding no significant temporal change (average α = .72). Individual performance was the identity-blind tutor grade, on a 0–100% scale, for a report analyzing the simulated company's operations and performance. Controls included year-1 GPA, team size, UK birth, gender, tutor dummies, team cultural diversity measured with Blau's index, and the two team-performance assessments.

The multivariate three-level model jointly predicted monitoring and performance, with varying intercepts across individuals, teams, and tutorials and fixed slopes. Continuous variables were grand-mean centered; the monitoring equation included dissimilarity × status, and the performance equation included linear and squared monitoring alongside dissimilarity, status, their interaction, squared dissimilarity, and squared dissimilarity × status, plus controls. Conditional instantaneous indirect effects were assessed at low, mean, and high dissimilarity using 95% Monte Carlo intervals based on 20,000 repetitions. Separately, 240 MBA/master's business students in 40 culturally diverse teams at the same university completed a scale-validation study during a ten-week organizational behavior course: monitoring and public/private self-consciousness were measured, followed four weeks later by individual team-member effectiveness, defined through effective peer relationships contributing to learning and well-being. This study used five-point scales, confirmatory factor analysis, and hierarchical linear modeling controlling for team membership and linear monitoring.

**Key Findings**
In the main study, H1 was supported: the dissimilarity × status coefficient predicting monitoring was −2.86 (p < .01; ΔR² = .11), with a positive simple slope for low-status members (0.81, p < .05) and a negative slope for high-status members (−2.06, p < .01). H2 was supported by the negative monitoring-squared coefficient (−1.41, p < .05; ΔR² = .02). Monitoring's slope was positive at low (−1 SD: 4.20, p < .01) and mean levels (2.01, p < .01), but nonsignificant at high levels (+1 SD: −0.18), indicating diminishing benefits without an established detrimental slope. These are unstandardized coefficients (Table 2).

The authors report support for H3: the moderated-curvilinear mediation product was 4.04, with 95% CI [0.21, 9.12]. Table 3 qualifies the conditional pattern: for high-status members, the instantaneous indirect effect at low/mean/high dissimilarity was respectively −1.20 [−6.13, 3.16], −3.46 [−7.55, −0.42], and −5.73 [−10.55, −2.04]; only the mean and high levels excluded zero. For low-status members, the corresponding estimates were 2.06 [0.01, 4.73], 1.72 [−0.01, 4.12], and 1.38 [−0.08, 3.66]; only the low level excluded zero. Thus the estimated indirect association became increasingly negative for high-status members and decreasingly positive for low-status members, with the stated nonsignificant regions.

In the separate validation study, the four-factor measurement model fit better than the one-factor model. Monitoring correlated positively with public self-consciousness (r = .36, p < .01), but not significantly with private self-consciousness (r = .08); its quadratic association with individual team-member effectiveness was negative (−.05, p < .05; ΔR² = .04). This validates aspects of the monitoring measure and its curvilinear outcome association, rather than independently testing the full cultural-status mediation model.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the social identity approach to relational demography by integrating uncertainty reduction and social self-regulation, shifting attention from social integration to behavioral performance monitoring as the mediating process. It explains status-contingent, curvilinear dissimilarity–performance relationships and argues that sampling only the usual low-dissimilarity range for high-status members and high-dissimilarity range for low-status members can obscure them. It also identifies performance monitoring as a mode of social self-regulation whose benefits diminish in a sustained team context.

**Practical Implication**
The authors propose optimizing rather than maximizing performance monitoring through practices that clarify goals, provide regular feedback, make social expectations salient, and connect performance to valued outcomes. They also suggest reducing uncertainty and perceived status distance through clear interaction norms, a culture of respect, diversity mindsets, and an inclusive climate. These are proposed interventions; the paper cautions that changing status hierarchies is difficult and need not improve performance because it may increase uncertainty about standards and peer expectations.

**Limitations**
Student teams in a business simulation do not establish causality or the relationships in organizational practice, and generalization to longer-tenured teams remains uncertain. Status was first measured after a full team-performance cycle, higher status was confounded with White British background, and the assumed salience of the Anglo/non-Anglo categorization was not directly measured. The dichotomous cultural-status approach also leaves room for more fine-grained cultural differences and for explicitly modeling cultural values.

**Future Research**
The authors call for experimental and organizational replications, including contexts where groups other than Caucasians hold higher status, and for direct measures of category salience and finer-grained measures of cultural dissimilarity and status, and contexts in which cultural values can be controlled for or modeled. They propose examining age and gender dissimilarity and outcomes such as citizenship, counterproductive behavior, and ethical behavior, while cautioning that these extensions require empirical testing. Further work could investigate boundary conditions at team, organizational, industry, and societal levels, including leadership, climate, culture, and practices that may influence monitoring, inequality, status distance, and uncertainty.
