# Let's Dance! Elastic Coordination in Creative Group Work: A Qualitative Study of Modern Dancers

**Abstract**
Coordination challenges are an inherent part of group work. They are perhaps more daunting for creative work because coordinating creative work requires allowing for dynamics that seem to “pull the group together,” or integrate, while also fostering dynamics that “pull the group apart,” or de-integrate. During an inductive study of modern dance group rehearsals, these interactions emerged as central to understanding how groups navigate coordinating creative work. Our emergent findings reveal how groups use autonomy and constraints to accomplish elastic coordination throughout a creative project.

**Research Question**
How do groups coordinate creative work while accommodating both integration and de-integration? How do autonomy and constraints operate within the interactions through which groups develop creative solutions?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs: Task autonomy is freedom and discretion in carrying out tasks; constraints are boundaries that promote or preclude particular responses. Integration brings group activities together; de-integration permits individual exploration that disrupts predictability and common understanding while retaining accountability.
- Surfacing boundaries: Recognizing autonomy, often through ambiguous instructions or open creative prompts, prompts questions and a need for psychological safety. Members exercise autonomy to establish shared constraints on the space for exploration.
- Discovering discontinuities: Errors, mismatches, and surprises during work stimulate curious exploration. Members push against and relax established constraints, generating alternatives that can extend beyond the initial problem.
- Parsing solutions: Multiple viable options or hesitancy to choose prompt a need for coherence. Through tactful collective negotiation and demonstrations, members limit autonomy and increase constraints to settle on a working solution; choreographers remain ultimately responsible for decisions.

Elastic coordination describes the interactions through which groups manage integration and de-integration to arrive at a working creative solution. The three interaction types tend to follow a loose sequence within rehearsals, interspersed with developing and practicing movement, rather than forming a seamless or rigid progression. Repeated cycles generate working ideas that groups subsequently combine and rearrange into an emerging creative composition; this work on the composition occurs less frequently and is scattered across the rehearsal process.

**Data & Measures**
Purposeful sampling identified four modern dance groups that reached the final concerts across two selection rounds. Of 21 applicants, seven met the criteria of at least three members and local rehearsals; four advanced and all four participated. The concert schedule ran from submission in week 1 through expert reviews in weeks 4 and 8 to performance in week 10. Expert selection vetted the creativity of the final choreography. A separate exploratory pilot interviewed six choreographers and six dancers, none belonging to the main study groups. Table 2 reports 25 formal interviews, 53 hours of rehearsal observation, attendance at 26 rehearsals out of a conservatively estimated total of 37, 48 hours and 8 minutes of video, and four two-hour focus groups. Formal interviews included repeated interviews with choreographers and some dancers; 25 is an interview count, not a count of unique interviewees. Informal conversations entered field notes, and focus groups used performance videos to elicit reflection. Weekly comparisons of field notes combined the authors' insider and outsider perspectives. Open coding identified statements, questions, and actions; axial coding developed relationships among triggers, emergent states, and coordinating actions. Interaction-to-interaction comparisons, video review, and counterexamples refined the model. This is an inductive process account, without standardized outcome measures or tests of causal effects.

**Key Findings**
The emergent model portrays autonomy and constraints as dynamic complements: groups use autonomy to establish constraints, explore discontinuities by stretching those constraints, and then limit autonomy to converge on a working solution. Psychological safety, curiosity, and coherence motivate different coordinating interactions. Constraints serve both as cognitive reference points for noticing novelty and as shared boundaries that support movement between independent and interdependent work. The findings concern trajectories of increasing or decreasing autonomy and constraints; the method does not establish their absolute levels. Breakdowns qualify the model: insufficiently clear constraints left dancers confused, failure to question established constraints sometimes left the first idea as the working solution, and failure to converge left decisions deferred or unresolved. The recurring interactions produced ideas that could be recombined into a larger composition, rather than a single linear progression from problem finding to a final product.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends coordination theory by introducing de-integration as a complement to integration in creative work and by linking specific interactions to the emergent states that motivate them. It recasts autonomy and constraints as separate but linked dimensions negotiated within groups, rather than fixed conditions or opposite ends of one dimension. Its account of recurring interactions and the composition of multiple working ideas challenges linear models of the creative process.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that imposing a practice such as brainstorming or design thinking is insufficient by itself: group members need flexible boundaries that they can adapt to support collective and individual work. It highlights the potential value of generating constraints and revisiting discontinuities throughout creative work. The authors regard their insights as most useful for experienced, relatively flat, temporary creative project groups able to prototype quickly, including computer coding, graphic design, and product design using rapid prototyping.

**Limitations**
The study followed successfully creative groups without comparing them with noncreative groups. Its experienced participants, relatively flat and temporary group structures, rapid physical prototyping, and almost entirely female sample condition transferability to other settings. The qualitative method supports interpretation of interaction patterns and trajectories, rather than definitive statements about levels of autonomy and constraints.

**Future Research**
The authors call for studying elastic coordination over longer projects, including whether particular interactions become more common at project beginnings, disruptions, or important decision points, and for examining the role of group experience. They propose examining gender composition and individual differences in more traditional organizational settings, as well as how groups create coherence from multiple creative ideas. They also suggest developing interventions based on the three interaction patterns, testing and refining them in laboratories before using them in organizations to improve meetings intended to be creative.
