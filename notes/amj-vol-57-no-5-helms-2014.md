# Eliciting Acceptance For “Illicit” Organizations: The Positive Implications of Stigma for MMA Organizations

**Abstract**
This paper explores the positive implications that stigma has for organizations and how it may lead to broader acceptance for them among social audiences. We conduct a qualitative study of stigmatized—but increasingly accepted—mixed martial arts (MMA) organizations and the audiences that evaluated them through an analysis of interviews, media reports, and texts documenting their experiences. We found the following. First, there were three different types of stigma associated with MMA organizations: aesthetic, lawlessness, and harm-based. Second, MMA organizational actors used stigma to their advantage in two, opposing manners. They coopted negative labels to gain the awareness of supportive audiences, as well as drew from them to correct negative evaluations held by critical audiences regarding their organizations. This demonstrates that, rather than attempting to “pass as normal,” stigmatized organizational actors can actively construct those attributes that are the focus of stigma and persuade audiences to reconsider their negative evaluations, rendering their organizations more acceptable.

**Research Question**
How are organizations stigmatized, and how do organizational actors respond to stigma to increase audiences’ acceptance of their organizations?

**Mechanism Process**
- Stigma types: aesthetic-based stigma concerns visual experiences and emotional responses; lawlessness-based stigma concerns perceived absence of rules; harm-based stigma concerns perceived physical harm to practitioners and moral harm to society.
- Label cooptation: actors strategically adopt negative labels to attract sympathetic combat sport audiences and provoke controversy that generates awareness.
- Practice construction and audience persuasion: aesthetic stigma is linked to production work and enticement; lawlessness stigma to rule work and pacification; harm stigma to safety work and defensive education.
- Acceptance: joining organizations, enabling their activities through public support and removal of sanctions, and reducing stigmatized perceptions; acceptance need not eliminate every negative evaluation.

The inductive account identifies two opposing uses of stigma: amplifying labels to attract supportive audiences and drawing on criticism to correct practices and persuade critical audiences. Audience members’ experience and professional knowledge shape how they interpret labels, so the same stigma can attract some audiences while alienating others. Actors use stigma as information for constructing practices and engaging audiences, rather than solely hiding stigmatized attributes or conforming during interactions.

**Data & Measures**
The corpus combines four historical texts, eight autobiographical texts, Lexis Nexis newspaper reports and televised news transcripts, 52 open-ended interviews, and documents supplied by interviewees. Interviews covered 24 organizational actors (eight practitioners, ten promoters or promotional employees, and six school owners, trainers, or employees), 22 audience members who came to support MMA organizations (seven sports reporters, eight physicians, and seven athletic commission employees), and six audience members who remained separate; interviewees came from six North American states/provinces. The paper reports interview lengths of 20 minutes to two hours and three interviews conducted by email; the final 12 used snowball/referral sampling to corroborate actor–audience interactions across three states/provinces. Data collection dates are not reported. The authors and a research assistant independently coded recurring negative labels and accounts, compared themes, and checked them against interviews; acceptance was coded as joining, enabling, and stigma reduction. Subsequent coding linked stigma types to reported responses and acceptance, cross-checking organizational actors’ accounts with audience accounts; retrospective reports were checked using media records. These are qualitative reconstructions, not experimental estimates. An internal denominator discrepancy remains: Methods reports six separate audience members, a later passage says four of five disliked MMA, and the following analysis reports harm-based themes in six of six.

**Key Findings**
Three types of core stigma had different implications for acceptance. Aesthetic stigma could attract combat sport audiences and coexist with joining or enabling despite continuing distaste. Lawlessness stigma generated both pride and political support, alongside fear of arrest; harm stigma deterred engagement and demoralized actors, and the authors found no audience supportive of harm-based stigma. All organizational actor interviewees were categorized as joining, and 12 of the 22 supportive audience members also joined through fandom, training, or identification. Actors coopted labels to reach martial artists, combat sport athletes, and male fight fans, including provoking opponents to generate publicity. Corrective responses comprised production work that incorporated and promoted MMA; safety work addressing physical risks and psychological fears; and rule work addressing perceived lawlessness. Rule work often concerned the appearance of safety rather than actual safety, and the comparative risk of MMA remained debated. Actors also offered resources or status to entice audiences, accommodated regulatory and medical requests through pacification, and used defensive education to challenge harm-based misperceptions. Audience accounts linked these interactions to joining, enabling, and reduced stigma, without implying complete acceptance by every audience.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper differentiates core stigma into types with distinct implications and emphasizes audiences’ differing capacities to interpret negative labels. It shows how label cooptation can build a supportive audience and develops an institutional work account in which stigmatized actors construct practices and persuade critical audiences. This presents stigmatized actors as proactive agents in gaining acceptance, rather than solely targets who avoid sanctions or pass as normal.

**Practical Implication**
The MMA case illustrates how stigmatized organizational actors can use negative labels to attract sympathetic audiences while addressing critical audiences’ concerns through changes to practices and direct engagement. Greater acceptance can involve public support and removal of sanctions even when aesthetic discomfort persists; the paper does not suggest that every stigma attracts support.

**Limitations**
The authors describe MMA as a relatively new, less extreme context involving non-visible stigma attached to a core practice, which actors can conceal or reshape more readily than stigma attached to historically stigmatized clientele traits. They caution that practice work may not be feasible when labels concern those clientele traits. The study relies predominantly on retrospective recollections, whose validity the authors checked against media reports.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining how different stigma types threaten identities or impede audience support, whether the typology extends to other contexts, and whether audiences’ decoding capacities predict acceptance. They also suggest studying institutional work through changes to rules and standards, comparing stigma attached to clienteles with stigma attached to core practices, and examining whether identity groups can gain acceptance by addressing misunderstood practices.
