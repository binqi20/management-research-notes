# Audience Heterogeneity and the Effectiveness of Market Signals: How to Overcome Liabilities of Foreignness in Film Exports?

**Abstract**
This study extends research on the importance of market signals in international markets for cultural products by examining not only when market signals are important but also what types of market signals are most important to which audiences. Specifically, we argue that audience heterogeneity influences the effectiveness of commercial performance and artistic acclaim as market signals in international film markets. Audience heterogeneity refers to the differences in the cultural knowledge and preferences of domestic and foreign consumers (end audiences) and product specialization of domestic and foreign distributors (intermediary audiences). Our empirical setting is the European film industry. We use a large sample of films produced and distributed domestically and internationally in Europe between 2004 and 2009. We find that domestic commercial performance and film festival participation increase international film success, but also that their effects depend on the cultural distance between countries and the use of major or independent distribution in the domestic and foreign markets.

**Research Question**
How does audience heterogeneity, expressed through cultural distance between domestic and foreign consumers and the product specialization of domestic and foreign distributors, moderate the effectiveness of domestic commercial performance and artistic acclaim as signals of film quality? The study distinguishes the likelihood of export from subsequent commercial performance in foreign markets.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Greater cultural distance weakens the effect of domestic commercial performance on (a) export likelihood and (b) foreign commercial performance.
- H2: Greater cultural distance strengthens the effect of artistic acclaim on (a) export likelihood and (b) foreign commercial performance.
- H3: The effects on export likelihood of (a) domestic commercial performance and (b) artistic acclaim are stronger with independent than with major domestic distribution.
- H4: With major rather than independent foreign distribution, the effect on foreign commercial performance of (a) domestic commercial performance is stronger, whereas that of (b) artistic acclaim is weaker.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Domestic commercial performance and artistic acclaim, represented by domestic admissions and participation in major international film festival competitions.
- DV(s): Export likelihood, reflecting foreign distributors' expectations of demand; foreign commercial performance, reflecting demand from foreign film audiences.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Cultural distance for both outcomes; independent versus major domestic distribution for export likelihood; major versus independent foreign distribution for foreign commercial performance.

The proposed mechanism concerns how audiences interpret signals, rather than uncertainty reduction alone. Cultural similarity preserves the match between domestic audience preferences and film attributes, making domestic commercial success more informative, whereas greater cultural distance increases reliance on experts whose artistic standards cross national boundaries. Independent domestic distribution leaves ambiguity between narrow audience appeal and low quality, so commercial success and artistic acclaim provide more diagnostic information to foreign distributors. Major foreign distribution signals the transferability of domestic popularity, but artistic acclaim can contradict expectations of mainstream appeal and discourage that audience; these interpretive processes are theorized rather than tested as mediators.

**Data & Measures**
The sample combines the European Audiovisual Observatory's LUMIERE annual admissions data with IMDb information for 1,669 films produced in 19 European countries during 2004–2006 and followed through 2009. Approximately 2,600 films were produced in those countries during the production window; films lacking IMDb information were mostly low-budget niche productions and experimental films. Each film could enter 32 foreign markets, comprising the other 18 producing countries and 14 additional countries; coproductions were assigned to their main production country according to financial investment. Export models contain 177,498 film/market-country/year time spells, ending at export or censoring in 2009; performance models contain 3,900 exported film/market-country dyads. The design is associational: Cox models account for export timing and right censoring, while OLS models use pre-export information and include an inverse Mills ratio that the authors describe as estimated from the Cox model to account for selection bias.

Domestic commercial performance is logged domestic admissions; foreign commercial performance is logged admissions in the importing country. Artistic acclaim is a binary indicator of selection to open in main competition at Cannes, Berlin, or Venice, rather than an indicator of winning an award. Cultural distance follows Kogut and Singh: square the difference between countries on each of Hofstede's four dimensions—individualism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity—divide each squared difference by the corresponding dimension's variance, then average the four values. Major distributors comprise subsidiaries of U.S. distributors and large European distributors distributing films for major U.S. studios; all non-major distributors are classified as independent, with domestic distributor type used for export and foreign distributor type for performance.

Controls include top national award or Oscar nominations, cumulative director awards and awards of the five leading actors, distributor market share in the relevant domestic or foreign market, genres, prior imports from the producing country, language match, destination cinema attendance per capita and population, coproduction, and missing director/actor birth-country indicators. Cultural specificity is a domesticity index based on domestic language, domestic director, at least one domestic actor among the five leads, and absence of coproducing countries. All models include producing-country fixed effects; performance models additionally include indicators for export in the opening year, first export destination, and export before national-award or Oscar nomination.

**Key Findings**
For export likelihood, additive Model 2 associates domestic commercial performance (b = 0.12, p < .001) and artistic acclaim (b = 0.24, p < .001) with higher export likelihood, while the cultural-distance main effect is nonsignificant (b = 0.01, p > .10). Table 3 supports H1a and H2a: cultural distance weakens the domestic-performance association (Model 3 interaction b = −0.01, p < .001) and strengthens the artistic-acclaim association (Model 4 interaction b = 0.06, p < .05). H3a and H3b are supported: independent domestic distribution strengthens the associations of domestic performance (Model 5 interaction b = 0.02, p < .01) and acclaim (Model 6 interaction b = 0.26, p < .01) with export likelihood. All four interaction directions remain significant in the joint Model 7.

For foreign commercial performance, additive Model 9 shows a positive domestic-performance association (b = 0.43, p < .001), but no significant artistic-acclaim main effect (b = 0.02, p > .05); cultural distance is negatively associated with foreign performance (b = −0.07, p < .001). Table 4 supports H1b and H2b: cultural distance weakens the domestic-performance association (Model 10 interaction b = −0.04, p < .001) and strengthens the acclaim association (Model 11 interaction b = 0.09, p < .05). H4a and H4b are supported: major foreign distribution strengthens the domestic-performance association (Model 12 interaction b = 0.08, p < .05) but weakens the acclaim association (Model 13 interaction b = −0.40, p < .05). All four interaction directions remain significant in the joint Model 14. Thus acclaim is not an unconditional predictor of higher foreign admissions: its effectiveness depends on cultural distance and distribution type.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends signaling theory by shifting attention from heterogeneous producers communicating with homogeneous audiences to heterogeneous recipients who interpret the same signals differently. Opposing moderation of commercial performance and artistic acclaim shows why variation in uncertainty alone does not capture signal effectiveness. In international film research, it distinguishes intermediary distributors' import decisions from end audiences' demand, theorizes domestic and foreign distribution at different export stages, and examines European films facing liabilities of foreignness beyond the widely studied Hollywood setting.

**Practical Implication**
The authors caution decision makers in the film industry and other cultural and non-cultural industries against using commercial performance and artistic acclaim or analogous expert evaluations indiscriminately as market signals. Managers should match signals to particular markets and audiences, since their effectiveness varies with the countries and products involved.

**Limitations**
Annual data can obscure finer timing differences among domestic releases, film festival awards, and foreign releases; weekly or monthly data were unavailable for the European countries studied. The authors also identify the symmetry assumption in Hofstede-based cultural distance as potentially problematic when cultural products flow unequally between countries, although they report basically the same results using asymmetric Eurovision Song Contest voting patterns.
