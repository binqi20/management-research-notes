# Interpersonal Trust within Negotiations: Meta-Analytic Evidence, Critical Contingencies, and Directions for Future Research

**Abstract**
Trust has long been recognized, by scholars and practitioners alike, as an important factor for negotiation success. However, there has been little effort to date to empirically review or theoretically synthesize the research on trust in the context of negotiations. We present a social exchange framework that describes the processes through which trust influences negotiation behaviors and outcomes. We identified three critical contingencies that modified the effects of trust on negotiation behaviors and outcomes. A meta-analysis on a sample of 38 independent studies provided considerable support for the model, and also confirmed the importance of the three contingencies for understanding the effects of trust. The framework and accompanying empirical evidence provide a necessary theoretical and empirical integration of the trust and negotiation literatures. Based on the theory and meta-analytical findings, we identified critical gaps and limitations in existing research, and we propose a research agenda to address key theoretical, empirical, and methodological issues identified by our framework and review.

**Research Question**
How is interpersonal trust related to negotiation behaviors, joint and individual extrinsic outcomes, and outcome satisfaction? Through which behavioral processes, and under which conditions concerning integrative potential, the level of trust, and its conceptualization, are these relationships stronger or weaker?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Trust is negatively related to distributive behaviors.
- H1b: Trust is positively related to integrative behaviors.
- H2a: Trust is positively related to joint outcomes.
- H2b: Integrative and distributive behaviors fully mediate the relationship between trust and joint outcomes.
- H3a: Trust is positively related to the trustor’s outcome.
- H3b: Integrative and distributive behaviors fully mediate the relationship between trust and the trustor’s outcome.
- H3c: The relationship between trust and the trustor’s outcome is weaker than that between trust and the joint outcome.
- H4a: Trust is positively related to outcome satisfaction.
- H4b: Integrative and distributive behaviors partially mediate the relationship between trust and outcome satisfaction.
- H5a: The positive effect of trust on integrative behaviors increases with the negotiation’s integrative potential.
- H5b: The positive effect of trust on the joint outcome increases with integrative potential.
- H6a: Dyadic trust has stronger positive effects on integrative behaviors and the joint outcome than individual trust.
- H6b: Dyadic trust has a stronger negative effect on distributive behaviors than individual trust.
- H7a: Perceived integrity is more strongly related to distributive behaviors than a broad measure of trust.
- H7b: A broad measure of trust is more strongly related to integrative behaviors and the joint outcome than perceived integrity.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Interpersonal trust in the negotiation counterpart.
- DV(s): Joint extrinsic outcome, the trustor’s own extrinsic outcome, and outcome satisfaction.
- Mediators: Integrative behaviors, which create value, and distributive behaviors, which claim value.
- Moderators: Integrative potential; individual versus dyadic trust; perceived integrity versus a broad measure of trust.

Social exchange theory proposes that trust encourages accepting vulnerability through integrative behaviors while reducing protective distributive behaviors. This favors joint gains, but cooperation also creates exposure to exploitation and reduced value claiming can diminish personal gains. Trust is additionally proposed to cast a positive halo on outcome evaluations, with behavioral pathways contributing to satisfaction; these proposed processes are examined through meta-analytic associations rather than established temporal dynamics.

**Data & Measures**
The literature search covered bibliographic databases, journal archives, citation searches, and requests for unpublished studies. Eligible reports examined trust as an interpersonal variable in negotiations between two individuals; third- or multi-party and organization-level negotiations, ultimatum, prisoner’s dilemma and trust games, allocation decisions, and studies examining only dispositional trust were excluded. Journal reports replaced duplicate dissertation/thesis reports. The authors coded 61 reports and retained variables with trust effects from at least three independent samples, yielding 32 papers and 38 independent samples. Table 1 reports relationship-specific independent-sample counts and individual Ns: integrative behaviors, k = 14, N = 2,194; distributive behaviors, k = 14, N = 1,984; joint outcome, k = 20, N = 2,327; trustor’s outcome, k = 8, N = 1,186; outcome satisfaction, k = 12, N = 1,463. These are overlapping relationship-specific counts, not a unique pooled participant total; Table 2 reports separate cumulative sample sizes for its correlation matrix, and path significance tests use harmonic mean n = 376.

Broad trust concerns willingness to accept vulnerability based on positive expectations; perceived-integrity measures focus on honesty, reliability, dependability, and fairness. Integrative behaviors seek joint gains, whereas distributive behaviors seek personal gains and include ethical and unethical tactics. Outcomes distinguish joint and personal extrinsic gains from satisfaction with the negotiation outcome. Integrative potential is the number of logrolling issues divided by the total number of negotiation issues, ranging from 0 to 1; contingency-contracting issues are excluded from the numerator, and including them produced a similar moderation pattern. Five studies examined dyadic trust; the level-of-trust moderation excluded one lacking empirical justification for the dyadic measure, without changing the reported pattern.

Reported effect statistics were converted to Fisher’s z for meta-analysis; measurement unreliability was corrected, using average reliability for similar measures when unavailable. Effects were inverse-variance weighted, combined, and back-transformed to correlations. Random-effects models were used except for the homogeneous trustor-outcome relationship, estimated with fixed effects; main effects were assessed with trim-and-fill. Categorical moderation required at least two effects per subgroup and used fixed-effects comparisons; continuous integrative potential used weighted least squares. Path analysis used the attenuation-corrected correlation matrix, allowed integrative and distributive behavioral residuals to covary, and assessed mediation through products of coefficients. To address within-dyad dependence, the meta-analysis used one party’s data when provided or dyadic data.

**Key Findings**
Table 1 supports H1a, H1b, H2a, H3a, and H4a: attenuation-corrected correlations are −.30 for distributive behaviors (95% CI [−.43, −.16]), .32 for integrative behaviors [.19, .45], .26 for joint outcome [.12, .39], .10 for the trustor’s outcome [.02, .18], and .48 for outcome satisfaction [.37, .57]. Only the integrative-behavior estimate was affected by the publication-bias correction, becoming .26 [.22, .31]. The trustor-outcome association is smaller than the joint-outcome association (z = −3.30, p < .001), supporting H3c.

The authors report support for full behavioral mediation of joint and trustor outcomes (H2b, H3b) and partial mediation of satisfaction (H4b). In the path model, trust predicts integrative behaviors positively (β = .32) and distributive behaviors negatively (β = −.30). Integrative/distributive paths to joint outcome are .11/−.20, to the trustor’s outcome −.14/.13, and to satisfaction .31/.56; the direct trust–satisfaction path is .52 (all p < .05). Thus both behavioral indirect paths from trust to the trustor’s outcome are negative, distinct from the positive bivariate trust–trustor correlation. Figure 1 includes no direct trust-to-joint-outcome or trust-to-trustor-outcome arrows; its connections among the three outcomes are covariances, not directed effects.

Higher integrative potential strengthens the trust relationships with integrative behaviors (WLS b = .31, corrected SE = .13, p = .01) and joint outcome (b = .32, corrected SE = .11, p < .01), supporting H5a–b. Table 3 supports H6a–b: dyadic versus individual trust has stronger positive associations with integrative behaviors (.42 versus .30) and joint outcome (.37 versus .20), and a stronger negative association with distributive behaviors (−.49 versus −.29). Table 4 supports H7a: integrity has a stronger negative distributive-behavior association than broad trust (−.41 versus −.14). H7b is only partially supported: broad trust has a stronger integrative-behavior association than integrity (.42 versus .17), but their joint-outcome associations do not differ significantly (.26 versus .24; Q_B = .10).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates trust and negotiation research through social exchange theory, distinguishing value creation, value claiming, joint benefits, personal benefits, and satisfaction. Its meta-analytic model identifies both beneficial and adverse behavioral pathways, while integrative potential, dyadic versus individual trust, and the meaning of trust help explain variation in observed relationships; trust’s conceptualization is therefore a substantive theoretical issue as well as a measurement choice.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that negotiation training emphasize building mutual, dyadic trust rather than focusing only on earning or extending one party’s trust. Establishing broader trust, potentially including integrity, ability, and benevolence, may promote integrative behaviors more strongly than emphasizing integrity alone. Integrative potential helps identify situations in which building trust may improve joint outcomes, while the personal-outcome findings caution against assuming that trust necessarily increases the trustor’s own gains.

**Limitations**
Most included studies used Western samples, limiting evidence about cultural variation. Trust was frequently measured contemporaneously with behaviors or outcomes; preliminary laboratory comparisons found stronger associations under contemporaneous measurement than when trust was measured or manipulated beforehand, and the meta-analysis cannot establish temporal dynamics or reciprocal relationships. Heterogeneous definitions and measures of trust, together with limited existing data, require caution in interpreting the estimates.

**Future Research**
Examine how trust is established and changes during negotiations, how asymmetries of trust matter, and when trust yields higher versus lower or negative personal outcomes. Investigate cultural influences on trust and its relationships with negotiation behaviors and outcomes. Align measurement timing with temporal predictions, specify the theoretically appropriate form of trust, and use validated measures rather than creating new scales without that foundation.
