# Servant Leadership and Serving Culture: Influence on Individual and Unit Performance

**Abstract**
In a sample of 961 employees working in 71 restaurants of a moderately sized restaurant chain, we investigated a key tenet of servant leadership theory—that servant leaders guide followers to emulate the leader’s behavior by prioritizing the needs of others above their own. We developed and tested a model contending that servant leaders propagate servant leadership behaviors among followers by creating a serving culture, which directly influences unit (i.e., restaurant/store) performance and enhances individual attitudes and behaviors directly and through the mediating influence of individuals’ identification with the unit. As hypothesized, serving culture was positively related both to restaurant performance and employee job performance, creativity, and customer service behaviors, and negatively related to turnover intentions, both directly and through employee identification with the restaurant. Same-source common method bias was reduced by employing five sources of data: employees, restaurant managers, customers, internal audits by headquarters staff, and external audits by a consulting firm.

**Research Question**
How does store-manager servant leadership relate to unit performance and individual employee outcomes through a shared serving culture? Does employee identification with the store help explain the cross-level relationships between serving culture and employee attitudes and behaviors?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Store-manager servant leadership is positively related to serving culture.
- H2: Serving culture is positively related to store performance, a composite of carryout accuracy, delivery accuracy, customer satisfaction, facility audit, and sanitation audit.
- H3: Serving culture mediates the relationship between store-manager servant leadership and store performance.
- H4: Serving culture is positively related to employee identification with the store.
- H5: Serving culture mediates the positive relationship between store-manager servant leadership and employee identification with the store.
- H6a–d: Employee identification with the store is positively related to employee in-role performance, creativity, and customer service behaviors, and negatively related to turnover intentions.
- H7a–d: Serving culture is positively related to employee in-role performance, creativity, and customer service behaviors, and negatively related to turnover intentions.
- H8a–d: Employee identification with the store partially mediates the relationships between serving culture and employee in-role performance, creativity, customer service behaviors, and turnover intentions.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Store-manager servant leadership; serving culture is the proximal predictor of store performance and individual outcomes.
- DV(s): Store performance; employee in-role performance, creativity, customer service behaviors, and turnover intentions.
- Mediators: Store-level serving culture between manager servant leadership and store performance or employee identification; individual employee identification between serving culture and individual outcomes.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The proposed mechanism combines social learning and social identity: leaders who prioritize others' needs provide attractive role models and encourage followers to serve others, developing shared norms of helping and support throughout the unit. These norms guide employee behavior and are proposed to strengthen identification with the store, through which employees incorporate group values into their self-definitions. Identification is proposed to complement direct culture–outcome relationships; the cross-sectional evidence supports associations consistent with this mechanism, without establishing its causal sequence or directly observing the development of servant leaders among followers.

**Data & Measures**
The main study invited general managers and hourly employees from 76 restaurants in six U.S. states, randomly selecting restaurants within the chain's ten metropolitan areas with the most locations. Responses came from 71 managers and 1,143 employees; retaining employees working at least 20 hours weekly with at least one month of store tenure yielded 961 employees in 71 restaurants. Managers completed web surveys and employees completed paper surveys on site during paid work hours; calendar dates were not reported. Table 1 gives variable-specific sample sizes of 952–961 individuals and 53–71 stores.

Employees rated their manager's servant leadership using seven items, one from each dimension of Liden et al.'s original 28-item scale; serving culture used the same seven dimensions with the referent changed to managers and employees collectively. Both constructs were aggregated to the store level: ICC(1)/ICC(2) were .11/.60 for servant leadership and .07/.50 for serving culture, with median within-store agreement of .94 and .95, respectively. Employees self-reported store identification on five items and turnover intentions on five items; managers rated each employee's in-role performance, creativity, and customer service behaviors on four-item scales. These survey responses used seven-point agreement scales. Store performance combined customer ratings of carryout accuracy, delivery accuracy, and satisfaction, headquarters staff facility audits, and an external firm's sanitation audits; the five indicators were z-standardized before model estimation so each contributed equally to the composite.

The associational multilevel path model used manifest variables, robust maximum likelihood, random slopes from individual identification to individual outcomes, and 20,000 Monte Carlo replications for indirect-effect confidence intervals. A separate scale-validation sample from a large real estate company comprised 190 employees with complete 28-item servant leadership data, including 178 complete dyads also supplying manager-rated organizational citizenship behavior directed toward the organization (OCB-O); the seven- and 28-item leadership composites correlated .97, and both predicted OCB-O. This supplementary sample validated the shortened scale rather than testing the restaurant model.

**Key Findings**
The authors report support for H1–H8. Figure 1 labels the direct coefficients as unstandardized: manager servant leadership was positively associated with serving culture (b = .54, p < .01; H1), which was positively associated with store performance (b = .33, p < .05; H2) and employee identification (b = .68, p < .01; H4). The reported indirect association of leadership with store performance through serving culture was .18 (95% CI [.03, .35]; H3), and that with employee identification was .37 (95% CI [.22, .54]; H5).

Identification was positively associated with in-role performance (b = .04), creativity (b = .06), and customer service behaviors (b = .06), all p < .05, and negatively associated with turnover intentions (b = −.23, p < .01), supporting H6a–d. Serving culture retained direct positive associations with in-role performance (b = .35, p < .05), creativity (b = .43, p < .05), and customer service behaviors (b = .52, p < .01), and a negative association with turnover intentions (b = −.53, p < .01), supporting H7a–d. Table 2 and the results text report identification-mediated indirect effects of .03 (95% CI [.002, .06]) for in-role performance, .02 ([.01, .07]) for creativity, .02 ([.01, .08]) for customer service behaviors, and −.16 ([−.23, −.09]) for turnover intentions, supporting H8a–d. The authors characterize identification's mediation as partial, with small indirect effects for the three behavioral outcomes and a moderate indirect effect for turnover intentions. In an alternative specification, additional direct paths from manager servant leadership to store performance, individual outcomes, and identification were nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces serving culture as a unit-level mechanism linking servant leadership with both collective performance and individual outcomes, extending the focus beyond leader–follower dyads. Integrating social learning and social identity theories, it distinguishes the shared behavioral norms of serving others from individuals' identification with their store and presents evidence consistent with both processes. The positive identification–creativity association also supports the authors' argument that a serving culture's empowerment and encouragement of divergent views can accompany identification rather than simply conformity.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend training and encouraging managers to adopt servant leadership behaviors, arguing that these relate to performance as well as a pleasant work atmosphere. They propose that coworker interactions can sustain serving norms when leaders cannot remain continuously present, and that such a culture can promote employees' cognitive acceptance of group values alongside affective attachment.

**Limitations**
The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; employee behaviors might shape culture perceptions and subsequently reinforce leaders' behavior. Seven-item short forms prevent analysis of the separate servant leadership and serving culture dimensions; their overlapping behavioral content and different but overlapping referents complicate isolation of leader influence, while the culture measure captures behavioral norms rather than the full domain of values and artifacts. Customer service behavior was measured, but employee servant leadership toward customers was not; the authors also note that the restaurant setting's customer orientation and interdependent jobs may favor the proposed processes, and that women constituted only 7% of the leaders.

**Future Research**
The authors call for longitudinal research on causal ordering and direct assessment of employees' servant leadership toward customers and the development of servant leaders among followers. They propose comparing serving culture with other group mediators such as justice climate and team potency, examining additional individual mechanisms such as trust and empowerment, and testing generalizability with professional employees in another industry. They also recommend samples with more women leaders to examine follower responses by leader sex and further theoretical integration across overlapping leadership approaches.
