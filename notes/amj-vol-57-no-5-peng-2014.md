# Social Exchange Implications of Own and CoWorkers' Experiences of Supervisory Abuse

**Abstract**
This study examines how the quality of individuals’ social exchange relationships with their leaders and separately, their work unit peers, mediate the interactive effects of abusive supervision directed toward themselves (own abusive supervision) and toward their work unit peers (peer abusive supervision) on individual task performance and helping behavior directed toward coworkers. Testing the model using a sample of 358 workers from 25 organizations, we found support for our hypotheses that leader– member exchange (LMX) and affect-based trust in peers each partially mediated the interactive effects of own and peer abusive supervision. Workers that exhibited high levels of task performance and engaged in more helping behaviors tended to have both low own abusive supervision and low peer abusive supervision. Own abusive supervision was unrelated to the behavioral outcomes among workers whose peers were victims of supervisory abuse. We discuss the implications of relational dynamics with peers and the immediate supervisor for understanding the scope of the impact of abusive supervision on follower outcomes.

**Research Question**
How does abusive supervision directed toward work-unit peers condition the relationships between an employee’s own abusive supervision and task performance and helping coworkers? Do leader–member exchange (LMX) and affect-based trust in peers separately mediate these interactive relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Own abusive supervision is more negatively associated with LMX when peer abusive supervision is low rather than high.
- H2: Own abusive supervision is more negatively associated with affect-based trust in peers when peer abusive supervision is low rather than high.
- H3: Own abusive supervision is more negatively associated with task performance when peer abusive supervision is low rather than high.
- H4a–b: LMX (H4a) and affect-based trust in peers (H4b) each partially mediate the interaction of own and peer abusive supervision on task performance; the negative indirect effect of own abusive supervision is stronger at low than high peer abusive supervision.
- H5: Own abusive supervision is more negatively associated with helping coworkers when peer abusive supervision is low rather than high.
- H6a–b: LMX (H6a) and affect-based trust in peers (H6b) each partially mediate the interaction of own and peer abusive supervision on helping coworkers; the indirect effect of own abusive supervision is stronger at low than high peer abusive supervision.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Own abusive supervision, referring to an employee’s experience of abusive behavior by the immediate supervisor.
- DV(s): Individual task performance and helping behavior directed toward work-unit coworkers.
- Mediators: LMX and, separately, affect-based trust in peers, representing the quality of social exchange relationships with the leader and peers.
- Moderators: Peer abusive supervision, the extent to which other work-unit members experience abuse from the same leader.

The authors propose a substitution pattern: either personally experiencing abuse or learning that peers are abused can undermine trustful exchange relationships, leaving less additional scope for own abuse to matter when peer abuse is already high. Abuse can signal an untrustworthy leader, diminished respect for the individual or group, and an interpersonal environment unfavorable to reciprocal exchanges. Weaker leader and peer exchanges may reduce resources, information, reciprocal obligations and willingness to help, linking abuse to poorer performance and helping. These mechanisms are theorized and assessed through cross-sectional indirect associations, not established causally.

**Data & Measures**
The main study recruited 120 work units from 25 organizations in China; 411 employees volunteered for an Internet survey, with a reported response rate of 75.4%. Excluding 53 individuals who received fewer than three peer ratings left 358 employees in 88 work units. The stated organizational breakdown—21 for-profit companies, two nonprofits and one governmental institute—does not reconcile with the reported total of 25; collection dates and specific industries are not reported. Own abuse used five Tepper items rated from 1 (never) to 5 (almost always), with alpha = .92; peer abuse was the mean of all other unit members’ own-abuse ratings, excluding the focal employee. An alternative measure asked the focal employee about the supervisor’s treatment of coworkers using the adapted five items (alpha = .93).

LMX used five Liden and Maslyn items covering affect and contribution (alpha = .86), interpreted as an index of affect-based trust in the leader. Affect-based trust in peers used four adapted McAllister items (alpha = .87). Each participant rated coworkers’ task performance using four Williams and Anderson items (alpha = .92) and helping using three Van Dyne and LePine items (alpha = .88); the multiple peer ratings were averaged for each target employee. LMX, peer trust and the behavioral outcomes used five-point agreement scales. Gender, education and job tenure were initially controlled but omitted from final analyses because their exclusion did not noticeably change results. The main analyses used hierarchical OLS with grand-mean-centered predictors and separate first-stage moderated mediation models with bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals; high and low peer abuse were defined at one standard deviation above and below its mean. Supplementary HLM addressed nesting, and a separate sample of 229 full-time employees completed LMX and leader affect-based trust measures for a convergence test; that sample’s country and collection period are not stated.

**Key Findings**
H1, H2, H3 and H5 received support: the own-abuse × peer-abuse interactions were positive for LMX (b = .17), peer trust (b = .18), task performance (b = .13) and helping (b = .16), all p < .01 (Table 2, unstandardized coefficients). LMX, peer trust and both behaviors were highest when both forms of abuse were low. Own abuse was negatively associated with performance at low peer abuse (t = −3.01, p < .01) but not high peer abuse (t = .35, nonsignificant); the corresponding helping slopes were t = −3.29 (p < .01) and t = −.50 (nonsignificant).

The authors report support for H4a–b and H6a–b, with stronger negative indirect effects at low than high peer abuse (Table 3). For task performance, the low/high conditional indirect estimates were −.04/−.02 through LMX (difference = .02, p < .05) and −.06/−.02 through peer trust (difference = .04, p < .01). For helping, they were −.08/−.04 through LMX (difference = .04, p < .05) and −.11/−.04 through peer trust (difference = .07, p < .01). All low-peer-abuse indirect effects were significant at p < .01; the high-peer-abuse effects through peer trust were significant at p < .05, whereas the high-peer-abuse LMX-to-helping indirect effect was nonsignificant. The paper is internally inconsistent about the high-peer-abuse LMX-to-performance indirect effect of −.02: the prose calls it nonsignificant, but Table 3 marks it p < .05. Table 3 also marks the high-peer-abuse own-abuse-to-peer-trust path of −.15 nonsignificant in the performance model but p < .05 in the helping model; these entries are retained as reported rather than reconciled.

The authors report that the two exchange processes together explain about half the interaction, with peer trust accounting for about twice the proportion explained by LMX. Supplementary HLM retained significant interactions for all four outcomes, and results with the alternative peer-abuse measure were reported as consistent with the main findings. In the separate convergence sample, LMX correlated .86 with affect-based trust in the leader; their latent correlation of .91 exceeded the square roots of average variance explained for LMX (.88) and leader trust (.81), leading the authors to conclude that these measures were not empirically distinct.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends abusive-supervision research beyond victims’ direct relationships with supervisors by incorporating coworkers’ mistreatment as a context for leader–follower and peer–peer exchanges. Its social exchange account combines direct and indirect reciprocity to explain why low own abuse may offer little behavioral advantage when peers are abused, and why damaged peer relationships merit attention alongside LMX. The authors also contrast the observed reduction in coworker helping with third-party justice accounts emphasizing support for victims, while treating competition, personal consequences and group cohesion as possible explanations or boundaries rather than tested mechanisms.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend educating managers about the direct and indirect interpersonal consequences of abusive supervision and subjecting supervisors with persistent abusive behavior to corrective discipline or possible termination. They also advocate training and empowering workers to confront abuse within an ethical, psychologically safe climate, supported by senior managers’ conduct. Awareness that abuse can weaken cooperation among peers may motivate senior management to establish a zero-tolerance policy.

**Limitations**
The cross-sectional design cannot establish the proposed causal order: performance or helping may influence exchange relationships, and performance or LMX may influence abusive supervision; LMX was assessed from followers’ perspectives. The exchange variables leave substantial portions of the interaction unexplained. Generalizability is limited by the Chinese setting and established work groups, because cultural tolerance of abuse and relationships still being formed may alter the observed associations.

**Future Research**
The authors propose measuring abuse, exchange relationships and performance over time to test causal direction, and examining additional attributional and emotional processes, including internal versus external explanations for mistreatment. They call for tests of whether social cohesion encourages observers to support abuse victims, and whether findings extend to other cultural contexts. They also suggest studying groups in which followers are establishing relationships with new leaders and peers.
