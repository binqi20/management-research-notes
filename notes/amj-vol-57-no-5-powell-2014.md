# It's What You Make of It: Founder Identity and Enacting Strategic Responses to Adversity

**Abstract**
We conducted a longitudinal field study of 13 resource-constrained founder-run textile and apparel firms to understand how and why firms vary in their strategic responses to the same adversity. We discovered that founders enact distinctly different definitions of the adversity and use their firms as vehicles to defend who they are or to become who they want to be. Bridging two formerly disparate social psychological theories of identity, we develop grounded theory and a process model that together contribute toward our understanding of how and why differences in the structure of founder identity—the set of identities that is chronically salient to a founder in her/his day-to-day work—drive patterned differences in firms’ strategic responses. The processes we describe help explain responses to adversity and also provide a platform for research that may generate new insights into the significance for founders of bringing “who I am” into closer alignment with “who I want to be.”

**Research Question**
How and why do resource-constrained founder-run firms respond to adversity in widely varied ways? The study examines how founders' chronically salient identities shape the adversity they enact and their firms' strategic responses.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key construct: Founder identity is the set of identities chronically salient to a founder in day-to-day work. Incongruence means that behaving in accordance with one such identity constrains behavior consistent with another; congruence means these identities do not impose that interference. Singular identity refers to salience in running the business, not to a person's complete set of identities.
- Transforming path: Multiple incongruent identities → adversity enacted as an opportunity to embrace → critiquing and valorizing narratives aimed at retaining some existing stakeholders and creating new ties → attempts to transform the firm and construct roles expressing previously suppressed social identities.
- Sustaining path: Multiple congruent identities → adversity enacted as a challenge to counter → authenticating narratives strengthening existing ties and, where needed, seeking replacements → attempts to sustain the firm's existing approach and affirm complementary identities.
- Accepting path: A singular keeper-of-the-bottom-line identity → adversity enacted as a threat to accommodate → justifying narratives accompanying the surrender of ties → cost cutting and shrinking operations to accommodate reduced revenues.

The inducted process links identity structure to strategic response through enactment and ideological narratives. Founders bracket some features of adversity as pertinent while downplaying others, producing different definitions of objectively similar conditions. Their narratives seek to align stakeholders with the response and elicit positive reflected appraisals that construct or defend valued role identities. Social identities can supply aspirations for new roles, while interactions with stakeholders can affirm those roles; these are grounded theoretical explanations rather than experimentally identified causal effects.

**Data & Measures**
The authors screened 42 candidate firms and studied 13 lead founders and their firms in southeastern U.S. textile and apparel manufacturing. Eligibility required founder ownership and management, similar objective adversity and inadequate tangible resource slack; resource constraints were defined from founders' reports of shortfalls and suboptimal choices, most often financial but also involving materials, equipment and human capital. Starting with four firms, the authors added theoretically informative cases; they report saturation with nine cases and narrate six in detail. Interviews with founders and other stakeholders extended over 20 months; Table 1 reports 2–24 interviews per case. Most interviews were recorded, producing approximately 1,000 single-spaced transcript pages; average interview duration was 89 minutes, ranging from 45 minutes to six hours. Direct observation, plant visits and secondary sources supplemented interviews. Life histories located the studied identities before the adversity, and prospective observation reduced reliance on retrospective accounts. Methods prose reports 3–700 current employees, whereas Table 1 lists zero employees for ITB Frocks and O2Tex; the paper also reports that 11 firms continued operating and two disbanded.

Open in vivo coding initially identified self-descriptions, values, commitments, meanings and aspirations; comparisons then distinguished “who I am” from “who I want to be.” About a year into the project, identity theory helped interpret those codes. Eight social and eight role identity categories informed coding of singular, congruent and incongruent structures; most salient identities combined social and role elements. Case reports, matrices and comparisons across cases and then across identity-structure groups connected these structures with definitions of adversity, ideological narratives and response paths. Both authors independently coded all 13 cases, reporting 100% agreement on identity structures and overall response patterns and initial agreement on at least 11 of 13 cases for other concepts, with remaining differences resolved through discussion. These are qualitative classifications supporting theory development, not quantitative measures or a causal identification design.

**Key Findings**
Table 3 maps three incongruent cases to transforming, seven congruent cases to sustaining and three singular cases to accepting. The corresponding combinations are opportunity/embrace with critiquing–valorizing narratives and new ties; challenge/counter with authenticating narratives and strengthened ties; and threat/accommodate with justifying narratives and surrendered ties. The prose introduction to congruent identities refers to “three founders,” whereas Table 3 classifies seven; Table 4 illustrates three congruent cases. These reported counts are distinct and are not silently reconciled. The keeper-of-the-bottom-line role was shared by every founder, but for most it served largely as a constraint enabling expression of other identities; only the singular group treated it as the sole chronically salient identity in running the firm.

Strategic responses are processes and attempted courses of action, not guaranteed outcomes. Transforming did not necessarily resolve incongruence: Greener's environmental focus still conflicted with its founder's caring-boss and financial roles. Successful transforming required valuable new stakeholder ties; successful sustaining required retaining or replacing ties; successful accepting required shedding unsupportable ties while maintaining viable ones. The authors explicitly limit their theory to intentions, goals and behaviors, with success contingent on stakeholder responses rather than explained by the model.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study develops a process account of variation in strategic responses to similar adversity, connecting the structure of founders' identities to how they enact situations and engage stakeholders. It bridges social identity theory and identity theory by showing how social identification can generate aspirations for roles that founders construct through their firms and seek to confirm through reflected appraisals. Focusing on singularity and congruence offers a more parsimonious account than cataloguing identity contents and treats single versus multiple salient founder identities as an empirical question.

**Limitations**
Theoretical sampling of resource-constrained, founder-owned and managed textile firms in the southeastern United States limits direct empirical generalizability despite the aim of theoretical generalization. Strong founder leadership is a boundary condition: in larger firms, subcultures or coalitions might dilute founder influence, and responses under environmental munificence remain untested. The tightly fitted grounded theory does not describe stochastic variation well; the authors expect quantitative testing to reveal greater variability and additional moderators of the three paths.

**Future Research**
The authors call for tests in other firms and environments, joint use of social identity theory and identity theory, and research on single versus multiple salient identities, additional identity-structure features, entrepreneurial passion and optimal distinctiveness. They propose studying the persistence of founder-identity effects after founders depart and whether multiple identities provide conceptual slack that can substitute for material or financial resource slack in responses to adversity. Further work could examine personal authenticity and how consistency with prior identities, ideological narratives and dramaturgy influence existing and new stakeholders' acceptance of attempts to transform a firm; these are proposed extensions rather than established results.
