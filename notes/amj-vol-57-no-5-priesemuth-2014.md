# Abusive Supervision Climate: A Multiple-Mediation Model of its Impact on Group Outcomes

**Abstract**
In this paper, we introduce the construct of abusive supervision climate, the collective perceptions employees hold regarding abusive supervision in their work unit. We thereby extend research on abusive supervision to the team level, which allows us to explore its relationship with outcomes not addressed by individual-level theories of abuse. First, we explain the emergence of abusive supervision climate through the lens of social information processing theory. Then, drawing on team process and effectiveness models, we develop a multiple-mediation model that identifies two distinct mechanisms by which abusive supervision climate impacts group-level outcomes: social identity and collective efficacy. Results demonstrate that abusive supervision climate influences social- and task-related group outcomes through these two mediation processes.

**Research Question**
How does abusive supervision become a shared work-unit climate, and how is that climate related to group cooperation, citizenship behavior, and performance through group identification and collective efficacy? A separate construct-validity study examines whether climate is distinct from personal abuse perceptions and predicts individual outcomes beyond those perceptions.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Group identification mediates the relationship between abusive supervision climate and group cooperation and OCB; the theorized indirect relationships are negative.
- H2: Collective efficacy mediates the relationship between abusive supervision climate and group performance; the theorized indirect relationship is negative.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Abusive supervision climate, the shared perception of supervisory abuse directed toward members of the work unit, distinguished from individual perceptions of personal abuse.
- DV(s): Group cooperation, group organizational citizenship behavior (OCB), and group performance in the main mediation model.
- Mediators: Group identification, representing interpersonal teamwork processes, for cooperation and OCB; collective efficacy, representing task teamwork processes, for performance.
- Moderators: None in the hypothesized mediation model; supplementary analyses examine the proportion of work-unit members surveyed as a possible moderator.

Social information processing and sensemaking explain how employees' interactions can produce shared interpretations of supervisory abuse; a referent-shift consensus model links perceptions of typical group experiences to the collective climate. The authors argue that an abusive climate communicates low group value and status and normalizes hostile interactions, weakening identification and group-oriented behavior. They also propose that abuse undermines psychological safety and beliefs in the group's capabilities, reducing collective efficacy and performance. Sensemaking and psychological safety are explanatory arguments, not separately tested mediators; the cross-sectional evidence cannot establish the causal ordering.

**Data & Measures**
The main study recruited regional organizations through 453 undergraduate business student contacts at a university in the southeastern United States; 222 contacts completed the process, yielding 882 employee responses from 222 work units and supervisor-rated outcomes. The protocol sought a focal employee, three coworkers, and their immediate supervisor; the realized mean was 3.97 employee respondents per unit. Employees reported climate, identification, efficacy, group size, and tenure; supervisors rated the three outcomes. Collection dates and an industry breakdown are not reported.

Abusive supervision climate uses five Mitchell–Ambrose items adapted from Tepper, shifted to the treatment of group members and averaged across respondents within each work unit (α = .94; rwg = .87; ICC(1) = .52; ICC(2) = .81). Group identification uses four Doosje–Ellemers–Spears items and collective efficacy four Jex–Bliese items with a group referent (both α = .87); agreement checks support aggregation. Supervisor-rated cooperation retains four Chatman–Flynn items after removing a reverse-coded item (α = .70); OCB uses the five-item Podsakoff et al. helping scale (α = .85); performance uses six Tjosvold et al. effectiveness/productivity items (α = .88). These focal scales use five-point agreement responses. The group-outcome analyses control average group size and average departmental tenure; CFA and SEM assess construct separation and mediation, allowing the two mediators' error terms to covary and the three outcomes' error terms to covary.

The separate construct-validity sample, recruited using the same sampling method, comprises 237 focal employees and 919 coworkers from 237 work units after approaching 843 organizational contacts. An eleven-factor CFA distinguishes climate, individual abuse, and nine individual outcomes. Established scales assess job satisfaction, turnover intentions, organizational commitment, work–family conflict, emotional exhaustion, overall well-being, performance, OCB, and antisocial behavior; regressions control age, gender, and personal abuse perceptions. This sample is separate from the main group-outcome sample, and its associations do not establish causality.

**Key Findings**
In the main study's full-mediation specification, climate is negatively related to group identification (β = −.48) and collective efficacy (β = −.50); identification is positively related to cooperation (β = .32) and OCB (β = .28), and efficacy to performance (β = .31), all p < .01. The partial-mediation model fits better (Δχ² = 9.4, Δdf = 3, p < .05). In that model, direct climate associations remain negative for cooperation (β = −.18, p < .05) and performance (β = −.22, p < .01), while the direct OCB path is nonsignificant. Reported indirect effects are negative for cooperation (β = −.12), OCB (β = −.12), and performance (β = −.10), all p < .01. The authors therefore support H1 and H2, describing full mediation for OCB and partial mediation for cooperation and performance within the fitted models.

In the separate construct-validity study, the eleven-factor CFA fits better than alternatives combining constructs. Controlling age, gender, and individual abuse, climate is positively related to turnover intentions (β = .56), emotional exhaustion (β = .55), and work–family conflict (β = .27), and negatively related to job satisfaction (β = −.42), commitment (β = −.34), and overall well-being (β = −.27); p < .01 except work–family conflict, p < .05. Climate has no significant incremental relationship with individual performance, OCB, or antisocial behavior.

Supplementary analyses of the main sample show that respondents represent 51% of work-unit members on average; the percentage participating does not significantly moderate climate's relationships with either mediator or any of the three outcomes. This addresses one representativeness concern without eliminating the acknowledged risk of selection bias.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends abusive supervision from individual victim perceptions to a collective work-unit climate, using social information processing and referent-shift consensus to explain its emergence and conceptual distinctiveness. Integrating work-climate research with group process and effectiveness models, it distinguishes identification and collective efficacy as interpersonal and task-related pathways to different group outcomes. The separate validity study also identifies climate associations with individual attitudes, conflict, and health beyond personal abuse, while retaining null incremental findings for individual behaviors.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest low tolerance for abuse and training for abusive supervisors as direct organizational responses. Because residual pockets of abuse may persist, they also propose training and activities that strengthen collective efficacy or social identity as potential buffers for work teams; these interventions were not tested in the study.

**Limitations**
The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and the mediation model does not test potential substantive moderators and likely does not fully capture the complexity of the relationships. Recruitment gave limited guidance on suitable work units and could not verify random distribution of surveys, creating possible selection toward similar coworkers and distorted within-group agreement. Sampling only some members of each work unit remains a representativeness concern despite the supplementary participation-percentage checks.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining climate strength and task interdependence as possible boundary conditions. Remaining direct associations with cooperation and performance motivate investigation of additional interpersonal and task-related mediating processes. They also suggest studying whether personal abusive supervision relates to individual identification with the group and individual efficacy, extending the collective mechanisms to individual-level outcomes.
