# Antecedents of Individuals' Interteam Coordination: Broad Functional Experiences as a Mixed Blessing

**Abstract**
This manuscript investigates the role of individual team members’ breadth of functional experience for their interteam coordination behavior. Integrating personal construct and social identity theories, we examine interpersonal cognitive complexity as a mediating variable and organizational identification as a moderator. We test our hypotheses across two independent field studies, comprising an international peace support training mission (Study 1) and a municipality administration (Study 2). Corroborating our predictions, interpersonal cognitive complexity appeared as a conditional mediating variable that can translate an individual’s breadth of functional experience into interteam coordination. The strength and direction of this indirect relationship, however, depended on the individual’s identification with the organization as a whole. Moreover, on the team level of analysis, we found members’ overall interteam coordination to positively relate with team performance in Study 2. All in all, this paper advances new knowledge on the antecedents, mechanisms, contingency factors, and team-level consequences of members’ boundary spanning.

**Research Question**
How and under what conditions does an individual’s breadth of functional experience relate to interteam coordination through interpersonal cognitive complexity? Does a team’s overall interteam coordination relate positively to its performance?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: An individual’s breadth of functional experience is positively related to interpersonal cognitive complexity.
- H2: Organizational identification moderates the cognitive complexity–interteam coordination relationship: positive when identification is higher and negative when it is lower.
- H3: Organizational identification moderates the indirect relationship between breadth of functional experience and interteam coordination through interpersonal cognitive complexity: positive at higher identification and negative at lower identification.
- H4: A team’s overall interteam coordination is positively associated with team performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Individual breadth of functional experience; team-level overall interteam coordination for H4.
- DV(s): Individual interteam coordination; team performance for H4.
- Mediators: Interpersonal cognitive complexity in the individual-level breadth–coordination relationship.
- Moderators: Organizational identification on the complexity–coordination relationship and, consequently, the indirect breadth–coordination relationship.

The authors propose that exposure to different functional domains expands the interpersonal constructs individuals use to interpret diverse social situations, creating cognitive capacity for interteam coordination. Organizational identification supplies the motivation to direct that capacity toward organization-wide goals: highly identified, cognitively complex members can reconcile different teams’ perspectives, whereas those with low identification may use their capacity to resist coordination demands. Refusal strategies are a proposed explanation rather than a directly measured process. Members’ coordination can also generate team-level access to external support and help resolve task uncertainties, potentially benefiting their own team’s performance; the studies test associations rather than establish these causal mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 examined a one-week international peace-support training exercise involving 34 teams. Of 172 targeted headquarters members, 69 returned usable surveys across 22 teams (40% response); Table 2 reports a regression sample of 67 individuals. Surveys were distributed two days before the exercise ended. Breadth of functional experience used Bunderson’s version of Blau’s heterogeneity index, 1 − Σᵢpᵢ²: divide years in each functional domain by total work-experience years, square each share, sum across domains, and subtract from 1; there were 11 domains, with a theoretical maximum of .91. Interpersonal cognitive complexity used an untimed Role Category Questionnaire describing a liked and a disliked work peer; two raters counted distinct interpersonal constructs, and their ratings were averaged (ICC[2] = .96). Organizational identification used Mael and Ashforth’s six-item measure, referring to the training coalition rather than respondents’ home agencies (α = .84). Individual interteam coordination counted the external teams with which respondents regularly coordinated activities; this count was square-root transformed. Perceived interteam interdependence was retained as a control; organizational tenure and time devoted to leadership activities were considered but excluded from the main models.

Study 2 surveyed a Dutch municipal administration: 295 randomly selected employees were invited, and 121 supplied complete responses across 36 of 40 teams (41% response). Breadth used the same formula across 16 functional domains; cognitive complexity used the same RCQ procedure (two-rater ICC[2] = .97). Identification used the same scale with its media-criticism item omitted (α = .87). Individual coordination counted specific contacts in other teams with whom respondents regularly coordinated policy advice, joint projects, task groups, or daily tasks; scores were square-root transformed. For team-level coordination, the authors also included 84 employees who supplied coordination information without completing the other focal measures: a team’s score counted distinct other teams with a linkage reported by at least one member on either side. Team performance covered 33 teams, based on supervisor ratings of 454 individuals gathered approximately three months after the initial survey. Its two components were members’ average contribution to collective team outcomes and average effectiveness in realizing the team’s strategic-plan goals; the two items were standardized separately and then averaged. Main individual-level controls were perceived interteam interdependence and formal leadership position; gender, education, and tenure were considered but omitted, as were the main team-level controls of size, average tenure, and average interdependence.

Both studies used OLS for individual-level hypothesis tests because coordination was not detectably clustered by team (ICC[1] = .00). Predictors were standardized, while Tables 2 and 4 report unstandardized regression coefficients. The Edwards–Lambert moderated-mediation procedure used 1,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for conditional indirect relationships at organizational identification ±1 SD. Individual-level variables were collected at one time, so these analyses are associational; Study 2’s later performance measurement does not establish causality.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported H1: breadth of functional experience was positively associated with cognitive complexity (B = .39, SE = .16, p < .05). The complexity × identification interaction was positive (B = .45, SE = .14, p < .01), but H2 received only partial support: complexity related positively to coordination at high identification (B = .70, SE = .19, p < .01), while the low-identification slope was nonsignificant rather than significantly negative (B = −.20, SE = .23). H3 also received partial support: the high-identification indirect relationship was .27 (95% CI [.04, .65]), whereas the low-identification estimate was −.08 (95% CI [−.36, .06]). H4 was not tested in Study 1.

Study 2 supported H1–H3. Breadth was positively associated with complexity (B = .67, SE = .21, p < .01), and the complexity × identification interaction was positive (B = .53, SE = .15, p < .01). Complexity related positively to coordination at high identification (B = .49, SE = .19, p = .01) and negatively at low identification (B = −.57, SE = .22, p = .01). The conditional indirect relationship was .33 at high identification (95% CI [.06, .86]) and −.38 at low identification (95% CI [−1.00, −.09]). The direct breadth–coordination coefficients in Tables 2 and 4 were nonsignificant. The authors regard the negative pathway as tentative across the two studies because Study 1 did not show a significant negative relationship.

Study 2 supported H4: overall team coordination correlated positively with team performance (r = .44, p < .05); a regression including all proposed team-level covariates also showed a positive association (B = .47, SE = .16, p < .01). Both studies produced largely equivalent individual-level results with untransformed coordination scores, and their individual-level hypothesis tests remained virtually unchanged when excluding or including the proposed controls. In Study 2, replacing the individual-contact count with an external-team count led to the same conclusions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper identifies individual-level foundations of team boundary spanning, showing why broad functional experience need not translate uniformly into interteam coordination. Integrating personal construct theory’s cognitive capacities with social identity theory’s motivational direction explains the conditional indirect relationship, while the evidence for a negative pathway is stronger in Study 2 than Study 1. It also identifies breadth of working adults’ functional experience as an antecedent associated with interpersonal cognitive complexity and links collective coordination to team performance in Study 2.

**Practical Implication**
For members whose roles require interteam coordination, including project teams and multiteam systems, the authors suggest selecting people with broad functional experience and advanced interpersonal cognitive complexity and using cross-functional job rotation, personnel movement, and training to provide diverse social experiences. These measures may be insufficient without organizational identification: managers can emphasize organizational distinctiveness and prestige, shared goals, and inclusive language such as “we” when referring to multiple teams.

**Limitations**
Cross-sectional individual-level data preclude causal conclusions, and the authors acknowledge low response rates and common-source concerns, although factual reports, the performance-based RCQ, and independent team-performance ratings provide counterarguments. H4 was tested only in Study 2, with a relatively small team sample and a performance measure designed by the host organization for applied use. Both settings involved relatively stable teams and long-tenured members, so generalization to flexible, dynamic team memberships requires further evidence; the negative low-identification pathway also remains tentative because its significance differed across studies.

**Future Research**
The authors call for longitudinal or experimental evidence, alternative measures such as peer-rated boundary spanning, and tests in settings with flexible team membership. They propose examining specific refusal tactics and whether political, self-interested climates or policies mandating or rewarding coordination condition the negative pathway; extending the model to scouting and ambassadorial activities may require attention to team identification, while complex stakeholder environments may moderate the team coordination–performance relationship. They also recommend improving the feasibility of the labor-intensive RCQ through trusting participant relationships, researchers’ personal commitment, and carefully limited survey length.
