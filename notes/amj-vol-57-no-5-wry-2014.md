# Hybrid Vigor: Securing Venture Capital by Spanning Categories in Nanotechnology

**Abstract**
This study develops and tests a set of novel theoretical predictions about the conditions under which category spanning is rewarded by external audiences. To do this, we revisit the assumption that comprehensible organizational identities are associated with individual categories. Drawing on insights from cognitive psychology, we suggest that category spanning does not necessarily lead to confusion, but, rather, to interpretations that rely on a “header–modifier” structure where one category anchors cognition but is modified by features of the other. Audiences may have clear understandings about how categories fit together and cognate schema for evaluating firms that hybridize by spanning between them. An empirical examination of venture capital in the carbon nanotechnology industry supports our approach: start-ups were rewarded or punished for hybridization contingent on how they mixed “science” and “technology” in their patents, top management team, and collaborations. As such, we show that the category a firm starts in, how it hybridizes, and the degree to which this affects core versus peripheral identity markers may all affect how it is perceived.

**Research Question**
When is category spanning rewarded rather than punished by external audiences? The study asks how a CNT start-up's category of origin and its hybridization of core versus peripheral identity markers shape its likelihood of securing venture capital.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Science start-ups that hybridize their product offerings by securing technology patents will be more likely to receive venture capital funding.
- H2: Technology start-ups that hybridize their product offerings by securing basic science patents will be less likely to receive venture capital funding.
- H3: Science start-ups that hybridize their top management team by hiring executives with technology commercialization expertise will be more likely to receive venture capital funding.
- H4: Technology start-ups that hybridize their top management team by hiring executives with basic science expertise will be less likely to receive venture capital funding.
- H5: Science start-ups that hybridize by collaborating with actors possessing technology commercialization expertise will be less likely to receive venture capital funding.
- H6: Technology start-ups that hybridize by collaborating with actors possessing basic science expertise will be more likely to receive venture capital funding.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cross-category patenting, top management team hires, and collaborations, each measured as yearly and cumulative counts relative to the firm's founding category.
- DV(s): Receipt of venture capital investment in a firm-year.
- Mediators: No mediator is directly tested; audience attention, interpretation, and header–modifier cognition supply the proposed explanation.
- Moderators: Science versus technology category of origin, examined in separate samples, and core identity markers (products and top management) versus peripheral markers (collaborations).

The composite concepts approach proposes that audiences can understand and value category combinations through an asymmetric header–modifier structure: one category anchors interpretation while the other modifies it. VC interviews indicate a preference for technology as the header and science as the modifier. Hybridizing core features is expected to change the header through which the firm is interpreted, favoring science start-ups moving toward technology but disadvantaging technology start-ups moving toward science; peripheral hybridization is expected to leave the header intact and reverse this pattern. These are theorized evaluative processes, not directly observed mediation effects, and the main results do not support every predicted penalty.

**Data & Measures**
The authors identified 62 CNT start-ups through USPTO patents in StarTechZD and industry sources, dropping four for unavailable data and retaining 58 firms. The panel contains 180 science and 202 technology firm-years; firms enter when they apply for their first CNT patent and exit upon acquisition or bankruptcy. Predictors cover 1993–2004 and are updated annually and lagged one year relative to 1994–2005 funding outcomes. Zephyr provides investment records; archived websites, publications, patents, and founder interviews provide firm histories. The archival search yielded approximately 15,000 web pages covering 60 start-ups, supplemented with interviews with the founders of 24 firms. Separate contextual interviewing comprised 13 interviews with eight people (five VC partners and three intellectual property specialists); the authors explicitly distinguish this from a mixed-method study.

Category of origin combines initial patent focus with founder expertise; website presentations or founder accounts resolve mixed or divergent cases, yielding 27 science and 31 technology start-ups. Patent knowledge in biology, chemistry and medicine, or other sciences is coded as science, with the other StarTechZD areas coded as technology. Product hybridization is proxied by out-of-category patents dated by application. TMT hybridization counts hires in the top two management levels: basic science PhDs are science hires, while engineering backgrounds or prior positions in other start-ups or established firms indicate technology hires; CFOs are excluded. Collaboration measures identify cross-category partners through patent assignees and inventors, publications, websites, and interviews, checking individuals' research backgrounds. Each hybridization dimension has yearly and cumulative measures. The binary DV equals 1 in years with VC funding, avoiding incomplete and noncomparable deal-size information.

Separate random-effects probit models include controls for the National Nanotechnology Initiative period, firm emergence and age, market focus, patent counts and citations, star founder status, TMT size, previous grants, and previous venture capital. This is an associational design; feedback analyses find hybridization responsive to investment, so lagging predictors and checking first-investment models do not establish causal identification.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: science start-ups' technology patenting predicts a higher likelihood of VC funding (Table 3, yearly Model 2: b = .380, p < .01; cumulative full Model 11: b = .083, p < .05).
- H2 is supported: technology start-ups' science patenting predicts a lower likelihood of funding (Table 4, yearly Model 2: b = −.471, p < .05; cumulative full Model 11: b = −.324, p < .01).
- H3 receives marginal support for yearly technology hires among science start-ups (Table 3, Model 5: b = .260, p < .10), with stronger support for cumulative hires (full Model 11: b = .766, p < .05).
- H4 is unsupported in the main probit analyses: science hires do not significantly predict technology start-ups' funding (Table 4, cumulative full Model 11: b = .097, nonsignificant).
- H5 is unsupported: cross-category collaborations do not significantly predict science start-ups' funding (Table 3, cumulative full Model 11: b = .247, nonsignificant).
- H6 is supported: scientific collaborations predict a higher likelihood of funding for technology start-ups (Table 4, yearly Model 8: b = .711, p < .01; cumulative full Model 11: b = .282, p < .01).

Reported significance uses one-tailed tests for directional constructs and two-tailed tests for controls. Exponential hazard and fixed-effects logit checks broadly reproduce the pattern, but H4 becomes negative and significant in fixed-effects logit; the authors caution that this excludes never-funded firms and that full-model results weaken. Results are similar when firms leave the panel after their first investment. Engineering and commercialization hires separately follow the science-start-up TMT result, whereas decomposed collaboration measures have too few observations for meaningful estimates. An ordered-probit check distinguishes no investment, small or unknown investment, and investment above US$10 million: patent and TMT hybridization are associated with larger deals for science start-ups, collaboration hybridization with larger deals for technology start-ups, science patenting with a lower probability of any funding for technology start-ups, and the H4/H5 relationships remain nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study challenges the categorical imperative's assumption that intelligible identities depend on single-category focus, locating meaning and evaluation in category combinations. Its composite concepts approach connects audience cognition with organizational identity by distinguishing origin, direction of hybridization, and core versus peripheral identity cues. This extends cultural entrepreneurship research by showing how particular forms of category spanning can be associated with favorable resource acquisition rather than inevitable identity dilution.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should attend to the direction of hybridization and the differing cue validity of identity markers, because similar category combinations need not elicit similar audience reactions. Understanding the external environment and relevant audiences may help firms use hybridity to cultivate positively distinctive identities; the findings do not imply that every form of hybridization improves funding prospects.

**Limitations**
The three observed hybridization modes are not exhaustive, and organizational measures miss internal competencies and some employee-level collaborations that could affect attractiveness to investors. Managers may shape perceived hybridity through narratives, while the study does not directly examine dynamic sensemaking or the underlying cognitive microprocesses. Multiple identity dimensions may act jointly, and the analysis addresses only two categories despite the potential for more complex configurations.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying firms that start in the same category but hybridize toward different categories and firms that are born hybrids. They also call for studying how varied audiences' perceptions and evaluations may depend on firms' management of institutional complexity, and how forces for and against hybridization affect firms' decisions, including institutional- and firm-level moderators of those decisions. Further work should examine alignment between managerial and external audience category perceptions, narrative management, and the cognitive microprocesses behind evaluation. They also call for examining joint identity configurations, more than two categories, interactions between temporal and composite hybridity, and combinations differing in similarity or hierarchical level.
