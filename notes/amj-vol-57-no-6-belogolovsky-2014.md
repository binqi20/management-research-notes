# Signaling in Secret: Pay for Performance and the Incentive and Sorting Effects of Pay Secrecy

**Abstract**
Although the vast majority of U.S. firms follow a policy of pay secrecy, research provides a limited understanding of its overall utility to organizations. Building on signaling theory, we develop and test a model of the incentive and sorting effects of pay secrecy—a pay communication policy that limits employees’ access to pay-related information and discourages the discussion of pay issues—under varying pay-forperformance (PFP) system characteristics. Results of a multiround laboratory simulation largely support the proposed moderated-mediation model. They indicate that pay secrecy has an adverse impact on individual task performance that is mediated by PFP perceptions, amplified when pay determination criteria are relative (as opposed to absolute), and attenuated when performance assessment is objective (as opposed to subjective). Results also indicate that pay secrecy has a similar adverse effect on participant continuation intentions (mediated through PFP perceptions, amplified when pay determination criteria are relative, and attenuated when performance assessment is objective), particularly among high performers. These findings suggest that weak signals associated with a particular managerial practice may become salient when interpreted in the context of other practice-based signals and that, under such conditions, even weak signals may drive negative-oriented inferences, having important behavioral implications.

**Research Question**
How does pay secrecy affect individual task performance and continuation intentions through pay-for-performance perceptions, and how do pay determination criteria, performance assessment subjectivity, and individual performance condition these incentive and sorting effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: PFP perceptions mediate the adverse impact of pay secrecy on individual task performance.
- H2: Task performance moderates the second stage of the mediated relationship between pay secrecy and continuation intentions, with stronger indirect effects through PFP perceptions when task performance is high.
- H3a: Relative rather than absolute pay determination criteria strengthen the first stage of the indirect effect of pay secrecy on task performance through PFP perceptions.
- H3b: Relative rather than absolute pay determination criteria strengthen the first stage of the indirect effect of pay secrecy on continuation intentions through PFP perceptions.
- H4a: Subjectivity of performance assessment moderates the second stage of mediation, with stronger indirect effects of pay secrecy on task performance through PFP perceptions when assessment is subjective.
- H4b: Assessment subjectivity and task performance jointly moderate the second stage of mediation, with stronger indirect effects on continuation intentions when assessment is subjective and task performance is high.
- H5a: Pay secrecy and assessment subjectivity jointly moderate the second stage of mediation, with stronger indirect effects on task performance when pay is secret and assessment is subjective.
- H5b: Pay secrecy, assessment subjectivity, and task performance jointly moderate the second stage of mediation, with stronger indirect effects on continuation intentions for higher performers when pay is secret and assessment is subjective.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Pay communication policy—pay secrecy versus transparency.
- DV(s): Individual task performance at T3 and intentions to continue participating after T3.
- Mediators: PFP perceptions at T2—the perceived relationship between performance and bonus pay.
- Moderators: Relative versus absolute pay determination criteria at the first stage; subjective versus objective performance assessment at the second stage; task performance for continuation intentions; additional second-stage moderation by pay secrecy in H5a/b.

The authors theorize that intentional information asymmetry can signal managerial opportunism or deception, encouraging employees to interpret weak pay-related signals negatively and underestimate performance–pay contingencies. Relative pay determination adds uncertainty about the rewards attached to performance, making the inferences drawn from secrecy more consequential. Subjective assessment is theorized to introduce perceived noise into the effort–performance relationship, weakening the translation of PFP perceptions into performance and continuation intentions, particularly for high performers. These signaling interpretations motivate the model; the measured mediator is PFP perceptions, and the source's inconsistency concerning the direction of the assessment moderator is retained in Key Findings.

**Data & Measures**
One laboratory experiment involved 320 undergraduates at an Israeli university; a software bug left five without recorded data, and 35 further observations were excluded for suspect or excessive missing data, leaving 280 analyzed participants. Participants were randomly assigned to four-person groups, and groups were randomly assigned to the eight combinations of pay communication policy, pay determination criteria, and assessment subjectivity; three performance phases supplied the repeated-measures factor. The approximately 90-minute experiment included three five-minute computer-task rounds with ten-minute breaks, NIS20 base pay, and a bonus of up to NIS8 per round. Calendar collection dates are not reported.

In the secrecy condition, participants saw their own performance and bonus and were instructed not to discuss pay; transparency added other group members' coded pay amounts, but not their performance scores, and allowed unrestricted e-mail communication between rounds. Absolute criteria allocated bonuses against predetermined performance standards; relative criteria used within-group point rankings. Objective assessment used task points, whereas participants in the subjective condition were told that points would be combined with investigator judgment; the actual subjective-condition score was 66% × objective score × a random number ranging from 0.50 to 1.50.

The analyzed PFP mediator was assessed after the second round, using a modified Erez and Isen measure: participants estimated probabilities of bonus amounts from no pay to NIS8 at performance levels of 100, 300, 500, 700, and 900, producing a five-point expected-bonus vector related to the specified performance levels. The source's computational description is ambiguous: it says probabilities assigned to bonus levels were multiplied by performance levels, summed, and divided by 100, then describes “correlating” the vectors “on the basis of regression analysis”; it does not unambiguously identify a conventional expected-bonus formula or the precise statistic. Individual task performance was the third-round score; continuation intention was a single seven-point item assessing willingness to participate in additional experimental rounds, rather than observed employee retention. Models used individual-level fixed effects and random group intercepts, held indirect effects constant across groups, controlled the performance outcome for T1 performance, and used REML for regression estimates, ML for fit comparisons, and parametric bootstrap for indirect effects.

**Key Findings**
Pay secrecy had negative total effects on task performance (estimate = −60.14 points, p < .01, Table 2 Model 2) and continuation intentions (estimate = −.38, p < .05, Table 3 Model 2). However, its unmoderated association with T2 PFP perceptions was nonsignificant (estimate = −.02), and the authors report no support for H1 or H2. Pay secrecy interacted negatively with relative pay determination in predicting PFP perceptions (estimate = −.21, p < .05): the secrecy slope was −.12 (p < .05) under relative criteria and .09 (n.s.) under absolute criteria, supporting the authors' H3a/b interpretation.

For task performance, PFP perceptions interacted negatively with subjective assessment (estimate = −226.49, p < .05, Table 2 Model 4b). The positive PFP–performance slope was 245.96 (p < .01) with objective assessment and 19.46 (n.s.) with subjective assessment. Under relative criteria and objective assessment, the reported indirect effect of secrecy was −30.02 (90% CI [−54.45, −8.88]), compared with a total effect of −112.61 (90% CI [−158.34, −67.29]); the indirect effect accounted for 26.66% of the total. The additional three-way interaction involving pay secrecy was nonsignificant, so H5a was not supported.

For continuation intentions, the PFP perceptions × subjectivity × task performance interaction was negative (estimate = −.01, p < .05, Table 3 Model 4b). Under objective assessment, the PFP slope was 3.09 (p < .05) for high performers, 2.13 (p < .05) for average performers, and 1.18 (n.s.) for low performers; none of these slopes was significant under subjective assessment. With relative criteria and objective assessment, the indirect effect of secrecy was −.26 (90% CI [−.52, −.04]) for average performers and −.38 (90% CI [−.74, −.07]) for high performers. The four-way interaction adding secrecy was nonsignificant (estimate = −.02), and H5b was not supported.

The authors label H4a/b supported, but the source contains a directional inconsistency: those hypotheses literally predict stronger adverse indirect effects under subjective assessment, and the abstract and discussion describe attenuation under objective assessment, whereas the Results report the negative conditional indirect effects above under objective assessment and stronger positive mediator–outcome slopes under objective assessment. The stated hypotheses, authors' support labels, and reported conditions are retained separately; they cannot be combined into an unqualified claim that the subjective-assessment prediction was confirmed.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling theory by treating the interpretation of a pay policy as contingent on signals inferred from related policies: secrecy's association with PFP perceptions depends on the pay determination context. Integrating positive–negative asymmetry and paranoid cognition, the authors argue that weak signals can acquire negative meaning under uncertainty and connect employer signaling through compensation practices to both performance and continuation intentions. The proposed signaling explanation and broader claim of context dependence should be distinguished from the source's inconsistent directional interpretation of assessment subjectivity.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that managers consider relaxing pay secrecy or changing PFP system attributes, particularly where relative pay criteria and subjective assessment are combined. They propose partial openness through pay ranges or aggregate schedules linking merit increases and bonuses to performance, while explicitly noting that the incentive and sorting effects of these intermediate communication practices remain untested. Where communication or PFP arrangements cannot be changed, they suggest visible rater accountability, such as evaluating raters on assessment quality, as a possible means of strengthening employees' PFP perceptions.

**Limitations**
The laboratory game may not reproduce workplace pay jealousy or appraisers' tendency to compress ratings under transparency; subjective scores were randomly modified rather than generated by actual appraisers. The minimalist assessment manipulation and approximately 90-minute duration limit mundane realism and the development of coworker relationships, and may capture immediate responses without establishing longer-term effects as employees learn about performance–pay contingencies. The authors also identify possible limited power for the higher-order interactions and the need to establish cross-cultural generalizability.

**Future Research**
The authors propose more job-like simulations with more meaningful outcomes, centrally rather than randomly biased subjective ratings, stronger assessment manipulations, and replication in both laboratory and field settings. They call for tests of whether seniority attenuates secrecy's negative effects and whether cultural norms concerning pay disclosure alter inferences of deceptive intent. They also suggest examining other pay system attributes, including pay mix and dispersion, as contextual influences on how employees interpret secrecy.
