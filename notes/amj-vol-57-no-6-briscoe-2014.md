# CEO Ideology as an Element of the Corporate Opportunity Structure for Social Activists

**Abstract**
In an effort to comprehend activism toward corporations, scholars have proposed the concept of corporate opportunity structure, or the attributes of individual firms that make them more (or less) attractive as activist targets. We theorize that the personal values of the firm’s elite decision makers constitute a key element of this corporate opportunity structure. We specifically consider the political ideology—conservatism versus liberalism—of the company’s CEO as a signal for employees who are considering the merits of engaging in activism. To test of our theory, we examine the formation of lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender employee groups in major companies in the period 1985–2004, when the formation of such groups was generally perceived to be risky for participants. Using CEOs’ records of political donations to measure their personal ideologies, we find strong evidence that the political liberalism of CEOs influences the likelihood of activism. We also find that CEOs’ ideologies influence activism more strongly when CEOs are more powerful, when they oversee more conservative (i.e., less liberal) workplaces, and when the social movement is in the early phase of development. We identify theoretical and practical implications, as well as future research opportunities.

**Research Question**
How does CEO political ideology shape the corporate opportunity structure for employee activism, and when do CEO power, workplace conservatism and movement expansion strengthen or weaken the association between CEO liberalism and LGBT employee-group formation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The more politically liberal a CEO, the greater the likelihood of employee activism within the company.
- H2: Greater CEO power strengthens the association between CEO liberalism and employee activism.
- H3: Greater workplace conservatism strengthens the association between CEO liberalism and employee activism.
- H4: The fewer companies a movement has reached, the stronger the association between CEO liberalism and employee activism.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO liberalism, representing the executive's position on a conservatism–liberalism continuum.
- DV(s): Formation of an LGBT employee activist group within the firm.
- Mediators: No focal mediator is tested; anticipated executive receptiveness and protection against resistance or reprisal are theorized processes. Supplementary tests find no evidence that firm CSR mediates the association.
- Moderators: CEO power, workplace conservatism and movement expansion; succession-related ideological contrast is examined separately as an exploratory predictor.

Employees considering risky collective action are theorized to read cues about their CEO's values to assess whether their demands will encounter tolerance, resistance or punishment. Greater CEO power makes those values more consequential, while a conservative workplace heightens activists' need for protection against reprisals. If a movement expands successfully and gains acceptance, uncertainty and perceived risk diminish, reducing reliance on individual CEO profiles; these processes interpret the observed associations rather than establish measured causal mediation.

**Data & Measures**
The HRC supplied a database in 2005 from which the authors identified 115 LGBT groups in Fortune 500 corporations; survey responses and follow-up sources yielded precise founding years for 85 groups, a 74% effective response rate. A group was considered formed when it had an explicit membership roster publicly available throughout the corporation, including to management; formal corporate recognition was not required. State-based sampling combined the 85 event observations with a random 1.5% of non-event firm-years, producing 125 non-events and 210 observations. The abstract and Methods identify 1985–2004, whereas the introduction and Figure 1 identify 1985–2005; the paper does not reconcile these endpoints. Table 1 reports a group-formation mean of .38 for n = 210, which does not agree arithmetically with the narrated 85-event count. Telephone interviews involving 21 LGBT group leaders lasted 30–90 minutes and supplied illustrations rather than systematic qualitative evidence.

CEO liberalism is a proxy constructed from verified FEC personal donations over the six to ten years before observation year t. The index averages four Democratic shares: number of donations, dollar amounts, years with donations and distinct recipients, each divided by the corresponding total for Democratic and Republican recipients; for years, the Appendix specifies years in which donations were made to either party. The Appendix states that .1 was added to all numerators and .2 to all denominators to handle zeros. The resulting four-item average has a stated 0–1 range and α = .92, with higher scores indicating greater liberalism; contributions to candidates, party committees and clearly partisan PACs are included, while other PACs are excluded. The authors do not assume that activists generally inspect donation records: the records proxy ideological leanings that employees may infer from other executive behaviors and signals.

CEO power averages five standardized components measured in year t: tenure in years, CEO-chair duality, founder status, CEO stock ownership divided by outside-director ownership, and the proportion of directors appointed after the CEO. Workplace conservatism applies the donation-index procedure to donors identifying the firm as their employer over the four years before t, using Republican rather than Democratic numerators. Movement expansion counts corporate LGBT groups formed through t−1. Controls, measured at t−1 unless otherwise indicated, cover headquarters politics, the San Francisco ordinance, technology industry, firm CSR, logged employee count, S&P 500 membership and financial performance; the performance index averages standardized industry-adjusted return on equity and market-to-book value. The observational logistic regressions adjust intercepts for state-based sampling, use robust standard errors and period dummies, and mean-center scores for moderator analyses. The exploratory succession measure subtracts predecessor liberalism from successor liberalism for the 24 observations with succession in the current or prior year and assigns zero otherwise.

**Key Findings**
All four hypotheses receive support in the reported main models (Table 2). H1: CEO liberalism is positively associated with group formation (Model 2: b = 1.77, SE = .69, p < .05). H2: the liberalism × power interaction is positive (Model 3: b = 2.36, SE = 1.08, p < .05). H3: the liberalism × workplace-conservatism interaction is positive (Model 4: b = 5.15, SE = 2.55, p < .05). H4: the liberalism × prior-group-count interaction is negative (Model 5: b = −.05, SE = .03, p < .05), consistent with a weaker association as the movement expands. In the joint Model 6, CEO liberalism remains positive (b = 2.32, p < .01), and the three interactions retain their predicted signs and p < .05: power, 2.78; workplace conservatism, 7.25; movement expansion, −.05.

An alternative comparison group matched on year, industry and size reaffirmed the reported main association between CEO liberalism and group formation; the account does not separately establish each interaction in that check. Firm CSR showed no evidence of mediation or moderation. Adding the succession contrast to Model 2 yielded a significant positive association with formation beyond incumbent CEO ideology, but no coefficient or exact p-value is reported. These are observational associations; the data do not establish actual CEO responses or whether activists relied on explicit signals versus inferred ideological leanings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper adds executives' personal values to the corporate opportunity structure, emphasizing the human decision makers whom social activists anticipate encountering. It extends stakeholder theory upstream from the influence of stakeholders to their decisions to mobilize and identifies conditions under which CEO ideology is more consequential. For upper echelons theory, it proposes that strategic stimuli may themselves be endogenous: stakeholders can anticipate executive inclinations and selectively present initiatives, complicating a one-way account from executive attributes to strategic choices.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise social activists considering whether to target a corporation to assess the likely receptiveness of key decision makers, especially the CEO. A sympathetic profile may suggest opportunities at relatively little cost, whereas a contrary profile may imply high costs and slim prospects; the study does not observe CEOs' actual responses to establish those outcomes.

**Limitations**
The data cannot distinguish explicit CEO cues welcoming LGBT initiatives from activists' inferences about otherwise known liberal leanings. Actual executive responses are unobserved, and data limitations prevented investigation of the links between CEO ideology and eventual policies such as domestic-partner health benefits or anti-discrimination protections. The theory and formal hypotheses concern liberal employee activism; conservative causes and external activists are proposed extensions rather than tested populations.

**Future Research**
The authors call for extensions to other environmental, social and governance movements and to customers, shareholders and professional activist organizations, comparing internal versus external activism, less versus more radical activists and conservative causes. They propose investigating tangible executive responses and policy changes, refining political ideology to distinguish positions such as libertarian conservatism, and using qualitative field research to examine actual reactions. Additional directions include entire top-management teams and boards, cause-specific versus counter-cause donors, and the phases through which activism and associated institutional change unfold.
