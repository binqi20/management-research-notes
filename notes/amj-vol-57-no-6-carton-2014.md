# A (Blurry) Vision of the Future: How Leader Rhetoric about Ultimate Goals Influences Performance

**Abstract**
One key responsibility of leaders involves crafting and communicating two types of messages—visions and values—that help followers understand the ultimate purpose of their work. Although scholars have long considered how leaders communicate visions and values to establish a sense of purpose, they have overlooked how these messages can be used to establish a shared sense of purpose, which is achieved when multiple employees possess the same understanding of the purpose of work. In this research, we move beyond the traditional focus on leader rhetoric and individual cognition to examine leader rhetoric and shared cognition. We suggest that a specific combination of messages—a large amount of vision imagery combined with a small number of values—will boost performance more than other combinations because it triggers a shared sense of the organization’s ultimate goal, and, in turn, enhances coordination. We found support for our predictions in an archival study of 151 hospitals and an experiment with 62 groups of full-time employees. In light of these findings, we conducted exploratory analyses and discovered two dysfunctional practices: leaders tend to (1) communicate visions without imagery and (2) over-utilize value-laden rhetoric.

**Research Question**
How do the amount of imagery in leaders’ visions and the number of values they communicate jointly influence collective performance quality? Does their combination establish a shared understanding of the organization’s ultimate goal that improves coordination?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: When leaders communicate rhetoric about ultimate goals, image-based words and the number of values interact such that the positive effect of image-based words on performance quality weakens as the number of values increases.
- H2: The positive indirect effect of image-based words on performance quality through a shared ultimate goal and then coordination weakens as the number of values increases; values moderate the imagery-to-shared-goal and shared-goal-to-coordination paths.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Vision imagery, conveyed through image-based words describing the organization’s ultimate goal.
- DV(s): Collective performance quality—heart attack readmission prevention in Study 1 and toy-design quality in Study 2; ROA is a Study 1 robustness outcome.
- Mediators: A shared ultimate goal, followed by coordination; directly examined in Study 2.
- Moderators: Number of values articulated by leaders, weakening the predicted imagery effect and the first two paths of the serial indirect effect as the number increases.

Image-based words help different employees visualize the same future, while a small number of values focuses their interpretation of that image. A shared ultimate goal supplies a common reference point and encourages employees to recognize how their roles depend on others. A focused set of values also supplies common behavioral standards during implementation, helping translate shared understanding into coordination and higher performance quality. A larger set of values can fragment both the meaning attributed to the vision and the principles guiding action.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 used website statements from 151 California hospitals, with 92 complete observations for readmission prevention and 143 for the ROA check. Two coders rated each codable word’s image strength from 0 to 10 and summed the scores within each vision; some multiword expressions were coded as single units. Values were counted as explicitly stated, organization-level descriptions of desired end states. The main outcome was the risk-adjusted percentage of Medicare heart-attack patients not readmitted within 30 days of discharge; 2010 outcomes were modeled with 2009 performance controls. Other controls covered vision specificity, prosocial impact, importance, difficulty and fluency; CEO tenure; hospital size, system membership, ownership, market concentration, teaching, technology, Medicaid share, specialization, magnet status and rankings. These are observational associations. Three supplementary validation waves across 42 organizations examined whether formal statements reflect leaders’ ongoing rhetoric and their communication at critical organizational junctures.

Study 2 recruited 186 full-time employees into 62 virtual teams of three. Strong versus weak imagery was randomly assigned between subjects; one versus nine values varied within subjects in counterbalanced order, and the single value was randomly selected from the nine-value set. Members successively designed a toy, selected materials and improved the design, then finalized it; vision and values appeared together at the beginning and once later, separately from task instructions. Shared ultimate goal was measured from open-ended responses: an algorithm awarded a point for each word or phrase shared by multiple members concerning the leader’s vision or values, summing concrete and conceptual descriptions. A separate overlap measure of present-task and short-term goals served as a shared-mental-model covariate. Coordination was proxied by prevention of errors in materials procurement, with errors measured as spending beyond the specified budget; coordination data were missing in 12 instances when teams did not attempt procurement. Seven children aged 7–12 rated desire to play with each toy on a 0–100 scale; designs that could not be built with the available materials, and missing designs, received zero. Analyses focused on final ideas generated after coordination began and controlled initial design quality; the conditional indirect-effect analyses also controlled shared mental models.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported H1: the imagery-by-values interaction predicted readmission prevention negatively (Table 2, model 3: b = −.00781, p < .05). Imagery’s simple slope was positive at one value (b = .037, p < .01) and three values (b = .021, p < .01), but nonsignificant at five, seven and nine values. The ROA robustness interaction was also negative (model 4: b = −.000963, p < .01). The reported interaction findings survived influential-observation removal and inclusion of an indicator distinguishing hospitals with no explicitly stated values from those with values statements. Study 1 did not measure the proposed mediating processes.

Study 2 supported H1 with an imagery-by-values interaction on performance quality, F(1,59) = 7.31, p < .01. Strong imagery improved performance in the one-value condition (b = 15.78, p < .01), but not in the nine-value condition (b = −3.23, nonsignificant). Supporting H2, the full serial indirect effect through shared ultimate goal and coordination was positive with one value (.09, 95% CI [.001, .88]); its interval included zero with nine values. The two component indirect effects were also significant only with one value: imagery through shared ultimate goal to coordination (4.03, 95% CI [.35, 14.73]) and shared ultimate goal through coordination to performance (.28, 95% CI [.04, 1.03]). Supplementary direct imagery-by-values interactions on shared ultimate goal and coordination were nonsignificant. H1 robustness tests generally remained significant at p < .05, although one alternative quality measure yielded only p < .10; the reported H2 robustness checks retained intervals excluding zero with one value, but not nine.

Exploratory analyses identified a gap between effective rhetoric and prevailing practice: hospital visions contained far more conceptual than image-based words, and leaders commonly articulated more values than the authors’ results favored. In a separate random sample of 30 Fortune 500 companies, only three visions depicted observable behaviors.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper explains how visions and values can establish a shared sense of purpose by jointly specifying concrete images and their conceptual meaning. It extends research on shared representations of past events and present tasks to the organization’s long-term future, linking leader sensegiving with collective mind and coordination. Distinguishing the number of values from their content also identifies how focused values can shape both the formation of shared cognition and its translation into behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend that leaders describing ultimate goals combine image-based words with four values or fewer. They suggest crafting vivid visions around familiar physical objects, observable actions, and experiences of using the organization’s products or services. To reduce an expressed value system, leaders can ask whether removing a given value would preserve or enhance the organization’s distinctive identity.

**Limitations**
Study 1 may omit objective leader and employee abilities, does not measure the proposed mechanisms, and cannot establish that shared purpose and coordination explain its performance associations, particularly ROA. Word-level coding may miss how phrases jointly create imagery, while the experimental visions may also differ in emotional intensity. In Study 1, vision–value content alignment was examined for a hospital subsample, and the research did not examine other leader behaviors or rhetorical features such as cadence, rhythm and delivery style.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining individual-level pathways linking rhetoric to performance and vision–value content alignment as a boundary condition. Further work could test whether many values remain effective when clearly prioritized and whether communicating visions and values simultaneously matters. They also call for behavioral nudges or repairs that mitigate the tendency to convey too little imagery and too many values.
