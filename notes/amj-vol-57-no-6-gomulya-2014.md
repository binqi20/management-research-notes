# How Firms Respond to Financial Restatement: CEO Successors and External Reactions

**Abstract**
Although past studies have paid considerable attention to firms’ reputations, few have investigated the actions that firms take following a reputation-damaging event. We identify firms involved in financial earnings restatements and examine whether naming a successor CEO with specific qualities serves to signal the seriousness of a firm’s efforts to restore its reputation. Using theories of market signaling, we argue that attributes of successor CEOs significantly influence the reactions of key external constituencies. In particular, firms with more severe restatement tend to name successors who have prior CEO or turnaround experience and a more elite education. The naming of such successors results in more positive reactions from the stock market, financial analysts, and mass media. We argue that these attributes send messages to stakeholders and the broader public about the CEO’s credibility and the firm’s efforts.

**Research Question**
How does the severity of a financial restatement relate to the characteristics of the successor CEO a firm selects, and how do those characteristics interact with restatement severity in shaping stock-market, financial-analyst and media reactions to the appointment?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: The more severe the financial restatement, the greater the likelihood that the successor CEO will have prior CEO experience.
- H1b: The more severe the financial restatement, the greater the likelihood that the successor CEO will have prior turnaround experience.
- H2: The more severe the financial restatement, the more elite the replacement CEO's educational background.
- H3: The more severe the financial restatement, the greater the likelihood that the successor CEO will have functional experience in finance or accounting.
- H4a: Following a financial restatement, greater severity increases the favorability of market, analyst and media reactions to a successor with prior CEO experience.
- H4b: Following a financial restatement, greater severity increases the favorability of market, analyst and media reactions to a successor with prior turnaround experience.
- H4c: Following a financial restatement, greater severity increases the favorability of market, analyst and media reactions to a successor with an elite educational background.
- H4d: Following a financial restatement, greater severity increases the favorability of market, analyst and media reactions to a successor with prior accounting or finance experience.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Restatement severity in the successor-selection models; successor CEO experience, turnaround experience, elite education and finance/accounting background in the external-reaction models.
- DV(s): The four successor characteristics; announcement-window cumulative abnormal returns, changes in analysts' earnings forecasts, and positive and negative media coverage.
- Mediators: No mediator is estimated; perceived successor competence and the firm's commitment to reputation recovery are proposed signaling interpretations.
- Moderators: Restatement severity moderates the relationship between successor attributes and external reactions.

A financial restatement damages credibility and creates pressure to demonstrate a serious response. Appointing a visibly qualified successor can signal commitment because relevant credentials are observable and potentially costly to obtain through recruitment. The authors argue that signal salience and fit, the severity of the signaler's problem, and the receiver's interpretation shape reactions; their discussion of finance/accounting taint is a tentative explanation for contrary findings, not a tested mediation process.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses U.S. Government Accountability Office restatements involving downward earnings revisions associated with revenue recognition or expenses, announced from 2003 through June 2006. Each of 352 restating firms is matched to a nonrestating firm in the same four-digit SIC industry and year with the closest total assets, yielding 704 firms. CEO replacement is a binary indicator of purposeful replacement within two years after restatement; matching firms use their paired firm's announcement date. Retirement, movement to another position in the firm or a similar position elsewhere, and clear health-related departures are excluded. Heckman models address selection into CEO replacement, using incumbent CEO equity and CEO–chair duality in the selection equation as instruments; their exclusion from the outcome equation rests on the authors' argument that incumbent power affects replacement but not successor characteristics. The design is observational. Table 2 total/uncensored samples are 554/109 for prior CEO experience, 558/113 for turnaround experience, 557/112 for elite education and 548/103 for finance/accounting background. Full interaction models use 463/36 for CAR, 488/43 for analyst forecasts and 529/84 for each media outcome; Appendix A discusses exclusions, missing data and additional CAR exclusions for unrelated announcements within the event window.

Restatement severity equals the total decrease in net income following restatement divided by revenue in the year before the announcement; matched nonrestaters receive zero. All four successor attributes are binary. Prior CEO experience means having been a chief executive before joining the focal firm. Turnaround experience requires having taken charge as CEO during a firm's severe loss and returned it to profitability during that tenure; the severe-loss screen identifies operating ROE above cost of equity for at least two consecutive years immediately followed by at least one operating-loss year. The Compustat screen covers 1990–2006 and identifies 20 turnaround CEOs, 17 at restating firms and three at matched firms. Elite education means an undergraduate or graduate degree from the institutions designated in Appendix B, encompassing Ivy League, selected public and private universities, and service academies. Finance/accounting background is coded from prior functional roles. CEO information is triangulated using SEC proxy biographies and other biographical sources.

Market reaction is CAR over the five-day window from two days before to two days after the successor announcement. Analyst reaction uses I/B/E/S annual mean EPS forecasts: the first forecast after announcement minus the last before announcement for the same target date; five observations with forecast gaps exceeding 63 days are excluded. Media outcomes are separate means of LIWC positive- and negative-tenor percentages across relevant articles for each firm in the same five-day announcement window; 2,305 downloaded articles yield 1,132 relevant articles. Controls include firm age, prior-year revenue, year indicators and pre-replacement ROA based on restated earnings, with incumbent attributes included in the specified models; analyst models also control for CAR and a count-change variable called the change in the number of forecasts in the methods but the change in the number of analysts in Table 3; selection models also include board composition and merger status. The paper reports logging and mean-centering nonbinary variables, adding the minimum necessary constant before logging where needed, and uses robust standard errors and two-tailed tests.

**Key Findings**
Table 2 supports H1a, H1b and H2: greater severity is positively associated with appointing successors with prior CEO experience (b = 0.14, p < .05), turnaround experience (b = 0.17, p < .05) and elite education (b = 0.10, p < .05). H3 is contradicted: severity is negatively associated with selecting a finance/accounting successor (b = -0.23, p < .001).

The full interaction models in Tables 3–4 provide heterogeneous support for H4. These are severity-by-attribute coefficients, not raw-unit appointment effects:

- H4a, prior CEO experience: positive interactions for CAR (b = 0.15, p < .001) and analyst forecasts (b = 0.05, p < .001), and a negative interaction for negative media coverage (b = -0.68, p < .001); the positive-media interaction is nonsignificant (b = -0.08).
- H4b, turnaround experience: positive interactions for analyst forecasts (b = 0.01, p < .001) and positive media coverage (b = 0.21, p < .01), and a negative interaction for negative media coverage (b = -0.01, p < .05); the CAR interaction is nonsignificant (b = -0.00).
- H4c, elite education: favorable interactions for CAR (b = 0.09, p < .001), positive media coverage (b = 0.18, p < .01) and negative media coverage (b = -0.16, p < .01), but a contrary negative interaction for analyst forecasts (b = -0.01, p < .001).
- H4d, finance/accounting background: a favorable CAR interaction (b = 0.04, p < .05), contrary interactions for analyst forecasts (b = -0.03, p < .001) and negative media coverage (b = 0.27, p < .001), and a nonsignificant positive-media interaction (b = 0.02).

Thus, the paper's 13 significant interactions include effects contrary to its predictions. In additional analyses, expanding turnaround experience to include 12 cases of TMT experience yields CAR and positive-media results reported at p < .10. The authors describe these as weaker than before, although the original turnaround-by-severity CAR interaction in Table 3 is nonsignificant; this comparison is not reconciled here. The authors also report unchanged conclusions after adding a restatement indicator, with weakened severity significance in some models.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends reputation-restoration research by examining the successor choices firms actually make after reputation damage and the external reactions accompanying those choices. It connects signaler characteristics (restatement severity), signal characteristics (successor credentials) and receiver differences within a single signaling framework. The contrasting responses to finance/accounting credentials qualify the assumption that the same signal receives a uniform interpretation across audiences.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that selecting a visibly qualified successor can communicate a firm's commitment to recovery, with particular credentials interpreted differently by markets, analysts and media. They discuss a possible dilemma between choosing expertise to prevent another restatement and distancing the firm from the financial/accounting taint of the original event; the latter is a tentative explanation for the findings.

**Limitations**
The study is confined to U.S. firms and the U.S. legal and governance context, so cultural differences and financial-reporting arrangements may affect applicability elsewhere. It measures external reactions without determining whether hiring particular successors actually repairs reputation. It also does not distinguish restatements arising from intentional misconduct from those arising from unintentional error or incompetence.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying reputation damage and restoration in other countries and cultures, including different financial and nonfinancial events, and distinguishing misconduct from incompetence. They suggest examining other remedial actions, socially constructed successor attributes such as celebrity and charisma, and the status and reputations of both incumbent and successor CEOs. Further work could investigate internal recognition of reputational damage, search for solutions and restoration efforts as severity varies, and develop the distinction between negative reputation and stigma.
