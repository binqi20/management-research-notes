# From Support to Mutiny: Shifting Legitimacy Judgments and Emotional Reactions Impacting the Implementation of Radical Change

**Abstract**
Based on a three-year inductive study of one organization’s implementation of radical organizational change, we examine the critical role played by middle managers’ judgments of the legitimacy of their top managers as change agents. Our analysis revealed middle managers’ shifting judgments of the change agents’ legitimacy that arose with their emotional reactions and produced rising resistance to the change effort. Our inductive model illustrates the dynamic, relational, and iterative relationships among change recipients’ legitimacy judgments of change agents and arising emotional reactions in various phases of planned change, which explain recipients’ emergent resistance to the change effort. Our model allows us to contribute to theory on radical organizational change, resistance to change, and legitimacy judgments.

**Research Question**
What altered middle managers’ initial acceptance of radical organizational change into resistance during its implementation? How did their evolving judgments of top managers’ legitimacy and their emotional reactions interrelate across the change effort?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs: Change agent legitimacy; recipients’ emotional reactions; acceptance of or resistance to change; interim change outcomes.
- Legitimacy criteria: Cognitive judgments based on culturally appropriate attributes and validity cues; relational judgments concerning respect and self-worth; moral judgments concerning ethical conduct and keeping promises; instrumental judgments concerning competence and results.
- Phases: Formulation, implementation, and evaluation, with cognitive, relational/moral, and instrumental judgments respectively becoming dominant rather than mutually exclusive.
- Temporal expectations and interactions: Recipients increasingly expect leaders to acquire operating knowledge, engage with them, and deliver promised results; agents’ actions and interim outcomes feed back into legitimacy judgments and emotions.

In the emergent model, legitimacy judgments and emotional reactions arise together and influence each other, energizing recipients’ support or resistance, which in turn affects interim change outcomes. At Tekco, implementation difficulties, inadequate top-management engagement, and unfulfilled promises prompted more negative judgments and emotions; emotional censorship reduced dialogue and allowed discontent to accumulate. Increasing resistance and disappointing outcomes then reinforced negative evaluations, culminating in overt defiance during evaluation. This is the authors’ inductive process explanation; changing legitimacy criteria need not always produce declining legitimacy or failed change.

**Data & Measures**
The study followed radical change at Tekco, a large information technology and communication-services company, over three years. The first author interviewed the incumbent CEO 3 times, two previous CEOs once each, 12 executives 26 times, and 114 middle managers 192 times, alongside additional lower-level employees and union officials whose interview counts are not reported. About half of the interviewed middle managers belonged to the change-championing group and half to the recipient group, although an individual could occupy both roles across projects. Semi-structured interviews lasted about an hour; informal conversations with close to 200 employees, observations, internal change-project and HR documents, employee surveys, and confidential archival material supported triangulation. Perspectives entered the analysis when provided by at least two informants. Names, geographical locations, technologies, and some numbers and calendar dates were disguised.

The authors constructed narratives and chronological flow diagrams, openly coded interviews using informants’ descriptions, compared patterns within and across informant groups and with existing theory, and mapped themes across change phases. Legitimacy judgments were identified in respondents’ evaluations of top managers’ actions and non-actions; resistance was coded from statements and actions or inaction concerning acceptance or support of agents and the change effort. Emotional reactions were identified through explicit emotion terms and inferred from relational themes and cognitive appraisals, initially using valence and arousal distinctions and ultimately aggregating reactions into positive and negative categories. First-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions underpinned the qualitative process model.

**Key Findings**
- Formulation: Outsider status and relevant industry experience supported predominantly cognitive judgments of top managers’ legitimacy. Involving middle managers in change design, generous severance terms, and the promise to remove work before jobs reinforced favorable evaluations; emotions were largely positive or neutral and resistance was low, even toward the proposed elimination of 13,000 positions.
- Implementation: Voluntary departures reached 16,000, exceeding the planned 13,000, while delayed projects and inadequate technological support burdened remaining employees. Insufficient top-management engagement and failure to honor or explain the work-before-jobs promise brought relational and moral legitimacy into question, alongside disappointment, anxiety, and frustration. Emotional censorship restricted open discussion; resistance became covert, including delayed implementation and reduced initiative. Instrumental concerns about operating knowledge and strategic direction also emerged during this phase.
- Evaluation: Facing a forecast shortfall against promised financial targets, top managers ordered an additional 3,000 service-employee layoffs. Instrumental legitimacy judgments became dominant, and strong negative emotions, including collective anger, accompanied explicit delay or refusal of the directive. The CEO resigned and his top team was effectively dissolved; a departing executive acknowledged cost reduction without the intended transformation. Middle managers continued to recognize the need for major change even as they rejected the agents leading it.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study explains resistance to radical change as an emergent, relational process involving recipients’ shifting judgments of change agent legitimacy across implementation. It extends predominantly cognitive accounts of legitimacy by showing how emotional reactions can facilitate and amplify changes in evaluative criteria, with disparate judgments coalescing into more holistic evaluations of human actors. It also distinguishes resistance to change content from resistance to change agency and highlights the temporal expectations through which initial legitimacy is continually reassessed.

**Practical Implication**
For top managers leading radical change, early support and initial legitimacy do not assure continued cooperation during implementation. The case highlights the practical importance of distinguishing opposition to change leaders from opposition to the change itself: middle managers could still endorse the need for major change while rejecting the top team’s authority to lead it.

**Limitations**
The process model derives from one company’s change effort, and the authors explicitly leave the generalizability of the observed shift from “who” to “how” to “what” to future research. They do not claim that shifting legitimacy bases necessarily produce delegitimation, a linear high-to-low trajectory, or failed transformation; shifts may also facilitate change. Identifying details, some numbers, and calendar dates were altered to protect confidentiality.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on time-based expectations and variations in change agents’ and recipients’ judgments, emotions, and actions across phases of change. They propose longitudinal research bridging recipient sensemaking and resistance to change, and examination of whether the observed sequence of legitimacy criteria generalizes. They also call for a distinct research focus on the legitimacy of human actors, or agentic legitimacy, including how managers and other power-holders establish and maintain it over time.
