# The Structural Elaboration of Board Independence: Executive Power, Institutional Logics, and the Adoption of CEO-Only Board Structures in U.S. Corporate Governance

**Abstract**
This study builds on structural elaboration theory by developing a model to explain the adoption of board structures that appear to conform to the prevailing institutional logic, but which in fact contradict it. We test our theory with the case of CEO-only board structures, a formal increase in board independence that prior research has shown to lead to greater CEO entrenchment rather than increased shareholder value. Using an event history analysis of the Fortune 250 over a 27-year period, we examine three mechanisms that drive its adoption: executive interests, executive power, and elaboration opportunities. We show that the CEO-only structure is more likely to occur in firms in which a higher proportion of insiders predate the CEO, and in which the CEO has greater formal power and agenda control. We also find that powerful CEOs are more likely to realize the structural change following institutional opportunities, such as the passage of Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX), and organizational contingencies, such as positive changes in firm performance. By exploring the mechanisms leading to the proliferation of the CEO-only structure, our study contributes to sociopolitical perspectives on corporate governance, as well as to theories of institutional logics and structural elaboration.

**Research Question**
Why do governance structures proliferate that appear to conform to the shareholder value logic while advancing CEO interests? The study examines how executive interests, executive power, and elaboration opportunities explain adoption of boards on which the CEO is the only insider.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater the proportion of inside directors appointed before the current CEO's selection, the more likely adoption of a CEO-only board structure.
- H2: The greater the CEO's power on the board, the more likely adoption of a CEO-only board structure.
- H3: In the post-SOX period, a CEO with a finance background increases the likelihood of adoption of a CEO-only structure.
- H4: The greater the increase in firm performance, the greater the effect of CEO power on the likelihood of adoption of a CEO-only structure.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Executive interests, proxied by the proportion of other inside directors appointed before the CEO; executive power, represented by CEO-chair duality, relative shareholdings, and finance background.
- DV(s): Adoption of a CEO-only board structure that persists for more than two years.
- Mediators: Not tested; information brokerage, relative status and power, and removal of potential internal contestants are theorized benefits of the structure.
- Moderators: Elaboration opportunities—SOX's interaction with finance background and improvement in firm performance interacting with CEO power.

Structural elaboration theory suggests that ambiguous institutional prescriptions allow executives to support structures that signal conformity while promoting their own interests. Removing other insiders is theorized to make the CEO the formal information broker between management and the board, enhance relative status and power, and restrict potential successors' access to outside directors. Insiders appointed before the CEO are more likely to be contestants than allies, strengthening the proposed incentive to remove them. SOX and performance improvements are proposed opportunities for mobilizing particular forms of CEO power; the study tests associated adoption patterns rather than directly observing these political processes.

**Data & Measures**
The panel starts from the 1995 Fortune 250, the first listing to include both industrial and service firms; missing financial data for 28 firms reduces the sample to 222 firms. The study covers 1981–2007 and reports 2,918 firm-years. Methods reports 144 adoption events, whereas Table 1 separately gives a CEO-only-event mean of .043 without reconciling these figures. Board and executive data come from Standard & Poor's Register of Corporations, Directors, and Executives, Marquis' Who's Who in Finance and Industry, and proxy statements; financial data come from COMPUSTAT, and CEO finance backgrounds from Forbes' annual CEO Compensation survey. Predictors and controls are lagged, succession years are omitted to avoid assigning the preceding CEO's power to the incumbent, and spells ending before 2007 are right-censored.

The adoption indicator equals 1 when a board moves from at least two insiders to the CEO alone, provided the CEO-only structure lasts more than two years, and 0 otherwise; family members count as insiders. Firms enter the risk set after having another insider alongside the CEO for at least one year, and adoption is repeatable if insiders subsequently rejoin. The interests proxy is the proportion of non-CEO insiders whose board appointments preceded the current CEO. Duality and finance background are binary; relative shareholdings equal shares held by the CEO divided by shares held by all board members. SOX equals 1 from 2003 onward. Performance change is the difference between the firm's industry-adjusted ROA in the two prior years. The models interact SOX with finance background and performance change with the power indicators; Table 2 also includes a performance-change × founder interaction.

Controls cover CEO relative tenure, founder status, age and change in duality; board size, insider proportion, insider departures, classified-board status, outsider appointments and relative share ownership; institutional blockholdings of at least 5%; firm performance, logged assets and sales, age, cash and debt-to-equity; prior CEO-only events and the number of other firms with the structure. The associational discrete-time probit models use calendar-year intervals, repeated events, SIC major-sector fixed effects, and firm-clustered robust standard errors. The authors report similar logit results and no substantial change with three- and four-year duration definitions.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: a higher proportion of insiders predating the CEO is positively associated with adoption (Table 2, Model 5: b = 2.055, p < .001).
- H2 receives partial support: CEO-chair duality is positively associated with adoption (Model 5: b = .741, p < .01), whereas relative shareholdings (b = .256) and finance background (b = .0511) have nonsignificant main coefficients.
- H3 is supported: SOX × finance background is positive (Model 4: b = .859; Model 5: b = .838; both p < .05). The SOX coefficient itself is negative and marginally significant in Model 5 (b = −.418, p < .10).
- H4 receives partial support: performance improvement interacts positively with duality (Model 5: b = .510, p < .05) and relative shareholdings (b = 1.133, p < .01), but not finance background (b = .159, nonsignificant). The additional founder interaction is also nonsignificant (b = −.584).

The source contradicts itself on the performance-change main coefficient: the results prose calls it nonsignificant, while Table 2, Model 5 reports b = −.391 (SE = .14), marked p < .01. The interaction patterns should therefore be distinguished from this unresolved reporting discrepancy. The authors also emphasize that their elaboration mechanisms become most consequential when relatively few insiders remain on the board; their interaction plots hold insider proportion one standard deviation below its mean and other controls at their means.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends structural elaboration theory from ambiguous legal mandates to ambiguous institutional logics, explaining how apparently conforming structures may advance powerful executives' interests. By integrating a sociopolitical perspective, it distinguishes executive interests, heterogeneous sources of power, and institutional or organizational elaboration opportunities, challenging the assumption that powerful CEOs necessarily favor more insiders. It also links institutional pressures to intraorganizational political dynamics and practice variation within a prevailing logic.

**Practical Implication**
Formal board independence can coexist with arrangements that favor powerful CEOs. The paper cautions that applying the shareholder value logic's principles may serve executive interests rather than its intended purposes; its focal evidence concerns adoption of the CEO-only structure, while the argument about entrenchment draws on prior research.

**Limitations**
The sample includes only the largest U.S. public corporations, whose political dynamics may differ from those of small, family-owned, or private organizations, particularly where a founding family member is present. The prevalence of multidivisional firms may make finance expertise especially relevant to acquisition and diversification strategies, limiting the indicator's applicability elsewhere. Political processes are not directly observed, and the assumption that CEOs perceive the CEO-only structure as serving their interests cannot be directly tested; the authors acknowledge that the proposed associations need not hold under all conditions.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how decision opportunities activate power in other contexts and affect the elaboration of other structures and processes. They also propose studying how structural elaboration develops or transforms institutional logics and contributes to endogenous institutional change, including the recursive relationship between institutional and organizational change.
