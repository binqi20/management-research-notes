# Substitutes or Complements? A Configurational Examination of Corporate Governance Mechanisms

**Abstract**
We conduct an exploratory qualitative comparative case analysis of the S&P 1500 firms with the aim of elaborating theory on how corporate governance mechanisms work together effectively. To do so, we integrate extant theory and research to specify the bundle of mechanisms that operate to mitigate the agency problem among publicly traded corporations and review what previous research has said about how these mechanisms combine. We then use the fuzzy-set approach to qualitative comparitive analysis (QCA) to explore the combinations of governance mechanisms that exist among the S&P 1500 firms that achieve high (and not-high) profitability. Our findings suggest that high profits result when CEO incentive alignment and monitoring mechanisms work together as complements rather than as substitutes. Furthermore, they show that high profits are obtained when both internal and external monitoring mechanisms are present. At the same time, however, monitoring mechanisms evidently combine in complex ways such that there may be simultaneity of substitution and complementarity among and across the various monitoring and control mechanisms. Our findings clearly suggest that the effectiveness of board independence and CEO non-duality—governance mechanisms widely believed to singularly resolve the agency problem—depends on how each combine with the other mechanisms in the governance bundle.

**Research Question**
How do corporate governance mechanisms combine as substitutes or complements in governance bundles associated with high and not-high profitability among S&P 1500 firms? Must CEO incentive alignment and monitoring coexist, and how do combinations within and between internal and external governance actors depend on CEO duality?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The exploratory analysis develops the following propositions from its findings; these are not a priori hypotheses tested through regression.

- Proposition 1: Effective governance bundles require both CEO incentive alignment and monitoring mechanisms.
- Proposition 2: Outside directors investing a substantial portion of their own net worth in firm equity play an essential complementary role by enhancing the effectiveness of director independence and CEO incentive alignment mechanisms.
- Proposition 3: TMT tournaments may contribute to governance effectiveness, whereas substantial equity ownership by TMT members other than the CEO contributes to governance ineffectiveness.
- Proposition 4: Effective governance bundles involve internal and external monitoring mechanisms working together as complements.
- Proposition 5: Effective governance bundles involve simultaneous substitution and complementarity among monitoring mechanisms.
- Proposition 6: Separating the CEO and board chairperson roles does not improve governance effectiveness per se; effectiveness depends on how CEO (non)duality combines with the remaining mechanisms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Configurations of CEO stock options and equity ownership; outside director independence and equity ownership; TMT pay tournaments and equity ownership; non-TMT inside director ownership; CEO duality; pressure-resistant external blockholders; and an active market for corporate control.
- DV(s): Membership in the sets of firms with high profitability and not-high profitability, based on industry-relative return on assets.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: No separately estimated moderation effects; CEO duality and the other governance conditions jointly qualify the configurational relationships.

The governance bundle is theorized to mitigate the agency problem through CEO incentive alignment, internal monitoring by directors and top managers, and external monitoring by blockholders and the takeover market. Substitution means that one mechanism can replace another, whereas complementarity requires their co-presence and is theorized as mutual enhancement. The findings motivate a configurational account in which incentive alignment and monitoring generally complement one another while particular monitoring mechanisms can substitute or complement depending on the remaining bundle. The analysis establishes neither causal effects nor the synergistic effects proposed by complementarity theory.

**Data & Measures**
The sample comprises firms in the 2005 S&P 1500, drawing on Risk Metrics, Compustat Annual and Execucomp, Compact Disclosure, and annual proxy statements. From 1,358 firms with initial data, the authors excluded 160 experiencing CEO succession in 2005 or 2006 and 63 with missing data, retaining 1,135 firms. Governance conditions are measured in 2005 and profitability in 2006; this is an exploratory associational design with a temporal lag.

Profitability is ROA, calculated as net income divided by total assets. High-profit membership is calibrated as fully out at or below the firm's industry median ROA, fully in at or above the industry upper quartile, and at the crossover halfway between these ROA thresholds. Industry benchmarks use all firms in the 2006 Compustat annual file sharing the focal firm's two-digit SIC; not-high profitability is the negation of the high-profit fuzzy set.

CEO contingent incentives are the dollar value of accumulated unexercised exercisable and unexercisable stock options; CEO ownership adds shareholdings and unvested restricted stock grants, valued at the end of 2005. Outside director independence is the proportion of the board simultaneously classified as independent by Risk Metrics, appointed before the incumbent CEO, and not currently serving as another company's CEO. Outside director ownership is the proportion of outside directors with substantial equity stakes, defined as $860,000; calibration of both board proportions distinguishes supermajority from simple-majority voting requirements. Non-TMT inside director ownership measures equity held by insiders outside the TMT, such as former CEOs or founders. CEO duality is a crisp indicator of whether the CEO also chairs the board. The TMT tournament measure divides CEO total pay by average TMT total pay using Execucomp TDC1; TMT ownership uses the largest individual stake among non-CEO TMT members, combining shareholdings and restricted stock grants rather than averaging ownership across members.

External blockholders hold at least 5% of outstanding shares and are neither managers nor directors. Membership in pressure-resistant blockholding is coded 1 for angel/venture-capital investors, hedge funds/private equity, and endowments/foundations; .66 for investment/asset managers, mutual funds, and investment counselors; .33 for pressure-sensitive insurance companies, banks, companies/holding companies, and nonbank trusts; and 0 when no external blockholders exist. The market-for-corporate-control proxy divides the number of firms delisted because of mergers during January 1, 2001–December 31, 2005 by the number of firms in the same two-digit SIC industry on December 29, 2000.

Fuzzy-set unions take the maximum membership across mechanisms to represent substitution; intersections take the minimum to represent complementary co-presence. Sufficiency consistency is the sum of the minima of configuration and outcome memberships divided by the sum of configuration memberships. The authors compare solutions on coverage, parsimony, and the qualitatively distinct configurations captured; the best-fitting solution's analysis uses raw consistency .82, PRI consistency .75, and seven cases per configuration, including 87% of the sample. These are analysis thresholds, distinct from the resulting solution consistency and coverage.

**Key Findings**
The best-fitting high-profit solution identifies six qualitatively distinct configurations: three involving non-dual CEOs, two involving dual CEOs, and one applicable regardless of CEO duality. Table 3 displays seven columns because configurations 1a and 1b are permutations of one scenario; overall solution consistency is .83 and coverage is .16, compared with .86 consistency and .10 coverage for the baseline high-profit solution. Coverage concerns membership in the high-profit outcome, not the percentage of sample firms correctly predicted. Across the six scenarios, at least one CEO alignment mechanism and at least one internal and one external monitoring mechanism are present.

Within actors, CEO ownership and stock options generally complement one another: the complementary specification has coverage .15 versus .07 for substitution. Outside director ownership appears throughout the high-profit configurations and generally complements independence; the exceptional non-TMT-inside-director scenario combines CEO ownership and outside director ownership with the absence of CEO options. TMT mechanisms fit better as substitutes than complements (.13 versus .08 coverage), and TMT ownership is absent as a core condition in five of the six scenarios, with a diminished role in the sixth. These patterns underpin the authors' propositions rather than constituting conventional hypothesis-test support.

Between actors, the strongest-fitting specification combines CEO ownership, CEO options, and outside director ownership as complements. Relationships among board independence, TMT tournaments, blockholders, and takeover pressure vary with the wider configuration and CEO duality, allowing both substitution and complementarity. For example, Table 3 configuration 6 combines CEO incentives, outside director ownership and independence, TMT tournament incentives, pressure-resistant blockholders, and the absence of an active takeover market, without specifying CEO duality; other routes involve different monitoring combinations. Thus neither board independence nor separating the CEO and chair roles is individually sufficient for high profitability.

The not-high-profit solution has consistency .82 and coverage .04 and contains two configurations: a non-dual CEO with an independent board, and a dual CEO with an independent board and substantial TMT ownership. Both lack CEO incentive alignment and outside director ownership. The results prose describes the first as lacking all other mechanisms, but Table 2 leaves the market-for-corporate-control condition unspecified; that blank should not be read as an observed absence. The authors report that using only accumulated unexercised exercisable options yields robust findings, and further substitution/complementarity analyses of not-high profits yield no additional configurations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper elaborates the governance bundle perspective by showing how CEO alignment, internal monitoring, and external monitoring jointly relate to profitability, challenging explanations based solely on isolated mechanisms or universal substitution. It highlights outside director ownership and the contrasting role of TMT ownership, while treating CEO duality as part of a configuration rather than an inherently effective or ineffective structure. Its complementarity interpretation rests on observed co-presence and set relations, not a direct estimate of synergistic gains.

**Practical Implication**
The authors urge researchers and policy makers to take a configurational approach when thinking about, designing, and studying corporate governance for particular desired outcomes. Board independence and separation of the CEO and chairperson roles should not be treated as stand-alone solutions; their effectiveness depends on their combination with other incentive and monitoring mechanisms.

**Limitations**
The number of conditions that configurational analysis can accommodate constrains the detail of the governance bundle, and the study omits firm-level, industry-level, and national-system contingencies. Its S&P 1500 sample and exclusive focus on ROA limit conclusions about other firm populations and market-based performance; fsQCA does not provide statistical generalization beyond the studied sample. Despite the temporal lag, the cross-sectional configurational design does not establish causality or rule out reverse causality and endogeneity in ownership, CEO duality, and performance.

**Future Research**
The authors call for closer examination of board committee structures, entrenchment practices that thwart the takeover market, and why substantial TMT ownership appears detrimental. They propose incorporating firm, industry, and national contingencies and examining smaller firms, IPOs, international firms, and market-based or hybrid performance outcomes. Future configurational research should also address causality and endogeneity.
