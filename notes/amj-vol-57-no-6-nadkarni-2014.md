# Bridging Yesterday, Today, and Tomorrow: CEO Temporal Focus, Environmental Dynamism, and Rate of New Product Introduction

**Abstract**
Using a multidimensional framework of CEO temporal focus (the degree to which CEOs characteristically devote attention to perceptions of the past, present, and future), we propose that a company’s rate of new product introduction (NPI) is predicted by a CEO’s focus on each of the three distinct time frames in interaction with environmental dynamism. Based on a longitudinal (from 1996 to 2003) analysis of 221 firms in 19 industries, we show that, in stable environments, new products are introduced faster in firms headed by CEOs with high past focus, high present focus, and low future focus. In dynamic environments, new products are introduced faster in firms headed by CEOs with low past focus, high present focus, and high future focus. These findings demonstrate how CEO temporal attentional bias shapes the rate of NPI.

**Research Question**
How do CEOs’ distinct past, present, and future temporal focuses relate to firms’ rates of new product introduction (NPI), and how does environmental dynamism condition these relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Environmental dynamism negatively moderates the CEO past focus–NPI relationship: positive in stable environments and negative in dynamic environments.
- H2: Environmental dynamism positively moderates the CEO present focus–NPI relationship: more positive in dynamic than stable environments.
- H3: Environmental dynamism positively moderates the CEO future focus–NPI relationship: positive in dynamic environments and negative in stable environments.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO past focus, present focus, and future focus—distinct tendencies to devote attention to each time frame, rather than mutually exclusive categories.
- DV(s): Rate of NPI.
- Mediators: No mediator is tested; detection and development of new products are the theorized processes linking temporal attention to NPI.
- Moderators: Environmental dynamism, encompassing the rate and unpredictability of environmental change.

The authors argue that temporal focus filters the information CEOs use to detect opportunities and develop new products. Stable environments support feedback-based learning, making past experience useful while potentially making futuristic speculation costly and disruptive. Dynamic environments render past recipes obsolete and opportunities fleeting, favoring current information, spontaneous adjustment, and anticipation of future contingencies. Present focus is expected to help in both contexts, but more strongly in dynamic environments.

**Data & Measures**
The main sample comprises 221 manufacturing firms in 19 industries and 1,105 firm-years. Selection required two-digit SIC codes 20–39, sales of at least $100 million, at least 10 years of existence, more than 70% of revenue from the dominant business, and no CEO changes during the study. CEO temporal-focus data cover 1996–2000; NPI data cover 1997–2003, using three-year averages and a one-year lag between predictor and outcome sequencing. This is an observational panel, with temporal ordering and endogeneity checks rather than experimental identification.

Past, present, and future focus are LIWC dictionary-word percentages of an annual CEO text corpus combining letters to shareholders, published interviews, speeches, and press releases containing direct CEO assertions. Environmental dynamism combines average yearly changes in industry sales value and units during 1996–2000 with shipment unpredictability: regress industry shipment values over five years on time, then divide the standard error of the time coefficient by average industry shipment value; the paper does not specify further aggregation weights for the composite. NPI is the three-year average of product-launch counts obtained through Factiva and Lexis-Nexis Academic; two coders checked headlines and removed duplicate reports. Table 2 controls include CEO education and tenure, firm size, slack resources, R&D intensity, past performance change, organizational temporal orientation measured through capital-investment durability, industry NPI, and year dummies. Mean-centered predictors enter random-effects negative binomial models; split-sample models compare marginal effects above and below mean environmental dynamism.

A separate validation study involved 144 middle-level executives enrolled in an MBA program at a major eastern university. LIWC scores from their three-page strategic case analyses were compared with Shipp et al.’s 12-item temporal-focus scale. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the three-factor structure and convergent and discriminant validity; this sample validates the measures rather than testing the firm-level NPI hypotheses.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: the past focus × dynamism interaction is negative (Table 2, Model 3: B = −0.16, p < .01). Table 3 reports dy/dx = 0.80 in stable environments and −2.13 in dynamic environments; their difference is significant (t = 4.47, p < .001).
- H2 is supported: the present focus × dynamism interaction is positive (B = 0.12, p < .01). Present-focus marginal effects are positive in both contexts and greater in dynamic environments (dy/dx = 2.71 versus 1.34 in stable environments; t = 2.28, p < .05).
- H3 receives qualified support: the future focus × dynamism interaction is positive (B = 0.13, p < .05). Table 3 reports dy/dx = 1.12 in dynamic environments and −0.84 in stable environments (difference: t = 2.70, p < .01), but the stable-environment coefficient is only marginally negative (B = −0.09, p < .10); the authors describe support for that negative relationship as weak.

Table 3’s dynamic and stable subsamples contain 519 and 586 observations, respectively. Its B and dy/dx columns are distinct; the results prose calls the B values “marginal effects,” whereas the marginal effects reported above follow the table’s dy/dx column. In combined-profile analyses using log-transformed NPI, low past/high present/high future focus is associated with higher NPI than every other profile in dynamic environments; high past/high present/low future focus exceeds other profiles in stable environments except the hypertemporal profile, high on all three dimensions, from which it does not differ significantly. The authors report consistent patterns with alternative averaging windows, firm fixed effects, additional controls, alternative sample splits, and endogeneity corrections; under the reported antecedent-based correction, the positive past-focus association in stable environments is only marginal (p < .10).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study identifies CEO temporal attentional biases as microfoundations of temporal considerations in NPI, extending accounts centered on organizational knowledge and improvisational capabilities. It treats past, present, and future focus as distinct dimensions that can coexist, and uses executive job demands to explain why their relationships with NPI depend on environmental dynamism rather than being universally beneficial or harmful.

**Practical Implication**
The findings alert CEOs to different temporal-focus profiles associated with higher NPI in stable and dynamic contexts. For firms where NPI is a top strategic priority, the authors suggest considering temporal focus in CEO selection to assess candidates more comprehensively and improve hiring decisions.

**Limitations**
The large, single-business sample limits generalization to SMEs and diversified firms, and secondary data and psycholinguistic measurement may retain biases despite validation. The argument assumes CEOs play a central role in NPI and may not hold where efficiency and cost-cutting take priority over innovation. Managerial discretion provides an alternative account of environmental moderation, and dynamism captures only one facet of environmental context.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining SMEs and diversified firms and using primary data to strengthen the evidence. They also call for investigation of managerial discretion as a boundary condition and other environmental facets such as complexity. Their archival approach could be extended to measure temporal depth, time urgency, polychronicity, and time pacing.
