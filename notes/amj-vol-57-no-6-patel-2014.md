# Structural Power Equality between Family and Non-Family TMT Members and the Performance of Family Firms

**Abstract**
The upper echelons of publicly traded family firms can comprise family members and non-family members. Due to ownership and control factors, family members often wield significant influence in the upper echelons. If non-family members lack sources of influence, they may exhibit lower participation. Limited participation by non-family members lowers access to and integration of knowledge from non-family members, thus lowering the ability to devise strategic actions that increase performance. Drawing on the structural basis of power, we set out that greater equality in structural power (or compensation, status, and representation) across family and non-family top management team members increases performance in family firms. Moreover, we posit that this relationship is stronger under increasing environmental dynamism and higher governance performance, but weaker under the presence of a founder CEO. Using a sample of 231 publicly traded family firms, representing 1,934 firm-year observations from 2001 to 2010 and controlling for endogeneity, we find support for these proposed relationships.

**Research Question**
Does greater equality in structural power between family and non-family TMT members improve family-firm performance, and how do environmental dynamism, governance performance, and founder versus non-founder CEO leadership condition this relationship?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Structural power equality—balance in compensation, status, and representation between family and non-family TMT members—increases firm performance.
- H2: The positive relationship between structural power equality and firm performance becomes stronger as environmental dynamism increases.
- H3: The positive relationship between structural power equality and firm performance becomes stronger as governance performance increases.
- H4: The relationship between structural power equality and firm performance is stronger under non-founder CEOs, whether family or non-family, than under founder CEOs.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Structural power equality between family and non-family TMT members in compensation, titles, and board representation.
- DV(s): Firm performance, primarily unsystematic stock returns; ROA, Tobin’s q, and ROS are alternative outcomes.
- Mediators: No formal mediation test; participation, information exchange, collaboration, and joint decision making are proposed processes. Post-hoc participation and strategic-action measures provide indirect evidence.
- Moderators: Environmental dynamism and governance performance strengthen the proposed relationship; non-founder CEO leadership strengthens it relative to founder leadership.

Family executives’ ownership power and socioemotional wealth priorities can restrict non-family executives’ participation and the use of their knowledge. Balancing structural power is proposed to improve information exchange, collaboration, and shared decisions while creating mutual accountability that accommodates economic and family goals. These processes become more valuable under dynamism and effective governance; founders can already bridge entrepreneurial and familial priorities, making structural equality less consequential under founder leadership.

**Data & Measures**
The panel covers 231 publicly traded U.S. manufacturing family firms and 1,934 firm-year observations during 2001–2010, selected from S&P 1500 firms. A family firm has at least one founder or relative serving as a top executive or director and at least 5% family blockownership; the TMT consists of the top five ExecuComp executives. Industry sales come from Compustat and trading information from CRSP, with executive/family information triangulated from ExecuComp, SEC filings, and corporate directories. Outcomes are measured at t and independent/control variables generally at t−1. The authors define unsystematic return as the mean residual from their quarterly Fama–French four-factor return model, including market excess return, size, book-to-market, and momentum factors.

Structural power is operationalized through total compensation, title counts, and board membership. For each indicator and their composite, equality is PDcat = 1 − [Σ(pi − 0.5)²]/(n/4), where pi is the non-family share of a power indicator and n is the number of indicators: square each share’s deviation from 0.5, sum the squared deviations, divide by n/4, and subtract from one; the composite uses all three indicators. Environmental dynamism averages instability and unpredictability from quarterly sales in the firm’s four-digit SIC industry over a rolling 20-quarter window: instability uses the antilog of the standard error from a regression of log industry sales, while unpredictability uses one minus adjusted R² from a sales/lagged-sales regression with growth, decline, and cyclical trends controlled. Governance performance uses GovernanceMetrics International ratings from 1 to 10 in 0.5 increments; the CEO indicator is 1 for non-founders and 0 for founders.

The analysis first predicts structural power equality through 2SLS, using family-CEO tenure/age, non-family-CEO tenure/age, and shared family/firm names as instruments, then estimates a dynamic-panel model with a one-year-lagged outcome and t−2 instruments. Each tenure/age instrument is missing when the corresponding CEO type is absent. Controls include R&D and capital intensity, Altman’s Z, slack, TMT demographic diversity, the family/non-family executive ratio and its square, family ownership, and industry. These observational estimates depend on instrument assumptions; the authors describe their proposed instruments as weak.

Post-hoc participation proxies are whether a non-family TMT member attends more than 75% of annual board meetings and the mean ratios of non-family members on audit, compensation, and nominating/governance committees; turnover is the percentage of non-family TMT members leaving at t. Strategic-action repertoire is a diversity measure based on annual action-category shares coded from 2,279 Predicast’s F&S news headlines. Its exact aggregation wording and category count are inconsistent: p. 1638 lists five categories but calls them six, and describes squaring shares and subtracting from one before referring to a “sum of the inverse square of proportions”; no unambiguous formula is supplied here.

**Key Findings**
The primary unsystematic-return model supports H1–H4 (Table 2): structural power equality is positive (β = 0.02, p < .05), as are its interactions with environmental dynamism (β = 0.41, p < .05), governance performance (β = 0.03, p < .05), and non-founder CEO leadership (β = 0.43, p < .05). The authors interpret the dynamism plot as showing higher returns with equality under high dynamism but no increase under low dynamism. When non-founder family and non-family CEOs are coded separately, the reported estimates are only marginally significant (β = 0.32 and 0.28, respectively, both p < .10).

Post-hoc tests associate greater equality with higher board attendance (coefficient = 0.40, p < .05) and committee representation (0.28, p < .05), and lower non-family turnover (−0.01, p < .10); the turnover association is marginal and none of its three moderator interactions is significant (Table 3). For strategic-action repertoire, the standardized composite-equality coefficient is 0.18 (p < .05), with positive interactions for dynamism (0.26, p < .01), governance (0.16, p < .05), and non-founder CEO leadership (0.16, p < .05). The last attribution follows Table 3 and the discussion on p. 1640; p. 1638 inconsistently labels it “founder CEO.” Component-level moderation is uneven: directorship equality × dynamism, compensation equality × governance, and directorship equality × governance are nonsignificant; title equality × non-founder CEO is marginal (p < .10). These analyses provide indirect support for participation and broader strategic action, without directly establishing the proposed behavioral processes.

The authors report consistent direction and significance for the primary hypotheses with alternative family-firm definitions and with ROA, Tobin’s q, and ROS as outcomes. Equality is also positively related to unsystematic returns in analyses of historical and industry-peer performance shortfalls (reported coefficients = 0.09 and 0.08, respectively, both p < .05). These robustness results do not eliminate the post-hoc component-level nulls or the source inconsistencies in the repertoire measure.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends upper echelons and family-firm research by distinguishing structural power equality between family and non-family coalitions from overall power dispersion within a TMT. It proposes participation as a means of integrating their different knowledge and economic/socioemotional priorities, and specifies environmental dynamism, governance, and founder leadership as boundary conditions. It also extends research on power dispersion in high-power teams through a measure of power distribution across pre-existing categories.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that family firms can preserve family ownership while giving non-family executives greater structural influence through compensation, titles, and board representation. They present such inclusion as particularly valuable after founder leadership, under increasing environmental dynamism, and alongside effective governance that helps integrate family and business goals.

**Limitations**
The study cannot directly measure closeness, cohesion, trust, benevolence, or the behavioral negotiation of family and non-family goals; attendance and committee representation are indirect participation proxies. Time and performance-feedback dynamics are not fully assessed, ownership/control may inadequately capture socioemotional wealth and differences among family members, and structural power may operate asymmetrically across the two coalitions. Generalization is limited to large publicly traded U.S. manufacturing firms: smaller firms may lack boards or larger TMTs; private firms may face different stakeholder pressures and may not obtain higher returns from greater structural power equality; regulated service industries may have different governance processes and emphases on economic reference points.

**Future Research**
The authors propose qualitative investigation of TMT microprocesses, including goal negotiation, decision comprehensiveness, speed and quality, power congruence, and relationship, task, and process conflict. They call for examining how performance feedback changes relative influence over time and for measuring socioemotional wealth across multiple family members while accounting for their unequal influence. They also suggest studying power distribution across other TMT categories, including gender, race, and functional background.
