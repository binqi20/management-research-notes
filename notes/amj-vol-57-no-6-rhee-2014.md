# Framing Controversial Actions: Regulatory Focus, Source Credibility, and Stock Market Reaction to Poison Pill Adoption

**Abstract**

We contribute to the research on organizational accounts by examining the role of different framing languages and the credibility of the frame articulator on justifying controversial organizational actions. Drawing on regulatory focus theory and the literature on source credibility, we develop novel arguments as to how a gains-versusnonlosses framing and the perceived credibility of the speaker influence stakeholder responses, as well as how the effectiveness of these aspects is influenced by context. We test our arguments using data on the framing of the adoption of “poison pills” by U.S. firms between 1983 and 2008. Using content analysis and an event study, we find that a gains framing aligned with the dominant institutional logic leads to a positive stock market reaction, while statements emanating from speakers with potentially selfserving interests negatively affect the stock market reaction. Our findings further show that the effectiveness of framing and source credibility are dependent on contextual attributes such as speaker visibility, prior performance, and practice prevalence.

**Research Question**

How do gains versus nonlosses framing and the identity of the frame articulator influence stock market responses to poison pill adoption, and how do speaker visibility, prior firm performance, and adoption prevalence condition these relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1a: Announcements using shareholder value enhancement framing are positively associated with stock market reaction.
- H1b: Announcements using shareholder value protection framing are positively associated with stock market reaction.
- H2: Shareholder value enhancement framing is more positively associated with stock market reaction than shareholder value protection framing.
- H3: Framing poison pill adoption by a dual CEO/chair is negatively associated with stock market reaction.
- H4: CEO/chair visibility weakens the negative effect of framing by the CEO/chair on stock market reaction.
- H5a: Greater prior firm performance enhances the positive effects of both shareholder value enhancement and shareholder value protection framing.
- H5b: Greater prior firm performance weakens the negative effect of framing by a dual CEO/chair.
- H6: More recent poison pill adoptions enhance the positive effects of both shareholder value enhancement and shareholder value protection framing.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Shareholder value enhancement framing, shareholder value protection framing, and a quotation from a dual CEO/chair in the adoption announcement.
- DV(s): Stock market reaction, measured as cumulative abnormal return (CAR) around the announcement.
- Mediators: No mediation test; investor regulatory focus and judgments of source trustworthiness are proposed explanations.
- Moderators: Speaker visibility for the CEO/chair relationship; prior firm performance for both framing languages and the CEO/chair relationship; recent adoption prevalence for both framing languages.

Both framing languages align poison pill adoption with shareholder value, but enhancement invokes promotion-focused gains and maximal goals, whereas protection invokes prevention-focused nonlosses and minimal goals. The authors argue that gains should be more compelling under the positive outcome expectations associated with shareholder value. A dual CEO/chair's potential self-interest raises credibility concerns about managerial entrenchment; visibility and strong prior performance are proposed to reduce these concerns, while prior performance and recent adoptions provide contextual information that can strengthen framing effectiveness. These psychological explanations are theorized rather than directly measured, and the empirical design establishes associations rather than experimental causal effects.

**Data & Measures**

The initial frame comprised 1,398 NYSE- and AMEX-listed U.S. firms first adopting poison pills during 1983–2008, excluding adoptions at spinoff or IPO and investment entities such as mutual funds and REITs. SDC Platinum records were checked against PR Newswire and Business Wire releases; about 15% lacked accompanying releases and about 30% were excluded for confounding events. The retained sample comprises 789 adoptions during 1984–2008, whereas Table 2 reports 711 regression observations; the paper does not explain this difference. The announcement date is the first public release date. Daily abnormal return equals actual firm return minus the market-model prediction, AR_it = R_it − (a_i + b_i R_mt), using pre-event OLS estimates and the CRSP value-weighted market index; CAR sums these abnormal returns over the main window (−1, +1). Alternative windows are (0, +1), (−1, 0), and (−2, +2).

Three business-school graduate students, including the first author, coded framing in the releases (Cohen's κ = .84). Enhancement framing is a dummy for statements promising gains or preserving opportunities for beneficial advancement, including favorable takeovers; protection framing is a dummy for preventing negative outcomes or ensuring fair takeover treatment. A separate dummy records whether a dual CEO/chair is quoted, operationalizing the theorized credibility concern rather than directly measuring perceived trustworthiness; 252 releases quote such a speaker, and 328 quote no speaker. Visibility counts the articulator's media mentions in the preceding year in the Wall Street Journal, Financial Times, Business Week, The Economist, and Forbes, retrieved through LexisNexis. Prior performance is lagged return on equity; adoption prevalence is the natural log of the previous year's poison pill adoption count. Controls include logged assets, M&A speculation, CEO duality, institutional ownership, outside directors, classified boards, release word count, the ratio of positive emotion to total emotion content measured with LIWC, pill duration, trigger percentage, and year indicators. OLS uses Huber-White robust standard errors; tests are one-tailed for directional hypotheses and two-tailed for controls.

**Key Findings**

For the main three-day window, H1a is supported: enhancement framing is positively associated with CAR (Table 2, Models 2 and 4: b = .011, p ≤ .01; Models 5–7: b = .012, p ≤ .001). H1b is unsupported: protection framing has no significant positive main association. H2 receives only marginal support in the reported coefficient-comparison test (p = .072). The comparison prose prints coefficients of 0.1 and 0.01, which differ from Table 2's separately entered enhancement and protection coefficients of .011 and .002; this source discrepancy is not resolved here. H3 is supported: a dual CEO/chair quotation is negatively associated with CAR after controlling for CEO duality (Model 5: b = −.009, p ≤ .01; Model 9: b = −.014, p ≤ .001). H4 is supported by positive CEO/chair-by-visibility interactions (Models 6 and 9, p ≤ .01; coefficients round to .000 in the table).

H5a is only partially and marginally supported: prior performance does not significantly moderate enhancement framing, but its interaction with protection framing is positive (Models 7 and 9: b = .005, p ≤ .10). H5b is supported: prior performance attenuates the negative CEO/chair association (Models 7 and 9: interaction b = .008, p ≤ .05). H6 likewise receives partial, marginal support: recent adoptions do not significantly moderate enhancement framing, but positively moderate protection framing (Models 8 and 9: b = .008, p ≤ .10). Supplementary mean comparisons show CAR of 0.51% with enhancement framing versus −0.39% without that framing and −0.56% with neither framing, with significant differences; protection-framing comparisons are nonsignificant. CAR is −0.30% for CEO/chair quotations versus 0.38% for the remaining announcements, a significant difference.

Robustness across alternative event windows is uneven. H1a and H2 are reported as confirmed in every alternative window; H3 holds except in (0, +1). H4 is supported in (0, +1) and (−2, +2); H5a has partial support only for protection framing in (−2, +2), and H5b is supported only in (−1, 0). H6 is supported only for protection framing and fails in (0, +1). An additional analysis reports a weak negative association when both framing languages are used simultaneously; the proposed explanation that mixed goals confuse investors remains speculative.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The paper connects organizational accounts and institutional framing to regulatory focus theory, explaining why two accounts aligned with the same shareholder-value logic can receive different responses. It extends framing analysis beyond content to the identity and credibility of the speaker and the organizational and adoption context. It also contributes to behavioral and sociological accounts of financial markets by treating organizational sensegiving as part of investors' interpretation of events, with implications for event studies that omit announcement content and speaker identity.

**Practical Implication**

For firms announcing poison pill adoption, the findings suggest that wording focused on shareholder gains can receive a more favorable short-term market response than the more commonly used language of shareholder protection. The credibility of the quoted speaker and the context of the announcement also matter; the results do not establish that framing creates durable shareholder value.

**Limitations**

Short event windows reduce contamination from other events but cannot establish whether framing effects persist or dissipate. The study focuses on trustworthiness inferred from speaker identity while assuming that CEOs and board chairs possess relevant expertise; differences in experience, reputation, or status could also matter. It does not examine media reactions to poison pill announcements, although public discourse may influence market responses.

**Future Research**

The authors call for research on long-term framing effects and their moderators, including constraints imposed by earlier framings and consistency between positions. They propose examining different sources of credibility and their interactions, public theorization and media reactions, and differences and interactions among media, stock-market, and analyst audiences. They also suggest investigating whether bull and bear markets or business-cycle expansions and contractions shape audiences' regulatory focus, while cautioning that market-level indicators may not capture individual investors' dispositions.
