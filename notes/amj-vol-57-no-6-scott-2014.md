# Why Do Managers Act Fairly in the First Place? A Daily Investigation of “Hot” and “Cold” Motives and Discretion

**Abstract**
Although considerable research has focused on employee reactions to organizational justice, far less research has examined why managers adhere to rules of justice in the first place. Taking a proactive approach to organizational justice, we address this void by examining managerial motives for adhering to distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal rules of justice on a day-to-day basis. Results of an experience-sampling study of 90 managers who completed daily surveys over a three-week period revealed that both “cold” cognitive (i.e., effecting compliance, identity maintenance, and establishing fairness) and “hot” affective (i.e., high positive affect and low negative affect) motives were associated with managerial adherence to justice rules. Moreover, “cold” motives were more strongly associated with justice rule adherence for justice dimensions over which managers perceived less discretion, while “hot” motives were more strongly associated with justice rule adherence for justice dimensions over which managers perceived greater discretion. We discuss the implications of our findings for both theory and practice.

**Research Question**
Why do managers adhere to distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal justice rules on some days more than others, and how does the relative importance of cognitive versus affective motives differ across justice dimensions affording different levels of discretion?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Managers are more likely to adhere to all four types of justice rules when motivated to (a) effect compliance in subordinates, (b) create and maintain a desired identity, and/or (c) establish fairness.
- H2: Managers are more likely to adhere to all four types of justice rules when experiencing (a) high positive affect and/or (b) low negative affect.
- H3: Perceived discretion increases from distributive to procedural to informational to interpersonal justice.
- H4: Across that same progression, cognitive motives explain less variance in justice rule adherence, while affective motives explain more.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cognitive motives of effecting compliance, identity maintenance, and establishing fairness; affective motives involving positive and negative affect.
- DV(s): Adherence to distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal justice rules.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Discretion is the proposed explanation for differences in motive importance across justice dimensions; the tests compare dimensions rather than estimate a motive-by-discretion interaction.

The actor-focused model proposes that managers follow justice rules to influence employees, maintain a desired social identity, or establish fairness, as well as for more spontaneous affective reasons. Systemic constraints, costs, public visibility, and formal exchanges are theorized to make adherence less discretionary and more cognitively demanding; greater freedom allows affective motives more influence. These proposed sources of discretion differences are not directly measured.

**Data & Measures**
The pilot recruited supervisors working at least 30 hours weekly through StudyResponse: 83 of 117 invitees responded; the subsequent CFA reports n = 82. Item reduction retained six compliance, six identity-maintenance, five fairness, three positive-affect, and three negative-affect items. The main study involved 90 managers: 34 in health insurance, 38 in a nonprofit, and 18 in retail. An initial discretion survey preceded three weeks of end-of-workday surveys; managers completed 960 of 1,350 possible daily surveys. For each justice dimension, managers first indicated whether a relevant action occurred that day, then rated adherence and motives for its most recent occurrence. Table 3 reports observations/managers of 309/77 for distributive, 406/79 for procedural, 570/84 for informational, and 731/88 for interpersonal justice.

Daily adherence used one item per dimension, adapted from Colquitt (2001): outcomes justified by subordinate effort/contribution; opportunity to express views/feelings; candid, thorough, timely information; and dignity, respect, politeness. The pilot-developed motive items asked why the action occurred, including affective reasons such as being happy or angry. Initial discretion items assessed perceived freedom over outcomes, procedures, information, and interpersonal treatment. Adherence, motives, and discretion used five-point extent scales. Random-coefficient HLM predicted each dimension's adherence from its five motives, controlling survey time of day and day of week; all level-1 predictors were centered at managers' means. The design estimates within-manager associations. Repeated-measures ANOVA and paired comparisons assessed discretion differences. Category dominance statistics averaged incremental R² across the two entry orders of cognitive and affective motives, then divided by total variance explained by those predictors above the controls to obtain shares of predictable criterion variance.

**Key Findings**
H1 and H2 received partial support. In Table 3, significant unstandardized coefficients (all p < .05) were: distributive justice—compliance B = .38, identity B = −.23, negative affect B = −.13; procedural justice—compliance B = .20, identity B = .25, positive affect B = .13, negative affect B = −.25; informational justice—compliance B = .09, fairness B = .07, negative affect B = .10; interpersonal justice—identity B = .08, positive affect B = .05, negative affect B = −.12. The other motive coefficients were nonsignificant. Identity's negative distributive association and negative affect's positive informational association ran counter to prediction. Adding level-2 mean-motive controls preserved significance except for positive affect predicting interpersonal adherence, whose p-value changed from .047 to .051.

H3 was supported: discretion means increased from 2.70 to 3.29 to 4.23 to 4.74 across distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal justice; the linear trend was significant (F = 157.86, p < .05), as were all six paired comparisons (p < .01). H4 was supported: cognitive motives accounted for 77.6%, 71.7%, 57.0%, and 35.9% of predictable criterion variance, respectively, while affective motives accounted for 22.4%, 28.3%, 43.0%, and 64.1%. Thus affective motives dominated cognitive motives for interpersonal justice. Within-manager variation constituted 80.0%, 79.1%, 74.0%, and 67.8% of adherence variance across the same four dimensions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study advances proactive organizational justice research by locating substantial variation in fair treatment within managers across episodes, complementing accounts based on stable differences between managers. It tests the actor-focused model's cognitive and affective motives and extends its precision by showing that their relative predictive importance differs across justice dimensions with different perceived discretion.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations focus on daily motive expression and encourage managers to use justice rule adherence as a means of gaining employee compliance rather than coercion. They also propose training managers to regulate negative moods, including deep acting, while ensuring that managers' resources are not overtaxed; these are recommendations rather than tested interventions.

**Limitations**
Managers' same-source reports collected close together raise common-method concerns, including demand effects and implicit theories, and self-reported adherence may be upwardly biased and range-restricted. Sampling only the most recent relevant event may miss important events, especially repeated interpersonal encounters. The study establishes perceived discretion differences but does not directly assess the proposed systemic, cost, observability, or exchange-versus-encounter factors explaining them.

**Future Research**
The authors propose alternative event-sampling methods, while noting the priming and demand risks of asking managers to recall especially fair or unfair actions, and direct investigation of the factors producing discretion differences. They also call for research on employees' inferences about managerial motives and resulting reactions, situations in which managers are motivated to act fairly but cannot, and external motives such as complying with company policy.
