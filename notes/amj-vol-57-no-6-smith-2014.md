# Dynamic Decision Making: A Model of Senior Leaders Managing Strategic Paradoxes

**Abstract**
Senior leaders increasingly embed paradoxes into their organization’s strategy, but struggle to manage them effectively. To better understand how they do so, I compared in-depth qualitative data from six top management teams exploring and exploiting simultaneously. The results informed a model of dynamic decision making in which strategic paradoxes can be effectively engaged. The details of this dynamic decisionmaking model extend and complicate our understanding of managing paradoxes by depicting dilemmas and paradoxes as interwoven, explicating a consistently inconsistent pattern of addressing tensions, and framing both differentiating and integrating practices as necessary for engaging paradox.

**Research Question**
How do senior leaders make decisions that sustain commitments to strategic paradoxes, specifically exploring and exploiting simultaneously, despite pressures to choose a single strategic domain?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Strategic paradox: contradictory yet interrelated demands embedded in organizational goals, examined through commitments to exploration and exploitation.
- Experienced tensions: resource allocation, organizational design, and product design issues arise as both immediate “either/or” dilemmas requiring choices and persistent paradoxical tensions that resist resolution.
- Differentiating practices: allocating domain-specific roles, comparing domains to raise novel distinctions, and seeking information about domains independently.
- Integrating practices: allocating integrative roles, stressing overarching goals, and solving problems jointly.
- Decision-making context: valuing distinct strategic domains and their interconnections, with attention shifting between products and the overall organization.
- Decision pattern: temporary, flexible decisions shift support between exploration and exploitation over time, with some decisions accommodating both.

The emergent model proposes that differentiating and integrating practices together sustain an adaptive context for making choices without abandoning either strategic commitment. Differentiating clarifies and supports each domain; integrating highlights their contributions to the whole and encourages confronting conflicts and seeking compromises. Individual decisions address particular dilemmas but generate further issues, so sustained paradox is expressed through a “consistently inconsistent” pattern over time rather than through every decision supporting both demands simultaneously. This is an inductively developed process explanation from case comparisons, not an experimentally tested causal mechanism.

**Data & Measures**
The six cases were strategic business units in one Fortune 500 corporation, each with an annual commitment to explore and exploit; top management teams comprised general managers and their direct reports. Software 1, Life Sciences, and Software 3 were real-time cases; Software 2, IT Services, and Semiconductor were retrospective cases. Each case was observed for a year, within data collection lasting more than two years. The corpus comprised 96 interviews with 65 distinct informants, 16 senior leadership meetings, and 40 archival documents. Interviews lasted 45 minutes to two hours and were documented through extensive notes rather than recordings; an internal consultant accompanied the researcher for 10% of interviews. Informants included unit leaders, division managers, internal consultants, and corporate executives. Existing products had been in the market for eight to 20 years; innovations had been introduced less than a year before the study.

Exploitation meant incremental improvements to an existing product’s technology or marketing to increase efficiencies in the same target market; exploration meant developing and commercializing nonincremental innovations. Analysis iteratively developed thick case descriptions, identified key issues, coded practices and decision patterns, and integrated the findings with literature. Issues had to be salient and challenging, involve both strategic domains, lie within senior leaders’ responsibility, and be raised by multiple informants. The analysis identified 47 issues in eight types grouped into resource allocation, organizational design, and product design; Table 3 reports 315 coded instances of responses to tensions. In vivo codes and short descriptions were grouped into six leadership practices and two decision-context themes. Decisions were classified as supporting exploration, exploitation, or both, with inaction classified according to the status quo; within-case decision records then identified patterns across issues. Informant feedback, triangulation, and external researchers’ review supported interpretation. The study builds theory through qualitative comparison rather than estimating effects on financial performance.

**Key Findings**
All six teams initially committed to exploration and exploitation, but only Software 1, Software 2, and IT Services sustained both commitments. These teams combined differentiating and integrating practices; Life Sciences and Semiconductor emphasized differentiation and ultimately only explored, whereas Software 3 emphasized integration and only exploited. The latter cases exhibited gaps in understanding or supporting both domains and suppressed conflicts, in contrast to the contexts valuing distinct domains and their connections in the three cases sustaining paradox.

In the three cases sustaining both commitments, individual decisions usually favored one domain, while their patterns shifted support across domains over time. Table 6 reports, respectively, exploitation-favoring, exploration-favoring, and joint-support decisions of 2, 6, and 1 for Software 1; 3, 7, and 1 for Software 2; and 2, 5, and 1 for IT Services. Leaders treated resources and organizational designs as flexible or temporary, and choices generated further issues. The resulting model links interwoven dilemmas and paradoxes, both practice sets, and a consistently inconsistent decision pattern; it does not establish a financial-performance effect or a statistically tested necessity claim.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study complicates the distinction between dilemmas and paradoxes by showing how the same strategic issues can demand immediate choices while embodying persistent tensions. It extends dynamic equilibrium theorizing by shifting attention from responses to isolated issues toward a pattern of choosing, accommodating, and accepting over time in service of sustained strategic paradox. It also specifies what leaders do to differentiate and integrate, proposing that both sets of practices are necessary for sustaining paradox and complementing ambidexterity research focused on leadership characteristics with a process account of leadership practice.

**Practical Implication**
Managers seeking to sustain strategic paradoxes should consider their pattern of decisions over time and embrace inconsistencies across decisions rather than requiring each decision to follow a consistent strategic direction. Senior leaders can embed differentiating and integrating practices in their top management teams, an approach the paper offers when hiring for or training paradoxical thinking may be difficult.

**Limitations**
The model’s generalizability remains open because the study concerns exploration and exploitation within one Fortune 500 corporation that encouraged and supported strategic paradoxes. Innovations had entered the market six months to a year before the study, so many observed tensions concerned expanding the innovation; other phases may generate different pressures. The paper explicitly conditions its broader implications on further empirical testing in broader settings.

**Future Research**
Research could examine other strategic paradoxes, such as social missions versus profits or global demands versus local needs, and investigate corporate contexts and innovation phases that facilitate or thwart dynamic decision making. It could also examine how leaders engage and communicate with subordinates who seek consistency and may interpret inconsistent decisions as hypocrisy or rationalize them around a single goal. Further work could investigate how the model sustains organizational dynamism and change through the mutually constitutive relationship between exploration and exploitation.
