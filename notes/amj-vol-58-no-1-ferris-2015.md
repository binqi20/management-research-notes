# Ostracism, Self-Esteem, and Job Performance: When Do We Self-Verify and When Do We Self-Enhance?

**Abstract**
Self-esteem level has been positioned as a key mediating mechanism accounting for the effects of ostracism on behaviors, invoking the notion that individuals seek to verify their self-perceptions by behaving in a way that is consistent with those self-perceptions. However, evidence supporting the relation of ostracism and self-esteem level to behavioral outcomes has been mixed. We argue that such mixed effects arise because individuals may engage in behaviors alternately to verify their self-perceptions (suggesting a relation between self-esteem level and behavioral outcomes) or to self-enhance (suggesting no relation between self-esteem level and behavioral outcomes). Within this framing, the question becomes: When do we self-verify and when do we self-enhance? To that end, we position contingent self-esteem—or the extent to which individuals base their self-worth on outcomes in a particular domain—as a determining factor in whether we self-verify or self-enhance, and present a moderated mediation model to account for varying relations between ostracism and job performance. Our predictions regarding self-verification and self-enhancement motivation are fully supported across two field samples using multi-wave, multi-source study designs. Theoretical and practical implications for self-verification and self-enhancement motivation, as well as negative interpersonal behaviors at work, are discussed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when ostracized employees self-verify through behavior consistent with lowered self-esteem and when they self-enhance by maintaining strong job performance. It examines whether the importance of performance to self-esteem determines when self-esteem level mediates the relationship between workplace ostracism and job performance outcomes.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Workplace ostracism will have a negative relation with self-esteem level.
- H2: Importance of performance to self-esteem (IPSE) moderates self-esteem level's relation with (a) in-role behaviors (IRBs), (b) organizational citizenship behaviors (OCBs), and (c) deviant behavior, such that relations are stronger when IPSE is low. The theorized relations are positive for IRBs and OCBs and negative for deviance.
- H3: IPSE moderates self-esteem level's mediating effect between workplace ostracism and (a) IRBs, (b) OCBs, and (c) deviant behavior; the theorized indirect effects are stronger when IPSE is low, decreasing IRBs and OCBs and increasing deviance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Workplace ostracism.
- DV(s): In-role behaviors, individually directed organizational citizenship behaviors, organizationally directed organizational citizenship behaviors, organizational deviance, and interpersonal deviance.
- Mediators: Self-esteem level.
- Moderators: Importance of performance to self-esteem.

The central mechanism is that workplace ostracism lowers self-esteem, and lower self-esteem can lead employees to act in ways that verify negative self-perceptions through poorer performance or more deviance. The paper argues that this self-verification path is strongest when employees' self-worth is not contingent on workplace performance. When performance is important to self-esteem, employees are motivated to self-enhance in that domain, so self-esteem level no longer predicts poorer job performance in the same way.

**Data & Measures**
Sample A comprised 158 matched employee/work-peer datasets, recruited through online forums. Employees reported ostracism in the first survey and self-esteem approximately one week later; nominated peers subsequently rated IPSE and performance. Sample B comprised 273 complete datasets from two data collections (185 and 88 participants), recruited through advertisements in public and commuter areas, newspapers, and online forums. Employees reported coworker ostracism and self-esteem in one survey; peers rated IPSE and deviance. These are observational field surveys with limited causal inference, including cross-sectional theoretical paths.

In both samples, employees completed a 10-item ostracism measure on a seven-point frequency scale; Sample A concerned others generally, whereas Sample B specified coworkers. Self-esteem used Rosenberg's 10-item scale with nine-point agreement responses. Peers rated IPSE using four items adapted from Crocker et al.'s competence-contingent self-esteem scale on a seven-point scale; one item was dropped from the original five-item measure to improve reliability in Sample B, and the change was mirrored in Sample A. Sample A's performance measures used Williams and Anderson's 21 items: seven each for individually directed OCBs (OCBI), organizationally directed OCBs (OCBO), and IRBs, rated by peers on a five-point agreement scale. Sample B used Bennett and Robinson's 19-item deviance scale, adapted for peers: 12 organizational and seven interpersonal deviance items, rated for frequency over the past year on a seven-point scale. Multiple regressions and simple effects at IPSE one standard deviation above/below its mean tested moderation and conditional indirect effects, with 1,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected confidence intervals.

**Key Findings**
H1 was supported in both samples: ostracism was negatively related to self-esteem (Table 3, unstandardized coefficients −.19 in Sample A and −.29 in Sample B, both p < .01). H2 was supported for all five outcomes. Table 4 interaction coefficients were −.12 for OCBI and −.13 for OCBO (both p < .05), −.18 for IRBs, .26 for organizational deviance, and .20 for interpersonal deviance (all p < .01). In Table 5, low-IPSE self-esteem slopes in Sample A were .30 for OCBI, .29 for OCBO, and .37 for IRBs; in Sample B they were −.40 for organizational and −.30 for interpersonal deviance. All were significant, while the corresponding high-IPSE slopes were nonsignificant; each low/high slope difference was significant.

H3 was fully supported (Table 5). At low IPSE, Sample A's conditional indirect effects were −.06 for OCBI (p < .01), −.06 for OCBO (p < .05), and −.08 for IRBs (p < .01); Sample B's were .12 for organizational deviance (p < .01) and .09 for interpersonal deviance (p < .05). At high IPSE, all five indirect effects were nonsignificant, and each low/high difference was significant. These patterns support the proposed moderated mediation model; the field design does not establish causal direction.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to ostracism research by explaining why prior findings on ostracism, self-esteem, and performance were mixed: the indirect effect depends on contingent self-esteem. It also contributes to motivation theory by positioning contingent self-esteem as a boundary condition separating self-verification from self-enhancement. More broadly, it offers a moderated mediation model that can extend to other negative interpersonal behaviors carrying rejection and self-worth threats.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that organizations should understand workplace ostracism as a threat to employees' self-esteem and job behavior, not only as an interpersonal discomfort. Interventions that support employee self-esteem may reduce performance costs of ostracism, but the authors caution that developing high performance-contingent self-esteem may have downsides for well-being.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the samples were convenience samples, so generalizability to the broader working population should be treated cautiously. They also state that the field data are cross-sectional for some theoretical paths, limiting causal inference. A further limitation is that performance could influence ostracism or self-esteem, and the available data are not well suited to test those reciprocal possibilities.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on whether relationship-contingent self-esteem moderates ostracism's effect on self-esteem. They also suggest examining other moderators of ostracism's direct performance effects, including task interdependence and situational interventions that cast doubt on whether ostracism occurred or improve affect after ostracism. Future studies could also integrate behavioral and well-being outcomes to test when contingent self-esteem buffers performance damage while worsening well-being.
