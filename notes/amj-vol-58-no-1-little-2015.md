# Professional Image Maintenance: How Women Navigate Pregnancy in the Workplace

**Abstract**
Women now constitute a significant portion of the workforce, making the effects of pregnancy on professional image (others’ perceptions of competence and character at work) more salient. While opinions regarding how pregnant women should manage others’ impressions and the consequences of doing so abound (Noveck, 2012) research to substantiate or disconfirm these opinions has lagged. In this paper, we present three studies that develop and test a model of social identity-based impression management (SIM) techniques used by pregnant workers. In Study 1 (n ⴝ 35), we utilized qualitative methods to identify the motives and strategies used by pregnant women to manage their professional images. In the second study, we collected two samples (n ⴝ 199 and n ⴝ 133) to develop and validate two scales based on the motives and strategies identified in Study 1. In Study 3 (n ⴝ 200), we employed a time-lagged design to examine how SIM motives and strategies affect important workplace outcomes: perceived discrimination, burnout, and returning to one’s job after maternity leave. Our findings demonstrate both positive and negative outcomes of the motives and strategies women use to manage their images at work when pregnant.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how pregnancy affects working women's professional experiences and how pregnant workers use social identity-based impression management to maintain professional image. It also asks how SIM motives and strategies relate to perceived discrimination, burnout, and returning to one's job after maternity leave.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Study 1 was inductive, without a priori hypotheses; Study 2 developed and validated the scales. Study 3 tested:
- H1: Legitimacy preservation positively relates to image maintenance.
- H2: Devaluation reduction positively relates to decategorization.
- H3a–c: Through image maintenance, legitimacy preservation is negatively and indirectly related to perceived discrimination (H3a) and burnout (H3b), and positively and indirectly related to returning to work after birth (H3c).
- H4a–c: Through decategorization, devaluation reduction is positively and indirectly related to perceived discrimination (H4a) and burnout (H4b), and negatively and indirectly related to returning to work after birth (H4c).

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / motives: Legitimacy preservation and devaluation reduction.
- Key constructs / strategies: Image maintenance and decategorization.
- Process / sequence: Qualitative identification of motives and strategies, scale development and validation, then time-lagged testing of links to workplace outcomes.
- Emergent outcomes: Perceived discrimination, burnout, and planned return to one's job.
- Boundary conditions / controls: Pregnancy stage, number of pregnancies at the organization, pregnancy risk, workplace gender composition, self-image at work, self-verification striving, and prior burnout.

The paper argues that pregnancy can make a new and potentially devalued social identity salient at work, threatening women's prior professional image. Pregnant workers respond through approach-oriented image maintenance, such as maintaining pace, avoiding accommodations, going the extra mile, or shortening leave, and through avoidance-oriented decategorization, such as passing or downplaying pregnancy. In Study 3, legitimacy preservation predicted image maintenance, which was associated with lower perceived discrimination, lower burnout, and greater likelihood of planning to return to the job; devaluation reduction predicted decategorization but was directly associated with higher perceived discrimination and burnout rather than being improved by avoidance strategies.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: Two interview phases included 18 and 17 women (35 total), generally pregnant with or recently having delivered their first child and working full-time during pregnancy. Interviews typically lasted 90 minutes and were recorded and transcribed. Four-stage grounded-theory analysis used line-by-line open coding, abstract categories, an additional coder for the first 18 interviews, refinement using the second phase, and iterative engagement with theory to identify motives, strategies, and their relationships.
- Study 2: Initial content-validity samples comprised 361 and 393 undergraduates for strategies and motives, respectively; another 67 assessed revised downplaying items. Pregnancy blogs, including Baby Center, supplied working pregnant respondents: 264 recruited for exploratory factor analysis, with 199 retained after listwise deletion, and 173 recruited for confirmation and validity assessment, with 133 retained. Five-point agreement items operationalized legitimacy preservation through proving one was the same and conveying professionalism; devaluation reduction through avoiding negative career consequences and convincing others one would not quit; image maintenance through maintaining pace, not requesting accommodations, going the extra mile, and shortening leave; and decategorization through passing and downplaying. The final scales contained 12 motive items and 21 strategy items (Tables 2–3; Figure 1). Confirmatory factor analysis and correlations with citizenship motives/behaviors, identity management, self-verification striving, and respect for nature assessed construct validity; means and covariance structure analyses compared trimesters and pregnancy experience.
- Study 3: A separate pregnancy-blog sample supplied 587 initial responses and 281 follow-ups; exclusions and listwise deletion yielded 200 women. The first survey occurred at 22–38 weeks pregnant; follow-ups were sent one week after due dates to women who had already disclosed pregnancy to supervisors. Completed surveys averaged 65.58 days apart, with the second averaging 18.73 days postpartum. Both waves measured the developed motives and strategies; the main path models used the first-wave measures to predict second-wave outcomes. Perceived discrimination used a modified eight-item, five-point scale; burnout used a seven-point frequency scale, both recalling late pregnancy. Return to one's job measured reported plans, not observed return: 168 planning to return to the same job were coded 1; not returning (15), changing companies (8), or being unsure (9) were coded 0. Controls included tenure, pregnancies at the organization, weeks pregnant, pregnancy risk, prior burnout, self-image, partner employment, workplace gender composition, and self-verification striving. Path analysis treated multi-item scales as single indicators and separately modeled continuous and categorical outcomes. This time-lagged, self-report design does not establish causality.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: Professional-image concerns were reported by 28 of 35 women (80%). Their six strategies formed approach-oriented image maintenance and avoidance-oriented decategorization; stated motives concerned legitimacy preservation and devaluation reduction. Participants generally described their own work identities as stable while others' views changed (Table 1).
- Study 2: The 21 strategy items yielded six factors explaining 73% of variance; the 12 motive items yielded three exploratory factors explaining 76%, with the two devaluation facets loading together. Subsequent confirmatory analysis favored the multidimensional devaluation model and supported higher-order motives and strategies (Figure 1); correlations generally supported convergent and discriminant validity (Table 4). Passing was lower in the second than first trimester (latent mean difference = −.61, p < .01), with no other significant trimester differences or differences by prior pregnancy experience.
- Study 3: The initially hypothesized continuous-outcome model fit poorly. The retained alternative added direct paths from devaluation reduction to discrimination and burnout. H1 and H2 were supported: legitimacy preservation related positively to image maintenance (γ = .39, p < .01), and devaluation reduction to decategorization (γ = .28, p < .01). H3a–c were supported by reported indirect effects through image maintenance on discrimination (−.10), burnout (−.12), and planned return (.50), all p < .05. H4a–c were unsupported: corresponding indirect effects through decategorization were .00, −.01, and −.00, all nonsignificant. Table 6 reports negative image-maintenance associations with discrimination (−.16) and burnout (−.14), both p < .05, and a positive association with planned return (.33, p < .01); decategorization's outcome paths were nonsignificant. Devaluation reduction instead had positive direct associations with discrimination (.44) and burnout (.18), both p < .01 in Table 6. These are associations and estimated indirect effects, not demonstrated causal effects or verified post-leave retention.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends professional image construction and SIM theory by showing that changing social identities can produce non-loss-focused image maintenance rather than the gain-focused positive distinctiveness emphasized in work on stable identities. It also develops and validates measures of SIM during pregnancy and integrates self-regulation theory to distinguish approach-related image maintenance from avoidance-related decategorization. The results show that approach-oriented SIMp strategies can have beneficial outcomes, whereas avoidance motives may be more consequential than avoidance behaviors.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that organizations should focus less on whether pregnant workers' priorities shift and more on reducing the devaluation concerns that make pregnancy feel professionally risky. Supportive supervisors and workplaces may diminish fears of pregnancy-related stigmatization and thereby reduce avoidance behaviors. The paper also indicates that image maintenance behaviors can be linked to plans to return to the job after maternity leave.

**Limitations**
The authors note possible common-method bias because the data came from the same source, although outcomes were collected in a separate time-lagged survey. They also state that the time lag may not have been long enough to observe meaningful changes in some SIMp constructs and that causality cannot be definitively established. The sample was largely U.S.-based, limiting claims about international generalizability.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on individual-level traits, additional mediators, and robust within-person designs across pregnancy stages and numbers of pregnancies. They also recommend testing whether the findings generalize to other new social identities, including chronic illness and other temporary or permanent identity changes. Future work should examine international differences in stereotypes of pregnant workers and the outcomes of gain-focused versus non-loss-focused SIM motives.
