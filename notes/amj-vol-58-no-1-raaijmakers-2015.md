# I Need Time! Exploring Pathways to Compliance under Institutional Complexity

**Abstract**
In this study, we examined responses to institutional complexity by analyzing when and how organizations respond to a coercive institutional demand from a powerful constituent when other important constituents do not accept the demand as legitimate. We experimentally manipulated institutional complexity and gauged the time to compliance of 100 childcare managers in the Netherlands, and then asked them to describe and explain their anticipated responses to multiple pressures. We found that institutional complexity leads decision makers to delay compliance, but usually not passively: decision makers used the time before compliance to attempt to reduce institutional complexity by neutralizing opposing pressures, challenging the coercive pressure, adapting the practice to suit opponents and their own personal beliefs, and/or waiting to see how the situation would unfold as multiple parties influenced one another. We found two factors influenced decision makers' choice of responses: (1) their interpretation of institutional complexity and (2) their personal beliefs toward the practice itself. Our findings contribute to an emerging understanding of how decision makers interpret and respond to institutional complexity, and thus complement recent studies in the institutional complexity literature.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when and how organizational decision makers respond to coercive institutional demands when important constituents contest the demand's legitimacy. It focuses on whether institutional complexity delays intended compliance and what pathways to compliance decision makers plan before complying.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a: Decision makers facing a coercive institutional pressure to adopt a new practice will intend to delay compliance longer when powerful external constituents do not support the practice than when they do.
- Hypothesis 1b: Decision makers facing that coercive pressure will intend to delay compliance longer when organization members do not support the practice than when they do.
- Hypothesis 2: Intended compliance delays will be shorter under low than moderate institutional complexity, and shorter under moderate than high institutional complexity. Study 1 tests both interaction and additive versions of this prediction; Study 2 develops an inductive account of planned responses.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key pressures: A coercive government demand to adopt a new childcare practice, combined with varying support or opposition from parents and employees.
- Timing outcome: Delay of intended compliance with the mandated digital wall.
- Response pathways: Early adoption, delay, accommodative responses such as neutralizing opposition and partial compliance, resistant responses such as fighting the practice and stimulating opposition, and exit.
- Interpretive conditions: Decision makers' interpretations of institutional complexity and their personal beliefs toward the mandated practice.
- Field process: Time before compliance is used either as a buffer against uncertainty or as a resource for action to reduce complexity.

The central process is that conflicting constituent pressures make the path to legitimacy ambiguous, so decision makers delay compliance rather than simply obeying the most coercive actor. During the delay, they may wait for others to clarify the situation or actively try to reshape pressures by influencing parents, employees, government actors, or the practice itself. Personal beliefs shape which responses they choose, especially whether they move toward accommodative or resistant pathways.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 used an experimental vignette with childcare managers in the Netherlands. Of 100 participants, one failed the manipulation check; three outliers among the remaining 99 were excluded from quantitative analysis but retained as extreme cases in Study 2, leaving n = 96 for the ANCOVA. Managers were randomly assigned to a 2 × 2 design manipulating high versus low parental support and high versus low employee support for a law requiring a digital wall, with enforcement beginning in five years. Parental support was conveyed through the views of the parent advisory committee and national parent association; employee support varied compatibility with existing work, norms, and skill acquisition. Low complexity meant both groups supported the practice, moderate complexity meant only one did, and high complexity meant neither did. The dependent variable was the intended compliance time marked on a five-year scale. Controls included gender, organizational size (number of locations), legal structure, trade association membership, commercial background, experience in the field and as a director, and interview-coded personal beliefs (fit, neutral, or misfit with the practice). Hypotheses 1a–1b were tested with ANCOVA; Hypothesis 2 used an interaction test and Scheffé comparisons across conditions.

Study 2 followed the questionnaire with recorded, transcribed, one-hour semi-structured interviews about managers' pedagogical views, interpretations of the law, reasons for their chosen timing, and anticipated actions. Table 6 covers 99 managers. One author coded all interviews, with other authors cross-checking codes and discussing themes. Action segments and their targets were grouped into second-order response categories, distinguishing accommodation, resistance, and delay, and then linked to views of time as superfluous, buffering, or a resource for action. Responses were tabulated by complexity and personal beliefs; neutral and favorable beliefs were combined into the fit category for these tabulations. These data concern anticipated responses, rather than observed implementation.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported Hypotheses 1a and 1b: low parental and employee support each increased intended compliance delays (reported F(1,82) = 33.34 and 29.01, respectively, both p < .01). Table 3 reports mean delays of 1.62 years with both groups supportive, 3.07 with only parents supportive, 3.14 with only employees supportive, and 3.92 with neither supportive. Hypothesis 2 was supported in its additive form: the paper reports shorter delays under low than moderate complexity and under moderate than high complexity; the two moderate conditions did not differ (p = .995). Its interaction form was not supported (F(1,82) = 2.06, p > .05; Table 4 interaction b = −.509, p = .155). Personal-belief misfit was not conventionally significant for timing (Table 4: b = .432, p = .064).

Study 2 identified three pathways: early adoption when time seemed superfluous, delay while waiting for uncertainty to resolve, and active use of time to reduce complexity. Active responses included neutralizing opposition and partial compliance, fighting the practice and stimulating opposition, and, as a last resort, exit; accommodation and resistance could be combined (Table 5). Table 6 shows average active responses rising from .68 in low complexity to 1.76 in high complexity. Personal beliefs differentiated response patterns: resistant responses constituted 50% of responses in the misfit category versus 17% in the fit category, while compliant plus accommodative responses constituted 35% versus 72%. These are proportions of coded responses, not percentages of managers. The qualitative findings suggest that interpretations of complexity and personal beliefs shape planned pathways, with delay providing either a buffer against uncertainty or time for action; they do not demonstrate that those plans resolved complexity in practice.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional complexity research by showing that coercive pressure does not automatically produce fast compliance when other constituents contest the practice. It adds microfoundations to institutional theory by explaining how decision makers interpret conflicting pressures, use time strategically, and engage in sensemaking and institutional work before compliance. It also extends work on institutionalization pace by showing that force-driven adoption can be slowed by influence-based efforts to construct a clearer path to legitimacy.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that regulators and powerful constituents should not assume that coercive mandates will be implemented quickly when target organizations face opposition from other salient constituents. The authors recommend that those seeking enduring institutional change provide opportunities for field members to jointly make sense of the proposed change, experiment with it, and develop shared understandings. The paper also suggests that delays may be active attempts to preserve legitimacy rather than mere resistance or passivity.

**Limitations**
The study uses hypothetical vignettes and anticipated responses rather than observed organizational implementation behavior. Its empirical setting is the Dutch childcare sector, where coercive regulation, parents, employees, and managers' pedagogical beliefs create a specific form of institutional complexity. The authors caution that generalizability is strongest for similar contexts where actors cannot ignore coercive pressure but also depend on important constituents who may oppose compliance.

**Future Research**
Future research should identify contingencies affecting responses to institutional pressures under complexity, including when actors can safely ignore pressures, when fields sustain differentiated meanings, and when complexity resolves into shared understandings. Further work could examine how personal beliefs condition flexibility or inflexibility in responses to contested practices. The paper also argues that experimental approaches can help investigate the microfoundations of institutional theory and institutionalization processes.
