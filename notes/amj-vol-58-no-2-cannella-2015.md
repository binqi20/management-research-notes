# Family- versus Lone-Founder-Controlled Public Corporations: Social Identity Theory and Boards of Directors

**Abstract**
We distinguish between “family companies,” involving multiple family members as owners and/or managers, and “lone-founder companies,” involving only the founder and no other family members. We apply social identity theory and organizational identification to elucidate the differences between these two types of firms, and how their differing organizational identities reflect unique desires for control and influence. We then consider how these differences are reflected in a firm’s board structure (i.e., directorship interlocks, director experiences, and director tenures). Specifically, we predict that family firms are more likely to interlock with other family firms, select directors with more experience in family firms, and keep directors on their boards longer. Correspondingly, we posit that family firms are less likely to interlock with lone-founder firms or to select directors with experience in lone-founder-controlled firms. Lone-founder firms follow a similar pattern. We also consider the consequences of family and lone-founder ownership and board structures on the investment behavior of three classes of institutional investors. We test our hypotheses with a sample of publicly traded corporations between 1991 and 2006, and report strong support for most of our predictions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how family-controlled and lone-founder-controlled public corporations differ in organizational identity and how those identities shape board structure, outside director experience, director tenure, and institutional investor ownership. It also asks whether treating family and lone-founder firms as a single category obscures meaningful governance differences.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: After controlling for board size, family, lone-founder, and regular public companies each have more interlocks to firms of the same type and fewer interlocks to firms of different types.
- H2: After controlling for board size, each of these three firm types has more outside director prior experience with the same type and less with different types.
- H3: Outside directors of family and lone-founder firms are less likely to end their service, and therefore serve longer tenures, than outside directors of regular public firms.
- H4: Outside directors' family-firm experience is negatively associated with exit from family-firm boards; lone-founder-firm experience is negatively associated with exit from lone-founder-firm boards.
- H5: The extent of outside directors' family-firm experience is negatively associated with the proportion of shares held by dedicated institutional investors; lone-founder-firm experience is positively associated with that proportion.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm control type, director prior family-firm experience, director prior lone-founder-firm experience, director prior regular-public-firm experience, and outside-director board experience profiles.
- DV(s): Interlocks to family, lone-founder, and regular public firms; outside director prior experience by firm type; outside director exit; and dedicated, transient, and quasi-indexed institutional ownership.
- Mediators: Not modeled as mediators.
- Moderators: Matching between firm identity type and outside director prior experience is modeled for director exit.

The mechanism is that family and lone-founder firms have distinct organizational identities even though both controlling owners seek to preserve control. Family firms' familial identity motivates ties to other family firms and directors with family-firm experience, while lone-founder firms' individualistic and entrepreneurial identity motivates ties to other lone-founder firms and directors with lone-founder experience. These identity-consistent board structures reduce perceived threats to owner control, lengthen matched directors' tenure, and signal different governance and shareholder orientations to dedicated institutional investors.

**Data & Measures**
The observational sample comprises 742 publicly traded firms (145 family, 70 lone-founder, and 527 regular public firms), retained if they appeared among the 1,000 largest firms by net revenue on Compustat in at least 10 years during 1991–2005. Board and officer data were gathered for 1989–2005 from proxy statements and annual filings, initially through Compact Disclosure. The paper reports 10,533 firm-year observations and 6,090 observations with institutional ownership information; Table 6 reports 6,077 observations for its ownership regressions. Firm-year panels test H1, H2, and H5; individual outside-director-year event histories test H3 and H4.

Family status requires at least 5% beneficial voting ownership held by family members, with founder and familial relationships established from company histories and filings. Lone-founder status requires at least 5% voting ownership by the founder and no other relative as a significant owner, officer, or director. Interlocks count received, neutral, and sent director/officer connections to other sample firms, classified by firm type. Experience counts years as an executive or director at other sample firms of each type, excluding the focal firm; it is time-varying, summed across outside directors for firm-year analyses, and transformed as log(1 + experience). Director exit is a binary termination-of-service indicator, with tenure as the event-history time variable and annual updating of covariates; H4 uses firm-type × corresponding experience interactions. Institutional ownership uses fourth-quarter data provided by Brian Bushee, excludes institutions holding less than 1%, and sums outstanding-share percentages separately for dedicated, transient, and quasi-indexed investors.

The paper describes generalized least squares probit panel regressions for interlocks and experience, generalized least squares regressions for institutional ownership, and random effects with robust standard errors. Exit is analyzed with a Cox proportional hazard model. Controls include firm size, age and performance, sales growth, board characteristics, and industry; panel analyses also include year controls. These are associational tests; organizational identity and owner control are not directly measured.

**Key Findings**
- H1 receives strong support in the authors' assessment (Table 3): relative to regular public firms, family firms have more family interlocks (b = .648) and fewer lone-founder and regular-public interlocks (b = −.652 and −.813); lone-founder firms have more lone-founder interlocks (b = .791) and fewer regular-public interlocks (b = −.683), all p < .001. Lone-founder firms have fewer family interlocks than family firms (b = −.714, p < .01), but do not differ significantly from regular public firms in family interlocks (b = −.067). Thus, not every different-type contrast is significant.
- H2 receives strong support in the authors' assessment (Table 4): relative to regular public firms, family firms have more family experience (b = 1.297) and less lone-founder and regular-public experience (b = −.710 and −1.403), all p < .001. Lone-founder firms have more lone-founder experience (b = 2.162, p < .001) and less regular-public experience (b = −.879, p < .01), but no significant difference in family experience (b = −.097). Relative to family firms, lone-founder firms have less family experience (b = −1.394) and more lone-founder experience (b = 2.872), both p < .001.
- H3 is supported: outside directors have lower exit in family and lone-founder firms than in regular public firms (Table 5, Model 1: exponentiated coefficients .607 and .847, respectively, both p < .001; the paper labels these odds ratios). H4 is supported by negative matching-experience interactions (Table 5, Model 3: family × family experience = .707; lone-founder × lone-founder experience = .519; both p < .001).
- H5 is supported: dedicated ownership is negatively associated with outside directors' family-firm experience (b = −.180, p < .05) and positively associated with lone-founder-firm experience (b = .226, p < .05; Table 6, Model 3). Additional, non-hypothesized firm-type × matching-experience interactions for dedicated ownership are nonsignificant. Lower institutional ownership in family firms is reported descriptively, with an explicit caution that ownership-based firm classification prevents a rigorous test of that comparison.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to corporate governance research by separating family-controlled firms from lone-founder-controlled firms rather than treating both as a single family-firm category. It extends social identity theory and organizational identification into board research by showing how owner and director identities shape interlocks, director selection, and director exit. It also links director identity to institutional investor behavior, arguing that outside directors' family- or lone-founder-firm experience signals a firm's openness to outside investor influence and shareholder-value priorities.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that firms may select directors for their experience in firms with similar identities, whose contextual knowledge and values can support controlling owners' priorities. It also proposes that director experience signals a firm's governance and shareholder orientation and resistance to outside influence to institutional investors. The analysis distinguishes family and lone-founder firms because their identities can shape board selection and investor reactions differently.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the sample is limited to publicly traded companies, so the findings are not generalizable to private firms. They also note that identifying family and lone-founder ownership is difficult and may not capture every qualifying firm, especially family firms with informal family involvement or late-stage generational complexity. The study only observes directors' experience within the sample firms and does not directly measure organizational identity, relying instead on theoretically grounded ownership and experience classifications.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine other governance mechanisms through which family and lone-founder firms preserve control, including leadership structure, CEO duality, and the types of directors they recruit. The paper also calls for research on generational transitions in family firms and how board configurations differ across first-, second-, and third-generation family control. Additional work could directly measure organizational identity and examine whether different types of director experience influence willingness to engage in traditional board functions, including whether executives perceive directors with family-firm experience as less likely to monitor and more supportive.
