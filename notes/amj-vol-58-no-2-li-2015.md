# A Confucian Social Model of Political Appointments among Chinese Private-Firm Entrepreneurs

**Abstract**
In China, many successful private-firm entrepreneurs hold representational appointments in political councils such as the People’s Congress (PC) or People’s Political Consultative Conference (PPCC). By considering Western theories of life career development and Confucian doctrines of personal development, we seek to understand the complex motivations of successful entrepreneurs for joining political councils. We propose a Confucian social model of role transition to explain the pursuit and attainment of political appointment in China. We hypothesize that the “proself” and “prosocial” motives for seeking political connections will, respectively, attenuate or strengthen the relationship between private-firm entrepreneurs’ business success and their intention and attainment in seeking political appointment. We conducted a cross-sectional survey of 166 private-firm chief executive officers (CEOs) and chairs, and a longitudinal archival study of 1,323 Chinese publicly listed private firms from 2006 to 2012. The findings support our hypotheses on the moderating effect of the proself and prosocial motives. The study offers insights into how Confucian cultural values may explain the dynamic between business success and political appointments among private-firm entrepreneurs in China.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why successful Chinese private-firm entrepreneurs increasingly pursue PC or PPCC political appointments despite declining economic returns to political connections. It examines whether proself and prosocial motives condition the relationship between business success and entrepreneurs' desire for, and attainment of, political appointment.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Proself motive negatively moderates the relationship between firm success and political appointment, desired or attained; the relationship is more negative for entrepreneurs with a stronger proself motive.
- H2: Prosocial motive positively moderates the relationship between firm success and political appointment, desired or attained; the relationship is positive for entrepreneurs with a stronger prosocial motive and negative for those with a weaker prosocial motive.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Entrepreneurial or firm success.
- DV(s): Desire to seek political appointment in Study 1; attained PC or PPCC political appointment in Study 2.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Proself motive and prosocial motive.

The proposed Confucian social model argues that business success gives entrepreneurs discretion to act on salient motives. When proself motives dominate, successful entrepreneurs are less likely to pursue costly political appointments because marginal economic benefits are limited. When prosocial motives dominate, successful entrepreneurs are more likely to pursue appointments as legitimate channels for influencing policy, addressing social problems, and serving the broader society.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 surveyed 166 private-firm owner-CEOs and chairs in networking clubs and executive courses in China, with two questionnaires two months apart. Motives and demographics were collected first and appointment desires second. Desire to seek political appointment averaged two five-point items on becoming a PC or PPCC member (α = .91). Firm success averaged standardized firm age, employee count, and total assets; the latter two used ten-point categories. Proself and prosocial motives each used six five-point items, developed through interviews and separate exploratory and confirmatory samples (n = 188 and 288). Indirect questions asked about the importance of motives based on personal experience or judgments of other entrepreneurs; proself items concerned economic benefits, while prosocial items concerned policy influence and social contribution (hypothesis-testing α = .92 and .82, respectively; Table 2). OLS regressions controlled demographics, former government service, psychological insecurity, and perceived appointment costs. Table 4 reports n = 155 for Models 1–2 and 154 for Models 3–4.

Study 2 assembled an unbalanced archival panel of 1,323 publicly listed private companies in China during 2006–2012, yielding 4,578 firm-year/chair-year observations. Sources included the Wind database, company websites and annual reports, the Hexun Information Network, and official PC and PPCC websites; CSR disclosure came from the Runling database. Attained appointment was coded 1 if the chair held PC or PPCC membership in the observation year and 0 otherwise. Firm success averaged standardized return on assets (net income/total assets), net assets per share, and three-year sales growth (current sales/sales three years earlier). Standardized annual cash dividend payout ratio proxied proself motive, with higher payouts interpreted as stronger proself motive in this ownership context. Issuing a CSR report in a given year proxied stronger prosocial motive (1 versus 0). Binary logistic regressions used generalized estimating equations with lagged predictors and moderators, year indicators, and individual, firm, industry, and institutional controls. These observational designs estimate associations; the one-year lag in Study 2 was intended to reduce simultaneity and reverse-causality concerns, not establish causality.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported H1: the firm success × proself interaction was negative (Table 4, Model 4: coefficient = −.47, p < .01). Success was negatively related to appointment desire at high proself motive (simple slope = −.82, p < .001), but not at low proself motive (.12, nonsignificant). H2 received partial support: the success × prosocial interaction was positive (.41, p < .05), but the slope was negative at low prosocial motive (−.76, p < .001) and nonsignificant at high prosocial motive (.06), rather than significantly positive as predicted. Model 4 explained 36% of the variance.

Study 2 supported the predicted negative proself interaction (Table 6, Model 6: −.20, p < .001) and positive prosocial interaction (.44, p < .05), using one-tailed tests for hypothesized effects. At high proself motive, success was negatively associated with attained appointment (simple slope = −.15, p < .05); at low proself motive, the slope was positive (.26, p < .01). At high prosocial motive, the slope was positive (.50, p < .001); at low prosocial motive, it was nonsignificant (.06). The authors describe H1 and H2 as fully supported in Study 2, although the low-prosocial slope did not show H2's predicted negative relationship. Alternative total-dividend and largest-shareholder-control analyses retained both predicted interaction directions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends political-connection research beyond the dominant economic lens by showing that personal motives, especially prosocial motives rooted in Confucian role-transition doctrine, help explain political appointment-seeking. It also links Western life-career development theories with an Eastern Confucian account of movement from self-orientation to social orientation, offering a cross-cultural model of how entrepreneurs define life goals after business success.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that political appointment among successful Chinese private-firm entrepreneurs should not be interpreted only as self-interested rent seeking. The authors argue for opening more channels for successful entrepreneurs' political participation and welcome business leaders who embrace contribution to society.

**Limitations**
The authors note that political appointment is only one possible channel for expressing prosocial motives, and its meaning may vary by cultural and institutional context. Study 1 may suffer from sample-selection bias, with women underrepresented and possible generational differences among entrepreneurs. The authors also caution that CSR disclosure may become institutionalized over time and may then be a weaker proxy for prosocial motive.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine other channels through which entrepreneurs pursue prosocial life goals, including social entrepreneurship and other social outcomes. It should test the proself and prosocial motive scales in other cultural contexts, compare them with related psychological measures, and develop better objective indicators of prosocial motive. Further work should also compare political appointments with other forms of political connection and investigate inauthentic prosocial motives.
