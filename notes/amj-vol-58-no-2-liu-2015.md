# The Dark Side of Socialization: A Longitudinal Investigation of Newcomer Alcohol Use

**Abstract**
Organizational veterans and external stakeholders such as clients often play an important and informal role in newcomer socialization, influencing newcomer cognition and behavior and providing learning opportunities and social support that facilitate employee adjustment and performance enhancement. However, from a sensemaking perspective, newcomers may also draw insight from stakeholder behavior in order to better understand how to best meet job-related objectives and expectations. These understandings may manifest as performance-related motives, leading to the adoption of risky behaviors that can be detrimental to newcomer health and organizational effectiveness. Using multisource, multilevel, and longitudinal data, we demonstrate that the alcohol use norms of both organizational veterans and clients are significantly associated with the performance drinking motives of newcomers in sales and client service teams, suggesting that veteran and client norms signal to newcomers that drinking alcohol is an effective and legitimate means to improve job performance. In addition, we demonstrate that performance drinking motives mediate the positive relationship between veteran and client alcohol use norms and newcomers’ frequency of work-based heavy drinking. Finally, we find that the frequency of work-based heavy drinking is positively related to newcomer alcohol misuse and mediates the positive relationship between performance drinking motives and newcomer alcohol misuse.

**Research Question**
How do veteran peers' and clients' alcohol use norms shape newcomers' performance drinking motives, work-based heavy drinking, and later alcohol misuse? The paper asks whether informal socialization can produce a "dark side" in which newcomers infer that risky behaviors are legitimate and effective ways to meet job-related objectives.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Veteran peers’ alcohol use norms are positively related to newcomers’ performance drinking motives.
- H2: Clients’ alcohol use norms are positively related to newcomers’ performance drinking motives.
- H3: Performance drinking motives mediate the positive relationship between veteran peers’ alcohol use norms and newcomers’ work-based heavy drinking.
- H4: Performance drinking motives mediate the positive relationship between clients’ alcohol use norms and newcomers’ work-based heavy drinking.
- H5: Newcomers’ work-based heavy drinking is positively related to the emergence of a more general pattern of alcohol misuse over time.
- H6: Performance drinking motives have a significant indirect effect on the emergence of a more general pattern of alcohol misuse via more frequent work-based heavy drinking.
- H7: Veteran peers’ alcohol use norms have a significant indirect effect on the emergence of a more general pattern of alcohol misuse via performance drinking motives and more frequent work-based heavy drinking.
- H8: Clients’ alcohol use norms have a significant indirect effect on the emergence of a more general pattern of alcohol misuse via performance drinking motives and more frequent work-based heavy drinking.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Veteran peers' alcohol use norms and clients' alcohol use norms.
- DV(s): Newcomers' frequency of work-based heavy drinking and alcohol misuse.
- Mediators: Performance drinking motives mediate the relationships between alcohol use norms and work-based heavy drinking; work-based heavy drinking mediates the relationship between performance drinking motives and alcohol misuse.
- Moderators: Not modeled as moderators.

The mechanism is a cross-level sensemaking process. Newcomers face uncertainty about how to achieve performance goals, observe veteran peers' and clients' descriptive alcohol-use norms, and form accounts that drinking is an effective and legitimate way to improve job performance. These accounts appear as performance drinking motives, which are associated with more frequent heavy drinking with clients; work-based heavy drinking is then associated with subsequent alcohol misuse. The evidence supports the indirect paths from veteran and client norms to work-based heavy drinking through performance motives, but it does not support the full hypothesized indirect paths from norms to alcohol misuse.

**Data & Measures**
The main study used three-wave, multisource survey data from 147 newcomers, 183 veteran teammates, and 57 supervisors in 57 sales and client service teams in two manufacturing companies headquartered in southern China. Newcomers had less than six months of employment at Wave 1; veterans had at least 12 months. Waves 2 and 3 followed three and six months later. Assessments were conducted in Chinese using translation and back-translation. A separate measure-development sample included 82 sales and client service employees and 20 supervisors; exploratory factor analysis supported three factors for the newly developed norms and performance-motive measures.

At Wave 1, veteran teammates rated five items on drinking with clients, aggregated to team-level veteran alcohol use norms; supervisors rated four items on client drinking norms. Both used five-point agreement scales. Newcomers rated five performance drinking motive items at Wave 2, indicating how often they drank for job-related reasons, from never to always. Work-based heavy drinking at Waves 1 and 2 was a single self-report item measuring past-month frequency of consuming five or more drinks during client engagements, from never to almost every day. Alcohol misuse at Waves 1 and 3 was the 10-item World Health Organization AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorder Identification Test), scored 0–40; the main outcome was the total score, with a supplementary dichotomous outcome for scores of 8 or higher. Controls included demographics, tenure, team size, baseline drinking and misuse, social and coping drinking motives, and work-based modal drinking (consumption frequency multiplied by quantity). Company membership and social desirability were excluded from further model testing after nonsignificant associations with outcomes. The associational analysis used multilevel modeling in Mplus 6.0, multilevel confirmatory factor analysis, a logistic link for hazardous and harmful use, and Monte Carlo confidence intervals for indirect effects; same-wave measurement of motives and heavy drinking limits causal ordering.

**Key Findings**
Table 2, Model 2 reports unstandardized coefficients: veteran norms positively predicted performance drinking motives (γ = .33, p < .05), as did client norms (γ = .25, p < .01), supporting H1 and H2. Performance drinking motives were positively associated with work-based heavy drinking at the individual level (γ = .47, p < .01) and team level (γ = .70, p < .01). The results text reports positive indirect effects of veteran norms (.23, 95% CI [.04, .47]) and client norms (.17, 95% CI [.05, .33]) on heavy drinking through performance motives (both p < .05), supporting H3 and H4. Individual-level heavy drinking positively predicted subsequent alcohol misuse (γ = 1.01, p < .05; Table 2), supporting H5; the supplementary logistic analysis gave an odds ratio of 2.70 (p < .01) for hazardous and harmful use. The individual-level indirect effect of performance motives on misuse through heavy drinking was .47 (95% CI [.02, 1.10], p < .05), supporting H6. H7 and H8 were not supported: the indirect effects of veteran and client norms on misuse were −.82 and −.61, respectively (both p > .05). Veteran norms nevertheless had a positive direct association with misuse (γ = 1.63, p < .05; Table 2), whereas client norms did not.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends newcomer socialization research by showing that informal socialization can generate dysfunctional outcomes, not only adjustment and performance gains. It contributes to sensemaking theory by treating motives as an operational manifestation of newcomers' accounts and by showing that both internal stakeholders and external clients provide inputs to those accounts. It also adds to workplace alcohol research by theorizing how permissive drinking cultures emerge through norm-based sensemaking rather than only documenting their effects.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should attend to informal socialization processes, especially in client-facing roles where newcomers may infer that risky practices are required for performance. The findings imply that laissez-faire reliance on peer and client norms can impose long-run health and organizational costs even when the behavior seems performance-enhancing in the short run. Organizations may need to clarify acceptable means of relationship building and guide newcomers away from risky client-service routines.

**Limitations**
The paper notes that its measures of workplace alcohol-use norms may not capture all relevant sources or forms of normative influence, including injunctive norms and norms from other newcomers or competitors. Work-based heavy drinking is measured with a single self-report item, and performance drinking motives and work-based heavy drinking are measured at the same wave, so the causal ordering between motives and drinking cannot be fully ruled out. The sample is limited to sales and client service employees in China, where business drinking is culturally salient, so generalizability to other occupations and national contexts requires replication.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine other sources and forms of workplace drinking norms, including injunctive norms and normative cues from newcomers, competitors, and other socialization agents. The authors call for experience-sampling designs, cross-lagged panel designs, and experimental designs to better capture drinking behavior over time and clarify reciprocal relationships between motives and behavior. They also encourage research on boundary conditions such as job demands, alcohol tolerance, age, and first-job status. Separately, they propose studying newcomer turnover and who leaves and why to understand socialization's role in cultural reproduction, and replicating the findings in other cultural settings.
