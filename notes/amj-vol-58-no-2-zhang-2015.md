# Paradoxical Leader Behaviors in People Management: Antecedents and Consequences

**Abstract**
As organizational environments become increasingly dynamic, complex, and competitive, leaders are likely to face intensified contradictory, or seemingly paradoxical, demands. We develop the construct of “paradoxical leader behavior” in people management, which refers to seemingly competing, yet interrelated, behaviors to meet structural and follower demands simultaneously and over time. In Study 1, we develop a measure of paradoxical leader behavior in people management using five samples from China. Confirmatory factor analyses support a multidimensional measure of paradoxical leader behavior with five dimensions: (1) combining self-centeredness with other-centeredness; (2) maintaining both distance and closeness; (3) treating subordinates uniformly, while allowing individualization; (4) enforcing work requirements, while allowing flexibility; and (5) maintaining decision control, while allowing autonomy. In Study 2, we examine the antecedents and consequences of paradoxical leader behavior in people management with a field sample of 76 supervisors and 516 subordinates from 6 firms. We find that the extent to which supervisors engage in holistic thinking and have integrative complexity is positively related to their paradoxical behavior in managing people, which, in turn, is associated with increased proficiency, adaptivity, and proactivity among subordinates.

**Research Question**
How can paradoxical leader behavior in people management be conceptualized and measured, and what cognitive and contextual antecedents explain its occurrence? The paper also asks whether paradoxical leader behavior is associated with subordinate proficiency, adaptivity, and proactivity.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The extent to which a leader engages in holistic thinking will be positively associated with the leader’s paradoxical behavior in managing people.
- H2: The extent to which a leader displays integrative complexity will be positively associated with the leader’s paradoxical behavior in managing people.
- H3: Paradoxical behavior in managing people will be more prevalent under a more organic organizational structure and less prevalent under a more mechanistic structure.
- H4: Paradoxical leader behavior in managing people will be positively associated with followers’ (a) task proficiency, (b) adaptive behavior, and (c) proactive behavior.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Holistic thinking, integrative complexity, organic organizational structure, and paradoxical leader behavior in people management.
- DV(s): Paradoxical leader behavior in the antecedent model; subordinate task proficiency, adaptive behavior, and proactive behavior in the consequence model.
- Mediators: Paradoxical leader behavior is the focal linking construct between leader cognition and subordinate work-role performance, but the paper does not estimate a formal indirect-effects mediation model.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The mechanism is that leaders with a both-and cognitive basis can accept, connect, and integrate contradictory structural and follower demands over time. Paradoxical leader behavior then provides both bounded and discretionary work environments: followers understand work roles and standards while retaining autonomy, flexibility, dignity, and individualized consideration. This combination is theorized to model openness to contradictions and support proficiency, adaptation, and proactive change behavior. The field evidence supports holistic thinking and integrative complexity as antecedents, rejects organic structure as a significant antecedent in the controlled model, and links paradoxical leader behavior to all three subordinate outcomes.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 developed and validated a 22-item, five-dimensional measure of paradoxical leader behavior (PLB). Item generation used theory, existing leadership measures, and 86 incidents from interviews with 28 supervisors and subordinates; double-barreled items assessed how frequently supervisors combined opposing behaviors (0 = not at all; 4 = a lot). Table 1 reports Samples 1 and 2 for exploratory factor analysis (n = 228 and 204), Sample 3 for confirmatory factor analysis and item-design tests (n = 193; subsamples 87 and 106), Sample 4 for reliability and discriminant/convergent validation (186, 172, and 116 at three measurement occasions), and Sample 5 for field validation (478). Samples 1–4 comprised full-time employees attending a continuing education program at a northern Chinese university; Sample 5 came from five manufacturing and service firms in China. Sample 3 measured leader identification with six items and job engagement with nine, comparing PLB with reversed-order, additive, and multiplicative alternatives. Sample 4 compared PLB with 36-item and 16-item behavioral-complexity measures and five-item managerial flexibility; its procedure reports a four-week PLB retest. Sample 5 measured subordinate-rated leadership first and, two weeks later, self-rated affective commitment, leave intentions, and leader effectiveness (six, four, and four items), plus supervisor-rated in-role task performance and task-oriented organizational citizenship behavior (11 and five items). Five alternative leadership measures supplied discriminant and incremental-validity comparisons.

Study 2 was an associational, time-lagged field survey in six privately owned technical or engineering services companies in China, with 76 supervisors and 516 subordinates in the reported analyses. At Time 1, subordinates rated leadership, while supervisors supplied holistic thinking, integrative complexity, and organizational structure; supervisors rated subordinate work-role behavior three weeks later. PLB used the 22-item scale, averaged across five dimensions and aggregated to the supervisory level. Holistic thinking used six self-report items assessing locus of attention. Integrative complexity used both independently coded responses to two open-ended questions (five-point differentiation/integration assessment, aggregated across questions) and an 11-item self-report measure (five differentiation and six integration items, averaged across dimensions). Organizational structure used seven supervisor-rated items, with higher values indicating a more organic structure, aggregated to the firm level. Proficiency, adaptivity, and proactivity each used a three-item supervisor-rated scale. Controls included relevant supervisor/subordinate age, gender, tenure, power distance, and relational orientation. Two-level hierarchical linear modeling tested antecedents of PLB, and three-level models tested subordinate outcomes; the design establishes associations rather than causal identification.

**Key Findings**
Study 1: Item reduction yielded five factors explaining 65.6% of variance (Table 2). The second-order PLB model fit in Sample 3 (CFI = .97, RMSEA = .07) and Sample 5 (CFI = .99, RMSEA = .06), with no significant fit difference from the respective first-order five-factor models (Table 3). Sample 3 showed no significant order effect and incremental prediction of leader identification and job engagement beyond additive and multiplicative indices. Sample 4 reported test–retest r = .69 and discriminant/convergent validity against behavioral complexity and managerial flexibility (Table 4 and accompanying text). Sample 5 PLB correlated positively with commitment (r = .37), task performance (.20), task citizenship behavior (.20), and leader effectiveness (.28), and negatively with leave intention (−.30), all p < .01 (Table 5). Incremental prediction beyond alternative leadership measures was significant except for task citizenship behavior beyond paternalistic leadership, where the change in multiple R was .02, p < .10 (Table 6).

Study 2: H1 and H2 were supported: holistic thinking and coded integrative complexity positively predicted PLB (unstandardized γ = .19, p < .01; γ = .15, p < .05; Table 8, Step 2); the item-based complexity measure yielded a similar pattern. H3 was not supported in the controlled model (organic structure γ = .12, p = .12), although the model without controls showed a positive association (γ = .13, p = .039; Table 8 note). H4a–H4c were supported: supervisory PLB positively predicted subordinate proficiency (γ = .35, p < .05), adaptive behavior (γ = .43, p < .01), and proactive behavior (γ = .40, p < .01), with corresponding pseudo R² values .09, .10, and .09 (Table 9, Step 2). These are measured associations; the proposed role-modeling and work-environment mechanisms were not directly tested.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends organizational paradox research from macro strategic tensions to supervisory people management, arguing that leaders face paradoxes in structural and follower demands. It contributes a five-dimensional measure of paradoxical leader behavior and shows construct and predictive validity beyond established leadership constructs. It also bridges Eastern yin-yang philosophy with Western paradox and leadership literatures to theorize both-and leader behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that leaders should be trained not only to choose situationally among behavioral alternatives, but also to handle competing demands through long-term paradoxical thinking and action. For international and complex organizational contexts, a paradox-based lens may help managers respond to uncertainty without reducing every tension to an either-or choice.

**Limitations**
The empirical setting is China, so the authors caution that findings may reflect Chinese-specific ways of integrating opposites and should be tested in Western and cross-cultural contexts. Study 2 uses a small firm-level sample and supervisor-rated organizational structure, which may explain the unsupported organizational-structure finding. The validation samples also include evening students from one university and professional, college-educated workers, and some Study 1 relationships may be vulnerable to common method effects.

**Future Research**
Future research should test paradoxical leader behavior in Western and cross-cultural contexts, examine whether different cultures display different paradoxical behaviors, and extend the construct beyond people management to stakeholder, globalization, and strategic leadership domains. Researchers could also examine downsides such as leader cognitive strain and subordinate discomfort when followers hold black-white views, and expand the nomological network to include open-mindedness, learning orientation, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, environmental uncertainty, work characteristics, and group-level outcomes.
