# Hot Buttons and Time Sinks: The Effects of Electronic Communication During Nonwork Time on Emotions and Work-Nonwork Conflict

**Abstract**
As advances in communication technologies have made organizations more easily connected to their workforce outside of normal work hours, there is increased concern that employees may experience heightened work-nonwork conflict when away from the office. The current study investigates the effects of electronic communication received during nonwork time using an experience sampling methodology to examine within-person relationships among elements of electronic communication (affective tone, time required), emotional responses (anger, happiness), and work-to-nonwork conflict in a sample of 341 working adults surveyed over a seven-day period. Hierarchical linear modeling results suggested that both affective tone and time required were associated with anger, but only affective tone was associated with happiness. Further, anger was associated with work-to-nonwork conflict and mediated the effects of affective tone and time required on work-to-nonwork conflict. Results also revealed cross-level moderating effects of abusive supervision and communication sender together, as well as segmentation preference. Implications of these findings for future theorizing and research on electronic communication during nonwork time are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do the affective tone and time required by work-related electronic communications received during nonwork time shape employees' anger, happiness, and work-to-nonwork conflict? The paper also asks whether abusive supervision, communication sender, and segmentation preference condition these within-person relationships.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Within individuals, more negative electronic communication affective tone is (a) positively related to anger and (b) negatively related to happiness.
- H2: Within individuals, electronic communication time required is (a) positively related to anger and (b) negatively related to happiness.
- H3: Within individuals, (a) more negative affective tone is positively related to work-to-nonwork conflict; this relationship is mediated by (b) anger and (c) happiness.
- H4: Within individuals, (a) time required is positively related to work-to-nonwork conflict; this relationship is mediated by (b) anger and (c) happiness.
- H5: The within-person indirect effects of (a) affective tone and (b) time required on work-to-nonwork conflict are stronger through anger than through happiness.
- H6: Abusive supervision and communication sender jointly moderate the within-person relationships of affective tone with (a) anger, (b) happiness, and (c) work-to-nonwork conflict. Relationships are strongest for supervisor-sent communications under higher abusive supervision and weakest for supervisor-sent communications under lower abusive supervision.
- H7: Segmentation preference strengthens the within-person relationships of time required with (a) anger, (b) happiness, and (c) work-to-nonwork conflict: more anger and conflict, and less happiness, at higher segmentation preference.
- H8a: The indirect effects of affective tone on work-to-nonwork conflict through anger and happiness are strongest for supervisor-sent communications under higher abusive supervision and weakest for supervisor-sent communications under lower abusive supervision.
- H8b: Segmentation preference strengthens the indirect effects of time required on work-to-nonwork conflict through anger and happiness.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Electronic communication affective tone and electronic communication time required during nonwork time.
- DV(s): Work-to-nonwork conflict.
- Mediators: Anger; happiness was modeled as an emotional response but did not mediate work-to-nonwork conflict in the reported results.
- Moderators: Abusive supervision, communication sender, and segmentation preference.

The paper treats electronic communications during nonwork time as affective work events that employees appraise as favorable or unfavorable. Negative affective tone and greater time demands are associated with anger, while affective tone is also associated with happiness; the reported indirect effects on work-to-nonwork conflict operate through anger, not happiness. Relational context matters because abusive supervision and supervisor-sent messages shape appraisals of affective tone, while segmentation preference makes time-demanding intrusions into nonwork time more conflictual.

**Data & Measures**
An initial survey and seven event-contingent daily surveys yielded 341 working adults and 1,572 daily reports of work-related electronic communication received during nonwork time. Tables 2–4 report 1,530 daily observations nested within those 341 participants; Table 1 reports 1,530–1,572 observations. Participants reported on their most recent after-hours work communication and provided no daily data when none was received. Daily affective tone was a single five-point item reverse-scored so higher values meant more negative tone; time required was a single report of hours and fractions of hours spent reading and complying with the message. Anger and happiness each used four emotion words rated from 1 to 5 (α = .90 and .89, respectively). Work-to-nonwork conflict used five items adapted to home and personal life generally, rated from 1 to 5 (α = .93). The initial survey assessed abusive supervision with nine frequency items (α = .89) and segmentation preference with four agreement items (α = .91). Daily sender was coded 1 for a supervisor and 0 for a coworker, subordinate, or client/customer.

Random coefficient modeling with HLM 6 centered daily predictors at individual means and person-level predictors at grand means, controlling age, gender, marital and parental status, work hours, and workplace segmentation expectations. Indirect effects were assessed with Sobel tests and distribution-of-products confidence intervals using RMediation. These are associational field data with concurrent daily measures, which cannot eliminate alternative causal orderings. A supplemental analysis used full text voluntarily supplied for 186 communications, rated for affective tone by five emotion scholars; their average rating operationalized objective tone for comparison with participants’ perceived tone.

**Key Findings**
Table 2 supported H1a–b: more negative tone was associated with more anger (b = .29, p < .01) and less happiness (b = −.45, p < .01). Time required predicted anger (b = .15, p < .01; H2a supported), but not happiness (b = .02, nonsignificant; H2b unsupported). Table 3 showed no significant total effect of tone on conflict (b = .05) and a positive total effect of time required (b = .34, p < .01), corresponding to no support for H3a and support for H4a. The accompanying prose instead gives tone b = .04 and reverses the H3a/H4a support labels; these are reporting discrepancies. Anger was positively associated with conflict (b = .15, p < .01), whereas happiness was not (b = .03). The reported indirect effects through anger supported H3b (.042, 95% CI [.017, .070]) and H4b (.022, 95% CI [.007, .043]); those through happiness were nonsignificant (−.012, CI [−.039, .015], and .000, CI [−.003, .005]), leaving H3c and H4c unsupported. Indirect effects through anger were stronger than through happiness, supporting H5a–b (z = 2.53 and 2.39, respectively, both p < .05).

Table 4 supported H6a: the tone × abusive supervision × sender interaction predicted anger (b = .24, p < .01), with the strongest tone–anger association for supervisor messages under high abuse and the weakest, nonsignificant association under low abuse. The corresponding interactions for happiness and conflict were nonsignificant (H6b–c unsupported). Segmentation preference strengthened the time-required–conflict relationship (b = .19, p < .01; H7c supported), but did not moderate anger or happiness (H7a–b unsupported). H8a was partially supported through anger: conditional indirect effects were .11 for supervisor messages under high abuse, .08 and .06 for non-supervisor messages under high and low abuse, respectively (all significant), and .04 for supervisor messages under low abuse (nonsignificant). Only that supported first-stage interaction proceeded to conditional indirect-effect testing; the proposed happiness pathway and H8b received no support. In the supplemental analysis, perceived tone carried significant indirect associations from expert-rated tone to anger (.155) and happiness (−.198), both p < .01; the authors caution about the small, voluntarily supplied message sample and possible reluctance to share very negative messages.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends work-nonwork and electronic communication research by shifting attention from overall communication volume to event-level properties of individual messages. It integrates affective events theory, appraisal theory, electronic communication emotion theory, positive-negative asymmetry, relational schemas, and boundary theory to explain why anger mediates the effects of affective tone and time required on work-to-nonwork conflict. It also shows that abusive supervision, sender status, and segmentation preference are boundary conditions for these processes.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should be careful about affective tone, timing, and effort demands when sending electronic communications after normal work hours. The findings suggest value in training employees to communicate clearly and constructively after hours, limiting unnecessary time demands, and recognizing that employees with stronger segmentation preferences may experience time-consuming after-hours messages as especially intrusive. The paper also implies that supervisor behavior is consequential because abusive supervisory relationships can intensify anger responses to negatively toned messages.

**Limitations**
The study used convenience and snowball sampling rather than random population sampling, so the authors caution against overgeneralizing to a defined population. The daily measures were collected concurrently, which prevents the design from fully eliminating alternative causal orderings such as anger shaping perceived tone. The focal within-person measures of affective tone and time required were single-item self-reports, leaving room for reliability limits, retrieval errors, and common method concerns.

**Future Research**
Future work should test the model in more controlled settings or a single organization and use time-separated measures of emotions and work-nonwork conflict across the end of the workday, evening communications, and the next morning. Research could examine additional message features such as salutations, closings, capitalization, response timing, emoticons, and time of day. The authors also call for research on other relational and receiver factors, including leader-member exchange, career ambition, empowerment, daily work-at-home expectations, and perceptual accuracy in affective tone interpretation.
