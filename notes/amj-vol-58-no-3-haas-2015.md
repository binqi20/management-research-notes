# Which Problems to Solve? Online Knowledge Sharing and Attention Allocation in Organizations

**Abstract**
Why do individuals allocate attention to specific problems in organizations? Viewing online knowledge sharing as a matching process between knowledge providers and problems, we examine attention allocation in the context of an online community within which knowledge providers respond to problems posted by other organization members. We argue that knowledge providers are more likely to allocate attention to solving problems that more closely match their expertise, but that decisions to allocate attention are also influenced by problem characteristics such as length, breadth, and novelty, as well as by problem crowding. Analyzing 1,251 realized matches and 12,510 nonrealized matches among knowledge providers and problems posted over a 32month period on an online discussion forum within a global engineering firm, we find evidence to support our claim that attention allocation is driven by the features of a particular provider–problem match, thereby shifting the discourse from knowledge provider–seeker relationships to knowledge provider–problem matches. The implications for theories of knowledge sharing, matching processes, and managerial attention are discussed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why organization members allocate attention to some online knowledge-sharing problems rather than others. It specifically shifts the explanation from provider-seeker relationships to provider-problem matches in an intraorganizational online discussion forum.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The likelihood that a provider allocates attention to a focal problem is positively related to the closeness of the provider–problem expertise match.
- H2a–c: Attention allocation is related to problem (a) length, (b) breadth, and (c) novelty in an inverse U shape.
- H3: Attention allocation is related to the number of concurrently posted problems in an inverse U shape.
- H4a–c: Expertise matching positively moderates the curvilinear relationships of attention allocation with problem (a) length, (b) breadth, and (c) novelty: closer matching steepens the positive slope and flattens the negative slope.
- H5: Expertise matching positively moderates the curvilinear relationship between attention allocation and concurrently posted problems: closer matching steepens the positive slope and flattens the negative slope.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Provider-problem expertise matching; problem length; problem breadth; problem novelty; problem crowding.
- DV(s): Whether a knowledge provider allocates attention to a focal problem by posting a response.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Provider-problem expertise matching moderates the relationships between attention allocation and problem length, breadth, novelty, and competing problems.

The mechanism is an attention-allocation process in which knowledge providers compare the expected benefits and costs of responding to specific problems. Closer expertise matching increases the expected value of responding and lowers cognitive costs, making providers more likely to respond. Problem length, breadth, and novelty attract attention up to a point but can become costly when cognitive load is high; problem crowding increases forum salience, with expertise matching amplifying providers' willingness to attend to the focal problem.

**Data & Measures**
The observational study combines structural engineering forum message logs from January 2003–August 2005 with the firm's expert yellow pages, personnel records, and project database. After missing-data exclusions, the source corpus includes 307 knowledge providers and 639 problems; choice-based sampling, excluding the first two months and pairing each realized dyad with 10 randomly selected nonrealized dyads, yields 13,761 provider–problem observations (1,251 realized; 12,510 nonrealized). Attention allocation equals 1 when the provider posts a response to the problem and 0 otherwise. Expertise matching is the normalized keyword similarity between a provider's self-declared expertise description and the focal problem, using 574 technical keywords from 3,948 employee descriptions and Salton cosine similarities; its range is 0–1. Problem length is word count. Breadth is Teachman’s entropy index of the distribution of problem keywords across 19 expertise domains derived through hierarchical clustering. Novelty is the inverse of the focal problem's average normalized keyword similarity to previously posted problems. Crowding counts problems posted in the preceding three working days. Rare-event logit models use weighted exogenous sampling maximum-likelihood estimation, provider-clustered standard errors, standardized focal continuous predictors, temporal controls, and provider–seeker, seeker, and provider controls. A Heckman two-stage selection correction uses a first-stage probit on 613 individuals and includes its inverse selection ratio in the outcome models. The design estimates associations; explanatory and control variables were constructed before the focal match where described to reduce reverse causality. Supplementary double random effects models examine logged response length as a preliminary measure of attention intensity, with corrections for provider participation and problems receiving responses.

**Key Findings**
Table 3 supports H1: closer expertise matching is positively associated with attention allocation (Model 2: b = .17, p < .01). H2a–c are supported in the individual tests: length (Model 3: linear b = .15, squared b = −.08, both p < .01), breadth (Model 4: b = .64 and −.31, both p < .01), and novelty (Model 5: b = .10 and −.03, both p < .05) have inverted U-shaped relationships, with turning points within the observed ranges. In the combined Model 6, the novelty linear term is marginal (p < .10). H3 receives mixed support: competing problems have a positive linear association (Model 7: b = .11, p < .01), but the squared term is positive and nonsignificant (b = .02), so the predicted downturn is absent.

Table 4's Model 18 shows positive linear interaction terms for expertise matching with length (b = .07, p < .10, marginal), breadth (b = .10, p < .05), novelty (b = .10, p < .01), and competing problems (b = .06, p < .05). Interactions with squared terms are nonsignificant in the models that include them. Inspection of predicted-probability differences gives partial support for H4a: closer matching steepens the positive length slope but does not flatten its negative slope. H4b–c receive full support because both predicted slope changes occur for breadth and novelty. H5 is supported with the explicit caveat that competing problems show no curvilinear main effect; closer matching amplifies their positive association throughout the observed range. Follow-up analyses find an inverted U for crowding only when using seven- or ten-day windows and restricting focal problems to those with at least 10 competing problems; this is supplementary evidence, not support from the main specification.

The preliminary response-length analysis finds positive associations for expertise matching (b = .07, p < .01), problem length (b = .14, p < .01), breadth (b = .10, p < .10), and novelty (b = .09, p < .01); only novelty has a negative squared term at the marginal level (b = −.03, p < .10). Crowding is nonsignificant, and expertise matching positively moderates only the length association (b = .06, p < .05). These supplementary results concern response length, not directly measured time, effort, or quality.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes by theorizing attention allocation as a matching process between providers and problems inside organizations. It extends online knowledge-sharing research beyond interpersonal provider-seeker ties by showing that expertise fit, problem characteristics, and problem crowding shape which problems receive attention. It also brings matching theory into an intraorganizational setting where the match is between an actor and an issue rather than between two actors.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that organizations using online discussion forums should not assume that making problems visible is enough for knowledge sharing. The study highlights provider-problem expertise fit, problem presentation, and competing problems as attention-allocation conditions that matter for managerial attention, while stopping short of a detailed forum-design prescription.

**Limitations**
The study is based on a single professional services organization, so the generalizability of the patterns to smaller firms or settings with stronger participation pressure is uncertain. The study measures attention allocation mainly as whether a provider posted a response; its supplementary response-length analysis is suggestive but cannot fully capture time, effort, or response quality.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine attention intensity with richer measures than response length and study the quality or helpfulness of responses, including peer-rated online contributions. Further work could also investigate why knowledge seekers post some problems to online forums, which individuals are more likely to post problems, and which problem types are more likely to be posted.
