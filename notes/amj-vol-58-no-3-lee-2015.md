# Discrimination in Selection Decisions: Integrating Stereotype Fit and Interdependence Theories

**Abstract**
We integrate stereotype fit and interdependence theories to propose a model that explains how and why decision makers discriminate in selection decisions. Our model suggests that decision makers draw on stereotypes about members of different social groups to infer the degree to which candidates possess the specific ability required for the task. Decision makers perceive candidates that have a greater ability required for the task as less (more) instrumental to their personal outcomes if they expect to compete (cooperate) with the candidate, and they discriminate in favor of candidates that are perceived as more instrumental to them. We tested our theory in the context of racial (Studies 1–3) and age (Study 4) discrimination in selection decisions with all-male samples and found evidence consistent with our predictions. By explaining when and why decision makers discriminate in favor of, but also against, members of their own social group, this research may help to explain the mixed support for the dominant view that decision makers exhibit favoritism toward candidates that belong to the same social group. In addition, our research demonstrates the importance of considering the largely overlooked role of interdependent relationships within the organization in order to understand discrimination in organizations.

**Research Question**
How do stereotype-based inferences about task fit and expected competition or cooperation with a candidate jointly shape discrimination in selection decisions? The paper asks when decision makers favor candidates from their own social group and when self-interested interdependence leads them to prefer candidates from a different social group.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: When decision makers expect to compete with a candidate, they discriminate against candidates stereotyped as having a greater ability required for the task.
- H2: When decision makers expect to cooperate with a candidate, they discriminate in favor of candidates stereotyped as having a greater ability required for the task.
- H3: Stereotypical perception of a candidate's greater ability required for the task leads to lower (higher) perceived instrumentality to the decision maker under expected competition (cooperation), in turn causing lower (higher) selection preference.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Candidate social group membership-task requirement stereotype fit; expected decision-maker-candidate interdependence as competition versus cooperation.
- DV(s): Selection preference or selection decision for candidates differing by race or age.
- Mediators: Perceived instrumentality of the candidate to the decision maker's personal outcomes.
- Moderators: Expected decision-maker-candidate interdependence (competition versus cooperation), which conditions the perceived-ability-instrumentality relationship. Task requirements and social-group contrasts operationalize stereotype fit.

Decision makers use stereotypes to infer whether a candidate has the ability required for the focal task. When the decision maker expects competition, a candidate stereotyped as more able becomes a more threatening future competitor and is perceived as less instrumental; when cooperation is expected, the same candidate becomes a more useful future collaborator and is perceived as more instrumental. Selection preference follows this perceived instrumentality, producing discrimination for or against the stereotypically stronger candidate depending on interdependence.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: A lab experiment recruited White males from a university subject pool. Participants were randomly assigned to competition versus cooperation and a stock-price forecasting task versus a debating task, operationalizing intellectual competence versus dominance requirements. Candidate race (Asian versus White) varied within participants through profile names and countries of origin; other profile differences were counterbalanced. Candidate intellectual competence was rated with intelligent, smart, knowledgeable, and skilled items; dominance with dominant, assertive, persuasive, and self-confident items. Instrumentality was a seven-point agreement item about whether choosing the candidate would make winning the first tournament round more likely. Selection preference was a seven-point likelihood-of-selection item.
- Study 2: A field experiment involved 78 responding male members of a business-school association at a university in South Korea, out of 98 invited members. Random assignment and task, interdependence, and candidate-race manipulations followed Study 1, delivered through an association email about a tournament. The dependent variable was the candidate chosen in the email reply. Stereotypical beliefs and instrumentality were not measured; this study tested H1 and H2 only.
- Study 3: An online recruitment simulation involved 122 males with work experience. Random assignment varied engineering versus sales department (intellectual competence versus dominance) and competitive versus cooperative departmental culture; Asian versus White candidate profiles varied within participants. Candidate ability ratings used the earlier items. Instrumentality was a seven-point agreement item about the candidate making the participant's career success within the department more likely; selection preference was a seven-point likelihood of recommending hiring the candidate.
- Study 4: An online experiment recruited 121 males using the Study 3 procedure. Random assignment varied corporate strategy versus product design department (stability versus creativity) and competition versus cooperation; candidate ages of 25 versus 50 varied within participants and were counterbalanced across profiles. Stability used stable, dependable, careful, cautious, and steady items; creativity used innovative, creative, adaptable, flexible, and curious items. Instrumentality and hiring preference used the Study 3 measures. Across Studies 1, 3, and 4, perceived ability and instrumentality were measured rather than separately manipulated; within-participant relative differences entered task-specific moderated mediation analyses with ordinary least squares regression and 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Study 2 used moderated logistic regression for binary selection.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported H1 and H2 (Figure 3): for intellectual-competence tasks, competition favored the White candidate (5.48 versus 3.41), whereas cooperation favored the Asian candidate (5.92 versus 4.29); for dominance tasks, competition favored the Asian candidate (5.92 versus 3.67), whereas cooperation favored the White candidate (5.87 versus 3.96). All four simple effects had p < .001. H3 was supported by conditional indirect-effect intervals: intellectual competence, competition [−0.62, −0.08] and cooperation [0.29, 1.62]; dominance, competition [−1.17, −0.33] and cooperation [0.23, 1.19]. The intellectual-competence-to-instrumentality path under competition was marginal (b = −0.35, p = .063).
- Study 2 supported H1 and H2 (Figure 4): with cooperation coded 0, competition 1, White selection 0, and Asian selection 1, the interdependence coefficient was negative for intellectual-competence tasks (b = −2.22, p = .003) and positive for dominance tasks (b = 3.47, p < .001). H3 was not tested.
- Study 3 supported H1 and H2 (Figure 5): competition favored the White candidate for intellectual competence (5.29 versus 3.79, p < .001) and the Asian candidate for dominance (5.45 versus 4.59, p = .012); cooperation reversed these preferences (Asian 6.45 versus White 5.41, p = .003; White 5.73 versus Asian 5.00, p = .030). The authors report H3 supported. Intellectual-competence indirect-effect intervals were [−1.65, −0.13] under competition and [0.26, 1.07] under cooperation; the dominance interval under competition was [−1.73, −0.38]. For dominance under cooperation, the text describes a positive instrumentality path (b = 1.09, p = .009) and higher relative selection preference, but prints a negative indirect-effect interval [−1.82, −0.54]; this reporting inconsistency remains unresolved.
- Study 4 supported H1 and H2 (Figure 6): for stability, competition favored the 25-year-old (5.97 versus 3.50) and cooperation the 50-year-old (6.12 versus 4.21); for creativity, competition favored the 50-year-old (5.89 versus 3.27) and cooperation the 25-year-old (6.30 versus 4.00). All four simple effects had p < .001. H3 was supported by conditional indirect-effect intervals: stability, competition [−0.85, −0.03] and cooperation [0.13, 0.92]; creativity, competition [−1.96, −0.09] and cooperation [0.18, 2.32].

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper qualifies the dominant in-group favoritism account of discrimination in selection decisions by specifying conditions under which decision makers discriminate against members of their own social group. It contributes by integrating stereotype fit theory with interdependence theory, showing that task requirements and expected intra-organizational relationships jointly determine whether stereotypes produce favorable or unfavorable treatment.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not treat discrimination risk as only a matter of generalized prejudice or similarity attraction. The paper suggests that selection systems should account for whether evaluators expect to compete or cooperate with the candidate, and that accountability practices may reduce self-interested reliance on stereotypes in hiring and assignment decisions.

**Limitations**
The studies used all-male decision makers and candidates, and Studies 1-3 focused on White-Asian racial comparisons, so generalization to other gender and race combinations requires caution. The experiments also focused on choices between equally qualified candidates; stereotype-based effects may be weaker when candidates clearly differ in qualifications or when decision-maker-candidate interdependence is weak.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the model with other gender and race combinations, and examine individual differences in self-interest, prejudice, and organizational orientation. The authors also call for large-scale passive observational studies of hiring committees and for research on how the strength of interdependence affects the model's explanatory power.
