# “You Wouldn’t Like Me When I’m Sleepy”: Leaders’ Sleep, Daily Abusive Supervision, and Work Unit Engagement

**Abstract**
We examine the daily sleep of leaders as an antecedent to daily abusive supervisory behavior and work unit engagement. Drawing from ego depletion theory, our theoretical extension includes a serial mediation model of nightly sleep quantity and quality as predictors of abusive supervision. We argue that poor nightly sleep influences leaders to enact daily abusive behaviors via ego depletion, and these abusive behaviors ultimately result in decreased daily subordinate unit work engagement. We test this model through an experience sampling study spread over 10 workdays with data from both supervisors and their subordinates. Our study supports the role of the indirect effects of sleep quality (but not of sleep quantity) via leader ego depletion and daily abusive supervisor behavior on daily subordinate unit work engagement.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether leaders' nightly sleep quantity and sleep quality explain day-to-day variation in abusive supervisory behavior. It further asks whether leader ego depletion and daily abusive supervisor behavior serially link poorer leader sleep quality to lower daily subordinate unit work engagement.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Daily leader sleep quantity is negatively related to daily leader ego depletion.
- H1b: Daily leader sleep quality is negatively related to daily leader ego depletion.
- H2: Daily leader ego depletion is positively related to daily abusive supervisor behavior.
- H3: Daily leader ego depletion mediates the effects of (a) daily leader sleep quantity and (b) daily leader sleep quality on daily abusive supervisor behavior.
- H4: Daily abusive supervision is negatively related to daily unit work engagement.
- H5a: Daily leader ego depletion and daily abusive supervisor behavior serially mediate the daily leader sleep quantity to daily unit work engagement relationship.
- H5b: Daily leader ego depletion and daily abusive supervisor behavior serially mediate the daily leader sleep quality to unit work engagement relationship.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily leader sleep quantity and daily leader sleep quality.
- DV(s): Daily abusive supervisor behavior and daily unit work engagement.
- Mediators: Daily leader ego depletion as the first-stage mediator; daily abusive supervisor behavior as the second-stage mediator for unit work engagement.
- Moderators: No primary moderator in the hypothesized model; supplementary analyses examined abusive supervision variability, abusive supervision trend, and sleep quantity by sleep quality interactions.

The mechanism is a self-regulation pathway grounded in ego depletion theory. Poor sleep quality is theorized to leave leaders with fewer self-regulatory resources, making them less able to suppress impulses toward hostile verbal or nonverbal behavior. Those daily abusive behaviors then reduce subordinates' willingness to invest physical, emotional, and cognitive energy in work. The results support this serial mediation for sleep quality but not for sleep quantity.

**Data & Measures**
The experience sampling study recruited supervisors and subordinates from domestic and multinational firms in Italy. Initially, 99 supervisors and 261 subordinates participated; after the stated survey and contact exclusions, matching, and aggregation, the paper reports 606 unit-days nested within 88 supervisors. Over two weeks comprising 10 workdays, supervisors reported sleep and ego depletion at the beginning of each workday, and subordinates reported abusive supervision and engagement at the end. This was an observational, associational design without manipulation or random assignment, analyzed using multilevel path analysis with daily ratings nested within leaders and leader trait anxiety controlled.

Sleep quantity was minutes asleep calculated from the Pittsburgh Sleep Diary's bedtime, sleep latency, waking time, and wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO); sleep quality was reverse-coded WASO. Daily leader ego depletion used a five-item, five-point scale (average α = .92). Subordinates rated five daily abusive behaviors on a six-point frequency scale from never to five or more occasions (average α = .78). Daily work engagement used three five-point items covering physical, emotional, and cognitive engagement (average α = .86), aggregated from individual responses to the work unit. Subordinate responses were included only on days with at least moderate supervisor contact; abusive supervision ratings were also aggregated to the leader-day. Leader trait anxiety was assessed using four adjectives rated for how well they described the leader on average (α = .74).

**Key Findings**
Within-supervisor variation accounted for 57% of abusive supervision variance, and within-work-unit variation accounted for 54% of engagement variance. Table 2's standardized estimates support H1b: sleep quality was negatively related to ego depletion (γ = −.13, p < .05), whereas sleep quantity was not significantly related to depletion (γ = .02, p > .05; H1a unsupported). Ego depletion was positively related to abusive supervision (γ = .35, p < .01; H2 supported). The indirect association of sleep quality with abusive supervision through depletion was negative (−.04, p < .05, 95% CI [−.084, −.003]; H3b supported); the corresponding sleep-quantity association was not significant (.01, 95% CI [−.036, .050]; H3a unsupported).

Abusive supervision was negatively related to unit engagement (γ = −.45, p < .01; H4 supported). The serial indirect association of sleep quality with engagement through depletion and abusive supervision was positive (.02, p < .05; H5b supported), with reported 95% CI [.000, .039] and 90% CI [.003, .036]; the authors state that the lower 95% bound is positive beyond three decimal places. The corresponding sleep-quantity indirect association was not significant (reported as −.00, 95% CI [−.022, .016]; H5a unsupported). These results support the proposed indirect pathway for sleep quality, not sleep quantity. Exploratory analyses found only marginal evidence at p < .10 for abusive-supervision variability as a moderator and for the sleep quantity–quality interaction predicting depletion; abusive-supervision trend effects and the sleep interaction predicting abusive supervision were nonsignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends abusive supervision theory by shifting attention from stable abusive supervisors to daily abusive supervisory behavior that varies within the same leader. It connects a nonwork antecedent, leader sleep quality, to leader behavior through ego depletion and then to subordinate unit engagement through a daily crossover process. The findings also qualify sleep-based self-regulation theory by showing stronger support for sleep quality than sleep quantity in this field setting.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should treat abusive supervision partly as a daily, preventable risk rather than only as a stable leader trait. Leadership training can help supervisors recognize poor sleep as a trigger for abusive behavior and delay sensitive interactions after especially poor sleep. The paper also suggests practical sleep-management strategies, including protecting off-hours from smartphones and work email, because better sleep quality may reduce next-day abusive behavior.

**Limitations**
The study does not manipulate sleep, ego depletion, or abusive supervision, so causal inference rests on theory, temporal separation, and the within-person design rather than random assignment. The authors also note that they did not include an exhaustive set of day-level controls such as workload, stress, health, or well-being. The study focuses on supervisor sleep and does not test subordinate sleep as either an antecedent or consequence.

**Future Research**
Future research should test day-level controls, subordinate sleep, and lagged crossover effects between supervisors and subordinates. Additional studies could examine individual-difference moderators such as trait self-control, agreeableness, and emotional stability. The authors also call for work on other outcomes, longer timeframes, chronic sleep deprivation, and the more detailed causal steps linking abusive supervision to work unit engagement.
