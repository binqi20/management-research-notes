# When Can Women Close the Gap? A Meta-Analytic Test of Sex Differences in Performance and Rewards

**Abstract**
Drawing on macro and micro domains in gender research, we meta-analytically test whether occupation-, industry-, and job-level factors mitigate or exacerbate differences in performance evaluations (k 5 93; n 5 95,882) and rewards (k 5 97; n 5 378,850) between men and women. Based on studies conducted across a variety of work settings and spanning nearly 30 years, we found that the sex differences in rewards (d 5 .56) (including salary, bonuses, and promotions) were 14 times larger than sex differences in performance evaluations (d 5 .04), and that differences in performance evaluations did not explain reward differences between men and women. The percentage of men in an occupation and the complexity of jobs performed by employees enhanced the male– female gap in performance and rewards. In highly prestigious occupations, women performed equally, but were rewarded significantly lower than men. Only a higher representation of female executives at the industry level enabled women to reverse the gender gap in rewards and performance evaluations. Our configurational analysis also revealed that some occupation-, industry-, and job-level attributes of the work context jointly contributed to differences in rewards and performance evaluations.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when women can close the gap in workplace performance evaluations and organizational rewards. It tests whether occupation-, industry-, and job-level attributes mitigate or exacerbate sex differences in performance evaluations, salary, bonuses, and promotions.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: A higher proportion of men in an occupational category enhances sex differences in performance evaluations such that men receive higher evaluations than women.
- H1b: A higher proportion of men in an occupational category enhances sex differences in organizational rewards such that men receive higher rewards than women.
- H2a: Occupational prestige enhances sex differences in performance evaluations such that men receive higher evaluations than women in highly prestigious occupations.
- H2b: Occupational prestige enhances sex differences in organizational rewards such that men receive higher rewards than women in highly prestigious occupations.
- H3a: A higher proportion of women at executive and senior managerial levels within an industry reduces sex differences in performance evaluations such that women receive equal or higher evaluations than men.
- H3b: A higher proportion of women at executive and senior managerial levels within an industry reduces sex differences in organizational rewards such that women receive equal or higher rewards than men.
- H4a: Job complexity enhances sex differences in performance evaluations such that men receive higher evaluations than women in more complex jobs.
- H4b: Job complexity enhances sex differences in organizational rewards such that men receive higher rewards than women in more complex jobs.
- H5: Differences in organizational rewards can be fully accounted for by differences in performance evaluations between men and women.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Proportion of men in an occupational category, occupational prestige, proportion of women at executive or senior managerial levels within an industry, and job complexity.
- DV(s): Sex differences in performance evaluations and organizational rewards.
- Mediators: Performance evaluation differences as a potential explanation for reward differences.
- Moderators: Occupation-, industry-, and job-level work-context attributes. The QCA separately explores sufficient configurations of these attributes.

The paper argues that gender inequality is shaped by cross-level work contexts rather than only by individual human capital or overt discrimination. Male-dominated occupations and complex jobs strengthen gendered status cues, ambiguous evaluation criteria, and discretionary reward allocation, while prestigious occupations can operate as hierarchy-enhancing settings in which women perform equally but receive lower rewards. Industries with more women in executive or senior managerial roles provide a contrasting context, because greater representation and scrutiny can reduce or reverse sex differences in both performance evaluations and rewards.

**Data & Measures**
The meta-analysis synthesized 190 effect sizes from 142 field studies conducted between 1985 and 2013, representing 474,732 individuals. Performance evaluations contributed 93 effect sizes and 95,882 individuals; organizational rewards contributed 97 effect sizes and 378,850 individuals (Table 1). Student samples, experiments, and artificial tasks were excluded. Performance measures assessed job, task, or in-role effectiveness, mostly through supervisor ratings; contextual performance, citizenship behavior, and creative performance were excluded. Multiple performance measures within a study were averaged into a composite effect size. Subjective performance effects were corrected for measurement unreliability; missing reliabilities were replaced by the average for the same variable, with mean performance reliability .89. Rewards were objective salary levels and increases, bonuses and incentive payments, and number of promotions. Standardized mean differences (d) were coded so positive values favor men.

Occupational male representation came from the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Current Population Survey, matched as closely as possible to the primary study year. Occupational prestige used the Nakao-Treas socioeconomic index, with occupation scores on a 100-point scale based on education and income. Industry executive and senior-manager female representation came from EEOC survey data, matched to the industry and timing of each study. Job complexity used the O*Net “complex problem solving” skill score, ranging from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater importance to the job. External-context coding excluded non-U.S. samples, unclear samples, and samples spanning very different work contexts; similar occupations could be averaged. The random-effects meta-regressions are associational: Table 2 reports k = 58, 54, and 61 for performance Models 1–3 and k = 71, 44, and 74 for reward Models 4–6. Controls were study design, publication year, and average sample tenure; publication status was dropped owing to multicollinearity. H5 used a semipartial correlation test, and exploratory crisp-set QCA examined configurations of contextual attributes.

**Key Findings**
The overall performance difference was nonsignificant (d = .04, 95% CI [−.12, .20]), whereas men received higher organizational rewards (d = .56, 95% CI [.45, .65]); the reward gap was 14 times the performance gap (Table 1). Reward subgroup estimates were larger for pay (d = .67, k = 67) than promotions (d = .24, k = 29).

H1a and H1b were supported: occupational male representation was positively related to performance and reward differences favoring men (Table 2: b = 1.37 and 2.08, respectively, both p < .01). H2a was not supported: occupational prestige was not significantly related to performance differences (b = .03, p > .10); H2b was supported for rewards (b = .06, p < .05). H3a and H3b were supported: industry female executive/senior-manager representation was negatively related to performance and reward differences (b = −2.80 and −3.34, respectively, both p < .01). H4a and H4b were supported: job complexity was positively related to performance and reward differences (b = .06 and .05, respectively, both p < .01). Significant associations remained significant after permutation tests. H5 was not supported: the gender–reward correlation remained .27 after accounting for performance differences (both the original and semipartial correlations p < .01; Table 3).

Exploratory QCA identified a configuration for higher male performance evaluations combining male-dominated, less prestigious occupations with low industry female executive representation, regardless of job complexity. Higher female evaluations occurred in routine jobs with either a balanced occupation and low female executive representation or a male-dominated occupation and high female executive representation, regardless of prestige. Higher male rewards occurred in prestigious, complex jobs with low industry female executive representation, regardless of occupational gender balance. All solutions had consistency 1.00; overall coverage was .25 for higher male evaluations, .40 for higher female evaluations, and .14 for higher male rewards (Table 4). The outcome of women receiving equal or higher rewards was excluded because cases were insufficient.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study bridges macro institutional and structural perspectives on gender inequality with micro sociopsychological perspectives on gender bias. Its central contribution is showing that sex differences in rewards are much larger than sex differences in performance evaluations and are not explained by performance gaps, making reward allocation a distinct site of gender inequality. It also adds an exploratory configurational account linking combinations of occupation, industry, and job attributes to higher evaluations for women or men and to higher rewards for men; configurations for higher female rewards were excluded because there were too few cases.

**Practical Implication**
The paper recommends that diversity management focus on accountability structures in performance management and compensation, job designs that reduce inequitable discretion in complex work, and industry-wide mentoring networks for women. It argues that scrutiny by neutral observers or advisors can help move calibration and reward-allocation discussions away from stereotypes and toward performance-relevant information.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the meta-regression analyses used smaller samples than the overall meta-analysis because some primary studies lacked information needed to code external context variables. They could not test a full model with all key and control variables simultaneously, incorporate consistent organization-level variables such as culture or HR policies, include fine-grained human-capital controls, or isolate selection effects into jobs and occupations. They also acknowledge possible file-drawer bias for null or politically uncomfortable gender findings.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on how high-involvement performance management and compensation practices affect gender inequality in male-dominated versus gender-integrated settings. It also proposes work on whether promotion practices that avoid zero-sum choices reduce hierarchy-enhancing reward allocation, how job complexity shapes social comparison and scrutiny, and how organizational attributes operate within broader occupational or industry contexts. Future meta-analyses could compare selection-biased and selection-corrected estimates and extend the argument to contextual performance, creativity, turnover, and specific reward types.
