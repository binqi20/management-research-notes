# Leadership Over-Emergence in Self-Managing Teams: The Role of Gender and Countervailing Biases

**Abstract**
We examine leadership over-emergence, defined as instances when the level of one’s leadership emergence is higher than the level of one’s leadership effectiveness, in a sample of intact self-managing teams who worked together for a period of seven months. We draw from Gender Role Theory and Expectancy Violation Theory to examine the role of gender in predicting leadership over-emergence. Building on arguments from Gender Role Theory, we find that all else equal, men over-emerge as leaders. However, Expectancy Violation Theory suggests that women are likely to benefit from a countervailing bias. Specifically, women are attributed with higher levels of leadership emergence than men when they engage in agentic leadership behaviors—even if the level of the behaviors exhibited by men is exactly the same. Because the impact of these behaviors on leadership effectiveness is not contingent on gender, however, women who engage in more task behaviors and boundary spanning behaviors in self-managing teams also over-emerge as leaders. We discuss the implications of this study for Gender Role Theory and Expectancy Violation Theory, along with practical implications for managing the problem of leadership over-emergence in work groups.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when leadership emergence exceeds leadership effectiveness in self-managing teams and whether gender helps explain that mismatch. It specifically examines whether men over-emerge because gender stereotypes favor men as leaders, and whether women over-emerge when they perform agentic task and boundary-spanning behaviors that violate gendered expectations.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Men over-emerge as leaders to a larger extent than women in self-managing teams.
- H2: Women who engage in task-related behaviors over-emerge as leaders to a larger extent than men who engage in task-related behaviors.
- H3: Women who engage in boundary spanning behaviors over-emerge as leaders to a larger extent than men who engage in boundary spanning behaviors.
- H4: Men who engage in social behaviors over-emerge as leaders to a larger extent than women who engage in social behaviors.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Gender; task-related behaviors; boundary spanning behaviors; social behaviors.
- DV(s): Leadership over-emergence, operationalized as the relative effect size difference of predictors on leadership emergence versus leadership effectiveness.
- Mediators: Not modeled as mediators; the theory explains over-emergence through perceptual bias, gender role expectations, expectancy violations, and recategorization.
- Moderators: Gender moderates the relationship between task-related and boundary spanning behaviors and leadership emergence versus effectiveness; the hypothesized gender moderation for social behaviors was not supported.

The mechanism is a perceptual-bias process in which leadership emergence and leadership effectiveness are related but distinct judgments. Gender Role Theory predicts that men receive more emergence credit than effectiveness credit because male gender roles are more congruent with leader roles. Expectancy Violation Theory predicts a countervailing bias: women who engage in agentic task and boundary spanning behaviors receive more emergence credit than equivalent men, while those behaviors do not produce a corresponding gender-contingent difference in effectiveness. Social behaviors were positively associated with perceived leadership effectiveness, but the predicted male over-emergence effect was not supported.

**Data & Measures**
The longitudinal, associational study sampled 181 full-time MBA students at a large research university in the United States, assigned to five-person self-managing teams: 130 men and 51 women. Table 1 reports n = 177–181; hypothesis-model tables report Level 1 n = 177 and 37 clusters. Personal data and traits were surveyed approximately two months before the program, before teammates met; peer behavior ratings followed approximately six weeks of team interaction, and peer leadership ratings followed approximately four months later. Other than personal data and traits, ratings came from an average of four teammates and were aggregated across raters.

Gender was coded women = 1, men = 0. Task-related behaviors used 20 items covering planning, organizing, and sharing task knowledge; boundary spanning used eight items covering coordination outside the team to acquire resources; social behaviors used 12 items covering listening, support, and conflict resolution. All used five-point extent scales. Leadership emergence used seven descriptive items assessing leading and influencing the team, rated from almost never to almost always; effectiveness used 11 normative items assessing effective leadership and contributions to group success, rated from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Leadership over-emergence was assessed through differences in predictors’ effects on emergence versus effectiveness in multilevel multivariate analyses, retaining the same timing and rating source for both outcomes. Agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion were controls, each measured with 10 International Personality Item Pool items. Hypotheses were tested in Mplus 7.11 using the cluster approach; multigroup analyses decomposed effects for women and men.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported with personality controls: female gender was negatively associated with emergence (B = −0.24, p < .05), but not significantly with effectiveness (B = −0.06); the effect difference was −0.17 (p < .05), indicating greater male over-emergence. In supplementary tests, this gender difference remained significant when boundary and social behaviors were controlled but became nonsignificant when task behaviors entered the regression.
- H2 was supported: the gender × task behavior interaction was positive for emergence (B = 0.37, p < .05) and nonsignificant for effectiveness (B = 0.10; Table 4). Decomposed over-emergence effects were 0.80 for women and 0.54 for men (both p < .01), with a difference of 0.26 (p < .05).
- H3 was supported: the gender × boundary behavior interaction was positive for emergence (B = 0.32, p < .05) and nonsignificant for effectiveness (B = 0.07; Table 5). Decomposed over-emergence was 0.24 for women (p < .05) and 0.00 for men (nonsignificant), with a difference of 0.24 (p < .05).
- H4 was not supported: gender × social behavior interactions were nonsignificant for emergence (B = 0.13) and effectiveness (B = 0.05; Table 6). Social behaviors were positively associated with effectiveness for women (B = 0.54) and men (B = 0.49; both p < .01), but not significantly with emergence for either. The unanticipated under-emergence effects were −0.39 for women and −0.45 for men (both p < .01), with no significant gender difference.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study introduces leadership over-emergence as a distinct problem in self-managing teams: the person seen as most leaderlike may not be the person judged most effective. It contributes to Gender Role Theory by showing that men over-emerge under baseline conditions, while also showing that agentic women can receive a countervailing emergence advantage. It contributes to Expectancy Violation Theory by supporting the counter-stereotypic benefit for agentic women but not the predicted gender-neutral benefit for men who perform communal social behaviors.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that organizations relying on self-managing teams should not assume informal leadership emergence identifies the most effective leader. Teams may need 360-degree feedback, after-action reviews, or rotational leadership schemes to surface discrepancies between emergence and effectiveness. For individuals, especially women seeking to emerge as leaders in small groups, task and boundary-spanning behaviors may increase emergence, although the authors emphasize that this is not a structural solution to gender stereotypes.

**Limitations**
Leadership emergence was measured through subjective perceptions, which may be more vulnerable to bias than objective indicators such as coded video behavior. Gender was also confounded with minority status because the MBA sample contained more men than women. The study did not measure likeability or other indicators of social backlash, and it did not examine other possible predictors such as cognitive ability or prior leadership experience.

**Future Research**
Future research should develop more objective and direct measures of leadership over-emergence and examine whether agentic and social behaviors matter differently across stages of group development. The authors also call for research on leadership under-emergence, male- versus female-dominated group composition, and whether other-advocating versus self-oriented agentic behavior produces different over-emergence patterns. Additional work should test whether agentic women who over-emerge are liked less than men who perform the same behaviors.
