# Strategic Responses to FDI in Emerging Markets: Are Core Members More Responsive than Peripheral Members of Business Groups?

**Abstract**
With increasing foreign direct investment (FDI) into emerging markets, local firms must make critical strategic decisions in order to remain competitive. When faced with multinational enterprises (MNEs) announcing FDI into their industry, local firms can expand operations and challenge the MNE head on, or refrain from responding directly, effectively ceding market share to the MNE investor. We propose that local firms’ responses are shaped by their affiliation with, and position in, a business group. Using data on investment announcements by MNEs and local firms in India from 1995 to 2010, we find that firms that are affiliated with business groups tend to be more sensitive to MNE investment announcements than are stand-alone firms. More professionally managed group affiliate firms are more likely to respond to MNE threats. Furthermore, firms that are in the group’s identity domain—that is, those holding more prominent positions within their group (especially as measured by centrality in the group’s directorship network)—appear more likely to respond to MNE investment announcements. Overall, our results highlight the importance of business group affiliation in examining FDI spillovers in emerging markets.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether local emerging-market firms respond to MNE investment announcements by announcing expansions of their own, and whether that response differs between stand-alone firms and business group affiliates. It further asks whether a business group's professionalization, directorship-network density, and a member firm's prominence within the group shape its responsiveness to FDI threats.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Business group membership positively moderates the relationship between an MNE's investment announcement in an industry and a local firm's propensity to announce a subsequent expansion in that industry.
- H1b: The competing hypothesis predicts negative moderation of that relationship by business group membership.
- H2a: The business group's overall level of professionalization positively moderates the relationship between an MNE's investment announcement and a group affiliate's propensity to announce a subsequent expansion in the same industry.
- H2b: The density of the business group's directorship network positively moderates that relationship.
- H3a: Ownership by the promoter and the promoter's family positively moderates the relationship between an MNE's investment announcement and a local firm's expansion announcement.
- H3b: The wedge between the promoter family's control rights and cash flow rights negatively moderates that relationship.
- H3c: The firm's centrality in the directorship network positively moderates that relationship.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): MNE investment announcements in a firm's industry-year; business group affiliation; group professionalization; group density; promoter ownership; control-cash flow rights wedge; group affiliate binary centrality.
- DV(s): Local firm expansion announcements, measured as whether the local firm announced one or more projects in the same industry-year; robustness models also examine the number of local investment projects.
- Mediators: Awareness, motivation, and capability are the theorized competitive-dynamics processes, but no mediator is directly estimated.
- Moderators: Business group affiliation, group professionalization, group density, promoter ownership, control-cash flow rights wedge, and group affiliate centrality moderate the relationship between MNE investment announcements and local expansion.

The mechanism is a competitive-response process in which MNE investment creates a strategic threat, and local firms differ in their awareness of the threat, motivation to respond, and capability to expand. Business group affiliation can supply resources and coordination, but group heterogeneity matters: professionally managed groups and central member firms are more likely to support expansion responses, while dense interlocks and larger control-cash flow wedges can dampen response. The empirical results support stronger responsiveness among group affiliates, professionally managed groups, and central group members, but do not support promoter ownership and run counter to the predicted positive role of group density.

**Data & Measures**
The archival panel covers 3,531 public limited companies in India during 1995–2010: 1,433 affiliates of 435 business groups and 2,098 stand-alone firms, across 1,118 industries. The authors combined financial, ownership, group-affiliation, and board data from Prowess 4.0.1 with investment announcements from CapEx, both maintained by CMIE, and supplemented board records with director biographies from the BSE directors database. Foreign-owned and government-owned firms were excluded. Observations are at the firm-industry-year level. Tables 3 and 4 each report 70,826 observations for the full-sample models; the affiliate-only models use 35,889 for professionalization, 35,085 for density, 26,896 for promoter ownership and the ownership wedge, and 35,559 for binary centrality.

The dependent variable, local firm expansion, indicates one or more announced projects in a given industry-year. When foreign and local announcements coexist, a local announcement counts as a response only if it follows the first MNE announcement of that calendar year by at least one day; same-day foreign/local announcements were dropped. Foreign announcement indicates at least one foreign investment project announced in the industry-year. Group affiliation is a membership dummy. Group professionalization is the proportion of group firms without the promoter or people sharing the promoter's family name in management; group density is the number of shared-director overlaps between member firms divided by the number of possible overlaps. Promoter ownership is the percentage of equity held by the promoter or family. The control–cash flow rights wedge subtracts promoter equity holdings from control rights, which also include holdings by Persons Acting in Concert. Binary centrality counts the other group firms with which an affiliate shares directors, calculated using UCINET 6.0 from the 2008 network and held constant over time. Ownership information begins in 2001; the authors assign the 2001 wedge values to 1995–2000 and report a robustness check restricted to 2001–2010.

The main analyses use logistic regressions with industry-clustered standard errors and firm, industry, and, where applicable, group controls. These are observational associations; the design does not establish a causal effect of FDI. Count-based robustness models use negative binomial regressions of local project counts on foreign project counts and their interactions; additional tests distinguish the first investment by an MNE into an industry.

**Key Findings**
In Table 3, foreign announcements are positively associated with local expansion (baseline b = 0.3815, p < .01; approximately 1.5 times the odds). The positive interaction with group affiliation supports H1a rather than H1b (b = 0.1552, p < .01), although the affiliation main effect is negative. Among group affiliates, professionalization strengthens the response (H2a: b = 0.5084, p < .05), while density weakens it, contrary to H2b (b = −0.0266, p < .01). Promoter ownership does not significantly moderate the response (H3a: b = 0.0006). The wedge interaction is negative (H3b: b = −0.0128, p < .05), and the binary-centrality interaction is positive (H3c: b = 0.0289, p < .01), supporting those hypotheses in the main logit models.

Support varies across robustness specifications. Table 4's project-count models retain positive interactions for group affiliation (b = 0.0139, p < .05), professionalization (b = 0.0608, p < .01), and centrality (b = 0.0038, p < .01), but density, promoter ownership, and the wedge interactions are not significant. In the unreported first-MNE-investment models, affiliation and centrality remain positive and density remains negative; professionalization and promoter ownership are unsupported. Alternative dependent-variable tests find no evidence of increased R&D or marketing expenses in response to FDI. Thus the wedge result does not extend to project-count responses, and professionalization does not predict responses to first MNE investments in the reported robustness account.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to FDI spillover research by shifting attention from longer-run productivity outcomes to local firms' immediate strategic responses to MNE investment announcements. It links spillover research with competitive dynamics and business group theory, showing that the governance and relational structure of local firms helps explain heterogeneous responses to FDI in emerging markets. It also extends the identity-domain concept to business groups by arguing and showing that core group members, especially those central in directorship networks, are more responsive than peripheral members.

**Practical Implication**
For managers of emerging-market firms, the findings imply that response to foreign entry depends not only on firm-level resources but also on the firm's position in a wider business group. The authors suggest that better understanding these strategic investment dynamics can help managers refine their strategies. Policy makers seeking to understand FDI effects should expect heterogeneous local responses rather than assuming a uniform productivity or crowding-out effect.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the data do not allow them to identify entry deterrence scenarios in which aggressive pre-positioning by incumbent firms deters MNE investment altogether. Their dependent variable captures announced expansion projects, so it may miss other strategic responses, although robustness tests using R&D and marketing expenses did not show evidence of those alternatives. The setting is Indian public limited companies during 1995-2010; the authors acknowledge that the Prowess database may omit some small private firms.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on whether increasingly capable emerging-market firms respond to MNE entry at home by making strategic moves in other markets where the MNE operates. It also suggests examining how relational embeddedness within business groups shapes such global strategic interaction.
