# Uneasy Lies the Head that Bears the Trust: The Effects of Feeling Trusted on Emotional Exhaustion

**Abstract**
The construct of feeling trusted reflects the perception that another party is willing to accept vulnerability to one’s actions. Although this construct has received far less attention than trusting, the consensus is that believing their supervisors trust them has benefits for employees’ job performance. Our study challenges that consensus by arguing that feeling trusted can be exhausting for employees. Drawing on Stevan Hobfoll’s conservation of resources theory, we develop a model in which feeling trusted fills an employee with pride—a benefit for exhaustion and performance—while also increasing perceived workload and concerns about reputation maintenance—burdens for exhaustion and performance. We test our model in a field study using a sample of public transit bus drivers in London, England. Our results suggest that feeling trusted is a double-edged sword for job performance, bringing with it both benefits and burdens. Given that recommendations for managers generally encourage placing trust in employees, these results have important practical implications.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether feeling trusted by a supervisor is uniformly beneficial or whether it can also deplete employees. It specifically examines how feeling trusted affects emotional exhaustion and job performance through pride, perceived workload, and reputation maintenance concerns.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Feeling trusted has a negative indirect effect on emotional exhaustion through pride.
- H2: Feeling trusted has a positive indirect effect on emotional exhaustion through perceived workload.
- H3: Feeling trusted has a positive indirect effect on emotional exhaustion through reputation maintenance concerns.
- H4: Pride moderates the positive indirect effect of feeling trusted on emotional exhaustion through perceived workload, such that the effect is weaker when pride is high than when it is low.
- H5: Pride moderates the positive indirect effect of feeling trusted on emotional exhaustion through reputation maintenance concerns, such that the effect is stronger when pride is high than when it is low.
- H6: Feeling trusted has a positive serial indirect effect on job performance through pride and emotional exhaustion.
- H7: Feeling trusted has a negative serial indirect effect on job performance through perceived workload and emotional exhaustion.
- H8: Feeling trusted has a negative serial indirect effect on job performance through reputation maintenance concerns and emotional exhaustion.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Feeling trusted by a supervisor.
- DV(s): Emotional exhaustion and supervisor-rated job performance.
- Mediators: Pride, perceived workload, reputation maintenance concerns, and emotional exhaustion in the serial paths to job performance.
- Moderators: Pride moderates the indirect effects through perceived workload and reputation maintenance concerns; the reputation maintenance moderation was supported, while the perceived workload moderation was not.

The mechanism is a conservation-of-resources process in which feeling trusted creates both resource gains and resource drains. Feeling trusted increases pride, which reduces emotional exhaustion and indirectly supports job performance. It also increases perceived workload and reputation maintenance concerns, which heighten emotional exhaustion and indirectly reduce job performance. Pride further amplifies the exhausting effect of reputation maintenance concerns because a valued positive image becomes more threatening to lose.

**Data & Measures**
The field study used complete data from 219 bus drivers at four depots of one large transportation company in London, England, with performance ratings from 20 supervisors; 94% of drivers were male. Employees reported feeling trusted at Time 1 and pride, perceived workload, reputation maintenance concerns, and emotional exhaustion six weeks later at Time 2. Supervisors rated job performance at Time 3. This was an observational survey design; the temporal and source separation did not establish causal identification, and the Time 2 constructs were measured together.

Feeling trusted used ten adapted items from Mayer and Gavin’s (2005) trust measure (α = .86). Pride used Tracy and Robins’s (2007) seven items assessing how employees generally felt when thinking about or interacting with their supervisor (α = .94). Perceived workload used four items: two from Rodell and Judge (2009) and two from Bolino and Turnley (2005), capturing working hard and the amount of work expected (α = .71). Reputation maintenance concerns used four newly developed items about preserving one’s image and others’ opinions (α = .87). Emotional exhaustion used the nine-item subscale of the Maslach Burnout Inventory (α = .93). Supervisor-rated job performance used four items adapted from MacKenzie, Podsakoff, and Fetter (1991), covering overall excellence and daily job activities (α = .90). All used five-point response scales; pride used extent ratings, while the other measures used agreement ratings. Structural equation models corrected for nonindependence of performance ratings within supervisors.

A separate content-validation exercise for reputation maintenance concerns recruited 141 undergraduates from a large university in the southeastern United States; 130 provided complete, valid data. They rated how well the four items matched the construct definition on a seven-point scale; mean correspondence was 5.92 out of 7.

**Key Findings**
H1–H3 were supported: feeling trusted had an indirect association with emotional exhaustion of −.11 through pride, .10 through perceived workload, and .04 through reputation maintenance concerns (Table 2; all p < .05). The direct effect (−.07) and total effect (−.04) on emotional exhaustion were not significant.

H4 was not supported: the workload-mediated indirect effect was .13 at low pride and .07 at high pride, but the difference (−.06) was not significant. H5 was supported: the reputation-mediated indirect effect was −.04 at low pride and .12 at high pride, with a significant difference of .16 (p < .05). Low and high pride were one standard deviation below and above the centered mean (Table 3).

H6–H8 were supported: the serial indirect effects on job performance through pride and emotional exhaustion (.02), perceived workload and emotional exhaustion (−.02), and reputation maintenance concerns and emotional exhaustion (−.01) were all significant. Thus, the fitted model identified beneficial and detrimental indirect paths, with pride amplifying the reputation-related burden but no supported buffering of the workload-related burden.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study challenges the prevailing assumption that feeling trusted is simply beneficial for employees and shows that it can operate as a double-edged sword. It extends trust research by using conservation of resources theory to distinguish resource gains, resource losses, and resource threats in the experience of being trusted. It also contributes to conservation of resources theory by simultaneously operationalizing gains, losses, and threats and showing that pride can intensify the emotional exhaustion associated with reputation maintenance concerns.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that managers should recognize that trusted employees may still become emotionally exhausted, especially when trust brings more responsibilities or pressure to protect a positive image. Managers can accentuate the positive meaning of trust, redistribute or remove other tasks when trusted employees receive extra responsibilities, and reassure employees that their reputation does not rise or fall with every stretch assignment. Social support is also presented as a way to reduce emotional exhaustion.

**Limitations**
The design used two sources and three waves, but pride, perceived workload, reputation maintenance concerns, and emotional exhaustion were measured in the same wave, so those links may still contain common-method inflation. The data established temporal precedence but did not measure all variables at all time periods, limiting causal authority compared with a panel design. The sample was predominantly male and had a final complete-data sample of around 200 participants, so larger replications would strengthen generalizability.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine additional mediators linking feeling trusted to performance, including psychological empowerment and idiosyncrasy credits, and compare the effects of different manifestations of managerial trust, such as delegating tasks, promoting ideas, and requesting favors. The authors also call for research on outcomes beyond traditional job performance, including creativity and adaptive performance. Further work should test whether the effects of feeling trusted vary by job control, work-unit dispersion of trust, and individual differences such as conscientiousness and prosocial motives.
