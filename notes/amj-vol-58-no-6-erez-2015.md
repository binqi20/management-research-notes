# Inherently Relational: Interactions between Peers’ and Individuals’ Personalities Impact Reward Giving and Appraisal of Individual Performance

**Abstract**
Introverted individuals may experience and evaluate their dyadic work relationships differently than extraverts. In two studies, we investigated the interaction effect of an individual’s and observing peer’s personality traits on performance evaluations and reward giving. Results from Study 1 showed that introverted (but not extraverted) peers consistently evaluated extraverted and disagreeable (but not introverted and agreeable) individuals’ performance as lower. Study 2 replicated these findings with regard to performance evaluation and reward giving using an experimental design that manipulated actor personality and held objective performance constant. The results also showed that introverts’ trait sensitivity and negative person impressions mediated these relationships. Overall, our results support an information utilization model of interpersonal dyadic evaluation, wherein introverts are more sensitive to interpersonal personality traits than are their extraverted counterparts, incorporating interpersonal traits in person impressions and subsequent evaluations and reward distributions. Our paper concludes with implications for dyadic workplace interactions, personality, and sources of emergent dyadic influences on performance evaluation.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether an observing peer's personality changes how that peer evaluates and rewards coworkers with different interpersonal traits. Specifically, it tests whether introverted peers are more likely than extraverted peers to penalize extraverted and disagreeable actors in performance evaluations, reward allocations, and promotion-related judgments.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Observing peers’ extraversion interacts with actors’ extraversion in predicting performance evaluations and rewards: introverted peers evaluate and reward extraverted actors more negatively than extraverted peers do.
- H1b: Observing peers’ extraversion interacts with actors’ agreeableness in predicting performance evaluations and rewards: introverted peers evaluate and reward disagreeable actors more negatively than extraverted peers do.
- H2a: Trait sensitivity mediates the interaction between observing peers’ extraversion and actors’ extraversion on performance evaluations and rewards given to the actor.
- H2b: Trait sensitivity mediates the interaction between observing peers’ extraversion and actors’ agreeableness on performance evaluations and rewards given to the actor.
- H3a: Person impressions mediate the interaction between observing peers’ extraversion and actors’ extraversion on performance evaluations and rewards given to the actor.
- H3b: Person impressions mediate the interaction between observing peers’ extraversion and actors’ agreeableness on performance evaluations and rewards given to the actor.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Actor interpersonal traits, especially extraversion and agreeableness/disagreeableness; observing peer extraversion/introversion.
- DV(s): Peer performance evaluations, peer reward decisions, and promotion recommendations.
- Mediators: Trait sensitivity and negative person impressions in Study 2.
- Moderators: Observing peer extraversion/introversion moderates the effects of actor extraversion and actor agreeableness/disagreeableness.

The proposed mechanism is an information-utilization process in dyadic evaluation. Drawing on the Realistic Accuracy Model of personality judgment, the authors argue that introverted peers are more sensitive to interpersonal trait cues and more likely to use those cues when forming general person impressions. Those impressions then shape ratings and rewards: introverted peers evaluate and reward extraverted or disagreeable actors more negatively, even when the experiment holds objective task performance constant. The results support this pattern in both the field dyadic team study and the virtual-team experiment, with trait sensitivity and person impressions mediating parts of the Study 2 effects.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 used an associational, round-robin design with graduate students in five management-course sections at a university in the Southeastern United States. Of 191 participating students, 178 entered the final analyses; 97 were working professionals or executive MBA students. Students worked in assigned four- or five-person teams and completed the questionnaire around midsemester. Each rated three or four teammates on 13 Role-Based Performance Scale (RBPS) items covering task, creative, team, and citizenship performance (1 = needs much improvement to 5 = excellent; α = .95). Self-reported agreeableness and extraversion each used 10-item Saucier (1994) mini-markers scales with five-point accuracy ratings (α = .84 and .91, respectively). Dyadic hierarchical linear models included actor and peer traits and their interactions. Table 2 reports three data sets of 83, 81, and 80 independent dyads; the sets share participants and are not independent replications.

Study 2 recruited 143 management-course students at a large Southeastern university in the United States; eight who suspected electronic confederates were excluded. Table 3 reports 135 participants: 66 in the extraversion conditions (43 introverted-actor, 23 extraverted-actor) and 69 in the agreeableness conditions (37 agreeable-actor, 32 disagreeable-actor). Participants completed a personality survey and, about a week later, a virtual four-member team task generating uses for a brick. Random assignment manipulated the target confederate’s personality through profiles and spoken/written comments while holding objective task contributions constant; peer extraversion was measured, using John and Srivastava’s (1999) eight-item scale (five-point agreement ratings; α = .91). Target performance used the RBPS (α = .96); promotion recommendations used six items from Kiker and Motowidlo (1999), with seven-point response scales (α = .96). The reward outcome coded whether the participant selected the target to receive a $5 Amazon gift card (1 = selected, 0 = otherwise). Person impressions used 20 positive adjectives from Anderson’s (1968) list, selected to be unrelated to extraversion and agreeableness (five-point accuracy ratings; α = .92). Trait sensitivity used 12-item Saucier (1994) ratings of target extraversion or agreeableness (five-point accuracy ratings; α = .96 and .97, respectively); these indexed sensitivity to strongly manipulated, prototypical traits rather than accuracy against an objective trait score. Analyses used multivariate and univariate analyses of variance, logistic regression, and separate mediated-moderation models for each mediator and outcome with 3,000 bootstrap iterations.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported H1a and H1b for performance evaluations. Across data sets 1–3, the actor-extraversion × peer-extraversion coefficients were .21, .13, and .18, and the actor-agreeableness × peer-extraversion coefficients were −.24, −.36, and −.20 (unstandardized; all p < .05). Introverted peers rated more extraverted actors lower in every set, whereas extraverted peers’ corresponding slopes were nonsignificant. Agreeableness was positively associated with ratings more strongly among introverted peers; extraverted peers also showed significant positive agreeableness slopes in data sets 2 and 3. Study 1 did not test reward outcomes or the proposed mediators.

Study 2 supported H1a: introverted peers gave introverted actors higher performance ratings and promotion recommendations than extraverted actors and were more likely to reward them (odds ratio = 5.78, p < .05); extraverted peers showed no corresponding differences. H3a was supported: person impressions carried negative conditional indirect effects of actor extraversion on performance evaluations, promotion recommendations, and rewards for introverted peers (−.44, −.50, and −.26, respectively; all p < .05), but not extraverted peers. H2a received only partial support: trait sensitivity mediated promotion recommendations for introverted peers (−.74, p < .05), but not performance evaluations or rewards.

Study 2 also supported H1b: both peer groups evaluated and recommended agreeable actors more positively than disagreeable actors, with larger differences among introverted peers. Introverted peers selected agreeable actors for 21 of 21 possible rewards versus 2 of 19 for disagreeable actors (p < .01); extraverted peers’ corresponding difference, 11 of 16 versus 5 of 13, was nonsignificant. H2b and H3b were supported: both trait sensitivity and person impressions carried negative conditional indirect effects of actor disagreeableness on all three outcomes for both peer groups, with larger magnitudes for introverted peers. For performance evaluations, the person-impression indirect effects were −1.33 for introverted peers and −.62 for extraverted peers; the trait-sensitivity effects were −1.47 and −1.01, respectively (all p < .01). Although objective task performance was held constant, the authors leave open whether these evaluations represent bias or broader judgments of interpersonal performance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to dyadic workplace-interaction theory by showing that peer evaluations are not only properties of actors or raters but can emerge from actor-by-peer personality combinations. It extends personality research by applying introversion-linked sensitivity to interpersonal traits in work settings. It also contributes to performance appraisal theory by identifying a dyadic source of rating variance that may be hidden when evaluations are modeled only as rater bias or ratee performance.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations using peer evaluations, peer bonuses, or 360-degree feedback should monitor whether actor-peer personality combinations systematically shape ratings and rewards. The findings suggest that highly extraverted or disagreeable employees may need feedback about how their trait-relevant behaviors affect introverted peers' experiences of dyadic work encounters. Managers can also design team interactions and workspaces to reduce overwhelming interpersonal exposure for introverted employees.

**Limitations**
The authors note that hidden psychological processes are difficult to observe directly, and Study 2 holds task output constant but cannot fully resolve whether introverted peers are biased or are evaluating broader interpersonal performance more holistically. The studies focus on dyads, so full-team processes could buffer or alter the effects. The experiment also uses salient personality manipulations, limited interaction time, and an introversion manipulation that may partly signal shyness or social anxiety.

**Future Research**
Future work should test whether introverted peers make more accurate judgments of interpersonal performance or whether accurate personality judgments spill into biased performance ratings. Research could examine larger team contexts and longer interactions where trait-consistent behaviors emerge without explicit personality descriptions. Additional work could also identify threshold levels at which introversion or extraversion begins to change dyadic evaluation patterns.
