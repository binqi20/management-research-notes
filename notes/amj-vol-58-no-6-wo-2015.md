# What Drives Trickle-Down Effects? A Test of Multiple Mediation Processes

**Abstract**
Organizational research has established the existence of trickle-down effects, wherein the perceptions, attitudes, or behavior of one person in an organization affects those of another person at a lower level. Although current research has explained the phenomenon using several different theoretical perspectives, prior studies have not explicitly tested the theorized mechanisms. This paper develops and tests a model that incorporates three theories of trickle-down effects for interactional justice perceptions: social exchange theory, social learning theory, and displaced aggression. Using crosssectional data from 200 supervisor–subordinate dyads in Study 1 and longitudinal data from 270 supervisor–subordinate dyads in Study 2, we test this multiple mediator model. The results demonstrate that the two aspects of interactional justice perceptions— interpersonal and informational justice perceptions—trickle down from supervisors to subordinates through two different mechanisms. Specifically, displaced aggression accounts for the trickle-down effects of interpersonal justice perceptions and social exchange accounts for the trickle-down effects of informational justice perceptions. We discuss the implications of the findings for research on trickle-down effects and organizational justice.

**Research Question**
The paper asks which theoretical mechanisms explain trickle-down effects in interactional justice perceptions from managers to supervisors to subordinates. It specifically examines whether interpersonal and informational justice perceptions trickle down through the same or different processes: social exchange, social learning, and displaced aggression.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Supervisors' perceived organizational support (POS) mediates the relationship between supervisors' perceptions of their own interpersonal justice experiences and subordinates' interpersonal justice perceptions.
- H1b: Supervisors' POS mediates the relationship between supervisors' perceptions of their own informational justice experiences and subordinates' informational justice perceptions.
- H2a: Supervisors' perceptions of their managers' role model influence mediate the relationship between supervisors' own interpersonal justice perceptions and subordinates' interpersonal justice perceptions.
- H2b: Supervisors' perceptions of their managers' role model influence mediate the relationship between supervisors' own informational justice perceptions and subordinates' informational justice perceptions.
- H3a: Supervisors' anger mediates the relationship between supervisors' own interpersonal justice perceptions and subordinates' interpersonal justice perceptions.
- H3b: Supervisors' anger mediates the relationship between supervisors' own informational justice perceptions and subordinates' informational justice perceptions.
- H4: Supervisors' negative affect in the form of anger/irritation mediates the relationship between supervisors' own interpersonal justice perceptions and subordinates' interpersonal justice perceptions.
- H5: Supervisors' felt obligation toward the organization mediates the relationship between supervisors' own informational justice perceptions and subordinates' informational justice perceptions.
- H6: Supervisors' interactional efficacy mediates the relationship between supervisors' own informational justice perceptions and subordinates' informational justice perceptions.

H1a–H3b are tested in Study 1; its comparison of affective and cognitive routes is exploratory. Building on Study 1, H4–H6 specify different mechanisms for the two justice facets in Study 2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Supervisors' perceptions of their own interpersonal justice experiences and informational justice experiences from their managers.
- DV(s): Subordinates' perceptions of interpersonal justice and informational justice from their supervisors.
- Mediators: Study 1 tests perceived organizational support, role model influence, and anger; Study 2 tests felt obligation toward the organization, interactional efficacy, and anger/irritation.
- Moderators: No moderator is central to the hypothesized mediation model.

The mechanism is a cross-level trickle-down process in which supervisors' treatment by managers shapes how supervisors treat subordinates. The two studies show different processes for the two justice facets: interpersonal justice trickles down through anger or anger/irritation, consistent with displaced aggression, while informational justice trickles down through perceived organizational support or felt obligation, consistent with social exchange. The studies do not find support for social learning as the mediating process for either justice facet.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 used cross-sectional online surveys from 200 matched supervisor–subordinate dyads across organizations in the Southeastern United States (Table 1). A snowball procedure recruited 421 students in MBA and upper-level undergraduate business courses to identify employees working at least 30 hours per week; those employees invited their immediate supervisors. Supervisors reported their own justice experiences and the three mediators; subordinates reported their justice experiences with those supervisors. Both parties completed Colquitt (2001) measures of interpersonal justice (four items concerning dignity and respect) and informational justice (five items concerning candid communication and explanations), using seven-point extent scales. Supervisors' POS used three items from a shortened organizational support measure associated with Eisenberger et al. (1986, 2002); role model influence used three items adapted from Rich (1997); state anger used six items from Buss & Perry (1992), contextualized with “at work” and without a general trait framing. These mediator items used seven-point agreement scales.

Study 2 used 270 matched supervisor–subordinate dyads (Table 3), recruited through 1,086 students in upper-level undergraduate business courses at a large Southeastern university using the same snowball procedure and protocol. The design separated supervisors' justice perceptions at Time 1, supervisors' mediators at Time 2, and subordinates' justice perceptions at Time 3, with three weeks between waves. Justice measures were the same Colquitt (2001) scales as in Study 1. Felt obligation used the six-item Eisenberger et al. (2001) scale assessing obligations to contribute to organizational goals and high-quality work. The authors developed three interactional-efficacy items assessing whether seeing supervisors' treatment made respondents feel more capable of treating their own subordinates fairly. Anger/irritation combined two anger items from Buss & Perry (1992) with two work-related irritation items from Caplan et al. (1975). All three mediator measures used seven-point agreement scales.

Both studies used confirmatory factor analysis and structural equation models with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect effects, based on 1,000 bootstrap samples. These are observational mediation analyses: Study 1 is cross-sectional, and Study 2 establishes temporal ordering through separated measurement waves. Study 2 also reports that controlling for baseline subordinate justice perceptions, shared supervisor–subordinate similarity, organizational benevolence, and supervisors' positive affect did not change the result pattern.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported H3a and H1b. Table 2 reports a positive indirect interpersonal-justice effect through anger of .08 (95% bootstrap CI [.02, .20]): supervisors' interpersonal justice perceptions were negatively related to anger (b = −.21), and anger was negatively related to subordinates' interpersonal justice perceptions (b = −.40), both p < .05. The informational-justice indirect effect through POS was .05 (CI [.01, .15]), with positive constituent paths (b = .28 and .19, both p < .05). H1a, H2a, H2b, and H3b were not supported. The same-facet direct effects were nonsignificant, which the authors interpret as full mediation by anger for interpersonal justice and POS for informational justice.

Study 2's hypothesized model supported H4 and H5, but not H6. Table 4 reports an interpersonal-justice indirect effect through anger/irritation of .10 (CI [.03, .20]) and an informational-justice indirect effect through felt obligation of .08 (CI [.03, .19]); the interactional-efficacy indirect effect was .02 (CI [−.02, .08]). The anger/irritation paths were negative (b = −.22 and −.42), while the felt-obligation paths were positive (b = .28 and .29), all p < .05. Same-facet direct effects were again nonsignificant. However, the alternative Study 2 model estimating all paths has a narrower support pattern: Table 5 retains a significant anger/irritation indirect effect for interpersonal justice (.09, CI [.01, .24]), while the felt-obligation indirect effect for informational justice is .03 (CI [−.04, .16]) and is not significant. Neither study supports social learning mediation; the social-exchange result in Study 2 depends on the model specification reported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to trickle-down and organizational justice research by directly testing, rather than merely invoking, three major theoretical explanations for trickle-down effects in a single multiple-mediation framework. It shows that the mechanism depends on the justice facet: interpersonal justice follows a more affective displaced-aggression route, whereas informational justice follows a more cognitive social-exchange route. It also challenges the common use of social learning theory as a default explanation for trickle-down effects by finding no support for role model influence or interactional efficacy as mediators.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that supervisors are not only transmitters of higher-level treatment but may also serve as buffers who can stop negative treatment from cascading downward. Because interpersonal justice trickles down through anger and irritation, organizations may reduce harmful spillovers by helping supervisors manage emotional responses to unfair treatment. The separate informational-justice pathway through felt obligation also shows that different forms of justice may cascade through different mechanisms.

**Limitations**
The authors note that they test three major trickle-down theories but do not exhaust all possible justice theories, such as relational, deontic, or needs-based accounts. They also caution that the evidence speaks directly only to interpersonal and informational justice, not every kind of trickle-down phenomenon. Additional limitations include potential unfairness in how the Study 2 mediators were operationalized, relatively fair samples that may understate displaced aggression, and the possibility that other operationalizations of the mechanisms could yield additional insight.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on additional mediating mechanisms from other justice theories, including self-identity, moral identity, and needs for autonomy or control. It also suggests examining whether the same affective and cognitive mechanisms generalize to other kinds of trickle-down effects beyond interpersonal and informational justice. Future studies should use more parallel mediator operationalizations and samples that include both fair and unfair environments.
