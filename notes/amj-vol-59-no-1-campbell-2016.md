# Fuzzy Logic and the Market: A Configurational Approach to Investor Perceptions of Acquisition Announcements

**Abstract**
Prior research on mergers and acquisitions (M&As) has substantially advanced our understanding of how isolated acquirerand deal-specific factors affect abnormal stock returns. However, investors are likely to perceive and evaluate M&As holistically—that is, as complex configurations (i.e., gestalts) of characteristics, rather than as a list of independent factors. And, yet, extant M&A literature has not addressed why and how configurations of factors elicit positive or negative reactions. Overlooking the interdependent nature of factors known to influence acquisition success has limited our understanding of both M&As and investor judgment. Taking an inductive approach to addressing this important issue, the present study relies on fuzzy set methodology. Our results provide compelling evidence that investor perceptions of M&A announcements are not only configurational in nature but also characterized by equifinality (or the presence of multiple paths to success) and asymmetric causality (i.e., configurations that represent bad deals are not simply a mirror image of good deals, but differ fundamentally). By constructing a typology of “good” and “bad” deals as perceived by market participants, we develop a mid-range theory of M&A stock market performance. As such, this study offers novel theoretical and empirical insights to scholars as well as implications for practitioners.

**Research Question**
The paper asks which configurations of acquirer- and deal-specific factors elicit positive investor reactions to M&A announcements and which configurations fail to elicit positive reactions. It examines investor judgment as holistic, configurational sensemaking rather than as the additive evaluation of independent deal attributes.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The study refrains from a priori hypotheses and develops six propositions inductively from its findings:

- Proposition 1: High acquirer ability, proper motivation, reinforcing signals of synergistic potential, and future integration potential reflected in acquirer–target size dissimilarity are jointly sufficient for a positive market reaction to geographic expansion deals.
- Proposition 2: Proper motivation, signals of valuable opportunity, and future integration potential reflected in acquirer–target size dissimilarity are jointly sufficient for a positive market reaction to related diversification by experienced acquirers.
- Proposition 3: Proper motivation and ex post ability reflected in financial flexibility, signals of valuable opportunity, and future integration potential reflected in geographic proximity are jointly sufficient for a positive market reaction to related diversification by novice acquirers.
- Proposition 4: Strong mixed signals of deal opportunity combined with either low acquirer ability (inexperience) or lack of proper motivation (hubris) are sufficient for a poor market reaction to merger-of-equals deals.
- Proposition 5: Strong mixed signals of deal opportunity and signs of excessive risk taking due to poor acquirer performance and substantial acquisition experience are jointly sufficient for a poor market reaction to turnaround deals.
- Proposition 6: Strong mixed signals of deal opportunity and signs of future difficulties investing in the deal ex post are jointly sufficient for a poor market reaction to related diversification by experienced acquirers.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Configurations of nine calibrated conditions: acquirer performance, acquirer leverage, acquirer experience, industry relatedness, acquisition premium, cash payment percentage, friendliness, geographic proximity, and acquirer-target size similarity.
- DV(s): Investor reaction to acquisition announcements, operationalized as short-window cumulative abnormal returns and analyzed as positive market reaction versus absence of positive reaction.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The mechanism is configurational: investors facing bounded rationality, information asymmetry, and high-velocity decision contexts use salient signals about opportunity, motivation, and ability to form holistic perceptions of an announced acquisition. Fuzzy set analysis shows equifinality, with multiple configurations producing positive reactions, and asymmetric causality, with poorly received deals differing qualitatively from well-received deals rather than simply reversing the same factors.

**Data & Measures**
The main archival sample comprises 2,403 completed acquisitions by North American public acquirers during 1990–2004, spanning all major industries and resulting in full target ownership. Deal data came from Thomson Financial’s SDC Platinum; firm information came from Compustat and Form 10-K filings in EDGAR; stock-return data came from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP). The sample narrowed from 99,240 deals to 37,632 with payment data, then to 2,835 with complete deal-level variables; missing firm/return data and 15 extreme premium outliers reduced it to 2,403.

Investor reaction was measured as cumulative abnormal returns over the announcement day and the immediately preceding and following trading days. Returns were calibrated into fuzzy membership in positive market performance, with zero as the crossover point; the absence-of-positive-performance analysis reversed the full-membership and full-nonmembership thresholds. The nine conditions were operationalized as follows:

- Acquirer performance: return on assets in the 12 months preceding announcement, calibrated against industry-adjusted performance benchmarks from Compustat.
- Acquirer leverage: debt-to-equity ratio.
- Acquirer experience: cumulative prior completed acquisitions from 1980 until the acquisition year.
- Relatedness: a 0–4 ordinal score for the number of matching leading SIC digits in the firms’ core businesses.
- Acquisition premium: percentage difference between the price paid per target share and its price one day before announcement.
- Payment: percentage of the deal paid in cash rather than stock.
- Friendliness: a binary indicator equal to one when there was no tender offer, proxy fight, or deployment of target defense tactics.
- Geographic proximity: same state/province, same country, or different countries, calibrated to 1, 0.80, and 0, respectively.
- Size similarity: target total assets divided by acquirer total assets.

The observational event study was combined with direct calibration and fsQCA 2.0. The truth-table frequency threshold was eight cases per configuration, retaining 82% of the sample, and the raw consistency threshold was 0.80. A separate supplemental exploratory analysis examined 586 deals with available abnormal-return data from 2007-11 through 2013.

**Key Findings**
The main analysis identified four configurations associated with positive market reactions and four associated with the absence of positive reactions, the paper’s “not high” performance outcome. These findings supplied the basis for the six emergent propositions rather than tests of a priori hypotheses. Table 2 reports overall solution consistency of 0.82 and coverage of 0.18 for positive reactions, compared with 0.86 and 0.28 for their absence; individual configuration consistency ranged from 0.82 to 0.90.

- Positive configurations 1a and 1b combined geographic distance, high acquirer performance, high leverage, substantial experience, cash payment, and low size similarity. Configuration 1a additionally included high industry relatedness; 1b instead included high acquisition premium.
- Positive configuration 2 combined high relatedness, high premium, and lack of friendliness with high acquirer performance, leverage, and experience, cash payment, and low size similarity. Configuration 3 combined high relatedness, high premium, and lack of friendliness with low acquirer experience, high performance, low leverage, cash payment, and geographic proximity.
- Configurations 4a and 4b, associated with the absence of positive reactions, shared high premium, lack of cash payment, and high size similarity. Configuration 4a also included low acquirer experience and low leverage; 4b instead included high acquirer performance and high leverage.
- Configuration 5 combined low acquirer performance, substantial experience, lack of industry relatedness, high premium, lack of cash payment, and low leverage. Configuration 6 combined substantial experience, high leverage, high industry relatedness, high premium, and lack of cash payment. Both were associated with the absence of positive reactions.

The multiple paths illustrate equifinality, while the differences between configurations associated with positive reactions and those lacking them illustrate asymmetric causality. Cash financing appeared in every positive configuration; high premium combined with no cash financing appeared in every configuration lacking positive reactions. These are patterns within configurations, not estimates of each factor’s independent causal effect. In the separate supplemental 2007–2013 analysis, most conclusions about individual factors were substantively unchanged, but all well-received deals included low leverage and unsuccessful deals included high leverage. The measured outcomes capture short-term market assessments; the study cannot establish these configurations’ long-term acquisition performance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a mid-range configurational theory of M&A stock market performance by treating acquisition announcements as complex signals evaluated as gestalts. It contributes to M&A research by showing that individual deal factors such as relatedness, financing, premium, leverage, and experience have contingent meanings within configurations, and it contributes to behavioral strategy by theorizing investors as boundedly rational market participants rather than purely reductionist evaluators.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that managers should consider how acquisition attributes will be read together before announcing a deal. Cash financing is especially consequential because investors reacted positively to configurations with cash financing and negatively to configurations combining high premiums with no cash financing, particularly when other factors suggested integration difficulty, hubris, turnaround risk, or overextension.

**Limitations**
The study explains only part of the variation in positive and negative M&A announcement reactions, and the authors note that the overall solution coverage is limited. The design uses short-term cumulative abnormal returns, so it captures investors' perceived value of strategy formulation rather than long-term acquisition implementation performance. The sample is limited to acquisitions by North American acquirers in developed financial and regulatory settings.

**Future Research**
Future research should refine the configurational framework by identifying additional deal prototypes, examining finer time periods, and testing boundary conditions such as changing credit-market environments. The authors also call for work on whether these configurations generalize to emerging-market contexts, whether deal communication wording and tone shape investor reactions, and how stock-financed deals can elicit positive reactions when cash financing is absent.
