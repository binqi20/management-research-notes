# Classical Deviation: Organizational and Individual Status as Antecedents of Conformity

**Abstract**
Beside making organizations look like their peers through the adoption of similar attributes ("alignment"), this paper highlights the fact that conformity also enables organizations to stand out by exhibiting highly salient attributes key to their field or industry ("conventionality"). Building on the conformity and status literatures, and using the case of major U.S. symphony orchestras and the changes in their concert programming between 1879 and 1969, we hypothesize and find that middle-status organizations are more aligned and middle-status individual leaders make more conventional choices than their lowand high-status peers. In addition, the extent to which middle-status leaders adopt conventional programming is shown to be moderated by the status of the organization and by its level of alignment. Thus, this paper offers a novel theory and operationalization of organizational conformity, and contributes to the literature on status effects, and, more broadly, to the understanding of the key issues of distinctiveness and conformity.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how organizational and individual status shape two distinct forms of organizational conformity: alignment with peer repertoires and conventionality through emphasis on salient canonical attributes. It examines whether middle-status organizations and middle-status individual leaders conform differently because they face different exposure to penalties and rewards.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Middle-status actors exhibit higher levels of alignment than high- and low-status actors.
- H2: Middle-status actors exhibit higher levels of conventionality than high- and low-status actors.
- H3a: Organizational status has a stronger effect on alignment than individual status.
- H3b: Individual status has a stronger effect on conventionality than organizational status.

H1 and H2 apply to both organizational and individual actors; H3a and H3b distinguish the relative effects across levels.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Orchestra status and musical director status, including linear and quadratic status terms.
- DV(s): Repertoire alignment and repertoire conventionality in orchestra programming.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Orchestra status and orchestra alignment moderate the director-status effect on conventionality.

The mechanism separates penalty avoidance from quality-comparison rewards. Alignment lets organizations blend in with peers and reduce sanctions for misalignment, so middle-status organizations have the strongest incentive to align. Conventionality lets individual leaders stand out on salient, comparable repertoire choices and claim quality recognition, so middle-status musical directors have the strongest incentive to adopt conventional programming. Higher orchestra status is associated with more conventional programming especially among high-status directors, narrowing the middle-status advantage. The inverted-U relationship is more pronounced when repertoire alignment is low.

**Data & Measures**
The historical panel uses repertoire records from Mueller (1973, 2008) and musical-director tenure records from Craven (1986). The analyses cover 1879–1969 and comprise 1,090 orchestra-years, 27 major orchestras in the United States, and 154 musical directors (Tables 2–5). Alignment is the negative log of the sum of squared differences between a focal orchestra's composer performance shares and those of all other orchestras in the same season, scaled by the maximum possible distance. Conventionality is the slope from regressing the focal orchestra's composer shares on other orchestras' composer shares: values above one indicate disproportionate emphasis on widely performed composers, and values below one indicate the reverse.

Orchestra and director status are proxies based on Bonacich and Lloyd's (2001) eigenvector-like centrality in separate networks of new-composer diffusion. Ties capture others' adoption of composers first introduced by the focal actor, with greater weight for faster adoption and no contribution from adoption five or more years after introduction. Orchestra centrality uses diffusion networks accumulated over the previous 25 years; director status uses a similar measure in the inter-director network. Status variables are standardized. Linear models include orchestra, director, and decade fixed effects, linear and quadratic status terms, log annual performances, and director tenure. Status and performance counts are lagged one year; the tables specify contemporaneous director tenure and, in the additional moderation analysis, alignment. The design estimates observational associations. Robustness checks add lagged outcomes, use random effects, and control cumulative introductions of new composers.

**Key Findings**
- H1 receives support for organizational status, but not individual status. In Table 2, Model 2, orchestra status has an inverted-U association with alignment (linear b = 0.114, p < .05; quadratic b = −0.067, p < .05). With director status included in Model 4, the orchestra quadratic term is marginal (b = −0.052, p < .10), while director status has no significant quadratic association. The authors interpret this pattern as consistent with H3a. The orchestra quadratic term is nonsignificant when cumulative innovation is controlled in Model 11.
- H2 receives support for individual status, but not organizational status. Middle-status directors favor more conventional programming (Table 3, Model 7: director quadratic b = −0.019, p < .01). In Model 8, director status retains its inverted-U association (linear b = 0.079, p < .01; quadratic b = −0.020, p < .001), while orchestra status terms are nonsignificant, supporting H3b. The director pattern persists in the reported robustness checks.
- Additional analyses find no significant moderation of director status by orchestra status for alignment. For conventionality, orchestra status moderates the director quadratic term (Model 18: b = 0.009, p < .05); high-status directors become more conventional in high-status orchestras, narrowing their gap from middle-status directors. Alignment's interaction with the linear director-status term is negative and marginal (Model 19: b = −0.025, p < .10), while its interaction with the quadratic term is nonsignificant. Predicted conventionality differs significantly between middle- and low/high-status directors at low alignment, but not at high alignment (Table 6). These programming patterns are consistent with the proposed penalty-avoidance and reward-seeking interpretation; the models do not directly measure those motivations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper decomposes conformity into alignment and conventionality, showing that organizations can conform by blending in or by standing out on highly salient field attributes. It refines middle-status conformity theory by linking organizational status more strongly to alignment and individual leader status more strongly to conventionality, thereby integrating status effects across organizational and individual levels. It also challenges the assumption that misalignment necessarily equals deviance, because misaligned actors may still be conventional.

**Practical Implication**
For organizations and leaders in status-ordered fields, conformity pressure is not a single strategic constraint. The study shows that organizations may reduce penalties by maintaining broad alignment with peer norms, while individual leaders may seek recognition by concentrating effort on salient, quality-comparable choices. Interpreting conformity therefore requires distinguishing between blending in with peers and emphasizing canonical features that invite quality comparison.

**Limitations**
The study is based on major U.S. symphony orchestras between 1879 and 1969, a setting with strong programming norms, elite cultural audiences, and unusually visible individual artistic leaders. The authors note that their analysis does not explain norm dynamics and considers conformity only with a contemporaneous norm. Generalization should be tested in fields with different audience structures, technological change, or competing institutional logics.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether audience heterogeneity changes the relationship between status, alignment, and conventionality. Other individual or organizational characteristics besides status may also shape responses to negative sanctions and positive rewards. Research on faster-changing normative environments could examine discrepancies between historical norms and contemporary imperatives for conformity.
