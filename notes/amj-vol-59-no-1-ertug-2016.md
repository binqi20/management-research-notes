# The Art of Representation: How Audience-specific Reputations Affect Success in the Contemporary Art Field

**Abstract**
We study the effects of actors’ audience-specific reputations on their levels of success with different audiences in the same field. Extending recent work that has emphasized the presence of multiple audiences with different concerns, we demonstrate that considering audience specificity leads to an improved understanding of reputation effects. Using data on emerging artists in the field of contemporary art from 2001 to 2010, we investigate the manner in which artists’ audience-specific reputations affect their subsequent success with two distinct audiences: museums and galleries. Our findings suggest that audience-specific reputations have systematically different effects with respect to success with museums and galleries. Our findings also illuminate the extent to which audience-specific reputations are relevant for emerging research on the contingent effects of reputation. In particular, our findings support our predictions that audiences differ from one another in terms of the extent to which other signals (specifically, status and interaction with other audiences) enhance or reduce the value of audience-specific reputations. Our study thus advances theory by providing empirical evidence for the value of incorporating audience-specific reputations into the general study of reputation.

**Research Question**
How do actors' audience-specific reputations affect their success with different audiences in the same field, and how do status, cross-audience interaction, and audience accountability condition these effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: An artist's reputation for artistic quality will have a stronger relationship with success with museums than with galleries.
- H1b: An artist's reputation for commercial viability will have a stronger relationship with success with galleries than with museums.
- H2: The positive contingent effect of an actor's audience-specific reputation, where the contingency is based on the actor's status, will be stronger for success with museums than for success with galleries.
- H3: High-status museums will discount an artist's reputation due to the artist's interaction with gallery audiences more than high-status galleries discount an artist's reputation due to interaction with museum audiences.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Audience-specific reputation, measured as award winning for artistic quality and magazine-cover appearance for commercial viability.
- DV(s): Artist success with museum audiences and gallery audiences, measured as annual museum or gallery exhibitions.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Prior exhibitions at high-status audience members and prior exhibitions with non-focal audiences. Audience accountability is a theorized audience-level contextual distinction, not a separately measured moderator.

The paper argues that reputation is valuable when it reduces uncertainty for a focal audience whose concerns match the attribute signaled by that reputation. Museums are more accountable to diverse stakeholders and care more about artistic quality, so a reputation for artistic quality is more valuable for museum success and is more strongly reinforced by high-status museum affiliations. Interaction with a non-focal audience can signal lack of fit, especially for high-status museums, reducing the value of an artist's audience-specific reputation.

**Data & Measures**
The observational study uses worldwide exhibition records from ArtFacts.Net for 58,014 newly emerging artists during 2001–2010, comprising 311,839 artist-year observations. Eligible artists had no museum or gallery exhibitions in the database before 2001 and at least one during 2001–2010; they were followed from their first exhibition through 2010 in separate museum and gallery panels. The artists exhibited at 5,131 museums and 7,293 galleries. Success is binary: at least one exhibition with the relevant audience in the current year versus none. Conditional logit estimation uses 116,590 artist-year observations for museum exhibitions and 154,098 for gallery exhibitions (Tables 1–2). For H3, the high-status museum, other museum, high-status gallery, and other gallery models use 10,535, 115,982, 10,386, and 152,903 observations, respectively (Table 3).

The authors report tracking 18 prestigious art awards and covers of 10 prominent art magazines during 1998–2010. Award Won measures a reputation for artistic quality: it equals 1 from the first award-winning year through 2010, and 0 otherwise. Magazine Cover measures a reputation for commercial viability: it equals 1 from the first year the artist's name or artwork appeared on a selected magazine cover through 2010, and 0 otherwise. Both reputation indicators enter the models with a one-year lag.

Status is measured through cumulative exhibitions at high-status members of the focal audience. The 100 museums with the most visitors in 2010, using attendance figures from The Art Newspaper, and the 100 highest-ranked galleries in the 2010 ArtFacts.Net rankings define high-status venues. Separate variables count prior exhibitions at other museums or galleries. Interaction with the non-focal audience is measured as cumulative gallery exhibitions for museum-outcome models and cumulative museum exhibitions for gallery-outcome models. Exhibition-history moderators are log-transformed after adding one, lagged one year, and mean-centered for interactions with reputation. Accountability is the theorized difference between museum and gallery audiences, rather than a separately measured variable. Models include artist and year fixed effects, artist-clustered standard errors, and controls for tenure and the geographical diversity of exhibition histories; cross-model tests use corrected covariances from seemingly unrelated estimations. The design estimates observational associations.

**Key Findings**
- H1a was supported: the award-winning reputation indicator had a stronger association with museum than gallery exhibition success (cross-model Wald χ² = 5.69, p < .05). In the models adding the reputation indicators without interactions, the award coefficient was 5.14 for museums (p < .01; approximately 170-fold odds) and 0.33 for galleries (not significant).
- H1b was not supported: magazine-cover reputation was positively associated with both museum and gallery success, but its effect was not significantly greater for galleries (Wald χ² = 1.03, not significant). The corresponding models without interactions reported coefficients of 1.89 for museums and 1.37 for galleries, both p < .001.
- H2 was supported: high-status affiliations enhanced the effect of audience-specific reputation more strongly for museum success than for gallery success (Wald χ² = 275.70, p < .001). The award-by-high-status-museum interaction was positive and significant; the magazine-cover-by-high-status-gallery interaction was not significant.
- H3 was supported: the negative reputation interaction with non-focal-audience exhibitions was stronger for success at high-status museums than at high-status galleries (Wald χ² = 6.89, p < .01). Prior gallery exhibitions reduced the award-reputation effect for high-status museums (b = −2.65, p < .001), but not for other museums. Prior museum exhibitions did not significantly moderate magazine-cover reputation for high-status galleries; the corresponding interaction for other galleries was only marginally significant (p < .10).
Additional analyses using exhibitions at previously unexhibited venues retained support for H1a, H2, and H3, while H1b remained unsupported. Measuring status through prior exhibitions at all high-status organizations across both audiences also retained support for H2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances reputation theory by showing that reputation effects are audience-specific rather than uniformly actor-level. It also contributes to research on reputation-status interplay and inter-audience spillovers by identifying audience accountability as a boundary condition that shapes whether other signals enhance or discount the value of reputation.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that people working across multiple client or audience groups should not assume that a positive reputation with one audience will transfer equally to another. They may need to build separate reputations around the attributes valued by each audience and attend to whether the focal audience must justify its decisions to diverse stakeholders.

**Limitations**
The paper reports no statistical support for the differential effect of magazine-cover appearances on gallery versus museum success, which the authors attribute to noise in measuring commercial viability. It also examines only two additional signals, status and interaction with other audiences, and focuses on the contemporary art field, limiting generalizability to other settings.

**Future Research**
Future research could use better measures of commercial viability, such as auction appearances or sales ratios, where systematic data are available. It could also examine additional signals or intangible assets and test the framework in other multi-audience settings, including academic careers, biotechnology scientists, and doctor-managers.
