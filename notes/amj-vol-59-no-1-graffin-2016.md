# Ready, AIM, Acquire: Impression Offsetting and Acquisitions

**Abstract**
Drawing on expectancy violation theory, we explore the effects of anticipatory impression management in the context of acquisitions. We introduce impression offsetting, an anticipatory impression management technique organizational leaders employ when they expect a focal event will negatively violate the expectations of external stakeholders. Accordingly, in these situations, organizational leaders will announce the focal event contemporaneously with positive, but unrelated information. We predict impression offsetting will generally occur in the context of acquisitions, but also more frequently for specific acquiring firms and acquisitions that are more likely to lead to an expectancy violation. We also posit that offsetting will effectively inhibit observers' perceptions of events as negative expectancy violations by positively influencing shareholder reactions to acquisition announcements. Consistent with our hypotheses, in a sample of publicly traded acquisition targets, we find evidence for impression offsetting, in which characteristics of both acquirers and their announced acquisitions predict its frequency of use. We also find evidence that impression offsetting is efficacious; on average, it reduces the negative market reaction to acquisition announcements by over 40%, which translates into approximately $246 million in market capitalization.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether acquiring firms use anticipatory impression management to offset the negative expectancy violations associated with acquisition announcements. It also asks whether impression offsetting is more frequent when acquirer or target characteristics make an acquisition more likely to be viewed negatively, and whether offsetting improves shareholder reactions.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Organizational leaders will release unrelated positive information around acquisition announcements more often than predicted by organizations' baseline count of positive announcements.
- H2: Releasing unrelated positive information will be positively associated with abnormal returns around acquisition announcements.
- H3: A firm's recent acquisition activity will be positively associated with releasing unrelated positive information around acquisition announcements.
- H4: High-reputation organizations will be more likely than other organizations to release unrelated positive information around acquisition announcements.
- H5: Target unrelatedness will be positively associated with releasing unrelated positive information around acquisition announcements.
- H6: Unprofitable targets will be more likely than profitable ones to be associated with releasing unrelated positive information around acquisition announcements.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Impression offsetting; prior acquisition activity; high reputation; target unrelatedness; unprofitable target.
- DV(s): Count of positive unrelated press releases around acquisition announcements; cumulative abnormal returns around acquisition announcements.
- Mediators: Not modeled as mediators.
- Moderators: Not modeled as moderators.

The mechanism is that managers anticipate that acquisitions may violate investors' expectations that firms should enhance shareholder value. They therefore release positive but unrelated information contemporaneously with the acquisition announcement, forcing investors to process multiple pieces of news at once and reducing the perceived negativity of the acquisition event. The authors further argue that riskier acquisitions, serial acquirers, and high-reputation acquirers create stronger predictive expectancy violations, increasing the likelihood of offsetting.

**Data & Measures**
The observational sample comprised 770 subsequently completed acquisitions announced during 1995–2009, each valued above $100 million, with both acquirer and target publicly held. Missing data reduced the impression-offsetting regressions to 758 observations (Table 5) and the cumulative-abnormal-return regressions to 701 (Table 4). Acquisition data came from Thomson Reuters SDC Platinum; Compustat supplied acquirer financial data and Risk Metrics supplied board characteristics. The authors collected and analyzed 4,969 event-window press releases, primarily from PR Newswire and Business Wire through Factiva and LexisNexis, supplemented by company websites.

Impression offsetting was the count of positive, material press releases issued by the acquirer during days −1 to +1 around the acquisition announcement; announcements of the focal acquisition were excluded. Clearly positive events were coded directly, earnings releases were classified against consensus expectations, and other ambiguous events required an unambiguously positive characterization. A second coder assessed 100 randomly selected eligible releases, with inter-rater agreement κ = .99. Baseline positive announcements used the same coding process and represented the average three-day count during the three-month period from four months to one month before the event. Prior acquisition activity counted acquisitions meeting the sample criteria in the preceding three years. High reputation was a lagged indicator for membership in the top 25 of either Fortune’s Most Admired or the Wall Street Journal and Harris Interactive corporate reputation rankings. Target unrelatedness indicated different two-digit SIC codes; unprofitable target indicated zero or negative net income in the year before announcement, verified against annual reports.

The cumulative abnormal returns outcome compared stock returns with a value-weighted market model over days −1 to +1; model parameters were estimated over a 250-day window between 295 and 45 days before the acquisition. H1 compared event and baseline counts using a paired t-test, with an additional comparison to a prior study using a two-sample z-test for proportions. H2 used OLS regression and H3–H6 used negative binomial regression, both with robust standard errors and year controls. Impression offsetting was the outcome for H3–H6 and a predictor of returns for H2. The authors also report a two-stage least squares robustness model for H2 using shares outstanding and average top management team total compensation as instruments. Managerial intent was inferred from announcement patterns rather than directly observed.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported: 205 of 770 acquisitions (26.6%) had at least one positive event announcement. The mean event-window count was .5221 versus .0897 at baseline, a 482% increase (paired t = 10.0619, p < .001). Negative announcements decreased relative to baseline, distinguishing the pattern from strategic noise.
- H2 was supported: impression offsetting was positively associated with cumulative abnormal returns (Table 4, Model 2: b = .0029, SE = .0013, p < .05; N = 701). In the Results section's illustration, two positive announcements, the mean among firms using offsetting, corresponded to a change in the mean market reaction from −1.4% to −0.8%; the authors describe this as a 44% reduction and approximately $246 million in market capitalization. The two-stage least squares robustness model yielded substantively unchanged conclusions.
- In Table 5, Model 2 (N = 758), prior acquisition activity was positively associated with impression offsetting (H3: b = .1614, SE = .0388, p < .01), as was high reputation (H4: b = .6466, SE = .2395, p < .01). Target unrelatedness was positive but nonsignificant (H5: b = .0860, SE = .1543), so H5 was not supported. Unprofitable targets were positively associated with offsetting (H6: b = .2836, SE = .1404, p < .05). These archival results support the predicted announcement patterns without directly observing leaders' intentions or investors' perceived expectancy violations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces impression offsetting as a distinct anticipatory impression management tactic, separate from strategic noise and big bath accounting. It extends expectancy violation theory to explain when firms proactively manage stakeholder perceptions before or during potentially negative events. It also contributes by quantifying the market benefits of an impression management tactic in the acquisition context.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that managers can reduce negative market reactions to acquisition announcements by timing positive unrelated disclosures around the focal event. For investors and analysts, the results suggest that press releases near acquisition announcements should be interpreted cautiously because contemporaneous positive news may bias the observed market reaction. Acquisition researchers should also account for confounding positive announcements when estimating market reactions.

**Limitations**
The authors could not directly observe managerial intent, so they infer agency from deviations between event-window press releases and baseline press-release behavior. The study focuses on public-company acquisitions with available press-release and market data. The authors also note that the tactic is inexpensive and effective, but the study does not explain why many firms still do not use it.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine CEO characteristics, reputation, or tenure as predictors of impression offsetting. Researchers could also study positive expectancy violations, the interaction between anticipatory and reactive impression management, and the timing of negative announcements. Additional work could re-examine acquisition market reactions after accounting for systematic positive-news offsets.
