# Magnification and Correction of the Acolyte Effect: Initial Benefits and Ex Post Settling up in NFL Coaching Careers

**Abstract**
What are the long-term consequences of initially beneficial high-reputation workplace ties? Under uncertainty, acolytes (i.e., subordinates with work connections to high-reputation industry leaders) are likely to benefit in terms of signaling fitness for promotion in the external job market. Analysis of promotion outcomes of coaches in the National Football League over 31 years showed that the acolyte effect was reduced for individuals for whom uncertainty was lowest (acolytes with considerable industry experience or high centrality in the co-worker industry network). There was no support for either a knowledge-transfer or an intrinsic-quality explanation for why acolytes initially gained advantage. Rather, evidence supported the idea that ties to high-reputation leaders were somewhat randomly distributed so that acolytes faced ex post settling-up consequences after their promotions: i.e., fewer further promotions or lateral moves, and more demotions. Thus, acolytes initially benefited from loose linkages between their unobservable quality and signals offered by their industry-leader ties, but also suffered as the unreliability of social network signals became evident. The results suggest that a competitive job market may exhibit selfcorrection over time. We offer countervailing theory and evidence to the prevailing view that high-reputation third-party endorsements perpetuate a rich-get-richer social structure that is resistant to performance outcomes.

**Research Question**
What are the long-term career consequences of initially beneficial high-reputation workplace ties? The paper asks which acolytes benefit most from high-reputation leader ties, whether the mechanism is knowledge transfer, intrinsic quality, or unreliable signaling, and whether acolytes continue to outperform after promotion.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Baseline Hypothesis: Among actors with work connections to industry leaders, acolytes connected to high-reputation leaders are more likely to gain promotions than non-acolytes connected to leaders without high reputations.
- H1: Acolytes' likelihood of promotion increases when evaluative certainty is low: when they have low centrality in the industry co-working network, little industry experience, or experience with few organizations.
- H2: Promotion is more likely for acolytes whose expert knowledge matches that of their high-reputation industry leaders than for acolytes without such a match.
- H3a: Acolytes achieve better post-promotion outcomes than non-acolytes.
- H3b: Non-acolytes achieve better post-promotion outcomes than acolytes. H3a and H3b are competing predictions.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Acolyte status, defined as having worked on the same management team as a high-reputation NFL head coach.
- DV(s): Promotion and post-promotion outcome, where post-promotion outcomes distinguish further promotion or lateral movement from demotion.
- Mediators: Not directly tested; the proposed explanatory mechanisms are signaling under uncertainty, perceived knowledge transfer, intrinsic-quality inference, and ex post settling up.
- Moderators: Evaluative certainty, measured through co-worker network centrality, NFL coaching experience, and number of teams worked for. Knowledge match is assessed by comparing acolyte categories, not as an interaction moderator.

The paper argues that acolyte ties operate as social signals when evaluators lack other information about a coach's quality. These ties initially magnify promotion chances, especially for less central and less experienced coaches, but the evidence does not support a knowledge-transfer or intrinsic-quality explanation. Instead, acolyte status appears to be an unreliable signal that later produces settling-up effects as promoted acolytes experience fewer favorable post-promotion outcomes.

**Data & Measures**
The archival study selected 1,298 first-time NFL coaches from 1,565 unique coaches, all male, on start-of-season rosters between 1980 and 2010. Career histories from the annual NFL Record and Fact Book, cross-referenced and supplemented with online league databases and pre-NFL playing/coaching histories, yielded 10,269 coach-years; Table 4 reports this N for the promotion models. Acolyte status was coded 1 after at least one year working with a high-reputation head coach, only when the individual was no longer on that leader's staff; it returned to 0 during any subsequent period on the same staff. Leaders qualified upon reaching at least one milestone: one Super Bowl win, two Super Bowl appearances, five playoff wins, ten playoff appearances, or 100 regular-season wins. Thirty-five of 152 head coaches qualified.

Promotion was a binary move to a higher rank on a five-level hierarchy: head coach, assistant head coach, coordinator, position coach, and assistant position coach or other. Evaluative certainty was measured separately through degree centrality in the annual co-worker network, prior years of NFL coaching experience, and the number of different teams coached before the focal year. A network tie required at least one year working together. Knowledge match compared leaders' and acolytes' accumulated experience in offense, defense, or special teams, yielding mutually exclusive knowledge-match acolyte, no-match acolyte, and non-acolyte categories. Career performance controls used mean schedule-adjusted offensive or defensive unit outcomes from Pro Football Reference, or overall team outcomes when the coach was not associated solely with either unit. Promotion models used random-effects logistic regression and a Heckman two-stage correction for selection into acolyte status; these archival associations do not directly establish the proposed cognitive mechanisms.

Post-promotion analyses traced the next role after a change of team or coaching level. Of 630 promotions, 114 were still ongoing and 83 had no subsequent NFL role by the sample's end. The remaining 433 comprised 96 further promotions, 133 lateral moves, and 204 demotions. Further promotion or lateral movement was coded positive, demotion negative; Table 5 compares 154 acolyte and 279 non-acolyte promotions.

**Key Findings**
The Baseline Hypothesis was supported: in Table 4, Model 2, acolyte status was positively associated with promotion (b = 0.44, p < .01; reported marginal effect = 0.022). The reported promotion probabilities were 7.13% for acolytes and 4.70% for non-acolytes. H1 was supported for all three evaluative-certainty measures: the acolyte interactions were negative for network centrality (b = -0.022), coaching experience (b = -0.07), and team total (b = -0.30), all p < .01. The promotion advantage was therefore greater when less information about the coach was available.

H2 was not supported. Knowledge-match and no-match acolytes both had positive promotion coefficients relative to non-acolytes (b = 0.51 and 0.39, respectively; both p < .01), but their difference was not significant (chi-square = 0.78). H3b was supported and competing H3a was not: 71 of 154 acolyte promotions (46%) led to positive subsequent outcomes, compared with 158 of 279 non-acolyte promotions (57%; chi-square = 4.413, p < .05). The remaining outcomes were demotions. The difference was significant for promotions to historically successful teams (47/94 versus 93/143 positive outcomes; chi-square = 5.303, p < .05), but not for historically unsuccessful teams (24/60 versus 65/136; chi-square = 1.020, nonsignificant).

Supplementary analyses qualified the initial benefit: the association was positive one year after leaving the high-reputation leader's staff (b = 1.41, p < .01) but negative after two or more years (b = -0.32, p < .05). Replacing binary acolyte status with years spent working with the leader did not support the baseline prediction (b = 0.03, nonsignificant), although the three H1 interactions remained supported. The post-promotion difference remained at p < .05 when exits without subsequent NFL roles were counted as negative; excluding lateral moves weakened it to p < .10. The authors interpret the pattern as consistent with fallible signaling and ex post settling up, while acknowledging that archival data cannot directly reveal the underlying cognitive and emotional processes.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends social network and signaling theory by theorizing both the initial benefits and later penalties of high-reputation third-party ties. It challenges rich-get-richer accounts of reputational endorsement by showing that, in a highly competitive labor market, misleading social signals can be corrected over time. It also introduces the acolyte as a theoretical construct for explaining how actors and evaluators simplify complex social-network information in career markets.

**Practical Implication**
The findings raise the possibility that high-reputation leader ties can convey misleading signals of candidate quality. For individuals, ties to prestigious leaders can help open promotion opportunities but may also create inflated expectations and later career risk if the signal is not backed by observable performance.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the NFL is an unusually competitive, interlinked industry with quantitative performance indicators and stochastic season outcomes, which may limit generalizability. They also could not link coaches to specific performance outcomes as directly as sports studies of players can, and the archival design cannot directly observe the cognitive and emotional processes behind evaluator reactions, envy, or halo effects.

**Future Research**
Future research could test acolyte effects in other settings, including academia and entertainment, where high-reputation endorsements may matter but performance is harder to observe. It could also examine alternatives to ex post settling up, such as acolytes resting on their laurels, losing aspiration after proximity to stars ends, or being punished by others' envy. Experimental work could isolate the halo effects and boundary conditions of network endorsements more directly.
