# The Online Shadow of Offline Signals: Which Sellers Get Contacted in Online B2B Marketplaces?

**Abstract**
This article extends the understanding of what impels buyers to contact particular sellers in online business-to-business (B2B) marketplaces, which are typically characterized by sparse social structures and concomitant limitations in observing social cues. Integrating an institutional perspective with signaling theory, our core argument is that offline seller characteristics that are visible online—in particular, geographic location and legal status—convey credible signals of seller behavior because they provide buyers with information on sellers’ local institutional quality and the institutionally induced obligations and controls acting on sellers. Using unique data from a large, Italian, online B2B marketplace between the fourth quarter of 1999 and July 2001, we find that both sellers’ local institutional quality and their legal statuses affect a buyer’s likelihood of contacting a seller. Moreover, consistent with the idea that a buyer’s own local institutional quality generates a relevant reference point against which sellers are evaluated, we find that a buyer is progressively more likely to contact sellers the higher their local institutional quality relative to the buyer. Jointly, our findings imply that, in online B2B marketplaces, signals conveyed by sellers’ geographic locations and legal statuses may constitute substantive sources of competitive heterogeneity and market segmentation.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what impels buyers to contact particular sellers in online B2B marketplaces when social structures are sparse and buyers have limited social cues. It specifically examines whether visible offline seller characteristics, especially geographic location and legal status, shape buyer-seller contact initiation online.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The higher a seller's local institutional quality, the greater the likelihood that a buyer contacts that seller in an online B2B marketplace.
- H2: The stronger the obligations and controls associated with a seller's legal status, the greater the likelihood that a buyer contacts that seller in an online B2B marketplace.
- H3: The higher a seller's local institutional quality relative to a buyer, the greater the likelihood that the buyer contacts that seller in an online B2B marketplace.
- H4: The stronger the obligations and controls associated with a seller's legal status relative to a buyer, the greater the likelihood that the buyer contacts that seller in an online B2B marketplace.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Seller local institutional quality; seller legal status; seller local institutional quality relative to buyer; seller legal status relative to buyer.
- DV(s): Whether a buyer submits a request for quotation to a seller.
- Mediators: Not formally modeled as mediators.
- Moderators: Buyer local institutional quality and buyer legal status as reference points for evaluating seller signals.

The proposed mechanism is that visible offline characteristics signal seller reliability in an anonymous online marketplace. Geographic location signals local institutional quality, especially judicial efficiency and the absence of corruption or organized crime, while legal status signals the strength of institutionally induced obligations and controls. Buyers use these signals to infer trading and value appropriation risks, and the effectiveness of institutional-quality signals depends partly on the buyer's own local institutional quality as a reference point.

**Data & Measures**
The observational study used proprietary activity records from an Italian online B2B marketplace from the fourth quarter of 1999 to July 2001. Of 475 initial quotation requests, 438 had complete information, involving 34 buyers and 301 contacted sellers. Each request was compared with potential contacts involving sellers in the same two-digit industry sector as the contacted seller at that time. The estimation sample comprised 116,417 buyer-seller dyads and 4,557 sellers across nine industry divisions. The dependent variable was 1 if a buyer requested a quotation from a seller and 0 otherwise.

Regional institutional quality was the first principal factor of five indicators (α = .82), shifted so the lowest regional score equaled zero. Three indicators measured judicial efficiency using ISTAT data for 2000: lengths of bankruptcy proceedings and civil judicial examination proceedings at first instance and on appeal, standardized and inverted; provincial bankruptcy scores were averaged within regions. A standardized corruption measure, Golden and Picci's (2005) “G,” captured lack of corruption using late-1990s infrastructure quantities relative to cumulative expenditure. Calderoni's (2011) “MIen” organized-crime rank, based on 11 measures spanning 1983–2009, was standardized, inverted, and averaged from provinces to regions. Scores were assigned to buyers and sellers by home region; relative institutional quality was the seller's score divided by the buyer's.

Seller legal status, visible in company names, was represented by two dummies for limited liability companies and corporations, with sole proprietorships omitted. Relative legal status used four dummies: seller two categories below the buyer, one category below, one category above, or two categories above, with equal status omitted. Rare events logistic regressions included robust standard errors, time, industry, and geographic location fixed effects, and controls for buyer characteristics, regional economic conditions and seller representation, shared characteristics, distance, previous quotation requests, and company and relational heterogeneity. These estimates describe associations with contact initiation.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported: seller institutional quality was positively associated with quotation requests (Table 7, Model 4: b = 1.37, SE = .33, p < .001). A one-standard-deviation increase corresponded to approximately 150% higher contact odds.
- H2 received partial statistical support. Relative to sole proprietorships, limited liability companies (Model 4: b = .55, SE = .19, p < .01) and corporations (b = .68, SE = .30, p < .05) had approximately 73% and 97% higher contact odds, respectively. However, the difference between these two coefficients was not significant (χ²(1) = .33, p = .56), so the predicted progressive increase across all three legal-status categories was not fully supported.
- H3 was supported: relative institutional quality was positively associated with contact (Model 5: b = 3.69, SE = .87, p < .001). A one-standard-deviation increase corresponded to 2.92 times the contact odds, consistent with buyers evaluating sellers against their own institutional-quality reference points.
- H4 was not supported. Only the lowest relative-status category was significant: a corporate buyer contacting a sole proprietorship had approximately 49% lower odds than an equal-status pair (Model 6: b = −.67, SE = .23, p < .01). The other categories were nonsignificant: one category below, b = −.04; one above, b = −.36; two above, b = −.40.
- Additional analyses separated the institutional-quality dimensions. Seller judicial efficiency, both absolute and relative to the buyer, was positively associated with contact; lack of corruption and organized crime showed no significant association in either form. Thus, the composite institutional-quality results appeared driven mostly by judicial efficiency.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates an institutional perspective with signaling theory to show how offline institution-based signals can shape online buyer-seller contact dynamics. It extends signaling research by showing that signal effectiveness can vary across signal recipients because buyers interpret seller institutional quality relative to buyer-specific reference points. It also contributes to research on online market segmentation by showing that sellers can become differentiated before pricing information is disclosed.

**Practical Implication**
For sellers in online B2B marketplaces, offline characteristics that are visible online may affect whether buyers even initiate contact. Sellers located in higher-quality institutional environments or operating under stronger legal-status obligations may enjoy advantages in attracting requests for quotation.

**Limitations**
The authors note that data limitations prevent complete coverage of company size and order types, although supplementary analyses did not suggest seller size confounded the main results. The study focuses on an Italian marketplace from 1999-2001, so formal institutional variation is largely ruled out by design, but this also limits direct evidence about cross-national or state-level formal institutional signals. The relative legal status test is limited by coarse categorical measurement.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how transaction-specific characteristics such as order quantity moderate institution-based signaling effects. Studies could also test formal institutional signals in online B2B markets that cross regional, state, or national borders, and examine other offline seller characteristics such as industry association membership or prominent partner affiliations. The authors also encourage research on how buyers combine multiple signals, including brand, reputation, certification, price, geographic location, and legal status.
