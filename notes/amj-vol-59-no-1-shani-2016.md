# Persona Non Grata? Determinants and Consequences of Social Distancing from Journalists Who Engage in Negative Coverage of Firm Leadership

**Abstract**
We consider how social and psychological connections among CEOs explain the propensity for corporate leaders to distance themselves socially from journalists who engage in negative reporting about firm leadership at other companies, and we examine the consequences for the valence of journalists’ subsequent coverage. Our theoretical framework suggests that journalists who have engaged in negative coverage of a firm’s leadership and strategy are especially likely to experience distancing from other leaders who (a) have friendship ties to the firm’s CEO, (b) are demographically similar to the CEO on salient dimensions, or (c) are socially identified with the CEO as a fellow member of the corporate elite. Our theory and findings ultimately suggest that, due to the multiple sources of social identification between CEOs, journalists who engage in negative coverage of firm leadership tend to experience social distancing from multiple CEOs, and that such distancing has a powerful influence on the valence of journalists’ subsequent reporting about firm leadership and strategy across all the firms they cover. We also extend our theoretical framework to suggest how the effect of social distancing on the valence of journalists’ coverage is moderated by the early and late stages of the journalists’ career.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when CEOs socially distance themselves from journalists who have written negative coverage of other firms' leadership and strategy, and how that distancing affects the valence of journalists' later coverage. It also asks whether journalists' career stage changes their vulnerability to this social influence.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Previous negative statements by a journalist about a firm's leadership or strategy will be positively associated with distancing by CEOs of other firms in the same broadly defined industry to the extent that they are friends of that firm's CEO.
- H2: This positive association will be stronger to the extent that the other firm's CEO is similar to the criticized firm's CEO on salient demographic attributes.
- H3: This positive association will be stronger to the extent that the other firm's CEO socially identifies with the corporate elite.
- H4: Journalists who experience social distancing from CEOs will issue (a) more positive and (b) fewer negative statements about leadership and strategy at the firms they cover.
- H5: The relationship between distancing and more positive/fewer negative statements will be weaker for journalists in the early or late stage of their careers than for journalists in the middle stage.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Journalists' prior negative statements about another firm's leadership or strategy, interacted with friendship ties between CEOs, demographic similarity between CEOs, and focal CEOs' social identification with the corporate elite.
- DV(s): CEO social distancing from journalists; subsequent negative and positive statements by journalists about firm leadership and strategy.
- Mediators: Supplemental analyses indicate that CEOs' negative affect and distrust toward the journalist mediate the social-connection effects on distancing.
- Moderators: Journalists' early, middle, and late career stage moderate the relationship between social distancing and subsequent coverage valence.

The mechanism is that CEOs socially identify with friends, demographically similar CEOs, and fellow members of the corporate elite, so negative coverage of those leaders elicits negative affect and distrust toward the journalist. Distancing withholds access, information, and status from journalists, creating negative reinforcement that encourages more positive and less negative subsequent coverage of firm leadership. The effect is strongest for middle-career journalists, who are theorized to be less protected by early-career idealism or late-career return to professional ideals.

**Data & Measures**
The observational panel combines surveys every six months during 2009–2012 with archival data on large public U.S. companies with more than $200 million in sales and journalists at major U.S. news and business publications. Of 800 focal CEOs in the initial frame, 367 initially participated and were included in the primary analyses; 311 participated throughout. Alter CEOs led firms in the same broadly defined industry, including competitors and current or potential buyers and suppliers. A separate random sample comprised 550 journalists, with an average survey participation rate of 39.8%. Table 2 reports 22,563 observations of focal CEO–journalist–alter CEO triads; Table 3 reports 1,752 observations for journalist coverage models.

Articles were obtained from sources including Factiva and Lexis-Nexis. Three independent coders classified sentences as negative, positive, or neutral and identified statements about firm leadership or strategy. H1–H3 use each journalist's count of negative statements about each alter's firm during the preceding 12 months. H4–H5 use counts of negative and positive statements across covered firms during the subsequent 12 months, controlling for prior reporting. Friendship is coded 1 when the focal CEO identifies the alter as a friend. Demographic similarity combines functional background, race, gender, and age using discrete principal component analysis; age similarity means a difference of less than one standard deviation. Social identification with the corporate elite is a multi-item survey factor score (α = .92).

Social distancing is a multi-item survey measure of willingness to communicate, interact, and spend time with a journalist, availability for interviews, responsiveness, and difficulty making contact (α = .94). For H1–H3, CEOs report distancing during the six months after the independent variables are measured. For H4–H5, journalist responses measure average distancing by CEOs whom the journalist tried to contact during the preceding six months. Early, middle, and late career stage are measured with separate perceptual survey scales (α = .86–.88). Supplemental mediation analyses use multi-item measures of CEO negative affect and distrust toward the journalist. Distancing models use random-effects GLS regression with Heckman selection correction and clustered variance estimates; coverage models use random-effects negative binomial regression. The panel and selection adjustments support associational tests rather than randomized causal identification.

**Key Findings**
H1–H3 were supported: negative reporting was more positively associated with distancing when the focal CEO and alter were friends, demographically similar, or when the focal CEO identified more strongly with the corporate elite. In Table 2, Model 2, the respective interaction coefficients are 0.317, 0.104, and 0.119 (all p < .001). Journalists who issued at least one negative statement about firm leadership or strategy experienced distancing from an average of 5.8 CEOs over the subsequent 12 months. Supplemental analyses supported mediation by negative affect and distrust; all six indirect paths were significant at p < .05.

H4 was supported: distancing was associated with fewer subsequent negative statements and more positive statements across the firms a journalist covered (Table 3, models without career-stage interactions: b = −1.281 and 1.196, respectively; both p < .001). H5 was also supported: early- and late-career interactions weakened both associations, with stronger attenuation in late career. The interactions for late career were 0.545 for negative statements and −0.610 for positive statements (both p < .001); corresponding early-career interactions were 0.271 and −0.342 (both p < .05). Supplemental models found similar significance when examining coverage of firms whose CEOs had not distanced themselves from the journalist.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends corporate governance and strategic leadership research by theorizing social distancing as a collective form of negative social influence over information intermediaries. It shifts attention from individual CEO influence tactics to elite-level patterns in which multiple CEOs' social and psychological connections jointly shape journalists' behavior toward corporate leaders as a group. It also adds a career-stage account of why information intermediaries vary in their susceptibility to elite social influence.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that journalists' access to CEOs can be socially conditioned by prior critical reporting about other leaders, which may reduce the objectivity and negativity of subsequent coverage. For stakeholders who rely on media as a governance mechanism, social distancing can weaken external pressure for needed changes in leadership, governance, and strategy. The authors suggest that greater awareness and publicity around distancing behavior could deter CEOs from using this form of influence.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on large public U.S. companies and journalists at major U.S. news and business publications. The design uses panel surveys and archival content analysis, but the core distancing construct depends on reported willingness and responsiveness in CEO-journalist interactions. The evidence concerns social distancing around coverage of firm leadership and strategy.

**Future Research**
Future research could test whether the theory generalizes to other information intermediaries, such as sell-side security analysts, or to auditor-client relationships. Studies could examine whether similar patterns of distancing arise in other elite communities where group members are affected by a third party's behavior. Further work could also investigate how other journalist attributes shape vulnerability to CEO influence.
