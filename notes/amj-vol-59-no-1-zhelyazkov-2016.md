# After the Break-Up: The Relational and Reputational Consequences of Withdrawals from Venture Capital Syndicates

**Abstract**
Organizational theorists are increasingly interested in the antecedents of terminating interorganizational relationships, but have paid little attention to the disruptive consequences of such terminations on future tie formations. To redress this imbalance, the present study focuses on how venture capital (VC) firms' withdrawals from VC syndicates are associated with their subsequent syndication in the period from 1985 to 2008. We argue that withdrawals disrupt the relationships of the withdrawing VC firms with the coinvestors and reduce the likelihood of them entering into subsequent exchange (relational consequences). Furthermore, public information on the withdrawals can undermine the withdrawing VC firm's reputation for reliability, making it a less desirable exchange partner overall (global reputational consequences). Finally, we find that abandoned coinvestors can spread negative private information about the withdrawing firm, reducing its chances of syndication with their other network contacts (local reputational consequences). We also show that the global and local reputational consequences attenuate each other, due to redundancy in the content of the information each provides. We discuss the implications of our theory for the research on network dynamics and reputation.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how a VC firm's withdrawals from prior syndicates shape its likelihood of forming subsequent syndication ties. It distinguishes relational consequences for abandoned coinvestors, global reputational consequences from public withdrawal histories, and local reputational consequences transmitted through abandoned coinvestors' network contacts.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: A focal VC firm is less likely to syndicate with alters that have previously withdrawn from a greater proportion of their syndicates with the focal firm.
- H2: A focal VC firm is less likely to syndicate with alters that have withdrawn from a larger proportion of their syndicates overall.
- H3: A firm is less likely to syndicate with an alter if it has more connections to the alter's abandoned syndication partners.
- H4: The negative effect of an alter's withdrawal history on syndication with a focal VC firm is stronger when the focal firm has more connections to the alter's abandoned syndication partners.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Direct withdrawals against the focal firm; overall withdrawal rate; focal firm's social overlap with the alter's abandoned coinvestors; focal firm's social overlap with the alter's non-abandoned coinvestors.
- DV(s): Indicator for whether two VC firms jointly invested in a first-round syndicate in the focal year.
- Mediators: Not modeled as mediators.
- Moderators: Social overlap with abandoned coinvestors and non-abandoned coinvestors moderates the effect of overall withdrawal rate.

The mechanism is that withdrawal can make abandoned coinvestors reassess a VC firm's reliability and damage interpersonal and interorganizational relationships, reducing repeat syndication. Public withdrawal histories can signal unreliability to the broader VC market, while abandoned coinvestors can transmit negative private information to their own contacts. Contrary to the authors' amplification hypothesis, global and local negative information attenuate each other, suggesting redundancy in the information available to prospective partners.

**Data & Measures**
The longitudinal archival study uses VentureXpert data on dedicated U.S. VC firms and investments, excluding firms with no investments in the preceding five years. The final sample contains 250,730 dyad-year observations from 1985 to 2008. Eligible pairs invested in the same industry and geographic region during the focal year; unrealized dyads were randomly sampled. Network measures use five-year sliding windows, with 1980–1984 data initializing the 1985 network. Seven formal interviews with VC principals and additional informal interviews informed the research question, mechanisms, measures, and controls.

The dependent variable equals one when two firms jointly invest in their first round in a portfolio company during the focal year; continued coinvestment in subsequent rounds is excluded. Withdrawal means permanent disappearance from the syndicate, excluding temporary skipped rounds, complete replacement of the syndicate, and departures after a successful exit. Direct withdrawal rate divides each firm's withdrawals from syndicates in which the other firm continued by their unique shared syndicates over the preceding five years. Overall withdrawal rate divides each firm's withdrawals by its unique syndicates over the same window. Social overlap uses a Jaccard index of partner sets; separate measures count ties to the alter's abandoned and non-abandoned partners, each divided by the two firms' combined degree centrality minus the corresponding shared-tie count. Node-specific variables are summed across the dyad. Interaction terms multiply mean-centered withdrawal rate and social overlap from each firm's perspective before summing across the dyad.

Rare-events logit models include year fixed effects, controls for network position, prior ties, financial resources, specialization and geographic distance, and standard errors clustered by both firm identifiers and year. The design is associational: withdrawals were not randomly assigned, and the controls and robustness checks cannot exclude all endogeneity.

**Key Findings**
H1 was supported: direct withdrawal rate was negatively associated with subsequent syndication (Table 2, Model 2: b = −0.22, p < .01). For a pair with one prior deal from which one partner withdrew, the reported syndication probability fell approximately 17%. H2 was supported: overall withdrawal rate was negatively associated with syndication (Model 3: b = −0.50, p < .01); a one-standard-deviation increase reduced the reported probability from approximately 2.3% to 2.1%. H3 was supported in the main models: social overlap with abandoned partners was negative (Model 4: b = −1.04, p < .01), whereas overlap with non-abandoned partners was positive (b = 1.93, p < .01).

H4 was not supported; the interaction had the opposite direction. Connections to abandoned partners attenuated the negative association of overall withdrawals with syndication (Model 5: interaction b = 4.94, p < .01). Connections to non-abandoned partners magnified that negative association (interaction b = −5.67, p < .01). The authors interpret the first result as consistent with redundancy between negative public and private information. In reweighted conventional logit models with fixed effects for all 1,812 firms, the abandoned-partner overlap coefficient lost statistical significance, although it remained statistically different from the non-abandoned-partner overlap coefficient. Post hoc analyses found weaker reputational consequences for withdrawals by liquidity-constrained firms, but no moderation by the abandoned syndicate's eventual success or failure.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to network dynamics research by showing that past ties do not always cast a positive shadow over future tie formation; withdrawals can disrupt repeat ties and relationships with structurally proximate actors. It extends reputation research by distinguishing global public reputation from local private reputational information and by showing that social ties shape audience reactions to the same behavioral record. It also provides evidence for network-based ex post punishment of behavior perceived as violating collaboration norms.

**Practical Implication**
For VC firms, withdrawal decisions can carry future syndication costs beyond the abandoned deal, especially through damaged relationships and negative information flows. The findings imply that prospective syndication partners attend not only to technical resources and network position but also to a partner's reliability record. Firms facing liquidity-driven withdrawals may suffer less reputational damage, suggesting that audiences interpret withdrawals partly through attribution processes.

**Limitations**
The authors note that withdrawal decisions are not randomly assigned and may be driven by unobserved factors that also affect future syndication. The archival data do not reveal the circumstances of each withdrawal or how these shaped observers' attributions. The VC setting also involves small, cohesive firms, so private information transmission may be stronger than in large, divisionalized organizations.

**Future Research**
Future research could use qualitative or survey evidence to examine withdrawal circumstances, attribution processes, and relationship dynamics before and after syndicate exits. Additional work could test whether the theory generalizes to strategic alliances, investment banking syndicates, or institutional contexts where collaborators can be forcibly removed rather than voluntarily withdraw. Research could also examine how internal cohesion within organizations affects their ability to transmit reputational information.
