# Integrating the Bright and Dark Sides of OCB: A Daily Investigation of the Benefits and Costs of Helping Others

**Abstract**
Although the general picture in the organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) literature is that OCB has positive consequences for employees and organizations, an emerging stream of work has begun to examine the potential negative consequences of OCB for actors. Drawing from the cognitive-affective processing system framework and conservation of resources theory, we present an integrative model that simultaneously examines the benefits and costs of daily OCB for actors. Utilizing an experience sampling methodology through which 82 employees were surveyed for 10 workdays, we find that daily OCB is associated with positive affect, but it also interferes with perceptions of work goal progress. Positive affect and work goal progress in turn mediate the effects of OCB on daily well-being. Moreover, employees’ trait regulatory focus influences the strength of the daily relationships between OCB and its positive and negative outcomes. We conclude by discussing theoretical and practical implications of our multilevel model.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether daily organizational citizenship behavior is good or bad for the actors who perform it, and more precisely for whom it is beneficial or detrimental and why. It examines whether daily OCB has positive and negative implications for emotional exhaustion, job satisfaction, and affective commitment through two paths: positive affect and work goal progress, each hypothesized to relate to all three well-being indicators, with promotion and prevention focus shaping these daily relationships.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: On a daily basis, engaging in OCB is positively related to positive affect.
- H2: Positive affect mediates the relationship between daily OCB and (a) emotional exhaustion, (b) job satisfaction, and (c) affective commitment; the predicted indirect relationship is negative for exhaustion and positive for satisfaction and commitment.
- H3: On a daily basis, engaging in OCB is negatively related to perceptions of work goal progress.
- H4: Perceptions of work goal progress mediate the relationship between daily OCB and (a) emotional exhaustion, (b) job satisfaction, and (c) affective commitment; the predicted indirect relationship is positive for exhaustion and negative for satisfaction and commitment.
- H5: The daily OCB–positive affect relationship is more positive for individuals high rather than low in promotion focus.
- H6: The daily OCB–work goal progress relationship is more negative for individuals high rather than low in prevention focus.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily interpersonal organizational citizenship behavior.
- DV(s): Daily well-being indicators: emotional exhaustion, job satisfaction, and affective commitment.
- Mediators: Positive affect as a resource-generating mechanism; perceived work goal progress as a resource-consuming mechanism.
- Moderators: Trait promotion focus moderates the daily OCB-positive affect relationship; trait prevention focus moderates the daily OCB-work goal progress relationship.

The mechanism is dual rather than uniformly positive or negative. Drawing on CAPS and COR, the authors argue that helping others can generate psychological resources and positive affect, improving job satisfaction and affective commitment, while also consuming scarce work time and reducing perceived progress toward work goals, thereby increasing exhaustion and lowering satisfaction and commitment. Promotion-focused employees are more attuned to resource gains from OCB, whereas prevention-focused employees are more sensitive to the resource losses and goal-progress costs of OCB.

**Data & Measures**
The observational experience-sampling study recruited employees of a Midwestern university and friends or colleagues reached through forwarded recruitment emails. The final sample contained 82 employees and 748 day-level observations over ten workdays; 65 employees worked at the university, while others worked in organizations such as banks, museums, or local government agencies. Online surveys were hosted by Qualtrics.com. A one-time survey measured trait regulatory focus approximately one week before daily data collection; daily surveys occurred at the workday midpoint (Time 1), late afternoon before leaving work (Time 2), and evening after work (Time 3).

- IV: Daily interpersonal OCB was measured with six self-report items at Time 1 concerning behavior since arriving at work, including trying to help someone at work.
- Mediators: Positive affect was measured at Time 1 with five items about feelings experienced “right now.” Perceived work goal progress was measured at Time 2 with six items about progress since the preceding survey; the same measure at Time 1 was controlled to assess change during the day.
- Outcomes: Emotional exhaustion used five items adapted from the Maslach Burnout Inventory at Time 2. Job satisfaction used four items and affective commitment six items at Time 3, referring to current feelings. These focal daily measures used five-point response scales.
- Moderators and controls: Trait promotion and prevention focus used measures developed by Neubert et al. (2008). Controls included five-item negative affect at Time 1 and seven-item task performance at both Times 1 and 2, alongside initial goal progress.

Multilevel path analysis in Mplus 7.11 modeled days within employees, random slopes for focal daily relationships, and cross-level moderation by regulatory focus. Daily predictors were centered within individuals, and person-level variables were grand-mean centered. Indirect-effect confidence intervals used a parametric bootstrap with 20,000 Monte Carlo replications. Temporal separation and controls address alternative explanations but do not establish causal identification.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported: daily OCB was positively related to positive affect (γ = .36, p < .05), explaining 9% additional within-individual variance in positive affect.
- H2a was not supported: positive affect was not significantly related to emotional exhaustion (γ = −.06, p > .05), and the indirect effect was −.017, 95% CI [−.055, .019]. H2b and H2c were supported: the indirect effects through positive affect were .024 for job satisfaction, 95% CI [.019, .071], and .026 for affective commitment, 95% CI [.005, .051].
- H3 was supported: daily OCB was negatively related to subsequent perceived work goal progress after controlling initial progress (γ = −.06, p < .05), explaining 5% additional within-individual variance.
- H4a–c were supported: the indirect effects through goal progress were .016 for emotional exhaustion, −.009 for job satisfaction, and −.006 for affective commitment; all reported 95% confidence intervals excluded zero. Thus, this pathway linked OCB to greater exhaustion and lower satisfaction and commitment.
- H5 and H6 were supported: promotion focus strengthened the positive OCB–positive affect relationship (γ = .18, p < .05), while prevention focus strengthened the negative OCB–goal progress relationship (γ = −.12, p < .05). The corresponding cross-over moderation tests were not significant.
- Supplemental moderated mediation was selective: promotion focus significantly moderated the indirect relationships through positive affect with job satisfaction and affective commitment, but not emotional exhaustion. Prevention focus significantly moderated the indirect relationships through goal progress with emotional exhaustion and job satisfaction, but not affective commitment, based on the confidence intervals for differences in conditional indirect effects in Table 3.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the OCB literature by integrating the bright-side and dark-side streams in one daily, actor-focused model. It extends COR by modeling resource generation and resource consumption simultaneously in a dynamic within-person design, and extends CAPS by specifying how daily helping episodes activate affective and goal-progress processes. It also positions regulatory focus as a cross-level boundary condition explaining why the same helping behavior can have different well-being implications for different employees.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that encouraging high levels of citizenship behavior may improve mood and commitment but can also drain actors by interfering with goal progress. Organizations that depend on helping should monitor exhaustion, avoid treating OCB as costless, and support practices that replenish resources, such as reflection on the prosocial impact of helping. The findings also suggest that employees' regulatory focus matters: promotion focus may amplify the benefits of OCB, while prevention focus may make the costs more salient.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study relies on self-reports, although they separated measures across time, controlled for related constructs, and centered daily variables within individuals. They also did not disentangle all possible mediators, such as recipient gratitude, pride, contentment, guilt, or recipient indifference, and the lagged next-day analyses were underpowered. The study examined interpersonally focused OCB only, with relatively mild helping items, and did not test whether organizationally focused or change-focused OCB would operate similarly.

**Future Research**
Future research could collect observer ratings of daily OCB and use designs better able to capture next-day spirals in OCB, well-being, and subsequent helping. The authors call for work on specific OCB event features, including complexity, novelty, routine character, and time or resource intensity. They also encourage research on additional individual boundary conditions, broader well-being outcomes such as physiological and behavioral consequences, and downstream effects such as home helping, workplace reciprocation, or deviant behavior after OCB.
