# Perceptions of Employee Volunteering: Is It “Credited” or “Stigmatized” by Colleagues?

**Abstract**
As research begins to accumulate on employee volunteering, it appears that this behavior is largely beneficial to employee performance and commitment. It is less clear, however, how employee volunteering is perceived by others in the workplace. Do colleagues award volunteering “credit” (e.g., associating it with being concerned about others) or do they “stigmatize” it (e.g., associating it with being distracted from work)? Moreover, do those evaluations predict how colleagues actually treat employees who volunteer more often? Adopting a reputation perspective, we draw from theories of person perception and attribution to explore these research questions. The results of a field study reveal that colleagues gave credit to employee volunteering when they attributed it to intrinsic reasons and stigmatized employee volunteering when they attributed it to impression management reasons. Ultimately, through the awarded credits, volunteering was rewarded by supervisors (with the allocation of more resources) and coworkers (with the provision of more helping behavior) when it was attributed to intrinsic motives—a relationship that was amplified when stigmas were low and mitigated when stigmas were high. The results of a laboratory experiment further confirmed that volunteering was both credited and stigmatized, distinguishing it from citizenship behavior, which was credited but not stigmatized.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how colleagues perceive employee volunteering and whether those perceptions affect how supervisors and coworkers treat employees who volunteer more often. It focuses on whether volunteering is credited, stigmatized, or both, and on how attributed motives shape those reputational evaluations.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Intrinsic attributions moderate the volunteering–credits relationship, making it more positive when intrinsic attributions are high rather than low.
- H2: Impression management attributions moderate the volunteering–stigmas relationship, making it more positive when impression management attributions are high rather than low.
- H3: The positive indirect relationship between volunteering and supportive reactions through credits is more positive when intrinsic attributions are high rather than low.
- H4: The negative indirect relationship between volunteering and supportive reactions through stigmas is more negative when impression management attributions are high rather than low.
- H5a: When intrinsic attributions are high, the positive indirect relationship between volunteering and supportive reactions through credits is weaker when stigmas are high and stronger when stigmas are low.
- H5b: When impression management attributions are high, the negative indirect relationship between volunteering and supportive reactions through stigmas is weaker when credits are high and stronger when credits are low.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employee volunteering frequency; experimentally manipulated high versus low volunteering in Study 2.
- DV(s): Volunteering credits, volunteering stigmas, and supportive reactions from colleagues, including coworker helping and supervisor resource allocation.
- Mediators: Volunteering reputation, separated into credits and stigmas.
- Moderators: Intrinsic attributions moderate the volunteering–credits relationship, and impression management attributions moderate the volunteering–stigmas relationship. The paper also hypothesizes that stigmas moderate the positive indirect effect through credits and credits moderate the negative indirect effect through stigmas.

The proposed mechanism is reputational. Colleagues observe employee volunteering, infer motives, and translate those inferences into positive credits or negative stigmas. Intrinsic attributions amplify the positive volunteering-credit relationship, while impression management attributions amplify stigmatization. Credits then increase supportive reactions, but their benefits are weaker when stigmas are also high.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 was an associational field survey of 260 full-time employee volunteers recruited through Internet classified advertisements, each paired with a colleague: 120 supervisors and 140 coworkers. Employees reported volunteering frequency on a five-item scale ranging from almost never to very often. Four weeks later, colleagues reported credits, stigmas, attributed motives, and supportive reactions. Credits comprised time management, other focus, sense of community, and ethical values; stigmas comprised distraction, evangelism, void filling, and self-righteousness. Each dimension had four items, rated from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Colleagues rated intrinsic and impression management attributions with four items each. Supportive reactions combined standardized coworker helping scores (six items) and supervisor resource-allocation prioritization scores (four items), with report source controlled. Colleague-rated job performance (five items) and employee-reported prosocial identity (three items) were additional controls. Structural equation modeling and bias-corrected bootstrapping tested the conditional indirect relationships.

The credits and stigmas scales were developed through an open-ended questionnaire with 31 executive MBA students, all full-time employees, and content validation with 492 undergraduate business students. Eight themes were retained from the qualitative responses; six candidate items per theme were reduced to four using ratings of correspondence with the intended versus other definitions.

Study 2 recruited students from an introductory business management class: 366 completed the initial survey and 305 completed the second survey two weeks later. Participants were randomly assigned to evaluate one of 16 fictitious student profiles in two fully crossed 2 × 2 designs. Profiles manipulated high versus low volunteering, high versus low citizenship behavior, and intrinsic versus impression management motives for each behavior. High and low behavior conditions described scores above the 80th versus below the 20th percentile; motive conditions described enjoyment versus seeking to impress others. Participants evaluated credits and stigmas using the Study 1 scales. Between-subjects ANOVAs tested H1 and H2 and compared volunteering with citizenship behavior; the experiment did not test the supportive-reaction mediation hypotheses.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported H1: the volunteering–credits slope was positive when intrinsic attributions were high (b = .13, p < .05), but nonsignificant when low (b = −.09). H2 was also supported: the volunteering–stigmas slope was positive when impression management attributions were high (b = .18, p < .05), but nonsignificant when low (b = −.07). Post hoc tests found the latter interaction for void filling and self-righteousness, but not distraction or evangelism.
- Study 1 supported H3: Table 3 reports an indirect relationship through credits of .06 (p < .05) at high intrinsic attributions and −.04 (nonsignificant) at low intrinsic attributions. H4 was unsupported because the stigmas–supportive reactions path was nonsignificant. H5a was supported: with high intrinsic attributions, the indirect relationship through credits was .03 at high stigmas and .09 at low stigmas (both p < .05, Table 3). H5b was unsupported; the conditional indirect relationships through stigmas were nonsignificant.
- Study 2 supported H1: volunteering increased credits, with a significant interaction indicating a stronger effect under intrinsic rather than impression management attributions (F[1, 302] = 4.87). Table 4 reports credits of 3.59 versus 3.02 for high versus low volunteering under intrinsic attributions, and 3.14 versus 2.76 under impression management attributions. Volunteering also increased stigmas (high M = 2.90; low M = 2.63), but its interaction with attributed motives was nonsignificant (F[1, 304] = .50), so H2 was not supported in this study. Citizenship behavior increased credits, particularly under intrinsic attributions, but did not significantly increase stigmas. Volunteering and citizenship behavior interacted positively for credits, but citizenship behavior did not mitigate volunteering-related stigmas.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to employee volunteering research by shifting attention from the volunteer's attitudes and behavior to colleagues' interpretations and treatment of the volunteer. It also extends reputation research by treating reputation as attribute-specific and potentially ambivalent: the same non-work behavior can produce both credits and stigmas depending on perceived motives.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that employee volunteering can affect interpersonal treatment at work, not only volunteer wellbeing or organizational image. Volunteer programs may be more beneficial when employees' participation is perceived as intrinsically motivated and less beneficial when it appears impression-management oriented, because stigma can weaken the rewards associated with positive credits.

**Limitations**
The field study contains some same-source, same-time colleague reports for credits, stigmas, attributions, and supportive reactions, leaving possible common method and causal-order concerns despite temporal and source separation where possible. The paper studies volunteering frequency rather than the initial decision to volunteer, persistence of volunteering, specific volunteer causes, or the timing of volunteering during versus outside work hours.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how different company supports for volunteering shape colleague attributions and whether formal volunteer programs reduce or intensify reputational evaluations. It could also study how intrinsic and impression-management attributions coexist, how specific volunteer causes or schedules affect stigma, and whether volunteering direction and persistence produce different workplace reactions than volunteering frequency.
