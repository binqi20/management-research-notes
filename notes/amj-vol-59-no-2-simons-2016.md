# There’s No Beer without a Smoke: Community Cohesion and Neighboring Communities’ Effects on Organizational Resistance to Antismoking Regulations in the Dutch Hospitality Industry

**Abstract**
This study highlights the importance of communities in explaining organizational resistance to institutional pressures. Examining the active resistance of small bars to smoking regulations in 427 Dutch municipalities (communities), we argue that the likelihood of organizational resistance to institutional pressure from a powerful actor is affected by the social cohesion of the focal community. In addition, we propose a contiguity effect that emphasizes the broader social context of the community—its neighboring communities—as a source for support or information about appropriate ways to resist such pressures. By incorporating community attributes to account for organizations’ heterogeneous responses to institutional pressure, the study advances current institutional scholarship and demonstrates empirically how such a theory can help explain the success of relatively weak organizational actors’ resistance in the face of strong institutional pressures by the state—that is, as a result of their embeddedness in a community.

**Research Question**
How do community cohesion and resistance in neighboring communities shape small bars’ active resistance to institutional pressure from a powerful state actor? The paper asks why resistance to the Dutch smoking ban varied across municipalities even though small bars faced the same national regulation.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Communities with greater residential stability will have more bars resisting the smoking ban than communities with lower residential stability.
- H2: Communities with higher kinship will have more bars resisting the smoking ban than communities with lower kinship.
- H3: Communities sharing borders with communities exhibiting high resistance will have more bars resisting the smoking ban than communities bordering communities with low resistance.
- H4: The effect of resistance in neighboring communities is weaker in communities with greater residential stability.
- H5: The effect of resistance in neighboring communities is weaker in communities with higher kinship.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Community residential stability, community kinship, and resistance in neighboring communities.
- DV(s): Number of bars in a municipality resisting the smoking ban, measured through SSB foundation membership.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Residential stability and kinship weaken the effect of neighboring communities’ resistance.

The mechanism links community embeddedness to organizational resistance. Residential stability creates a latent relational infrastructure that can be mobilized when the smoking ban threatens valued local bars, while kinship generates belonging, mutual obligation, and willingness to defend the community’s social function. Resistance in neighboring communities supplies signals, information, and coordination opportunities, but cohesive communities have less permeable boundaries and are therefore less influenced by neighboring resistance.

**Data & Measures**
The qualitative component used 563 Dutch newspaper articles from 2008, retrieved through Lexis/Nexis after removing similar articles and accounts of other countries, and 19 telephone interviews with small bar owners conducted and transcribed in 2012. Quotes were sorted into latent social ties, kinship, willingness to act, and collaboration across communities; resistance actions were grouped as symbolic actions, monetary actions, and actions against inspections.

The quantitative analyses covered 427 municipalities, using the 2010 municipal classification and excluding the four largest urban municipalities. Resistance was the number of pubs and bars in each municipality appearing in the SSB foundation membership files at the end of 2008. This measure correlated .83 with the number of fines for smoking-ban violations in 2008–2009, based on VWA data. Residential stability averaged the standardized, inverted shares of residents entering and leaving a municipality through domestic residential moves in 2007, using Statistics Netherlands data. Kinship used nine items from the 2008 National Housing Survey, whose sample contained 78,071 respondents: seven-point Likert responses were averaged within individuals (α = .83), then across respondents within each municipality. Neighboring resistance was represented by a distance-weighted spatial lag of resistance; interactions with residential stability and kinship tested H4 and H5.

The observational design estimated negative binomial spatial-lag models, with all independent and control variables log-transformed and robust standard errors clustered by province. Controls covered the number of pubs and bars, population density, socioeconomic development, local anti-smoking activism, political opposition to the ban, local competition, and local demand from smokers. Reported coefficients are average elasticities across observed values of the other variables. Temporal lags and qualitative evidence support the interpretation, but the design does not rule out omitted common causes of cohesion and resistance.

**Key Findings**
- H1–H3 were supported in Table 6, Model 2: residential stability, kinship, and resistance in neighboring communities were positively associated with resistance, with reported average elasticities of 0.484, 1.901, and 0.520, respectively (all p < .05). Thus, a 1% increase in each predictor corresponded to a 0.484%, 1.901%, or 0.520% increase in resistance, respectively.
- H4 and H5 were supported: neighboring resistance had weaker effects in more cohesive communities. The full model's interactions were negative for residential stability (−1.345, p < .01) and kinship (−0.258, p < .01). Lower cohesion increased susceptibility to neighboring communities whether their resistance was high or low; when neighboring resistance was low, lower focal cohesion also reduced predicted resistance.
- Robustness tests retained the focal coefficient signs. In the specification using the share of bars resisting, kinship and its interaction with neighboring resistance were significant only at p < .10 (Table 7, Model 7), whereas the corresponding full-model results were significant at p < .01.
- Qualitative evidence complemented the statistical associations: patrons supported bars and donated toward fines, owners and customers described family-like bonds, and owners coordinated resistance and warned colleagues in neighboring communities about inspections. The evidence linked community ties and kinship with willingness to act and identified symbolic, monetary, and inspection-related forms of resistance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional theory by showing that community-level social cohesion helps explain heterogeneous organizational resistance to the same institutional pressure. It brings community back into the study of institutional processes by showing that resistance is socially embedded rather than only a narrow strategic response by individual organizations. It also develops a spatial account of resistance by showing that neighboring communities matter, but that their influence depends on the focal community’s cohesion.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that managers should attend to local community characteristics when anticipating organizational responses to new rules. Even small and resource-constrained organizations may resist powerful actors when they serve an important community function and can draw on cohesive local support.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the theory mainly applies to small and relatively powerless organizations facing pressure from a powerful and legitimate actor. They also note that the arguments are most applicable when the institutional pressure threatens the normative framework of the community. Although the time structure and qualitative evidence make reverse causality unlikely, the study cannot fully rule out omitted historical community factors that influence both cohesion and resistance.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine longer time periods to capture the dynamics of organizational resistance to regulatory pressures at the community level. The authors also call for more fine-grained measures of local social endowments, including histories of community activism, and for studies of other communities such as virtual or open-source communities that share collective identity and may face powerful adversaries.
