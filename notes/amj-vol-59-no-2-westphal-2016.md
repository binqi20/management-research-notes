# Psyched-up to Suck-up: Self-regulated Cognition, Interpersonal Influence, and Recommendations for Board Appointments in the Corporate Elite

**Abstract**
This study reveals how self-regulated cognition in advance of social interaction can resolve the so-called “ingratiator’s dilemma,” which is that the most attractive targets of social influence—those who have relatively high social status—tend to be the most difficult to ingratiate successfully. Our theoretical argument suggests that in anticipation of social interaction with a relatively high-status colleague, corporate directors tend to reflect on characteristics shared with that colleague, while avoiding thoughts about characteristics not shared. We explain how this pattern of cognition induces genuine positive feelings and admiration for the colleague, such that their interpersonal behaviors are less likely to appear as insincere attempts to curry favor, and thus more likely to engender influence. We further suggest that this pattern of cognition is especially valuable when interacting with relatively high-status colleagues who are demographically dissimilar from the focal actor. Finally, our theoretical framework indicates how this self-regulated cognition facilitates advancement in the corporate elite by positively moderating the effects of one’s interpersonal behavior on the likelihood that colleagues will recommend him or her for a board appointment. We test our hypotheses with a unique dataset that includes survey data from a large sample of directors collected before and after meetings.

**Research Question**
How can corporate directors overcome the ingratiator's dilemma when trying to influence relatively high-status colleagues? The paper asks whether anticipating interaction with a high-status alter leads directors to reflect on shared characteristics and avoid reflection on nonshared characteristics, and whether this self-regulated cognition makes expressions of liking and admiration more effective in securing board-appointment recommendations.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: A focal director anticipating social interaction with another director is more likely to reflect on personal and social characteristics held in common, and less likely to reflect on characteristics not shared, to the extent that the other director has relatively high social status in the corporate elite.
- H2: This pattern of anticipatory cognition is stronger when the other director has relatively high social status and is demographically dissimilar from the focal director.
- H3: Expressions of liking and admiration interact with the focal director's self-regulated cognition to increase the likelihood that the other director recommends the focal director for a board appointment; this relationship is especially strong when the other director has relatively high social status and is demographically dissimilar.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Anticipation of social interaction with a relatively high-status alter; reflection on characteristics held in common with the alter; low reflection on characteristics not shared with the alter; expressions of liking and admiration.
- DV(s): Director cognitions and whether the alter recommends the focal director for a board appointment.
- Mediators: Perceived liking and admiration by the alter, tested in supplemental mediation analyses.
- Moderators: Alter's relative social status and demographic dissimilarity between focal director and alter.

The mechanism is that anticipating interaction with a high-status colleague prompts directors to focus cognitively on shared personal and social characteristics while avoiding nonshared characteristics. This pattern induces more genuine positive affect and admiration, making later expressions of liking and admiration appear less like strategic flattery. The influence benefit is especially pronounced when the target is both high-status and demographically dissimilar, because such targets are attractive sources of opportunity but also more vigilant for insincere ingratiation.

**Data & Measures**
The observational study combined longitudinal surveys with archival director data. The population comprised directors of large and mid-sized public U.S. companies with more than $100 million in sales, listed in Reference USA in 2005. There were 434 participating directors and 374 complete dyads. A questionnaire pretest involved 27 current or former directors. During a six-month observation period, directors reported cognitions at five points around each anticipated meeting: the preceding day, the morning of the meeting, immediately before, shortly after, and the following day. The cognition analyses used 4,002 observations and negative binomial random-effects models with clustered robust variance estimates. Recommendation analyses used logit models for 248 complete dyads in which the alter served on another board's nominating committee; the high-status, demographically dissimilar subsample comprised 108 dyads. These are associational analyses.

- Self-regulated cognition: Counts of occasions on which the focal director reflected on characteristics shared or not shared with the alter, based on reported thoughts and subsequent reports of whether the focal director possessed each characteristic. For recommendation models, premeeting counts were accumulated from the preceding day through the meeting and averaged across interactions; the nonshared-characteristic measure represented low reflection.
- Expressions of liking and admiration: Separate multi-item scales, measured primarily from the alter's postmeeting reports of the focal director's smiling, verbal liking, compliments, and expressions of respect or admiration. Scale reliabilities were .89 and .93, respectively; factor scores were averaged across meetings for recommendation models.
- Anticipation of interaction: Multiple survey items about thoughts concerning an upcoming interaction, combined with inverted minutes until the next meeting. Relative social status: A factor based on corporate board appointments, nonprofit board seats, elite education, and being CEO of a Fortune 500 firm, calculated as the alter's status minus the focal director's status. Demographic dissimilarity: A principal-component index of differences in functional background, age, gender, ethnicity, and primary industry of employment.
- Board recommendation: A binary indicator that the alter recommended the focal director for an appointment where the alter served on the nominating committee, using reports by participating directors and fellow committee members. Participating directors reported recommendations three months after the committee meeting; inter-rater agreement was 96%. Supplemental mediation measures distinguished the focal director's own liking and admiration from the alter's perception that the focal director liked and admired them, using multi-item survey scales. A separate sample of 112 director dyads was surveyed only once before or after a meeting to check potential survey priming.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported: Anticipation of interaction interacted positively with the alter's relative status in predicting reflection on shared characteristics (b = .043, p < .001) and negatively in predicting reflection on nonshared characteristics (b = -.046, p < .001; Table 2, models 2 and 5).
- H2 was supported: The three-way interaction of anticipation, relative status, and demographic dissimilarity was positive for reflection on shared characteristics (b = .029, p < .001) and negative for reflection on nonshared characteristics (b = -.031, p < .001; Table 2, models 3 and 6). The table uses one-tailed tests for hypothesized effects.
- H3 was supported: In the full recommendation sample, liking interacted positively with shared-characteristic reflection (b = .412, p < .05) and low nonshared-characteristic reflection (b = .424, p < .01); the corresponding admiration interactions were .277 (p < .01) and .397 (p < .05). All four interactions were stronger for high-status, dissimilar alters (b = .952, 1.112, .751, and .937, respectively; all p < .001; Table 3), with each between-sample difference significant at p < .001. In this subsample, a director one standard deviation above the mean on all four interaction terms was, on average, 2.9 times as likely to receive a recommendation as a director at their mean levels.
- Supplemental analyses supported mediation through the alter's perceived liking and admiration; results were unchanged in the separate 112-dyad sample. Exploratory analyses of 101 complete dyads with a CEO alter and available archival data found no significant moderation by the alter's narcissism.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to social influence research by identifying a cognitive route through which actors can resolve the ingratiator's dilemma rather than merely using more subtle influence tactics. It extends self-regulation research by theorizing self-regulated social cognition: cognition about another person that regulates feelings or attitudes before interaction. It also contributes to corporate governance research by showing how micro-level interpersonal psychology helps directors convert elite ties into recommendations for board appointments.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that directors' interpersonal influence depends not only on what they say or do in meetings but also on how they prepare cognitively before interacting with powerful colleagues. For leaders in demographically diverse elite settings, reflecting on shared characteristics may increase sincere liking and reduce the risk that compliments or admiration are read as opportunistic. The study also suggests that influence behaviors can shape access to board seats and other elite opportunities beyond formal governance performance.

**Limitations**
The paper does not present a formal limitations section, but the empirical setting bounds the claims to corporate directors at large and mid-sized public U.S. firms. The design relies heavily on survey reports around director interactions, although the authors use inter-rater checks, archival controls, selection models, and robustness analyses. The theory focuses on self-regulated cognition about shared personal and social characteristics, leaving other forms of anticipatory cognition less directly tested.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on whether the mechanism generalizes to firm leaders beyond corporate directors, lower-level managers, and professionals outside the corporate sphere. Future work could examine whether influence-target characteristics such as personality traits moderate the effects of self-regulated cognition. The paper also suggests studying other kinds of anticipatory cognition, such as reflection on organizational characteristics or strategy, before interactions with important constituents.
