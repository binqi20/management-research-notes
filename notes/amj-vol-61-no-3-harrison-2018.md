# Saving Face: How Exit in Response to Negative Press and Star Analyst Downgrades Reflects Reputation Maintenance by Directors

**Abstract**
This paper explores the extrinsic and intrinsic motivations driving individual-level responses to reputational threats in the context of the director labor market. Integrating work on reputation with self-determination and identity theories, we theorize that negative attention from the media and star equity analysts threatens directors’ reputations, motivating proactive behavior to mitigate both the external and internal consequences of reputation damage. Using a sample of directors of S&P 1500 firms between 2003 and 2014, we argue and find that negative media coverage and downgrades by star equity analysts are positively related to director exit, even after controlling for firm performance, overall media visibility, and negative events such as lawsuits and financial restatements. We also find that director status intensifies the effect of negative media coverage on exit, serving as the board chair attenuates the effect of star analyst downgrades on exit, and director tenure intensifies the effects of both negative media coverage and star downgrades on exit. In post-hoc testing, we provide further evidence of director reputation maintenance by demonstrating the counterintuitive finding that negative attention from the media and star analysts also increases directors’ likelihood of joining the boards of other S&P 1500 firms.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how prominent individual directors respond when negative external attention toward the firms whose boards they serve on threatens their reputations. It specifically examines whether negative media coverage and star equity analyst downgrades increase director exit, and whether director status, board chair position, and director tenure condition those effects.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Negative media coverage is positively related to the likelihood of director exit from the firm.
- H2: Stock downgrades by star analysts are positively related to the likelihood of director exit from the firm.
- H3a: Higher director status increases the likelihood of director exit in response to negative media attention.
- H3b: Higher director status increases the likelihood of director exit in response to star downgrades.
- H4a: Serving as board chair decreases the likelihood of director exit in response to negative media coverage.
- H4b: Serving as board chair decreases the likelihood of director exit in response to star downgrades.
- H5a: Director tenure increases the likelihood of director exit in response to negative media coverage.
- H5b: Director tenure increases the likelihood of director exit in response to star downgrades.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Negative media sentiment; star analyst downgrades.
- DV(s): Director exit from the focal firm within a three-year window.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Director status; board chair position; director tenure.

The mechanism is that negative external attention toward a firm threatens directors' reputations and activates both extrinsic motives to avoid external stigma and intrinsic motives to protect identity, competence, fulfillment, and identification with board service. Directors are therefore more likely to exit boards associated with negative media sentiment or star analyst downgrades. The primary models support positive main effects of both forms of negative attention, but moderator support is mixed: H3a is marginal, H4b and H5a–H5b are supported, and H3b and H4a are not. Supplemental models consistently support the main effects and the star-downgrade moderators, whereas status by media is nonsignificant in all supplemental models and tenure by media is nonsignificant in the multilevel models. Post hoc analyses suggest that directors may also seek additional S&P 1500 board appointments as a reputation-maintenance response.

**Data & Measures**
The archival director-firm-year data combine ISS and BoardEx records for director exit, board membership, and demographics; TRMI media sentiment; IBES analyst recommendations; Institutional Investor awards for analyst star status; and Compustat firm data. Director exit is a binary indicator of leaving the focal firm within three years. Negative media sentiment is the reverse-scored negative portion of TRMI's industry-adjusted daily sentiment average; star analyst downgrades are the proportion of downgrades among all recommendation revisions by prior-year star analysts. Director status sums standardized indicators of an elite degree, an S&P 500 executive role at vice president or above, and other S&P 500 board seats; board chair is binary; tenure is years served on the focal board. This is an observational, associational design estimated with firm-stratified Cox proportional hazard models and director-clustered robust standard errors. Post-hoc random-effects logit models, with a Heckman correction, predict new S&P 1500 appointments within one, two, and three years.

**Key Findings**
H1 and H2 were supported: negative media sentiment (2.69, p < .001) and star analyst downgrades (0.40, p < .001) positively predicted director exit. H3a received marginal support (0.19, p < .10), while H3b was not supported. H4a was not supported; H4b was supported, with board chair attenuating the star-downgrade effect (-0.43, p < .01). H5a and H5b were supported: tenure intensified the effects of negative media sentiment (0.07, p < .01) and star downgrades (0.02, p < .001). Supplemental models consistently supported the two main effects and the star-downgrade moderators, but the media interactions were less robust: status by media was nonsignificant in all supplemental models, and tenure by media was nonsignificant in the multilevel models. In post-hoc tests, negative media attention predicted additional board appointments for exiting directors, whereas star downgrades did so only marginally within three years; among directors who did not exit, both forms of negative attention predicted new appointments.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends reputation research by shifting reputation maintenance from mostly organizational responses to individual-level responses by prominent actors. It integrates reputation theory with self-determination and identity theories to argue that reputational threats can create simultaneous extrinsic and intrinsic motivations for action. It also contributes to corporate governance research by challenging the view that director exit is mainly involuntary settling up and by showing how external market actors shape director labor-market behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The findings show that negative media sentiment and star analyst downgrades were associated with director exit even after controls for firm performance, media visibility, lawsuits, and financial restatements. Post hoc analyses further indicate that negative attention sometimes preceded new S&P 1500 board appointments. External evaluations can therefore affect director labor-market behavior beyond firm performance or crisis events.

**Limitations**
The authors state that, as with much board research, the study is inferential and uses secondary data, so other motivations beyond reputation or identity may influence director exit and new appointments. They note that directors could leave because negative attention creates additional time demands, or could receive new appointments because other firms target or poach them. They also caution that the context of directors in large corporations may be somewhat unique and may limit generalizability to other prominent individuals.

**Future Research**
The authors call for primary-data or qualitative research that can measure reputation and director identification more directly. They encourage work in other contexts to develop theory on individual-level reputation maintenance and the possible actions individuals use to protect reputations. They also suggest further theory and analysis on additional board appointments, promotions at directors' home firms, and whether internal prestige or positive external attention increases positive outcomes.
