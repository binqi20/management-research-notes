# To Delegate or Not to Delegate: Gender Differences in Affective Associations and Behavioral Responses to Delegation

**Abstract**
Effectively delegating work to others is considered critical to managerial success, as it frees up managers’ time and develops subordinates’ skills. We propose that female leaders are less likely than male leaders to capitalize on these benefits of delegating. Although delegation has communal (e.g., relational) and agentic (e.g., assertive) properties, we argue that female leaders, as compared to male leaders, find it more difficult to delegate tasks due to gender-role incongruence. In five studies, we draw upon social role and backlash theories to show that women imbue delegation with more agentic traits, have more negative associations with delegating, and feel greater guilt about delegating than men. These associations result in women delegating less than men and, when they do delegate, having lower-quality interactions with subordinates. We further show that reframing delegation as communal attenuates women’s negative associations with delegation. These findings reveal that even when a given behavior has both agentic and communal elements, perceptions of agency can undermine women’s engagement in them. However, emphasizing the communal nature of seemingly agentic acts may encourage women’s engagement in such critical leadership behaviors. These findings have theoretical and practical implications for research on gender differences and leadership behavior in the workplace.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether women and men differ in how they perceive, emotionally associate with, and behaviorally enact delegation. It specifically tests whether delegation’s agentic and communal properties create gender-role incongruence for women, leading to more negative affective associations, less delegation, lower-quality subordinate interactions, and whether communal framing can attenuate these responses.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Women will associate the act of delegating (in general) with more agentic traits (e.g., aggressiveness, power) compared to men.
- Hypothesis 2a: Women, as compared to men, will have more negative affective associations when anticipating or engaging in the act of delegation.
- Hypothesis 2b: Women, as compared to men, will have the specific negative affective association of guilt when anticipating or engaging in the act of delegation.
- Hypothesis 2c: Women, as compared to men, will have the specific negative affective association of fear (of backlash) when anticipating or engaging in the act of delegation.
- Hypothesis 3a: Women will be less likely to delegate as compared to men.
- Hypothesis 3b: Men’s and women’s differential affective associations with delegation will mediate their likelihood of delegating.
- Hypothesis 3c: Women’s interactions with subordinates when delegating will be perceived by both subordinates and independent observers as being poorer in quality compared to men’s behavior when delegating.
- Hypothesis 3d: Delegators will outperform nondelegators in the tasks they have been given to complete.
- Hypothesis 4: Framing delegation in a manner that emphasizes its communal properties will attenuate women’s negative associations with delegation.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader or participant gender; delegation condition versus no-delegation condition; delegation-as-communal framing versus control framing.
- DV(s): Perceived agency and communality of delegation, negative and positive affective associations, guilt, fear of backlash, delegation choice and amount, interaction quality with subordinates, and task performance.
- Mediators: Negative affect and positive affect in Study 4 mediation tests; guilt and fear of backlash were theorized mechanisms but were measured after delegation and not tested as mediators there.
- Moderators: Communal framing of delegation in Study 5; subordinate gender and confederate gender were manipulated or controlled but were not the focal theoretical moderators.

The mechanism is that delegation is both communal and agentic, but the agentic element is more salient and costly for women because social role and backlash expectations penalize women’s agency more than they reward women’s communality. This produces more negative affective associations with delegation, including anxiety, guilt, and fear of backlash. Those associations can reduce women’s willingness to delegate and degrade the quality of interactions when women do delegate. Reframing delegation as a way to help subordinates develop makes the communal element more salient and reduces women’s negative affective responses.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (survey of MBA students at a large, private East Coast university; 104 recruited, 7 excluded for having no delegation experience, final sample 97: 63 men, 34 women, average age 28.05): participants were asked how much they associate a list of adjectives with delegating, on scales ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). Following Koenig and Eagly (2014) and Diekman and Eagly (2000), agency was measured with four adjectives — powerful, confident, aggressive, controlling (α = .51) — and communality with four adjectives — other-oriented, supportive, developmental, considerate (α = .79). The paper reports the agency alpha separately for U.S. students (n = 31; α = .69) and international students (n = 66; α = .35).
- Study 2 (MTurk scenario experiment, 2 [participant gender: male vs. female] × 2 [condition: delegate vs. do not delegate] × 2 [subordinate gender: male vs. female, "Michael" or "Michelle"]; 200 recruited, 86% passed the attention checks, final sample 171: 83 women, mean age 37.0): affective associations were measured by Linguistic Inquiry Word Count (LIWC) coding of each participant’s free-response text into percentages of negative and positive emotion words, square-root transformed because of skewness. Guilt of overburdening (delegate condition) or relief from not overburdening (do-not-delegate condition) was captured with three condition-matched items on 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) scales (α = .93). Concerns about one’s own competence (six items, α = .95) and about the subordinate’s competence (three items, α = .71) were used as control variables in all analyses.
- Study 3 (correlational MTurk survey of participants holding a managerial role; 164 recruited, 19 removed for lacking authority to delegate, and 14 of the remainder did not report work experience or failed the attention check, with the paper reporting a final sample of 130: 66 women, mean age 34.02): the same LIWC procedure was applied to open-ended thoughts, feelings, and emotions associated with delegation, tabulating positive and negative emotion words plus the anger, sadness, and anxiety subscales. Guilt of overburdening was measured with two items (r = .81) and self-reported delegation with two items on how much and how effectively participants delegate (r = .59), on 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) scales.
- Study 4 (behavioral laboratory experiment at a large, private university using a trained confederate cast as a junior "Committee Member" to the participant’s senior "Sponsorship Coordinator" role; 148 recruited, 11 removed for suspicion about the confederate, final sample 137: 75 women, mean age 21.97): anxiety used the PANAS items nervous, afraid, and scared on 1 (not at all) to 5 (a great deal) scales, collected on arrival (Time 1) and after learning about the delegation activity (Time 2) and scored as change (alphas .71 to .85); an agentic positive-affect subscale used confident, proud, and strong (alphas .78 to .82). Delegation was assessed by confederate reports of whether and how many tasks were delegated, confirmed by independent video coders, together with the number of seconds the participant spent in the confederate’s room. Interaction quality was measured by confederate ratings on six items based on the "showing concern" dimension of the empowering leadership questionnaire (α = .68) and by five research assistants’ video ratings on four consideration items (inter-coder α = .88; inter-item α = .90), with the two sets correlated r = .57, p < .001. Performance was an average z-score standardizing metrics across the six tasks, each scored by two research assistants, with delegated tasks credited full points. Guilt of overburdening (three items, α = .82) and fear of backlash (four items, α = .83) were asked only of participants who delegated. Confederate gender and a self-reflection condition were manipulated and controlled in all analyses; mediation used the PROCESS macro for SPSS with 5,000 bootstrap resamples.
- Study 5 (in-class manager–subordinate feedback role play with MBA students in an introductory organizational behavior course at a large private East Coast university; 135 students, 47 women, mean age 28.11): managers were randomly assigned to a delegation-as-communal condition, which added text telling them that assigning the tasks makes them a good mentor helping the subordinate develop, or to a control condition with no such text. Before the role play, anxiety used the same nervous/afraid/scared items (α = .76), positive affect the confident/proud/strong items (α = .67), and guilt of overburdening the three items used in Study 4 (α = .77) on 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) scales; subordinates rated their desire to take on the manager’s tasks with two items (r = .71).

Gender is a measured participant characteristic rather than a manipulated factor in all five studies, so the gender comparisons are associational; random assignment applies to the delegation instruction in Study 2, the communal-framing instruction in Study 5, and confederate gender and the self-reflection condition in Study 4. Delegation in Study 4 was a participant choice, not an assigned condition, and the paper describes Study 3 as a correlational study.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported in Study 1: women associated delegation with agency (M = 4.57, SD = .84) more than men did (M = 4.16, SD = 1.03), F(1, 95) = 3.86, p = .05, ηp² = .039. As the authors expected, there was no gender difference in associations of communality with delegation (women M = 5.24, SD = 1.18; men M = 5.17, SD = 1.18), F(1, 95) = .07, p = .80.
- Hypothesis 2a supported in Studies 2 and 3. Study 2: a significant gender × delegation condition interaction on negative affect, F(1, 170) = 4.67, p = .03, ηp² = .03, with women told to delegate using more negative-affect words (M = 1.36, SD = 1.91) than men told to delegate (M = .66, SD = 1.38), F(1, 170) = 4.47, p = .04, ηp² = .03, and no difference between men and women told not to delegate; there were no effects of gender, condition, or their interaction on positive affect. Study 3: a main effect of gender on negative affect, F(1, 128) = 6.99, p = .009, ηp² = .05 (women M = 3.31, SD = 3.57; men M = 1.87, SD = 2.55), and on the anxiety subscale, F(1, 128) = 10.26, p = .002, ηp² = .07 (women M = 2.93, SD = 3.58; men M = 1.26, SD = 2.16), with women listing marginally fewer positive-affect words (M = 2.77, SD = 3.02) than men (M = 3.82, SD = 3.27), F(1, 128) = 3.63, p < .06, ηp² = .03.
- Hypothesis 2b: not borne out in Study 2, where there were no effects of gender on guilt or relief, F(1, 170) = 0.15, p = .70, and no gender × condition interaction, F(1, 170) = 0.05, p = .82, only a condition main effect (relief in the do-not-delegate condition M = 5.10, SD = 1.54 vs. guilt in the delegate condition M = 3.65, SD = 1.59), F(1, 170) = 34.86, p < .0001, ηp² = .16. Supported in Study 3, where women expressed greater guilt about overburdening (M = 4.55, SD = 1.42) than men (M = 4.02, SD = 1.53), F(1, 129) = 4.07, p < .05, ηp² = .03, and in Study 4, where among delegators women felt more guilt (M = 5.36, SD = 1.41) than men (M = 4.65, SD = 1.49), F(1, 76) = 5.24, p < .03, ηp² = .06.
- Hypothesis 2c supported in Study 4: women who delegated reported more fear of backlash (M = 2.91, SD = 1.38) than men who delegated (M = 2.34, SD = 1.10), F(1, 75) = 4.26, p = .04, ηp² = .05.
- Hypothesis 3a supported in Study 4 behaviorally: a binary logistic regression showed a significant main effect of gender, with women (51%) delegating less than men (68%), B = −.72, SE = .36, p < .05, Wald z = 4.01. Among those who did delegate, there was no gender difference in the number of items delegated, F(1, 76) = .42, p = .52. In Study 3, by contrast, self-reported delegation showed no gender difference, F(1, 128) = .50, p = .48 (men M = 4.64, SD = 1.18; women M = 4.78, SD = 1.08), although negative affect correlated negatively with self-reported delegation (r = −.21, p < .05).
- Hypothesis 3b not supported: neither negative (CI95 = −.02, .23) nor positive affective responses (CI95 = −.12, .18) mediated the effect of gender on delegation, and neither mediated the effect of gender on interaction quality (negative CI95 = −.007, .28; positive CI95 = −.21, .03). The authors report a marginal indirect effect of gender on interaction quality through anxiety (indirect effect = .07, SE = .07, CI90 = .002, .24) and describe it as inconclusive. Guilt and fear of backlash could not be tested as mediators because they were assessed after delegation and only for those who delegated.
- Hypothesis 3c supported in Study 4: confederates rated female participants as less considerate, trustworthy, motivating, and supportive (M = 3.25, SD = 1.01) than male participants (M = 3.96, SD = 1.15), F(1, 75) = 11.07, p = .001, ηp² = .13, and independent coders rated female participants as less considerate (M = 2.94, SD = 1.22) than male participants (M = 3.44, SD = 1.16), F(1, 73) = 3.48, p < .07, ηp² = .05, a marginally significant effect. Women also spent fewer seconds with the confederate when delegating (M = 33.28, SD = 25.98) than men (M = 54.20, SD = 59.68), F(1, 70) = 4.85, p = .03, ηp² = .07.
- Hypothesis 3d supported in Study 4: participants who delegated performed significantly better (M = .11, SD = .45) than those who did not (M = −.15, SD = .44), F(1, 129) = 10.44, p = .002, ηp² = .08. There was no effect of gender on total performance, F(1, 129) = .02, p = .89.
- Hypothesis 4 supported in Study 5 on anxiety, marginally at the interaction level: no main effect of gender, F(1, 68) = 0.01, p = .90, and no main effect of delegation condition, F(1, 68) = 2.68, p = .11, but a marginally significant gender × condition interaction, F(1, 68) = 3.55, p = .06, ηp² = .05; planned contrasts showed women in the control condition had greater anticipatory anxiety (M = 2.31, SD = .76) than women in the delegation-as-communal condition (M = 1.67, SD = .55), F(1, 23) = 4.75, p = .03, ηp² = .07, with no condition difference for men, F(1, 44) = 0.04, p = .84. For guilt of overburdening there was a main effect of condition (control M = 4.03, SD = 1.13 vs. delegation-as-communal M = 3.37, SD = 1.27), F(1, 68) = 7.04, p < .01, ηp² = .10, but no main effect of gender, F(1, 68) = 1.46, p = .23, and no gender × condition interaction, F(1, 68) = 1.99, p = .16. Positive emotions showed no main effects or significant interactions (ps > .29), and subordinates showed no gender differences in their desire to take on the manager’s tasks (p = .48).
- Study 4 also found a delegation main effect on positive affect, F(1, 130) = 4.67, p = .03, ηp² = .04, qualified by a gender × delegation interaction, F(1, 130) = 3.90, p = .05, ηp² = .03: men who did not delegate showed decreases in positive affect (M = −.32, SD = .67) while men who delegated showed increases (M = .17, SD = .70), F(1, 130) = 7.28, p = .01, whereas women showed no such difference (M = .04, SD = .64 vs. M = .02, SD = .65), F(1, 130) = .02, p = .88. Anxiety showed a gender × delegation interaction, F(1, 130) = 4.04, p < .05, ηp² = .03, with women who delegated showing greater increases in anticipatory anxiety (M = .64, SD = .88) than women who did not (M = .31, SD = .68), F(1, 130) = 3.57, p = .06, and no difference for men, F(1, 130) = 1.07, p = .30.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to gender and leadership research by showing that leadership behaviors can be gender-role paradoxical rather than simply agentic or communal. It provides empirical evidence that women and men differ in delegation behavior and identifies affective associations as a process through which gender-role incongruence shapes delegation. It also extends backlash and social role theory by showing that communal reframing can change women’s responses to a seemingly agentic leadership behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that organizations should not assume that delegation is experienced similarly by male and female leaders, even when delegation is formally encouraged as a developmental practice. Framing delegation as mentoring, developing, and empowering subordinates may help women view delegation as more communal and selfless, reducing negative affect and potentially increasing delegation. The paper also suggests that failure to address these affective barriers may hinder women’s time management, subordinate development, and leadership effectiveness.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Study 3 did not find gender differences in self-reported delegation among managers, while Study 4 found behavioral differences in a student laboratory sample, leaving open whether behavioral gender differences generalize across organizations, experience levels, and hierarchy. They also note that Study 4 involved participants with no ongoing subordinate relationship, which may suppress or exaggerate delegation behavior compared with real work relationships. A further limitation is that Studies 2 and 4 operationalized delegation in a time-scarce, agentic way, which may have minimized delegation’s communal aspects.

**Future Research**
The authors call for behavioral studies of delegation across men and women in organizations with varied work experience and hierarchical levels. They recommend longitudinal work on how familiarity and relationship length with subordinates affect women’s likelihood of and comfort with delegation. They also call for research that distinguishes associated, prospective, and retrospective anxiety; tests broader negative affective mechanisms such as anticipatory guilt; and compares communal with noncommunal or agentic-legitimating reframings of delegation.
