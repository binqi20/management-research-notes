# The Paradox of Family Structure and Plans after Work: Why Single Childless Employees May Be the Least Absorbed at Work

**Abstract**
Existing research has shown that positive family experiences can affect work positively. In this article, however, we consider how family may enhance work even when family experiences are not explicitly positive. We draw on boundary theory and cognitive psychology’s current concerns theory to evaluate how employees’ family structures and associated after-work activities affect their work absorption. A survey of business school alumni (Study 1) revealed that single, childless workers reported lower absorption than workers with other family structures. Further, a daily diary study of university employees (Study 2) showed that employees’ planned after-work activities explained the relationship between family structure and work absorption. Specifically, single, childless workers anticipated fewer domestic after-work activities, resulting in lower work absorption. Due to similarities between domestic responsibilities and work tasks—for example, their obligatory and goal-directed nature—anticipating domestic responsibilities after work reinforces, rather than distracts from, the work mindset, thus keeping employees more immersed psychologically in their work. This finding suggests that having a spouse and/or children can affect employees’ work absorption positively through the anticipation of domestic duties after work. Thus, our study contributes to a more comprehensive view of how employees’ work and nonwork lives are connected.

**Research Question**
How does family structure affect employees' work absorption, and do anticipated after-work domestic or leisure activities explain why single, childless employees may be less absorbed at work than employees with other family structures? The paper asks whether family responsibilities can enhance work absorption even when the relevant family experience is not explicitly positive.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. There is a significant relationship between family structure and work absorption, such that single, childless employees report lower work absorption than employees with other family structures.
- Hypothesis 2. There is a positive relationship between domestic activities and work absorption, such that anticipating domestic activities after work increases work absorption.
- Hypothesis 3. There is a negative relationship between leisure activities and work absorption, such that anticipating leisure activities after work decreases work absorption.
- Hypothesis 4. There is a significant indirect effect of family structure on work absorption operating through the anticipation of domestic activities after work, such that, compared to workers with families, single, childless people anticipate fewer domestic activities after work—and anticipating fewer domestic activities results in lower work absorption.
- Hypothesis 5. There is a significant indirect effect of family structure on work absorption operating through the anticipation of leisure activities after work such that, compared to workers with families, single childless workers anticipate more leisure activities after work—and anticipating more leisure activities results in lower work absorption.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Family structure, especially being single and childless versus all other family structures; anticipated proportions of domestic and leisure after-work activities.
- DV(s): Work absorption, measured as employees' psychological immersion and concentration in work.
- Mediators: Anticipated domestic after-work activities; anticipated leisure after-work activities; supplemental construals of activities as obligatory, goal directed, or an escape from routine.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The mechanism is anticipatory rather than affect-transfer based. Because domestic responsibilities are obligatory and goal directed, anticipating them can reinforce a work-like mindset and keep employees psychologically immersed in work. Single, childless employees anticipate fewer domestic activities, which reduces this reinforcing process and helps explain their lower work absorption; anticipated leisure activities are associated with lower absorption but do not mediate the family-structure effect.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data and procedure: the authors randomly sampled 1,966 undergraduate business and MBA alumni of a private U.S. university as part of a larger work-life study, using a mixed-mode four-contact strategy (web survey by email first, then a combination of email and paper reminders; Dillman, 2000). They received 562 surveys (a 29% response rate) and removed respondents who were not currently employed (n = 46) or retired (n = 47), leaving 469 available respondents, of whom 61.8% were male, average age 39.07, 82.4% Caucasian, 72.9% married, mean number of children .94, and 81.7% in management and business occupations. The final sample size was 353 (75.3% of the 469 available participants) after listwise deletion for missing data; the authors report that the analytic sample resembles all available respondents on gender, marital status, level within the firm, highest degree, and work absorption.
- Study 1 measures: work absorption = four items from the absorption subscale of Rothbard's (2001) engagement scale, averaged, rated from 1 "strongly agree" to 7 "strongly disagree" (a = .78). Family structure = a dummy coded 1 for single/childless and 0 for all other family structures; the singles category included never-married (86.7%), divorced (10%), and widowed (3.3%) respondents, the married category included married (98.8%) and same-sex domestic partnership (1.2%) respondents, and respondents were coded as parents if they had minor children in the home. Controls: respondents' sex as a proxy for gender (0 = male, 1 = female), age in years, and job level (1 = entry level, 2 = lower level, 3 = middle level, 4 = senior level). Analysis: ordinary least squares regression.
- Study 2 data and procedure: employees of one nonacademic division of a large, private university, composed primarily of professional staff engaged in one area of administration, recruited with the same four-contact strategy. A one-time general survey measuring person-level variables was completed at least three weeks before the first daily diary survey; out of 216 invited employees, 103 participated (a 48% response rate), of whom 88.4% were salaried professional staff and 11.6% hourly support staff, 46.8% married, mean number of children 1.75, ages 25 to 65 (M = 43.35, SD = 10.81), 81% women, 84% Caucasian and 14% African American. For five consecutive days participants received two surveys, one at the beginning of the day asking about anticipated after-work plans and one at 3:00 PM with the work absorption items. Out of the 103 general survey participants, 97 completed at least one daily survey, and 354 matched morning and afternoon responses were received (a 73% response rate), a mean of 3.6 matched responses per participant.
- Study 2 measures: daily work absorption = the Study 1 items reworded to refer to that specific day (a = .99). Family structure was identical to Study 1, with 1 = single, childless (n = 23) and 0 = all others (n = 66). Mediators: on the morning survey participants were asked whether they had nonwork activities, commitments, or responsibilities planned after their regular workday; those answering yes listed up to five activities (mean = 1.3) and categorized each from four options (domestic or family-related responsibility, leisure, community/volunteer commitment, or personal development and wellness). Proportion domestic and proportion leisure were computed by dividing the number of domestic activities and the number of leisure activities each by the total number of activities listed for the day, so each proportion ranged from 0 to 1 and was set to 0 on days with no plans; respondents reported an average of 0.78 planned domestic and 0.24 planned leisure activities across the five days. Controls: sex, age, monthly pay as the informant-suggested indicator of job complexity and schedule variation (1 = biweekly-paid nonexempt, 2 = monthly-paid exempt), respondents' overall trait-like absorption from the general survey (a = .72), and the start time of the earliest after-work activity in elapsed minutes from midnight.
- Study 2 analysis: multilevel structural equation modeling (MSEM) in Mplus for a 2-1-1 mediation model, following Preacher (2011) and Preacher, Zyphur, and Zhang (2010), decomposing variance into within- and between-person components; the intraclass correlation coefficient for absorption was .46, indicating that 46% of the variance was between person. The notes to Tables 2 and 4 report n = 97 at the person level and n = 439 at the day level, based on maximum likelihood estimation models using all available data, with an average of 4.56 observations per person and fixed-slope models. A footnoted 2-1 multilevel analysis including between-person controls was also run, which the authors caution should be interpreted with care given the person-level sample size after listwise deletion.
- Study 2 supplement data and procedure: participants were Qualtrics panel members, restricted to full-time employees who correctly answered attention filter items. On a morning survey respondents reported their anticipated after-work activities and their construal of each activity; on an afternoon survey they reported work absorption. Three hundred and eighty participants completed the morning survey, but 123 reported no plans after work and were excluded because the analysis focused on construal of anticipated activities; the final sample included 196 participants with matched morning and afternoon surveys, reporting a total of 564 activities, of which 429 were categorized as domestic or leisure.
- Study 2 supplement measures and analysis: absorption (a = .90) and the activity categories were identical to Study 2, but the proportions of anticipated domestic and leisure activity were computed from how long respondents expected to participate in each activity, which the authors describe as an improvement over the count-based proportions used in Study 2. Construals, aggregated to the person level by averaging across all activities anticipated that day: goal directed = four items (a = .92) modified from Woolley's (2009) measure of outcome focus; obligatory = five questions (a = .81) adapted from Weinstein and Ryan's (2010) controlled motivation scale; escape from the routine = three questions (a = .93) modified from the escaping pressures subscale of Schulz and Watkins's (2007) leisure meanings inventory. Controls: earliest activity start time, job level, age, and gender. Activity-level construal differences were tested with the mixed model function in SPSS, treating after-work activities (Level 1) as nested within people (Level 2), and the mediation tests used the PROCESS macro for SPSS (Hayes, 2013) with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals.
- Design status: all three data collections are self-report surveys and the evidence is associational; the authors state that the single-survey Study 1 design limits causal inference and can produce common method variance, and that the daily diary design of Study 2 strengthens the causal inference of their effects. No experimental manipulation or econometric identification strategy is used.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (OLS, n = 353), Hypothesis 1 supported: the effect of being single and childless on work absorption was negative and significant (b = -.31, SE = 0.13, p < .05), controlling for gender, age, and job level. The authors write that "Hypothesis 1 was thus supported."
- Study 2 (MSEM, n = 97 persons / n = 439 days), Hypothesis 2 supported: anticipating domestic activities had a positive effect on daily work absorption (estimate = 0.50, SE = 0.22, p < .05). Hypothesis 3 supported: anticipating leisure activities had a negative effect on daily work absorption (estimate = -0.52, SE = 0.23, p < .05).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 4 supported: family structure had a significant negative indirect effect on work absorption via anticipated domestic activities (coefficient = -.17, SE = 0.09, p <= .05). In Table 4 the between-level component paths for this model are family structure to proportion domestic = -0.16 (p < .01) and proportion domestic to absorption = 1.02 (p < .01), with the direct path from family structure to absorption not significant (-0.22, SE 0.22).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 5 not supported: the indirect effect of family structure on absorption via anticipated leisure activities was not significant (coefficient = -.17, SE = 0.17, p > .05). In the leisure model, family structure predicted a higher proportion of leisure activities (path ab = 0.08, p < .05) but the path from proportion leisure to absorption was not significant (-2.04, SE 1.46).
- Study 2, footnoted replication of Hypothesis 1: a 2-1 multilevel analysis including between-person control variables showed that single, childless people reported lower absorption (estimate = -.41, SE = 0.22, p < .05, one tailed), consistent with Hypothesis 1, though the authors flag the small person-level sample size for between-person tests.
- Study 2 alternative explanations: the correlation between the daily count of activities and daily absorption was not significant (r = -.04, p > .05), and the indirect effect of the number of activities (M = 2.16, SD = 0.84) was not significant (coefficient = -.00, SE = 0.03, p > .05). Controlling for positive affect, the indirect effect remained significant with proportion of domestic activities as the mediator (coefficient = -.17, SE = 0.09, p <= .05) but not with proportion of leisure activities (coefficient = -.20, SE = 0.19, p > .05); positive affect tested as a mediator in a 2-2-1 model was not significant (coefficient = -.01, SE = 0.04, p > .05). Controlling for breadwinner status produced the same pattern (domestic mediator coefficient = -.17, SE = 0.09, p <= .05; leisure mediator coefficient = -.17, SE = 0.17, p > .05), and breadwinner status as a mediator was not significant (coefficient = -.01, SE = 0.04, p > .05). There was no significant difference between single, childless employees and others in the overall number of anticipated after-work activities, and a footnoted analysis found that single, childless employees reported more personal development and community volunteering activities but that neither category explained the family-structure effect on absorption.
- Study 2 supplement (n = 196), construal differences as expected: category type had a significant effect on construal of goal direction, F(2, 537.60) = 22.83, p < .001; obligation, F(2, 511.02) = 48.56, p < .001; and escape from the routine, F(2, 562.04) = 115.70, p < .001. Pairwise comparisons showed domestic activities were construed as more goal directed (M = 4.01) than leisure activities (M = 3.42, p < .001) and more obligatory (M = 3.80) than leisure activities (M = 2.57, p < .001), while leisure activities were perceived as more of an escape from the routine (M = 5.75) than domestic activities (M = 3.27, p < .001).
- Study 2 supplement, indirect effects of anticipating domestic activities on absorption (PROCESS, bootstrapped 95% CIs): through obligatory construal the effect was positive and significant (estimate = 0.18, CI [0.02, 0.42]); through goal direction it was not significant (estimate = 0.08, CI [-0.02, 0.24]); through escape from the routine it was significant but negative (estimate = -0.32, CI [-0.67, -0.07]), which the authors state was contrary to their expectations.
- Study 2 supplement, indirect effects of anticipating leisure activities on absorption: through obligatory construal the effect was negative and significant (estimate = -0.14, CI [-0.37, -0.01]); through goal direction it was also negative and significant (estimate = -0.24, CI [-0.52, -0.08]); through escape from the routine it was positive and significant (estimate = 0.40, CI [0.14, 0.75]), again contrary to expectations. The authors conclude that construing activities as obligatory drives the effects on absorption for both domestic and leisure activities, whereas goal direction is relevant only for explaining the negative effect of leisure activities.
- Study 2 supplement alternative explanation: neither anticipating domestic nor anticipating leisure activities was significantly related to positive affect (domestic r = -.12, p > .05; leisure r = .02, p > .05), and there was no significant indirect effect on absorption through positive affect for domestic activities (estimate = -0.20, CI [-0.45, 0.01]) or for leisure activities (estimate = 0.048, CI [-0.16, 0.27]).
- Support pattern across the paper: Hypothesis 1 was tested and supported in Study 1 and re-examined in a footnoted Study 2 analysis with a consistent result; Hypotheses 2, 3, and 4 were tested and supported in Study 2; Hypothesis 5 was tested in Study 2 and not supported. The Study 2 supplement tested the construal assumptions rather than the five numbered hypotheses, and two of its six indirect effects ran opposite to the authors' expectations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends work-family enrichment research by showing that family can benefit work through anticipated responsibilities, not only through positive affect, skill transfer, or prosocial motivation. It contributes to boundary theory by linking anticipatory boundary transitions to current work absorption rather than only to after-work recovery or role conflict. It also broadens ideal-worker theorizing by directly examining single, childless workers and showing that being less encumbered by family responsibilities does not necessarily translate into greater work absorption.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not assume that single, childless employees are naturally more available, focused, or work-absorbed because they lack spouse or child responsibilities. Organizations may need broader work-life policies that respect nonfamily commitments and support diverse forms of nonwork life. Employees and managers can also use reminders or planning around goal-directed domestic or nonwork tasks selectively, because anticipated activities can shape absorption before the workday ends.

**Limitations**
The studies rely partly on self-reported survey behavior, creating potential common method variance, although Study 2 uses multiple survey points and family structure is not an attitude measure. The Study 2 and supplement samples are predominantly female, and the authors note that spousal employment status, gender, and unstudied cohabitating heterosexual couples require further exploration. The paper studies absorption rather than time spent at work or productivity, so the findings do not establish effects on objective performance.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how spousal employment status, gender, parental status, marital status, and cohabitation jointly shape the family-structure effects documented here. Additional work should compare the anticipatory effects of after-work activities with their following-day recovery effects, including moderators such as how active, passive, challenging, goal directed, or routine the activities are. The authors also state that future research considering how family structure affects a variety of other work outcomes would help expand theory regarding the effect of nonwork on work.
