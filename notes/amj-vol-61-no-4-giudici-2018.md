# Open-System Orchestration as a Relational Source of Sensing Capabilities: Evidence from a Venture Association

**Abstract**
Research on innovation networks has highlighted the pivotal role that actors with more prominence and power, such as hub firms, may play in orchestrating the activities of other network members along a collective innovation effort. Our study examined the under-theorized, but no less important, type of orchestration that characterizes other organizations, such as business incubators and venture associations, who seek to support the dispersed entrepreneurial efforts of network members. We refer to this type as “open-system” orchestration, as opposed to the commonly studied “closed-system” type performed by hub firms. Our findings reveal how the processes of open-system orchestration differ markedly from those of closed-system orchestration, and detail how these processes influence the micro-foundations of network members’ sensing capabilities. By doing so, we also offer empirical substantiation and theoretical elaboration to the idea that dynamic capabilities might not reside exclusively inside firms, but could be co-created relationally with other parties in the business ecosystem.

**Research Question**
How do open-system orchestrators support the dispersed entrepreneurial efforts and sensing capabilities of network members? The paper asks how this form of orchestration differs from closed-system orchestration by hub firms and how it helps firms recognize new opportunities and form new business relationships.

- Emergent outcomes: recognition of new entrepreneurial opportunities and formation of new business relationships.
- Boundary conditions: the case was a highly successful, larger-than-average matchmaking initiative, mostly involving SMEs, and WT predominantly engaged in open-system orchestration.

The process model begins with a venture association creating a collaborative context around B2BMatch rather than directing members toward a centrally controlled innovation goal. Through preparatory activities, referrals, event design, and interaction monitoring, WT helped firms reflect on their own resources and identify complementarities with other members. These interactions supported members' sensing capabilities as relationally co-created capacities, while repeated participation also helped some firms internalize search routines beyond the event.

**Data & Measures**
Interpretive field study (Gephart, 2004) of the venture association Working Together (WT) and its B2BMatch initiative, combining primary data (semi-structured interviews and direct observation) with secondary archival material; focal constructs are coded qualitatively rather than measured as variables, and the paper makes no causal-identification claim. Interviews ran in three rounds: January–April 2011 (six interviews with WT informants and four with firms that participated in the 2010 event), May–July 2011 (six interviews with participant firms and four with WT informants in LB2), and August 2011–October 2012 (44 interviews with 41 firms from 20 local branches, plus 17 interviews with WT informants). In total the authors collected data from 81 interviews: 50 with participant firms from 22 local branches, four with buyers from multinational enterprises invited to the event, and 27 with WT informants. Interviews lasted on average one hour and all were tape-recorded excluding two due to technical problems and one due to lack of authorization, producing 917 single-spaced pages of transcripts. Observation covered three full-day WT meetings, two half-day training sessions, one three-hour internal coordination meeting, the three days of the 2011 B2BMatch-event, a one-day local replica event that attracted over 200 participant firms, and guest-participant monitoring of WT's private online community. Archival material included internal WT documentation (14 presentations for 2009 and 2011–2014, 265 slides; post-initiative final reports for 2008 and 2010–2013, 103 pages; internal-meeting transcripts and notes), public documentation (WT's 2010 monthly magazine, 1,398 pages; 2007–2013 Annual General Meeting proceedings, 222 pages; B2BMatch brochures and catalogs, 497 pages), press coverage of 665 articles (954 pages), and 30 CNBC videos plus nine similar regional-media and WT videos and three untranscribed institutional videos. Analysis was "abductive" (Locke, Golden-Biddle, & Feldman, 2008) in three steps: event analysis and open coding generating a large dataset of in-vivo codes collapsed into first-order categories; axial coding comparing first-order categories with prior research and structuring them into second-order themes and higher-level aggregate dimensions (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013); and building a grounded model linking those aggregate dimensions. Trustworthiness (Lincoln & Guba, 1985) was established by submitting emerging interpretations to a panel discussion at the 2013 B2BMatch attended by approximately 100 participants, and through unsolicited feedback following two short pieces published in WT's magazine.

**Key Findings**
Members' innovation output took the forms Teece (2007) conceptualizes as the outcomes of sensing capabilities: recognition of new entrepreneurial opportunities (new knowledge and ideas; initiatives related to new market development) and formation of new business relationships (new connections; new contractual agreements). The analysis identifies four orchestration processes distributed across three phases: building collaborative engagement during Event Design (February to October); facilitating members' introspection during Event Preparation (mid-March to mid-November); encouraging members' exploration of complementarities during Event Preparation (August to mid-November); and fostering dispersed collaboration during Event Participation (end of November). Converging evidence from interviews and field observations suggests that these four processes enhanced and energized members' search for business opportunities by diffusing assumptions of mutual trustworthiness among participants, by enhancing their organizational self-awareness, and by promoting conditions for a positive emotional experience. The authors additionally report experiential learning, for which they state they collected only exploratory evidence (marked with dotted lines in Figures 2 and 3), manifesting in two ways: members strengthened their capacity to contribute to and take advantage of the orchestrator's support and became less reliant on it in subsequent years; and more experienced, engaged members extended the search approach to potential partners outside B2BMatch. Comparatively, the paper concludes that open-system orchestration differs markedly from closed-system orchestration — it is more akin to building loosely coupled communities of actors than to designing and enforcing a set of contractual relationships, it essentially decentralizes the assessment of fit with the rest of the network to individual members, and the orchestrator also acts as a "temporal broker" (Reinecke & Ansari, 2015) managing the timing of its initiatives in synchrony with the pace of firms' search activities. As an inductive interpretive study it reports no hypothesis tests, coefficients, or significance levels; a footnote reports that in 2011 respondents to a survey collected by WT reported an average of 45.9 new ideas plus 41.4 appointments over the three days of the main B2BMatch-event, of which 10.8 were considered "useful" and 3.8 resulted immediately in new contracts (based on a sample of 237 respondents, with an average of 1.8 prior participations in the main event).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends network orchestration research by theorizing open-system orchestration as a distinct form used by organizations such as incubators, regional agencies, and venture associations to support decentralized entrepreneurial search. It contributes to dynamic capability theory by showing how sensing capabilities can be co-created relationally rather than residing exclusively inside firms. It also elaborates the cognitive and emotional micro-foundations through which trust, self-awareness, and positive affect support opportunity search in innovation networks.

**Practical Implication**
The study suggests that venture associations, incubators, and similar intermediaries can improve members' opportunity search by designing collaborative norms, reflection spaces, high-quality referrals, and interaction settings that lower barriers among participants. It also implies that member firms need to engage thoughtfully and systematically; the benefits of open-system orchestration depend on their preparation, willingness to collaborate, and repeated learning from participation.

**Limitations**
The authors state two boundary conditions. First, most B2BMatch participants were SMEs, where strategic decisions about business development, purchasing, and sales were concentrated in a small team, often including owner-managers. Second, WT engaged predominantly in open-system orchestration, so settings that combine open- and closed-system orchestration may show different or interfering processes.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine open-system orchestration in larger organizations where responsibility for search and business development is more dispersed. The authors also call for research on how open-system orchestration affects new business models, leadership development, and tensions among different classes of dynamic capabilities. Additional work could compare orchestrators that combine open- and closed-system processes to test whether the mechanisms are compatible.
