# Silver Bullet or Ricochet? CEOs’ Use of Metaphorical Communication and Infomediaries’ Evaluations

**Abstract**
We combine literature on rhetoric and socially situated sensemaking to illuminate the challenges that emerge when chief executive officers (CEOs) try to influence infomediaries by using metaphorical communication—figurative linguistic expressions that convey thoughts and feelings by describing one domain, A, through another domain, B. Specifically, we theorize that because different infomediaries are situated in different thought worlds, CEOs’ use of metaphorical communication has contradictory effects on journalists’ and securities analysts’ evaluations: while it triggers more favorable statements from journalists, it prompts more unfavorable assessments from analysts. Moreover, we integrate findings from cognitive psychology to argue that these contradictory effects increase the more a firm’s performance falls behind market expectations. Our hypotheses find support in an extensive analysis of 937 quarterly earnings calls in the U.S. pharmaceutical, hardware, and software industries, and of journalists’ statements and analysts’ earnings forecasts and recommendations. Our novel theorizing and findings suggest that the use of discursive frames, especially in the form of metaphorical communication, in firms’ interactions with critical audiences creates thought-provoking and thus-far neglected dilemmas. In developing and testing these thoughts, we contribute to and link ongoing conversations in management science, especially discussions of organizational reputation, executive communication, and impression management.

**Research Question**
How does the degree to which a CEO uses metaphorical communication affect the favorability of different infomediaries' assessments of the CEO's firm? The paper focuses on whether journalists and securities analysts respond differently to the same CEO rhetorical device because their sensemaking is socially situated in different professional thought worlds.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Ceteris paribus, the more a CEO uses metaphorical communication, the more favorably journalists will report about that CEO's firm.
- H2: Ceteris paribus, the more a CEO uses metaphorical communication, the more unfavorably analysts will evaluate that CEO's firm.
- H3a: Ceteris paribus, the more negative the firm's earnings surprises, the stronger the positive marginal effect of the CEO's metaphorical communication on the favorability of journalists' reporting about the firm.
- H3b: Ceteris paribus, the more negative the firm's earnings surprises, the stronger the negative marginal effect of the CEO's metaphorical communication on the favorability of analysts' evaluations of the firm.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO's use of metaphorical communication in quarterly earnings conference calls.
- DV(s): Favorability of journalists' reporting about the firm; favorability of analysts' evaluations, measured through EPS forecast revisions and buy-hold-sell recommendation changes.
- Mediators: Not formally modeled as statistical mediators; the theorized process is infomediaries' socially situated, constituent-minded sensemaking and reliance on rhetorical cues as cognitive shortcuts.
- Moderators: Negative earnings surprise, with positive earnings surprise included as a check.

The mechanism is audience-specific. Metaphorical communication provides broad, familiar, engaging frames that reduce journalists' effort in translating firm information for a mainstream audience, so journalists evaluate firms more favorably when CEOs use more metaphorical communication. Analysts, by contrast, work in a fact-oriented thought world and need precise, unambiguous information for investor audiences, so metaphorical communication becomes distracting or suspect and leads to less favorable evaluations. Negative earnings surprises intensify both effects because poor performance increases reliance on cognitive shortcuts under uncertainty.

**Data & Measures**
The sampling frame was firms in the U.S. pharmaceutical industry (GICS 3510 or 3520) and the U.S. computer hardware and software industries (GICS 4510 or 4520) that were headquartered in the U.S. in 2002, had revenue of more than USD 100 million in 2002, and had two or more CEOs between 2002 and 2011; the authors excluded 22 firms owing to limited availability of conference-call transcripts and 15 extraordinary cases involving interim CEOs or co-CEOs. After further reductions due to missing control data, the final samples were 43 firms for Analysis I (n = 449 comparisons before or after the conference call; 98 CEOs) and 47 firms for Analysis II (n = 624 comparisons of aggregated analyst forecasts, 101 CEOs; 270 comparisons of aggregated analyst recommendations, 94 CEOs). IV, CEOs' use of metaphorical communication: quarterly earnings conference calls were the discursive vehicle, with transcripts obtained from Thomson Research and Seeking Alpha; the final sample covered 937 hand-coded calls, approximately 8,000 pages of text, in which the authors identified 2,229 instances of metaphorical communication, 95% of them metaphors. Three two-person teams of trained coders independently coded all transcripts using a noncomputerized, context-sensitive content-analytical instrument built on Mio et al. (2005), excluding "dead metaphors" and noncontentual communication such as welcomes, compliments, and questions directed to other firm representatives; interrater agreement was Krippendorff's alpha = 0.74, and the first author acted as independent evaluator in cases of continued disagreement. The measure divides the total number of words in a CEO's metaphorical communication during a conference call by the total number of words in the CEO's contentual communication during the same call. DV in Analysis I, favorability of journalists' reporting: a manual content analysis of New York Times and Wall Street Journal statements about the sample firms, drawn from a Factiva search of articles published between 2001 and 2011 that mentioned the firm and then filtered through Deephouse's (2000) progressive article-selection process, yielding 10,155 articles; after excluding 947 statements in which journalists directly quoted analysts, 25,415 statements were coded as favorable, ambiguous, or unfavorable (Krippendorff's alpha = 0.83) and summarized with the Janis-Fadner coefficient of imbalance, with the dependent variable being the change in favorability from the period before the focal call to the period after it. DV in Analysis II, favorability of analysts' evaluations: two complementary measures used in separate estimations. The first uses one-year EPS forecasts from the I/B/E/S detailed forecast database for analysts who issued a forecast both before and after the focal call, giving 6,969 before-after pairs; the difference between an analyst's first post-call forecast and that analyst's last pre-call forecast was standardized by the share price at the close of the focal quarter, aggregated to the median change across analysts for the specific call and firm, and multiplied by 100. The second uses I/B/E/S recommendations classified as positive (codes 1 and 2), negative (codes 4 and 5), or neutral (code 3) for analysts issuing a recommendation both before and after the call, giving 393 before-after pairs, with the Janis-Fadner coefficient computed before and after the call and the difference between the two scores taken as the measure. Moderator: earnings surprises were computed as the difference between a firm's quarterly EPS and the mean of analysts' EPS forecasts for that quarter, scaled by the actual EPS, then splined into negative earnings surprises and, as a check, positive earnings surprises. Controls covered firm factors (prior firm performance change and volatility, operationalized as the change in ROA and the standard deviation of ROA over the 12 quarters preceding the focal quarter, firm size, number of press releases, and media attention to the firm), CEO factors (age, tenure, duality, contender and outsider succession status, media attention to the CEO, celebrity measured as counts of American Business Awards and International Business Awards, and sales-and-marketing and finance functional backgrounds), conference-call and communication factors (share of the CEO's words, CFO involvement, future orientation, image-based language, comprehensibility measured with the Gunning Fog Index, numerical language, noncontentual words, and optimism), counts of post-call statements in the NYT and the WSJ in Analysis I, and in Analysis II the number of analysts following the firm plus debt-to-equity ratio, dividends per share, liquidity, cash flow from operating activities, abnormal returns, and shares traded, together with dummy variables for every quarter from 2002 to 2011. Both analyses use a robust firm fixed effects panel estimator with the Huber-White standard error correction (xtreg, fe robust in Stata), with the components of all interaction terms mean-centered; the design rests on firm fixed effects and before-after change scores rather than an experiment or quasi-experiment, and the authors report an extensive set of robustness checks, including a reevaluation of Analysis II at the level of the individual security analyst and a set of preliminary endogeneity tests reported in Appendix A.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: in the full journalist model (Model 3, Table 4), the coefficient of CEOs' use of metaphorical communication is positive and significant (coefficient = 7.64, p < .01), which the authors read as increases in a CEO's metaphorical communication rendering journalists more benevolent toward the CEO's firm. Hypothesis 2 was also supported: in both full analyst models (Model 3 with the EPS-forecast measure and Model 6 with the recommendation measure, Table 5), the coefficient of CEOs' use of metaphorical communication is negative and significant (p < .05; coefficient = -15.42 for the EPS-forecast measure and -19.35 for the recommendation measure), meaning firms receive less favorable analyst assessments if CEOs use more metaphorical communication in a given conference call. The authors report support for both Hypothesis 3a and Hypothesis 3b: negative earnings surprises amplify the association between metaphorical communication and infomediaries' (un-)favorability, with a positive interaction for journalists (coefficient = 53.57, p < .05, Model 3 in Table 4) and negative interactions for analysts (coefficient = -120.25, p < .01 for the EPS-forecast measure in Model 3 and -107.30, p < .05 for the recommendation measure in Model 6 of Table 5); the paper states that this is not the case for positive earnings surprises. Simple slope analyses indicated significant effects for low and high levels of the CEO's metaphorical communication in situations involving a negative earnings surprise, for both journalists and analysts. Illustrating the analyst effect with the EPS-based measure, the paper reports that at a level of metaphorical communication of 0.01 the favorability of analysts' evaluations is approximately 0.37 units lower in the case of a major earnings disappointment (-0.16) than in a situation with a minor earnings disappointment (0.21). Within-model R2 was 0.18 for the full journalist model (n = 449) and 0.30 and 0.34 for the full EPS-forecast (n = 624) and recommendation (n = 270) models. The authors note the non-significant pairwise correlation in Table 2 between CEOs' use of metaphorical communication and the favorability of journalists' reporting, which they disregard in the test of Hypothesis 1 on the grounds that a pairwise correlation is a biased estimator when the outcome is influenced by other factors correlated with the predictor; they also report that dropping the variables with variance inflation factors above 3 left the results unaffected in both analyses.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on executive communication and CEO-infomediary relations by showing that the rhetorical form of CEO communication, not only the amount or content of information, shapes firm evaluations by critical audiences. It also complicates the common view of metaphor as a rhetorical asset by showing that metaphorical communication can be beneficial with journalists and harmful with analysts, creating a rhetorical dilemma when CEOs address multiple audiences simultaneously. More broadly, it links organizational reputation, executive communication, and impression management through the lens of socially situated sensemaking.

**Practical Implication**
Corporate leaders, coaches, and speechwriters should treat metaphorical communication as an equivocal lever rather than a universal persuasive tool. When communicating with infomediaries, CEOs may need to decide which audience matters most at a given time and adjust rhetorical style accordingly, especially when firm performance disappoints market expectations. More metaphor may help journalists produce favorable coverage but can reduce analysts' favorability; less metaphor may preserve analyst confidence while forfeiting some journalistic appeal.

**Limitations**
The authors note that they use only preliminary measures for whether CEOs' metaphors were aligned with the message being conveyed, leaving open whether metaphor quality, fit, or source-domain choice changes the effects. The design analyzes conference calls and cannot fully rule out endogeneity in CEOs' use of metaphorical communication, although the paper reports extensive robustness checks, simultaneity tests, and residualized models. The setting is limited to U.S. pharmaceutical, hardware, and software firms and to two types of infomediaries, so effects may differ for other industries, cultures, communication channels, or audiences.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine characteristics of metaphorical communication, including whether specific source domains such as sports, journeys, or violence have different effects and whether automated methods can classify such domains reliably. Scholars could also study other CEO communication vehicles, such as interviews, public speeches, presentations, and roadshows, as well as nonverbal delivery cues such as facial expressions, gestures, and tone of voice. The paper further calls for work on other audiences, including customer-advocacy groups, rating agents, shareholder groups, and internal organizational members, where metaphorical communication may produce additional rhetorical dilemmas.
