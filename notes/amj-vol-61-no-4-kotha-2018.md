# Experience and Signaling Value in Technology Licensing Contract Payment Structures

**Abstract**
When commercializing technology, the lack of proven results and a reluctance to invest upfront in resources hamper efforts by firms to work jointly with inventors to bring new discoveries to market. An effective contract payment structure—a mix of upfront and royalty payments—can help overcome these hurdles. We conduct our research in university technology licensing, where licensing managers act as intermediaries to unite inventors and licensee firms. Rather than leveraging their experience to bargain for maximum payments, highly experienced managers offer contractual payment structures that trade lower upfront payments for higher royalty payments in order to signal value. The signal instills confidence in the value of the partnership for skeptical licensee firms, and experienced licensing managers can amplify signals as needed to overcome the uncertainties inherent in technology commercialization. By explicitly addressing these variations in signal strength, we develop new theory that builds on classical signaling principles. We test and confirm these predictions in a sample of over 950 invention-licensing contracts. In addition to advancing signaling theory, our work has implications for academic entrepreneurship, and for how experience shapes valuesharing agreements in collaborative innovations.

**Research Question**
How does licensing manager experience shape technology licensing contract payment structures, and when do experienced managers use lower upfront fixed fees and higher royalty rates as a signal of value rather than bargain for maximum immediate payments? The paper examines whether signaling ability and signaling need jointly explain contract terms in university technology licensing.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Licensing manager experience is positively related to the commercial success (licensing revenue) of the licensed invention.
- H2: As licensing manager experience increases, the upfront fixed-fee payment decreases while the performance-based royalty rate increases.
- H3: For inventions heavily dependent on academic science, the upfront fixed-fee payment decrease (H3a) and the royalty rate increase (H3b) become more pronounced as licensing manager experience increases.
- H4: For inventions by experienced inventors, the upfront fixed-fee payment decrease (H4a) and royalty rate increase (H4b) become more pronounced as licensing manager experience increases.
- H5: For innovations with a wide patent scope, the upfront fixed-fee payment decrease (H5a) and the royalty rate increase (H5b) become more pronounced as licensing manager experience increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Licensing manager experience, measured as the number of prior contracts negotiated and signed by the focal licensing manager at the time of invention licensing.
- DV(s): Commercial success of the licensed invention; negotiated upfront fixed-fee payment; negotiated royalty rate.
- Mediators: The paper theorizes selection of promising inventions and matching with capable licensees as the mechanisms, and tests them in post-hoc analyses rather than as formal mediation models.
- Moderators: Science intensity of the invention, inventor experience, and patent scope.

The mechanism is a signaling tradeoff in contract design. Experienced licensing managers are better able to select promising inventions and match them with capable licensees, so they can credibly give up guaranteed upfront payments in exchange for higher, performance-contingent royalties. This lower-upfront, higher-royalty structure signals confidence in the invention-licensee match to skeptical licensee firms. The signal becomes stronger when information asymmetry is higher or when signal need changes, including science-intensive inventions, more experienced inventors, and broader patent scope.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses original data from the technology transfer office (TTO) of a prominent U.S. university. The sample consists of 964 invention-contract pairs (527 contracts for patented and 437 contracts for non-patented inventions) licensed from 1990 to 2002, and also represents 427 inventions licensed through 472 contracts (314 single-invention licenses and 158 bundled-invention licenses); the main SUR estimations of contract terms use the patented sample (n = 527), while the OLS test of H1 is reported for both the full sample (N = 964) and the patented sample (n = 527). Three dependent variables are used: commercial success, which is the licensing revenue earned by the TTO from an invention (used to test H1); the negotiated upfront fixed-fee payment; and the negotiated royalty rate, a percentage of annual sales based on the licensed invention (both used to test H2 through H5). Following standard procedures to correct for skewness, natural log transformations of the dependent variables are used, and for bundled contracts with multiple inventions the payments were divided according to the contract allocation rules, most of which specified equal sharing. In the sample the average revenue earned by an invention was $51,997, the average fixed-fee payment was $12,025, and the average royalty rate was 1.63% of sales. The focal independent variable, licensing manager experience (LME), is created by counting the number of prior contracts that were negotiated and signed by the focal licensing manager at the time of invention licensing, rescaled by dividing it by 10. Three explanatory variables, treated as proxies for the need to signal, are entered as cross-products with LME: science intensity, the proportion of the number of backward citations to academic-scientific articles out of the total number of backward citations (academic articles plus other patents) in the invention's patent; inventor experience, the average number of prior disclosed inventions among inventors; and patent scope, based on the number of sub-classes into which the United States Patent and Trademark Office assigns the patent (Lerner, 1994: 320). Licensing-manager controls are the number of unlicensed inventions in the manager's portfolio over the previous five years and two spline variables for positive and negative performance relative to own past performance and to other managers, plus licensing-manager fixed effects; during the 1990 to 2002 sample period there were 11 licensing managers, each specialized in a different scientific domain, so no additional domain fixed effects were included. Inventor controls are a star-scientist indicator (135 of the 4,950 unique inventors in the sample were identified as star scientists using an adaptation of Zucker and Darby's citation-based method), the number of inventors, and prior collaboration, the proportion of inventors who had worked together before. Invention controls are invention quality, counted as the number of citations the technology's patent received until 2007 excluding self-citations, a funded-research indicator, previously licensed and its square, the number of inventions in a bundled contract, and a continuing-invention indicator. Licensee controls are licensee expertise, measured as the number of patents owned by the licensee on the agreement signing date, the licensee-manager relationship history counted as the number of inventions the focal licensing manager had previously licensed to the firm, licensee size as a publicly listed indicator, and the licensee-TTO distance between the university and the licensee firm's headquarters. Estimation uses ordinary least squares with clustered standard errors and licensing-manager and year fixed effects to predict commercial success for H1, and seemingly unrelated regression with licensing-manager fixed effects to jointly predict the royalty rate and fixed payment amount for H2 through H5, because the two are determined jointly and their error terms are correlated. Because not all inventions in the TTO's portfolio are licensed, a simple sample selection correction (Hamilton & Nickerson, 2003) is implemented and the sample selection hazard is used as an additional control variable in all estimations of contract payment structures. The design is observational and associational rather than causally identified; the paper relies on fixed effects, control variables, and the selection hazard rather than an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
All five hypotheses are supported. H1 is supported: LME is positively related to the income earned by an invention (Model 2; b = 0.09, p = 0.052) in the full sample, with similar results in the patented sample (Model 3; b = 0.10, p = 0.119), and the authors report that as LME increases from one SD below to one SD above the mean the income earned by an invention increases by 207% (from $1,796 to $3,711). H2 is supported in the SUR estimations of the patented sample (Table 3, Model 1): LME is negatively related to upfront payments (b = -0.17, p = 0.04; the fixed-fee payment variable is natural-log transformed) and the negotiated royalty rate increases with LME (b = 0.02, p = 0.04); over the same one-SD range the fixed amount decreases by 17% (from 7.91 to 6.55) and the royalty rate increases from 1.62% of sales to 1.97%, an increase of 22%. H3 is supported (Model 2): for science-intensive inventions the direct and negative LME-fixed-fee relationship intensifies (b = -0.32, p = 0.02) and the direct and positive LME-royalty rate relationship intensifies (b = 0.05, p = 0.005); the upfront fixed-fee payment for science-intensive inventions decreases from 7.88 to 6.15 (22%) whereas for low science-intensive inventions it increases from 8.04 to 8.25, and royalty payments for science-intensive inventions increase from 1.65% to 2.12 (over 28%) whereas for low science-intensive inventions the royalty rate decreases from 1.54% to 1.44 (6%); H3 is also supported in the full model with all the interactions (Model 5). H4 is supported (Model 3): for inventions by experienced inventors the negative LME-fixed-fee relationship strengthens (b = -0.09, p = 0.02) and the positive LME-royalty rate relationship strengthens (b = 0.01, p = 0.01), with the fixed-fee payment falling from 8.07 to 5.13 (36%) for inventions by experienced inventors versus 8.10 to 7.69 (5%) for inventions by inventors without experience; the paper notes that due to multi-collinearity in the full model only the fixed-payment coefficient is statistically significant (p = 0.03). H5 is supported (Model 4): the interaction is negative and significant for upfront fixed payments (H5a: b = -0.18, p = 0.07) and positive for royalty rates (b = 0.08, p = 0.000), with the upfront payment for widely applicable inventions decreasing from 7.70 to 5.93 (23%) versus 8.12 to 7.30 (10%) for more narrowly defined inventions, and royalty payments for widely applicable inventions increasing from 1.52% to 2.20 (nearly 45%) while for narrow inventions the royalty rate marginally decreases from 1.73% to 1.67; the paper notes that due to multi-collinearity in the full model only the royalty-rate coefficient is statistically significant (p < 0.001). Several control results are read as consistent with signaling rather than bargaining: larger licensees receive a higher fixed-fee payment (b = 1.20, p < 0.001) and a lower royalty rate (b = -0.27, p < 0.001), and geographically distant licensees receive a higher upfront payment (b = 0.36, p < 0.001) and a lower royalty rate (b = -0.03, p = 0.02). Post-hoc analyses (Table 4) support the selection and matching mechanisms unevenly: using selection for commercialization as the dependent variable, experienced licensing managers select fewer inventions for commercialization but limit their selectivity for inventions by experienced inventors (b = 0.07, p < 0.001), and this test could not be run for H3 and H5 because inventions not selected for commercialization are also not patented; using exclusive licensing as the matching proxy, the probability of signing an exclusive agreement increases with LME (b = 0.02, p = 0.02) and increases even more for science-intensive inventions (b = 0.04, p = 0.01) and inventions with a broad patent scope (b = 0.06, p < 0.001), but the matching test is not significant for inventor experience (b = 0.01, p = 0.26), which the authors report as consistent with having argued only the selection mechanism for that hypothesis. Additional analyses show that as LME and prior relationships increase, reduced upfront fixed-fee payments (b = -0.32, p = 0.02) and increased royalty rates (b = 0.05, p = 0.02) occur at an increasing rate. Robustness checks find similar results in the combined sample of patented and non-patented inventions and at the bundled-contract level; substituting inventor experience for LME does not reproduce the main findings and substituting the TTO organization's licensing experience in a domain yields weak but statistically significant results, which the authors read as confirming that LME with actual licensing is most strongly associated with signaling; and no support is found that learning from unlicensed inventions in the portfolio improves selection or matching.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling theory by integrating the sender's ability to signal with the receiver-side need for a signal, rather than assuming superior information and signal need are fixed. It shows that contract payment structures can function as intentional, recipient-specific signals whose strength varies with licensing manager experience and invention conditions. It also contributes to academic entrepreneurship by linking opportunity selection, partner matching, and licensing contract design as mechanisms through which experience shapes commercialization outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
Technology transfer offices and other organizations involved in licensing should treat experience as a capability for selecting inventions, matching them with appropriate partners, and crafting credible contract signals. The findings imply that inexperienced licensing managers may benefit from mentoring, pairing with senior managers, and repeated exposure to licensing deals. Experienced managers should not standardize every contract; they can adjust upfront fees and royalty rates to the uncertainty and information asymmetry in each agreement.

**Limitations**
The authors note that although post-hoc analyses isolate selection and matching mechanisms, future work should verify their relative magnitude more directly. The study is based on one technology transfer office, a research site the authors say offers advantages for observing and isolating inventions and licensing capabilities that may not be as evident in other contexts, and they speculate that in most large organizations that license technology the theoretical principles from their framework will generally apply. The data also do not provide comprehensive information on unsuccessful licensing attempts, although the analyses control for unlicensed inventions in licensing managers' portfolios.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how initial negotiation reference points influence contract structure and should distinguish contract experience from prior interactions with multiple inventors more carefully. Studies using licensing data from multiple TTOs and non-academic corporate licensors could test whether the framework generalizes beyond the focal university context. The authors also suggest applying the framework to other settings where intermediaries help buyers and sellers agree on uncertain quality and value, such as artistic and cultural products or professional athletics.
