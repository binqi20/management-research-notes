# The Benefits of Not Seeing Eye to Eye with Leadership: Divergence in Risk Preferences Impacts Multiteam System Behavior and Performance

**Abstract**
Multiteam systems are large structures of interdependent teams that coordinate via planning before embarking on important projects. Although rarely studied, theory suggests that expression of convergent versus divergent goals and preferences during planning by leadership and component teams matters for multiteam system performance. Addressing this important issue, in a sample of multiteam systems consisting of Air Force officers, we examine how convergent versus divergent preferences for risk expressed during planning by the leadership and component teams impact multiteam system behavior and performance during the performance episode. Drawing from literatures on multiteam systems, diversity, and risk we posit and show that divergent (vs. convergent) preferences for risk have positive implications for multiteam system behavior and performance. Specifically, when the leadership and component teams hold divergent risk preferences, multiteam systems engage in more aspirational behavior and perform better. Alternatively, when the leadership and component teams hold convergent risk preferences, multiteam systems perform worse, partially because they display lower aspirational behavior. Consistent with a learning perspective, the beneficial effects of divergent preferences for risk increase over time. We discuss the implications of our findings for research and practice.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how convergence versus divergence in risk preferences between leadership and component teams during planning affects multiteam system behavior and performance during execution. It further asks whether divergent risk preferences facilitate multiteam system learning over time.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Multiteam system performance is highest when leadership and component teams hold divergent preferences for risk, and decreases when they hold more convergent preferences.
- Hypothesis 2. Aspirational behavior is highest when leadership and component teams hold divergent preferences for risk, and decreases when they hold more convergent preferences.
- Hypothesis 3. Aspirational behavior mediates the relationship between risk divergence/convergence and multiteam system performance.
- Hypothesis 4. Unwarranted risk behavior is lowest when leadership and component teams hold divergent preferences for risk, and increases when they hold more convergent preferences.
- Hypothesis 5. Unwarranted risk behavior mediates the relationship between risk divergence/convergence and multiteam system performance.
- The learning argument was posed as a research question rather than a hypothesis: "Do divergent (vs. convergent) preferences for risk facilitate multiteam system learning?"

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Vertical convergence or divergence between component-team risk preferences and leadership-team risk preferences; horizontal convergence or divergence between East and West component-team risk preferences in supplementary assumption tests.
- DV(s): Multiteam system performance, aspirational behavior, unwarranted risk behavior, and growth trajectories for these outcomes over ten simulation rounds.
- Mediators: Aspirational behavior and unwarranted risk behavior, with mediation supported for aspirational behavior and interpreted cautiously for unwarranted risk behavior because the main effect was not supported.
- Moderators: Time was used to test the learning assumption through growth trajectories rather than as a conventional interaction moderator.

The mechanism is that divergent risk preferences prevent premature consensus during planning and stimulate consideration of broader information, contingencies, and alternative plans. In these authority-differentiated multiteam systems, vertical divergence between leadership and component teams helps the system pursue well-vetted, high-reward aspirational behavior, which in turn improves objective performance. Convergence suppresses this aspirational behavior and is associated with weaker performance. The growth-curve results suggest that divergence becomes more beneficial over time for performance and aspirational behavior, consistent with the authors' learning interpretation.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from 92 fourteen-person multiteam systems composed of 1,288 United States Air Force officers enrolled in a leadership training course. Each system played the Leadership Development Simulation (LDS) in a divisional structure and consisted of three six-person interdependent teams — the East team, the West team, and the Leadership team; the simulation ran for 10 rounds, giving 10 round-level observations per system. Risk preference was recorded from pre-simulation planning worksheets that listed and explained six levels of risk-taking strategy ranging from low (1) to high (6) risk; each team discussed the worksheet and selected the strategy it thought the system should follow, and the chosen strategies were converted into a 6-point Likert-type scale (1 = low risk, 6 = high risk). The analyses use the risk plan chosen by the leadership team (L) and the average of the risk plans chosen by the component teams (C). Multiteam system performance is the objective score LDS generates at the end of the simulation, equal to the points gained by destroying enemy targets (offensive points) minus points from assets lost and base hits (defensive points). Aspirational behavior is a simulation-estimated standardized measure of the number of missions sent to the north in pursuit of high-value targets that were based on high-quality information from multiple parties (info RPAs and intelligence assets). Unwarranted risk behavior is the total number of unescorted missions sent to unexplored areas of the simulated grid, referred to as blind missions in this context.

Hypotheses 1, 2, and 4 were tested with polynomial regression and response surface modeling. Component team (C) and leadership team (L) risk preferences were scale-mean centered before the three second-order terms (C2, C x L, and L2) were calculated, and the congruence effect was tested as the curvature along the incongruence line (b3 - b4 + b5); 10,000 bootstrapped samples were used to construct 95% CIs for the slopes and intercepts of the principal axes. In the two mediator models the other mediator was entered as a control. Hypotheses 3 and 5 were tested with the block variable approach of Edwards and Cable (2009) estimated in Mplus 7.3, with 95% CIs for the indirect effects obtained from the Monte Carlo method with 20,000 replications. The learning research question was tested with multilevel latent growth curve analyses in Mplus 7.3 that use the 10 level-1 round observations to estimate a level-2 intercept and a level-2 slope of growth for each focal variable, with the polynomial risk preference terms predicting that growth slope. A working assumption was checked by re-estimating the polynomial models with Eastern (E) and Western (W) component team risk preferences to capture horizontal convergence/divergence. Risk preferences were recorded from the teams' own planning-worksheet choices rather than manipulated, and the paper reports no causal identification strategy for the risk preference effects.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported. The three second-order polynomial terms were jointly significant (F(3,86) = 3.28, p = .03) and the curvature along the incongruence line was positive and significant (curvature = 44.40, p < .01); the intercept of the second principal axis was not significantly different from zero (95% CI [-1.39, 3.88]) and its slope was not significantly different from 1 (95% CI [-1.65, 13.04]). Multiteam system performance is lowest when the leadership and component teams hold convergent preferences for risk at any level, whether they converge at high or low levels, and increases as they diverge. Hypothesis 2 was supported: the three second-order terms were jointly significant (F(3,85) = 3.77, p = .01) and the curvature along the incongruence line was positive and significant (curvature = 3.96, p = .01), so aspirational behavior is lowest under convergence at any level of risk and increases as the teams diverge. Two effects that were not hypothesized also emerged along the congruence line for aspirational behavior — a significant slope (2.52, p = .04) and a significant curvature (1.85, p = .02) — indicating that among systems that agreed, convergence at high levels of risk preference yielded more aspirational behavior than convergence at moderate or low levels. Hypothesis 3 was supported: aspirational behavior had a significant and positive relationship with performance (b = .51, p < .01) and the CI for the indirect effect of risk preference convergence/divergence on performance through aspirational behavior excluded zero (.09, .32).

Hypothesis 4 was not supported. The three second-order terms were not significant (F(3,85) = .66, p = .58) and the curvature along the incongruence line was not significant (curvature = -5.42, p = .21), although the pattern of results was in the expected direction. Support for Hypothesis 5 was not claimed. Unwarranted risk behavior had a negative and significant relationship with performance (b = -.26, p < .01) and the CI for the indirect effect did not contain zero (-.18, -.02), but because the main effect of risk preference convergence/divergence on unwarranted risk behavior was null (Hypothesis 4) and the block variable is a composite, the authors state that the Hypothesis 5 result should be interpreted with caution. The slope of the incongruence line was not significant for multiteam system performance (26.13, p = .14), aspirational behavior (.70, p = .69), or unwarranted risk behavior (-3.13, p = .51), so it did not matter whether the leadership team or the component teams held the higher risk preference.

On the learning research question, the multilevel growth curve analyses show that risk preference divergence had a significant effect on the slope of growth for performance (.98, p < .01) and on the slope of growth for aspirations (.45, p < .01), but not on the slope of growth for unwarranted risk behavior (-.05, p = .39); growth in performance and in aspirational behavior over time is greatest at the end points of the incongruence line, which represent the points of greatest divergence. Disaggregating performance, risk preference divergence significantly affected the slope of growth for both offensive (.60, p < .01) and defensive (.38, p < .01) scores. Consistent with the second working assumption, horizontal convergence/divergence among the East and West component teams did not affect multiteam system performance (curvature = 1.33, p = .92), aspirational behavior (curvature = -1.06, p = .40), or unwarranted risk behavior (curvature = -4.69, p = .19).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the prevailing multiteam systems argument that goal and plan convergence is generally preferable for system performance. It shows that, for risk preferences in divisional, authority-differentiated multiteam systems, divergence between leadership and component teams can improve performance rather than undermine it. It also contributes to team learning, risk, and diversity research by showing that deep-level divergence can be beneficial in a specific meso-level structure and that its effects strengthen over time.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that leaders of multiteam systems should not treat early disagreement about risk as evidence that the system is failing. During planning for complex, high-stakes work, leadership teams may need to create room for component teams to express divergent risk preferences and avoid rapid agreement for its own sake. The paper also suggests that leaders can deliberately elicit component-team perspectives before stating their own risk preferences, while recognizing that the benefits of divergence may emerge during execution rather than immediately.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the design did not collect survey data on members' attitudes or affective processes, such as psychological safety, team identification, satisfaction, or conflict. They also did not observe individual team-member risk preferences or the decision process through which teams formed a shared risk preference. Additional limitations include the simulation-specific operationalization of aspirational and unwarranted risk behavior, inability to record the full communication process, wide confidence intervals around principal-axis tests, and the fact that authority differentiation was a context rather than an experimentally varied construct.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how individual risk preferences become team-level risk preferences and how within-team divergence interacts with multiteam-system-level divergence. They also recommend studying aspirational and unwarranted risk behavior in other contexts, such as medical, disaster relief, or new product launch multiteam systems. Future work should test other strategic goals beyond risk, examine systems with different coordination structures and boundary-spanning arrangements, and distinguish system-level from component-team-level outcomes.
