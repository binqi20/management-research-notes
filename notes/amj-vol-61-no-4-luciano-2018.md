# Shared Team Experiences and Team Effectiveness: Unpacking the Contingent Effects of Entrained Rhythms and Task Characteristics

**Abstract**
This study explores the conditions under which shared team task-specific (STTS) experiences in crew-based arrangements may negatively influence team effectiveness. We suggest that the entrained rhythms featured in social entrainment theory act as a dual-edged sword with the potential to generate complacency detriments in addition to the commonly cited synchronization benefits. We argue that the manifestation and influence of the countervailing forces (i.e., synchronization and complacency) on the STTS experience—team effectiveness relationship will depend on salient task characteristics (i.e., frequency and difficulty). More specifically, frequently performed tasks create conditions for complacency to manifest (generating an inverted-U shaped relationship between STTS experience—team efficiency), whereas infrequently performed tasks do not (generating a positive, linear relationship). We further this distinction by layering on task difficulty that, we posit, acts to amplify the respective negative and positive consequences. Analyses of archival data from 8,236 surgeries performed over one year at a large hospital located in the southwestern region of the United States were consistent with our hypotheses and 30 semi-structured interviews with operating room personnel added richness and precision to our theory. Ancillary analyses on patient post-surgery recovery rate yielded additional insights. Implications and future directions are discussed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when shared team task-specific experience improves or harms team effectiveness in crew-based teams. It specifically examines whether task frequency and task difficulty shape whether entrained rhythms produce synchronization benefits or complacency detriments for surgical team efficiency and patient post-surgery recovery.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: For infrequent tasks, there will be a positive linear relationship between shared team task-specific experience and team efficiency.
- H1b: For frequent tasks, there will be a curvilinear (inverted-U) relationship between shared team task-specific experience and team efficiency.
- H2a: For infrequent tasks, task difficulty will moderate the relationship between shared team task-specific (STTS) experience and team efficiency; specifically, the positive relationship between STTS experience and team efficiency will be strengthened to the extent that task difficulty is relatively higher.
- H2b: For frequent tasks, task difficulty will moderate the relationship between shared team task-specific (STTS) experience and team efficiency; specifically, the relationship between STTS experience and team efficiency will shift from an upwardly trending positive curve at lower levels of task difficulty to a relatively negative trending curve at higher levels of task difficulty.
- H3: Team efficiency will be positively related to patient post-surgery recovery rate for both (a) infrequent and (b) frequent tasks.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Shared team task-specific experience, including a squared term for curvilinear effects in frequent tasks.
- DV(s): Team efficiency as the focal outcome; patient post-surgery recovery rate as an ancillary team-effectiveness outcome.
- Mediators: Knowledge accrual, synchronized entrained rhythms, and complacency are theorized mechanisms; they are not modeled as formal statistical mediators.
- Moderators: Task frequency and task difficulty.

The mechanism is that prior shared experience with the same team members on the same type of task creates shared knowledge and entrained rhythms that can help crew members coordinate efficiently. For infrequent tasks, the paper argues that these synchronization benefits are less likely to be offset by complacency, producing a positive relationship between STTS experience and efficiency, especially on difficult tasks. For frequent tasks, deeply familiar procedure rhythms can reduce vigilance and system monitoring, so high STTS experience can become inefficient through complacency; task difficulty amplifies these negative consequences because difficult cases have less room for error.

**Data & Measures**
The study draws on two phases of data collected in the perioperative unit of one large general medical and surgical community hospital in the southwestern region of the United States. Phase 1 collected archival data from the operating room medical records and patient billing records on all surgeries performed in the surgical suite from January 1, 2012 to December 31, 2013; the focal sample is the 8,236 surgeries performed in 2013, and the 2012 data (N = 6,799 surgeries) were used exclusively to compute shared experiences. Crew membership was determined from a pool of 62 unique surgeons (individual surgeries performed M = 132.82; SD = 145.02) and 171 other health care providers (individual surgeries performed M = 228.22; SD = 272.50), and 6,342 surgeries (77%) had unique team configurations. Phase 2 consisted of 30 semi-structured interviews with operating room personnel from the same hospital, conducted mostly in unoccupied hospital offices and break rooms during one of two site visits lasting a total of eight days, audio recorded and then transcribed; 26 individuals were interviewed during the first site visit (a 34% overall response rate, consisting of 6 surgeons, 7 anesthesia providers, 6 nurses, and 7 technicians) and four of those individuals were interviewed again during the second site visit, with an average interview length of 29 minutes; the authors reviewed the interview transcripts and notes to identify patterns related to team composition, task characteristics, team dynamics, and team effectiveness. Shared team task-specific (STTS) experience, the focal predictor, is operationalized as prior work experience with the same team members on the same type of procedure: for each surgery the authors counted the number of surgeries of the focal procedure type that each pair in the team had performed together during the prior rolling 12 months and then averaged across pairs at the team level (M = 43.40; SD = 79.60). Task frequency splits the 13 procedure types represented in the focal sample into four frequent types — general (n = 2,444; 29.7%), ear/nose/throat (n = 1,423; 17.3%), orthopedic (n = 1,331; 16.2%), and urology (n = 923; 11.2%) — each representing 10% or more of total procedure volume, and nine infrequent types: gynecology (n = 663; 8.1%), neurology (n = 409; 5.0%), vascular (n = 329; 4.0%), ophthalmology (n = 272; 3.3%), robotic assisted gynecology (n = 190; 2.3%), oral/dental (n = 141; 1.7%), plastics (n = 81; 1.0%), robotic assisted general (n = 17; .2%), and robotic assisted urology (n = 13; .2%); the authors state that this partition was not an arbitrary median split and that independent consultation with hospital administrators confirmed the threshold. Task difficulty is an equally weighted composite of five patient case characteristics — patient age, diabetes status, physical status (the anesthesia providers' ASA rating at the pre-surgery interview), out-patient/in-patient designation, and level of anesthesia — each standardized across the total number of surgeries and scored so that higher values represent greater task difficulty (M = .00; SD = .55). Team efficiency, the focal outcome, is the actual duration of each surgery relative to its scheduled time (one minus actual surgery duration divided by planned surgery duration; M = .46; SD = .33), where the scheduled time is set by the surgeon and the hospital on the basis of procedure complexity. Patient post-surgery recovery rate, the ancillary outcome, is the patient's post-surgery length of stay — the time between leaving surgery and being discharged from the hospital — rescaled so that higher numbers represent a faster recovery rate and converted from minutes into days. Covariates are procedure volume (the only variable modeled at the procedure grouping level), patient sex, task urgency, members' task experience, and membership churn. Hypotheses were tested with multi-level hierarchical linear analysis using procedure type as the higher-level (level 2) grouping and all other variables at the surgery (level 1); variables were converted to z scores at their respective levels of analysis, the sample was split by task frequency into frequent (n = 6,121; 74.3%) and infrequent (n = 2,115; 25.7%) subsamples, and a model-building approach added in sequence the linear effects, the curvilinear STTS experience squared term, the linear two-way interactions, and the interactions with curvilinear terms, with pseudo R2 reported for comparative purposes. The design is archival and observational and the estimates are associational: the authors describe the hospital as providing "a naturally occurring experiment of different levels of STTS experience with different task characteristics," but no formal causal-identification strategy is claimed beyond covariate adjustment and the procedure-type grouping.

**Key Findings**
The quantitative phase supported all six hypothesis tests. For infrequent tasks, STTS experience was positively related to team efficiency (b = .49, SE = .17, p < .01) and the STTS experience squared term was, as anticipated, not significantly related to team efficiency (b = -.32, SE = .22, n.s.); for frequent tasks, STTS experience was negatively related to team efficiency in Model 1 (b = -.14, SE = .03, p < .001) and the STTS squared term added in Model 2 was significant and negatively related to team efficiency (b = -.05, SE = .01, p < .001). The authors read this as a positive linear STTS experience—team efficiency relationship for infrequent tasks and a curvilinear (inverted-U) relationship for frequent tasks, providing support for Hypotheses 1a and 1b respectively. Adding the STTS experience by task difficulty interaction, the interaction was positive for infrequent tasks (b = .29, SE = .08, p < .001) and negative for frequent tasks (b = -.11, SE = .02, p < .001), consistent with Hypotheses 2a and 2b respectively; Model 4 indicated no evidence of a reversal of the curvilinear relationship from an inverted-U shape to a U shape for either infrequent or frequent tasks. Task difficulty was negatively related to team efficiency for both infrequent (b = -.26, SE = .03, p < .001) and frequent tasks (b = -.15, SE = .01, p < .001), and total efficiency variance explained (pseudo R2) was 57% for infrequent tasks and 19% for frequent tasks. For infrequent tasks the positive relationship between STTS experience and efficiency became stronger as tasks became more difficult, with simple slopes significant at relatively low (-1 SD; b = .45, SE = .20, p < .05) and relatively high (+1 SD; b = 1.02, SE = .21, p < .001) task difficulty and significantly positive throughout the upper 92% of the task difficulty range. For frequent tasks the negative interaction reflects a downward rotation of the inverted U as tasks become more difficult: at average task difficulty the relationship is nonsignificant through the lower 88% of the STTS experience distribution and becomes significantly negative among the highest 12% of values; at relatively low task difficulty it is positive through the lower 42% of the distribution, nonsignificant across the next 54%, and significantly negative only among the highest 4%; at relatively high task difficulty it is nonsignificant for the lower 49% and significantly negative beyond that point. Hypotheses 3a and 3b were also supported: team efficiency was positively related to patient post-surgery recovery rate for both infrequent tasks (b = .14, SE = .02, p < .001) and frequent tasks (b = .15, SE = .01, p < .001). The authors emphasize that the ancillary analyses of patient post-surgery recovery rate are exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously. For infrequent tasks those analyses generally paralleled the team efficiency results: STTS experience had a positive linear relationship with recovery rate, such that higher STTS experience was related to faster recovery (b = .28, SE = .13, p < .05), task difficulty had a negative linear relationship with recovery (b = -.30, SE = .02, p < .001), and STTS experience and task difficulty evidenced a positive interaction (b = .30, SE = .07, p < .001), with the simple slope nonsignificant at relatively low task difficulty (b = .15, SE = .16, n.s.) and significant at relatively high task difficulty (b = .74, SE = .17, p < .001); the recovery-rate pseudo R2 increased from about 12% to about 45%. For frequent tasks, task difficulty exhibited a consistent negative relationship with recovery rate (b = -.46, SE = .01, p < .001) while, in a result the authors flag as surprising, STTS experience exhibited a U-shaped curvilinear effect (STTS experience b = -.18, SE = .05, p < .001; STTS experience squared b = .02, SE = .01, p < .05), together with a positive linear interaction with task difficulty (b = .15, SE = .02, p < .001) and a small negative curvilinear interaction term (b = -.03, SE = .01, p < .05); here the pseudo R2 increased from about 4% to about 37%. The qualitative phase converged with and added precision to this pattern: interviewees described the shared knowledge and entrained rhythms that develop on frequently performed procedures, articulated complacency in their own terms ("People are more comfortable with each other, so they're more lax on things"), and, when directly probed on the most surprising ancillary result, explained that recognizing their own laxity on a frequent but difficult case prompts additional checks beyond standard operating procedures — so frequent tasks engender a sense of comfort and lower vigilance, but when the team realizes its complacency it adjusts and delivers quality care, albeit at a delayed pace.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends social entrainment theory by treating entrained rhythms as a dual-edged process that can generate both synchronization benefits and complacency detriments. It contributes to team effectiveness research by showing that shared experience is not uniformly beneficial and that task frequency and task difficulty are important boundary conditions in crew-based arrangements. It also integrates archival and interview evidence to connect shared team experience, task characteristics, efficiency, and patient recovery in a field setting rather than a laboratory context.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that crew staffing decisions should consider both task frequency and task difficulty rather than simply maximizing repeated team membership. For infrequent and more difficult procedures, managers may benefit from building higher levels of shared team task-specific experience; for frequent procedures, especially difficult ones, managers should watch for complacency and use interventions such as briefing, teamwork training, and debriefing to maintain vigilance without destroying useful rhythms. In hospitals, the paper frames these staffing and intervention choices as consequential for operating-room efficiency, patient recovery, and cost.

**Limitations**
The authors note that complacency is difficult to examine outside the laboratory because interviews and observations are vulnerable to evaluation apprehension and Hawthorne effects. The archival data cannot directly measure the strength or dynamics of entrained rhythms during each surgery, so the study infers those processes from records and interview evidence. The empirical setting is a single U.S. hospital and one type of crew-based team, which limits comparison across hospitals, procedure-volume profiles, and team structures.

**Future Research**
The authors call for richer measures of entrained rhythms, including experience-sampling designs that track rhythm dynamics over time and distinguish why surgeries become faster or slower. They also recommend studying additional task characteristics, unanticipated events, attention, and situational cues that may shape complacency and synchronization. Further work should compare hospitals with different procedure volumes, examine other team structures such as intact teams, ad hoc teams, and multiteam systems, and assess individual differences, composition, strategic staffing, and caregiver well-being implications.
