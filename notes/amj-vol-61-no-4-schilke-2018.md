# A Micro-institutional Inquiry into Resistance to Environmental Pressures

**Abstract**
This article contributes to the emerging stream of micro-institutional research, which zooms in on the internal organizational processes that are responsible for organizations’ differential responses to the external environment. Specifically, the investigation offers new knowledge of how organizational identity processes can shape whether decisionmakers will resist versus give in to environmental pressures. Building on the notion that organizational identity acts as a filter through which decision-makers relate to the external environment, I develop the theoretical argument that strong organizational identification increases resistance to environmental pressures due to two mechanisms: (1) it bolsters the decision-maker’s certainty and (2) it deflects the decision-maker’s attention from the environment. A series of laboratory experiments not only test the mediated relationship between organizational identification and resistance to environmental pressures but also contrast different types of organizational identity. The empirical results support the hypothesized positive link between organizational identification and resistance, which becomes particularly strong when the organizational identity is normative (vs. utilitarian). The findings reported here enrich institutional theory by adding microfoundations to organizational practice adoption decisions and shedding new light on relevant enabling conditions for agency and within-field heterogeneity.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why organizational decision-makers facing the same environmental pressures vary in whether they resist those pressures or give in to them. More specifically, it asks whether organizational identification affects resistance, whether certainty and attention explain that effect, and whether normative versus utilitarian identity content changes resistance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. There will be a positive effect of organizational identification on resistance to environmental pressures.
- Hypothesis 2. Organizational identification has a positive effect on certainty, and certainty in turn has a positive effect on resistance to environmental pressures, such that certainty mediates the positive effect of organizational identification on resistance to environmental pressures.
- Hypothesis 3. Organizational identification has a negative effect on attention, and attention in turn has a negative effect on resistance to environmental pressures, such that attention mediates the positive effect of organizational identification on resistance to environmental pressures.
- Hypothesis 4. Resistance to environmental pressures will be stronger when the organizational identity is normative (vs. utilitarian), and this effect will be mediated by attention to environmental stimuli.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Strength of organizational identification; organizational identity type, especially normative versus utilitarian identity.
- DV(s): Resistance to environmental pressures, measured as staying with an initial decision despite mimetic peer-group information or resisting ISO 31000 adoption pressure.
- Mediators: Decision-maker certainty and attention to environmental stimuli.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The proposed mechanism is cognitive and identity-based. Strong organizational identification increases resistance because it bolsters the decision-maker's certainty and reduces attention to environmental stimuli. Normative identity further strengthens resistance relative to utilitarian identity because it turns attention inward toward organizational ideals rather than outward toward competitive or institutional comparison.

**Data & Measures**
Three experiments test the four hypotheses (Table 1). Study 1 (Hypotheses 1-3; mimetic pressures) recruited 187 participants through the subject pool of the behavioral lab of a large public university; 18 were excluded (2 for self-reported English proficiency of novice or less, 16 for failing a hidden screener item), yielding a usable sample of 169 (72% female, Mage = 21) randomly assigned to weak (n = 84) or strong (n = 85) organizational identification. Identification was manipulated with a variant of the minimal group paradigm: a word-and-number association test that ostensibly sorted participants by thinking style (in reality all were assigned to the "inductive thinkers" group), a voluntary group-commitment question in the strong condition only, a Tajfel-matrix intergroup reward allocation task (administered before the main task in the strong condition and after it in the weak condition), and a purported "group involvement score" of 53 (strong) versus 27 (weak) against a stated average of 40; the manipulation check was a 4-item identification measure on a 9-point scale (alpha = 0.79). The main task was a computerized variant of Berger and Fisek's (1970) standardized experimental situation: 25 binary strategic decisions for a group-run flower shop, in which a competing group's computer-generated response disagreed with the participant's initial choice in 20 of the 25 trials; resistance, the dependent variable, was each participant's average proportion of "stay with the initial choice" responses across those 20 trials (1 = stay, 0 = change). Certainty was measured post-task with three items on a 5-point scale (alpha = 0.82), and attention was proxied at the trial level by reaction time for the second-stage decision, log-transformed because of skew and averaged across the 20 trials. Study 2 (Hypothesis 4; mimetic pressures) used 180 business-major undergraduates at a large public university, excluded 7 on the same English-proficiency and attention-screener criteria for a usable sample of 173 (51% female, Mage = 21), and randomly assigned them to a utilitarian (n = 92) or normative (n = 81) identity condition; identity content was manipulated by having participants add the final sentence to a group "mission statement" after seeing three normative or three utilitarian statements ostensibly written by teammates, then choose between two decision-premise completions, with all other procedures identical to the strong identification condition of Study 1; certainty used the same 3-item measure (alpha = 0.84) and attention again used the logarithm of reaction time. Study 3 (Hypothesis 4; normative pressures) recruited 531 adults via Amazon Mechanical Turk, excluded 25 by an attention screener for a usable sample of n = 506 (48.0% female, mean age 36.6 years, SD = 11.8; mean 14.6 years of full-time work experience, SD = 11.1; 56% reported having had a significant say in an organization's key strategic decisions), and randomly assigned them to a control condition with no identity manipulation (n = 170), a utilitarian identity condition (n = 172), or a normative identity condition (n = 164); the main task presented background plus pros and cons on the ISO 31000 risk-management standard, and resistance was the average of two reverse-coded 9-point support items (alpha = 0.96); certainty used the 3-item scale (alpha = 0.90) and attention was captured by the natural logarithm of the time spent reading about the ISO standard. Identification is causally identified by random assignment in a randomized experiment, which the paper argues allows "strong internal causal inference"; estimation used one-way ANOVAs plus bootstrapped mediation with the PROCESS macro in SPSS (Hayes, 2013, Models 4 and 6; 5,000 bootstrap samples), with Study 3's multiple-mediator model estimated on the two identity conditions only (n = 336).

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3. A one-way ANOVA showed significantly greater resistance in the strong identification condition (M = 0.89) than in the weak identification condition (M = 0.81), F(1, 167) = 10.44, d = 0.50, p ≤ .01, supporting Hypothesis 1. Simple mediation tests produced positive, significant indirect effects via certainty (ab = 0.019, 95% CI [0.005, 0.042]) and via attention (ab = 0.029, 95% CI [0.008, 0.057]); in the multiple-mediator model both parallel indirect effects remained significant (certainty ab = 0.017, 95% CI [0.005, 0.036]; attention ab = 0.027, 95% CI [0.005, 0.055]) and the total indirect effect was significant (ab = 0.046, 95% CI [0.020, 0.079]), while the serial indirect effect through both mediators was not (ab = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.013]). The individual paths ran in the predicted directions: identification to certainty b = 0.56, p ≤ .001, and certainty to resistance b = 0.03, p ≤ .05; identification to attention b = -0.16, p ≤ .05, and attention to resistance b = -0.17, p ≤ .001. The direct effect of identification on resistance was positive but no longer significant once certainty and attention were included, which the paper reads as full or indirect-only mediation. Study 2 supported Hypothesis 4: resistance was significantly greater in the normative identity condition (M = 0.92) than in the utilitarian identity condition (M = 0.87), F(1, 171) = 4.69, d = 0.33, p ≤ .05, and the indirect effect via attention was significant (ab = 0.007, 95% CI [0.001, 0.021]), whereas the indirect effect via certainty (ab = 0.000, 95% CI [-0.010, 0.008]), the serial indirect effect (ab = 0.000, 95% CI [-0.001, 0.001]), and the total indirect effect (ab = 0.007, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.021]) were not significant. Study 3 supported Hypothesis 4 again, under normative rather than mimetic pressures and with a nonstudent sample: resistance differed across the three conditions, F(2, 503) = 11.07, η² = 0.04, p ≤ .001, and was significantly greater in the normative identity condition (M = 5.67) than in the utilitarian identity condition (M = 4.52), F(1, 334) = 18.47, d = 0.47, p ≤ .001; resistance in the control condition (M = 4.71) was significantly smaller than in the normative identity condition, F(1, 332) = 13.94, d = 0.41, p ≤ .001, but not significantly different from the utilitarian identity condition, F(1, 340) = 0.55, d = 0.08, p > .10. In Study 3 the indirect effect of identity type via attention was significant (ab = 0.139, 95% CI [0.035, 0.310]) and the total indirect effect was significant (ab = 0.138, 95% CI [0.024, 0.320]), while the indirect effect via certainty (ab = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.031, 0.067]) and the serial indirect effect (ab = -0.004, 95% CI [-0.026, 0.002]) were not; the main effect of identity type remained statistically significant in that model, which the paper reads as partial mediation. Certainty thus carried a significant indirect effect only in Study 1, where it was the hypothesized mediator; in Studies 2 and 3, where it was added as a second potential mediator alongside attention, its indirect effect was not significant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional theory by adding microfoundations to explanations of organizational responses to environmental pressures. It shows that organizational decision-makers' identification and identity content help explain within-field heterogeneity rather than treating organizations as passively conforming to macro-level pressures. It also integrates organizational identity research with institutional theory and demonstrates how experiments can be used to test micro-level institutional mechanisms.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests, with explicit caution, that organizations may manage environmental conformity versus resistance partly through identity salience and identity content. When organizations seek to implement practices that challenge established field templates, leaders who strongly identify with the organization may be more willing to resist environmental pressure. The implication is not that resistance is always beneficial, but that identity processes can shape whether organizations automatically conform or maintain agency under pressure.

**Limitations**
The paper largely brackets how organizational identity is constructed and instead focuses on its consequences for resistance. It also treats individual decision-makers as representatives of organizational actors, while acknowledging that organization-level outcomes may emerge through more complex aggregation, interaction, and interdependence. Finally, the experimental findings generalize through theory rather than direct one-to-one correspondence with large organizations in field settings.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how organizational identity develops and is negotiated in organizations, especially through qualitative, practitioner, field-replication, and mixed-method designs. Further work could study how social interaction and collective aggregation translate individual-level responses into organization-level action. The paper also calls for research on competing institutional logics, non-binary responses to institutional pressure, institutional work strategies such as challenging or manipulating institutions, and legitimacy judgments by external audiences.
