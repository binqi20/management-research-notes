# Examining Follower Responses to Transformational Leadership from a Dynamic, Person–Environment Fit Perspective

**Abstract**
We invoke the person–environment fit paradigm to examine on a daily basis follower affective, attitudinal, and behavioral responses to transformational leadership needed and received. Results from two experience sampling method (ESM) studies suggested that positive affect was higher on days when transformational leadership received fit follower needs (compared to days when the amount received was deficient or in excess of follower needs) and on days when absolute levels of fit was higher. We also found that positive affect mediated the within-person effects of transformational leadership needed and received on subordinates’ daily work attitudes (Studies 1 and 2) and organizational citizenship behaviors (Study 2). Supplemental analyses in Study 2 revealed that subordinates need more transformational leadership when they experience more challenge stressors, face greater uncertainty at work, and perform more meaningful work.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how followers respond on a daily basis when the transformational leadership they receive fits, falls short of, or exceeds the transformational leadership they need. It also asks whether positive affect explains the within-person links from transformational leadership needed and received to daily work attitudes and organizational citizenship behaviors, and which daily conditions predict stronger need for transformational leadership.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Within individuals, positive affect will be higher when transformational leadership needed and received are equal compared to when transformational leadership is deficient of, or exceeds, needed amounts.
- Hypothesis 2. Within individuals, positive affect will be higher when transformational leadership needed and received are both high compared to when transformational leadership needed and received are both low.
- Hypothesis 3. Within individuals, positive affect will mediate the relationship of transformational leadership needed and received to subordinates' job satisfaction (Hypothesis 3a) and satisfaction with supervision (Hypothesis 3b).
- Hypothesis 4. Within individuals, positive affect will mediate the relationship between transformational leadership needed and received and subordinates' OCB-O (Hypothesis 4a) and subordinates' OCB-I (Hypothesis 4b).
- Scope of testing: Study 1 tested Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3; Study 2 tested Hypotheses 1 through 4. The Study 2 examination of within-person predictors of transformational leadership needed (challenge stressors, hindrance stressors, uncertainty, and work meaningfulness) was exploratory rather than hypothesized; the authors state that opposing perspectives left it unclear whether followers desire transformational leadership when their work lacks meaning or when performing work that is already meaningful.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily transformational leadership needed, daily transformational leadership received, their fit/misfit, and absolute level of fit.
- DV(s): Positive affect, job satisfaction, satisfaction with supervision, OCB-O, OCB-I, and exploratory daily transformational leadership needed.
- Mediators: Positive affect mediates the joint within-person effects of transformational leadership needed and received on job satisfaction, satisfaction with supervision, and organizational citizenship behaviors.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper as formal moderators; the paper models fit, deficiency, excess, and absolute level of fit through polynomial response-surface terms.

The mechanism combines person–environment fit theory with affective events theory. Transformational leadership is treated as an environmental supply, while followers' daily need for such leadership varies with activated psychological needs and work conditions. Fit between needed and received transformational leadership should raise positive affect; deficiency and excess should reduce it. Positive affect then carries the daily fit experience into work attitudes and discretionary citizenship behavior, while challenge stressors, uncertainty, and meaningful work help explain when followers need more transformational leadership.

**Data & Measures**
- Design status: two experience sampling method (ESM) studies of within-person variation; the design is associational rather than experimental. The authors note that in Study 1 participants completed all measures at the same time each day, so they cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causality, and that Study 2 was designed with time-separated measures of transformational leadership needed, transformational leadership received, and positive affect.
- Study 1 data and procedure: participants were 69 MBA students who held full-time jobs, with participation satisfying a course requirement. They completed an in-class survey plus 15 online daily surveys at the end of each workday, Monday through Friday, for three weeks. Responses from four individuals who completed zero or one daily survey were eliminated, leaving 65 individuals who provided 846 daily responses out of a possible 975 (65 participants x 15 workdays). Controlling for lagged scores on the dependent variables reduced the sample to 747 daily observations. The final sample was 36 women and 29 men with an average age of 29.9 years (SD = 3.8).
- Study 1 measures: transformational leadership needed and received = four items from Avolio and Bass' (2004) Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ) that respectively capture idealized leadership, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration; the daily instrument presented each item once and asked two questions about each behavior, how much the supervisor had used the behavior that day (received) and how much of the behavior would have been adequate (needed), on a scale from 1 = "hardly any" to 7 = "a great amount" (average a = .89 needed, .85 received). Positive and negative affect = Watson, Clark, and Tellegen's (1988) 20-item PANAS, 1 = "not at all" to 5 = "extremely" (average a = .96 and .87). Job satisfaction = three items from Edwards and Rothbard (1999) and satisfaction with supervision = three items from Spector's (1997) job satisfaction survey, both on a 7-point agreement scale (average a = .97 and .95).
- Study 2 data and procedure: employees of a large Midwestern university were contacted by e-mail and could forward the invitation to family or friends; the first 109 individuals who signed up composed the initial sample. Participants completed three surveys per day (workday-start, mid-workday, workday-end) for 15 consecutive workdays and were compensated up to $75. Responses were eliminated from individuals who worked independently of their formal supervisor, and missing or unusable data reduced the sample to 93 individuals who provided 913 day-level observations, defined as days on which individuals completed all three surveys. Participants were mostly women (78.9%) and Caucasian (82.3%), average age 43.3 years (SD = 10.96), average tenure with the employer 7.4 years (SD = 6.9), employed in a variety of technical, clerical, and administrative positions.
- Study 2 measures: transformational leadership needed and received = 10-item measures sampling more broadly from the content domain than Study 1's four items, built from the two strongest-loading items for each of the four MLQ sub-dimensions (idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, individualized consideration) plus the two strongest-loading remaining MLQ items. Transformational leadership needed was measured on the workday-start survey (for testing Hypotheses 1 through 4) and on the mid-workday survey (for the supplemental predictor analysis), prefaced "how much of this behavior do you want from your supervisor today?"; transformational leadership received was assessed on the mid-workday survey, prefaced "how much of this behavior have you received today?"; both used the Study 1 response format (average a = .96 workday-start needed, .97 mid-workday needed, .97 mid-workday received). Affect, job satisfaction, and satisfaction with supervision used the Study 1 measures, with affect collected at the beginning and end of the workday and satisfaction on the mid-workday and workday-end surveys. Organizational citizenship behavior = eight items from Lee and Allen's (2002) measures of OCB-O (four items) and OCB-I (four items) on a 7-point agreement scale, reported at mid-workday for the period since the start of the workday and at workday-end for the period since mid-workday.
- Study 2 predictors of transformational leadership needed, measured at workday-start: challenge stressors and hindrance stressors = Rodell and Judge's (2009) eight-item measures, with participants randomly assigned four of the eight items from the challenge and hindrance stressor subscales, respectively; uncertainty and work meaningfulness = four-item scales from Colquitt, LePine, Piccolo, Zapata, and Rich (2012) and from Bunderson and Thompson (2009), respectively, adapted to reflect daily experiences (average a = .93 challenge stressors, .92 hindrance stressors, .95 uncertainty, .94 work meaningfulness).
- Analysis: both studies used first-order autoregressive HLM. The joint effects of transformational leadership needed and received on positive affect were estimated with polynomial regression including both squared terms and the product term (Edwards, 2002), from which the slope and curvature of the fit and misfit lines were derived; models controlled for the lagged dependent variable in Study 1 and the baseline value in Study 2. Mediation was tested with a block variable approach (Heise, 1972; Marsden, 1983), with Study 1 confidence intervals for the indirect effect estimated using PRODCLIN. Confirmatory factor analyses supported the proposed six-factor structure in Study 1 and 12-factor structure in Study 2, and in both studies a two-factor leadership model separating needed from received fit better than a one-factor model.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (65 individuals; 747-846 daily observations), Hypothesis 1 supported: along the misfit line for positive affect there was positive slope (.33, p < .01) and negative curvature (-.25, p < .01). Probing 1 SD into each region, the slope was positive in the region of deficiency (.95, p < .01) and negative in the region of excess (-.28, p < .01), meaning positive affect was lower on days when transformational leadership received was deficient of, or in excess of, followers' needs. The authors report that the effects of deficient transformational leadership were stronger than the effects of excess transformational leadership.
- Study 1, Hypothesis 2 supported: along the fit line for positive affect there was positive slope (.14, p < .01) and null curvature (-.01, p > .10), so fit at higher levels was associated with higher positive affect than fit at lower levels.
- Study 1, Hypotheses 3a and 3b supported: the transformational leadership block variable predicted positive affect (g' = .29, p < .01), and positive affect predicted job satisfaction (g' = .46, p < .01) and satisfaction with supervision (g' = .22, p < .01). The indirect effect through positive affect was significant for job satisfaction (IE' = .13, p < .01, 95% CI [.10, .17]) and for satisfaction with supervision (IE' = .06, p < .01, 95% CI [.04, .09]).
- Study 1 supplemental analyses of negative affect: the polynomial equation explained less level-1 variance in negative affect (pseudo-R2 = .03) than in positive affect (pseudo-R2 = .12). Negative affect showed positive slope along the fit line (g = .03, p < .05) and negative slope along the misfit line (g = -.15, p < .01), with negative indirect effects through negative affect on job satisfaction (IE' = -.04, p < .05, 95% CI [-.06, -.02]) and satisfaction with supervision (IE' = -.02, p < .05, 95% CI [-.04, -.01]). Results were unchanged when controlling for participant age and gender and when the lagged dependent variables were excluded.
- Study 2 (93 individuals; 913 day-level observations), Hypothesis 1 supported: along the misfit line for workday-end positive affect there was null slope (-.03, p > .10) and negative curvature (-.10, p < .05); 1 SD into the region of deficiency the slope was positive and significant (.25, p < .01) and 1 SD into the region of excess it was negative and significant (-.31, p < .01).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 2 supported: along the fit line for positive affect there was positive slope (.05, p < .05) and null curvature (.02, p > .10), so positive affect was higher on days of higher absolute fit.
- Study 2, Hypotheses 3a, 3b, 4a, and 4b supported: the transformational leadership block variable predicted positive affect (g' = .11, p < .01), and positive affect predicted job satisfaction (g' = .20, p < .01), satisfaction with supervision (g' = .09, p < .05), OCB-O (g' = .17, p < .01), and OCB-I (g' = .16, p < .01). The indirect effect through positive affect was significant for job satisfaction (IE' = .02, p < .01), satisfaction with supervision (IE' = .01, p < .05), OCB-O (IE' = .02, p < .01), and OCB-I (IE' = .02, p < .01).
- Study 2 negative affect: the polynomial terms were unrelated to negative affect, with no evidence of slope or curvature along the fit or misfit lines. The block variable was nonetheless associated with negative affect (g' = .08, p < .05) and had a negative indirect effect on job satisfaction through negative affect (IE' = -.02, p < .05).
- Study 2 exploratory predictors of transformational leadership needed, controlling for positive affect (g = .19, p < .01) and negative affect (g = .36, p < .05): workday-start transformational leadership needed was associated with challenge stressors (g = .15, p < .01), uncertainty (g = .14, p < .01), and work meaningfulness (g = .13, p < .05). At smaller magnitudes these associations persisted to mid-workday transformational leadership needed: challenge stressors (g = .11, p < .05), uncertainty (g = .11, p < .05), and work meaningfulness (g = .12, p < .10). Hindrance stressors were unrelated to transformational leadership needed at workday-start (g = .04, n.s.) and at mid-workday (g = -.03, n.s.).
- Cross-study pattern: the authors report that results were largely consistent across the two ESM studies with one difference. In Study 1 positive affect was lower on days when transformational leadership was deficient compared to days involving excess levels, whereas in Study 2 excess and deficient transformational leadership were equally problematic; the authors describe transformational leadership as "moderately antagonistic" in Study 1 and "highly antagonistic" in Study 2 and state that they can only speculate as to why.
- Sub-dimension re-analyses: rerunning the polynomial regressions separately for each of the four transformational leadership sub-dimensions, the fit hypotheses were supported for individualized consideration and partially supported for the other dimensions in Study 1, and were partially supported for idealized influence only in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to transformational leadership theory by challenging the assumption that more transformational leadership is always better. It theorizes and tests transformational leadership as a dynamic, episodic phenomenon whose effects depend on day-level fit with follower needs. It also extends P–E fit theory by showing the value of modeling need fulfillment within persons over time rather than treating within-person variation as error.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that transformational leadership training should help leaders diagnose followers' dynamic needs and calibrate the amount of transformational behavior they provide. Leaders should avoid both under-supplying and over-supplying transformational leadership, because excess can be less favorable than fit. They should also pay attention to the type of stressors followers face, because challenge stressors and uncertainty evoke need for transformational leadership whereas hindrance stressors may call for a more transactional response.

**Limitations**
The authors note that their conceptualization relies on Bass' model of transformational leadership, despite criticisms of that model and unresolved questions about whether its between-person structure is isomorphic at the within-person level. They also did not examine fit for other forms of leadership needed and received, such as transactional leadership. Finally, the studies focused on a limited set of outcomes and did not test why some leaders are better than others at tailoring transformational behavior to follower needs.

**Future Research**
The authors call for more focused tests of the Bass model at within- and between-person levels, and for dynamic P–E fit research that embeds transformational leadership within broader models of leadership needed and received. They encourage future ESM work on additional outcomes such as justice, stress, intrinsic motivation, creativity, trust, empowerment, and organizational commitment. They also recommend research on leader-side predictors of fit, including depletion, personality, trait empathy, and political skill.
