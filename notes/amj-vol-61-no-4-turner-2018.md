# Watching the Clock: Action Timing, Patterning, and Routine Performance

**Abstract**
This study contributes to research on the temporality in routines by proposing that action timing is a patterning mechanism, distinct from the sequence-based patterning that has been the focus of current research. We examine the effect of this mechanism by investigating how recurrent action timing—and its reproduction—affect the effectiveness of routine performance. We further theorize and show that multiplicity in the performative and the ostensive aspects of the routine influence the strength of timingbased patterning and its effects. We test our ideas using a unique dataset that tracks specific garbage collection actions and captures their precise timing, combined with corresponding data on customer complaints about missed collections. Our findings support our theoretical predictions and advance current understanding about different temporal patterning mechanisms in routines.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how the timing of actions affects patterning and routine performance in dispersed, interdependent service routines. It specifically examines whether reproducing action timing improves effective routine performance and whether multiplicity in performative and ostensive aspects strengthens or weakens that timing-based effect.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: Reproducing the timing of organizational actions in a specific routine performance is positively associated with its effective performance.
H2: The greater the multiplicity in the timing of routine performances, the weaker the effect of reproducing action timing on the effective performance of the routine.
H3a: The more outsider participants with bureaucratic experience are involved in the routine, the weaker the effect of reproducing action timing on its effective performance.
H3b: The more outsiders have a co-present point of view on the routine, the stronger the effect of reproducing action timing on its effective performance.
H4: Different conditions for performing the routine will weaken the effect of reproducing action timing on its effective performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Reproducing the timing of organizational actions in a focal routine performance, operationalized as same-street/same-time matches between the current route performance and prior performances within the previous 30 days.
- DV(s): Effective routine performance, measured as the count of customer reports of missed waste collection service.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Historical consistency in action timing, customers' bureaucratic experience, customers' co-present point of view, and different performance conditions represented by city-observed holidays.

The mechanism is timing-based patterning. Recurrent action timing creates a temporal structure that lets outsiders anticipate insider actions using clock time rather than the full action sequence of the routine. When current performances reproduce historically established timing, customers' actions align more closely with collection crews' actions, reducing missed-service complaints. This effect is stronger when historical timing consistency and customer co-presence make the timing pattern more recognizable, and weaker when bureaucratic experience makes customers more likely to follow formal rules rather than timing-based expectations.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: The City of San Diego and its Environmental Services Department supplied work-order records of customer reports of missed waste collections and GPS satellite tracking that records the time and location of garbage collection actions on the routes every few seconds. Explanatory and control variables also draw on informational databases such as Factiva, the City of San Diego web site, U.S. Census Bureau data on San Diego residents, and the National Climatic Data Center.
- Sample construction: Weekly route sets were randomly selected from the total population of routes for the city, producing 67 route sets involving approximately 300 distinct routes and 100 employees, with a typical route covering approximately 1,000 customer households. The data span July 2005 through December 2005; the first month is used as a baseline for measures such as employee performance experience, so the statistical analyses cover August 2005 through December 2005. Missing data were treated as missing at random and handled by listwise deletion. The regression models in Table 2 report 4,356 observations and 98 employees.
- Qualitative component: 12 open-ended interviews with industry informants such as waste organization presidents and state government leaders, plus 28 semistructured interviews with directors, supervisors, and field-level employees in six North Carolina waste collection organizations; interviews averaged a little over an hour and ranged between 30 minutes and four hours. The hypotheses are tested on the San Diego quantitative data, with the qualitative data providing corroboration and illustration.
- DV: Effective routine performance is measured by the count of customer reports of missed waste collection service, which the authors describe as a reverse indicator. Reports are aggregated by route from the collection day through the sixth day after it and include only initial reports, excluding follow-up calls; for a day of service on a route the range of complaints was 0 to 11.
- IV: Reproduction is built from a time index that records the collection truck's street location at 10-minute intervals. A match occurs when the truck was on the same street at approximately the same time across two performances, using a plus-or-minus 30-minute window drawn from field accounts of customer tolerance for being "on time." Matches are scaled by the number of possible matches so the measure runs from 0 to 1, then averaged across all comparisons between the focal performance and prior performances on that route within the previous 30 days (mean 0.24, SD 0.15).
- Moderators: HistoricalConsistency is a reverse indicator of multiplicity in timing, averaging matches across all prior performances of the route within the past 30 days on the same 0-to-1 scale. Bureaucratic experience is GovernmentEmp, the percentage of customers in the route's dominant zip code who are government employees; co-present point of view is RetirementAge, the percentage of that zip code's population aged 65 or older. Both Census measures are averages of the 2000 Census and either the 2010 Census or the 2011 estimates, used as proxies for 2005 conditions. Different performance conditions are captured by binary MajorHoliday (Thanksgiving, Christmas) and MinorHoliday (Labor Day, Columbus Day, Veterans Day) indicators for holiday weeks, which Model 5 splits into day-off and workday collections.
- Controls: monthly dummies for September through December with August as the reference, RouteWorkload, light and moderate precipitation dummies, wind speed, customer income, driver age, driver department tenure, and driver experience on the focal route.
- Estimation: negative binomial panel regressions using the xtnbreg procedure in Stata, chosen over Poisson because the sample variance is more than twice the sample mean, with fixed effects for drivers based on a Hausman test, incidence rate ratios for interpreting interactions, simple slope tests at low, mean, and high reference levels, and one-tail significance tests. A two-stage residual inclusion test using the focal driver's average reproduction on other routes within the past 30 days as an instrument did not provide evidence that reproducing action timing was endogenous, so it was treated as exogenous. The focal prediction is stated as an association rather than as a causally identified effect.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. Reproduction carried a negative and significant coefficient in Models 2 and 3 (p < .01 and p < .05, respectively), meaning greater reproduction of action timing predicts fewer missed-collection complaints. From Model 3, a one-standard-deviation increase in reproducing action timing produced a 6% decrease in the expected number of customer complaints.
- H2 supported. The interaction between HistoricalConsistency and Reproduction was negative in Model 4 (p < .05), consistent with the prediction that greater historical consistency strengthens, and greater multiplicity in timing weakens, the effect of reproduced timing. Simple slope tests show the effect of Reproduction was not statistically significant at low historical consistency, produced a 5% decrease in complaints at the mean (p < .10), and an 8% decrease at high historical consistency (p < .05).
- H3a supported. The interaction between GovernmentEmp and Reproduction was positive and significant (p < .05), indicating that bureaucratic experience weakens the effect of reproduced timing. Simple slope tests show the effect of Reproduction was not statistically significant where government employment was high, produced a 5% decrease in complaints at the mean (p < .10), and a 10% decrease at low government employment (p < .05).
- H3b supported. The interaction between RetirementAge and Reproduction was negative and significant (p < .01). Simple slope tests show a one-standard-deviation increase in Reproduction produced a 12% decrease in complaints where the retirement-age share was high (p < .01) and a 5% decrease at the mean (p < .10); at the low level of retirement age the effect was not statistically significant.
- H4 not supported in the main test. Model 4 shows no statistically significant interaction between Reproduction and either major or minor city-observed holiday weeks. Splitting holiday-affected collections into "days off" and "workdays" in Model 5, the major-holiday day-off interaction was positive and marginally significant (p < .10) consistent with the H4 prediction, while the major-holiday workday interaction and both minor-holiday interactions were not statistically significant. The authors read this pattern as providing some support for Hypothesis 4. Model 5 reproduces the same support for Hypotheses 2, 3a, and 3b as Model 4.
- Main effects in Model 3: retirement age and different performance conditions have positive effects on complaints, government employment has a negative effect on complaints, and historical consistency is not statistically significant.
- Sensitivity checks: no curvilinear moderating effect was found for either retirement age or government employment, and the results were robust to adding the dominant zip code's overall employment percentage and its interaction with reproduction; that added interaction itself weakened the effect of reproduction (p < .10).
- Convergence with the qualitative component: field interviews corroborated that complaints reflect performances that failed to accord with customer expectations shaped by prior performances, and that historical consistency strengthens timing-based patterning, captured in a supervisor's comment that people "sort of set their watches by the garbage collection."

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends routine dynamics theory by distinguishing timing-based patterning from sequence-based patterning. It shows that clock time can function as an external temporal patterning mechanism for routines performed across time, space, and organizational boundaries. It also contributes by showing that timing-based patterning is contingent: multiplicity in performative and ostensive aspects shapes whether reproduced timing improves routine effectiveness.

**Practical Implication**
For managers of interdependent service routines, the paper implies that consistent timing can improve customer-perceived effectiveness but can also create stronger customer dependence on historically established timing. Organizations therefore need to recognize that formal rules, such as putting containers out by a specified time, may be displaced by customers' learned timing expectations when service timing becomes predictable.

**Limitations**
The archival data limited the authors' ability to observe customer presence, action, or inaction directly. The study uses demographic proxies for customer point of view, such as retirement age and government employment, which leaves room for alternative explanations. The effectiveness measure captures customer complaints and therefore reflects the customer vantage point more than organizational or crew evaluations of performance.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for future studies that observe customer or outsider cognition and action more directly, for example by combining fine-grained workflow data with surveys or diary accounts. It also encourages research comparing insider and outsider evaluations of routine effectiveness and examining timing-based patterning in settings with greater unpredictability, innovation demands, or interactions across multiple routines.
