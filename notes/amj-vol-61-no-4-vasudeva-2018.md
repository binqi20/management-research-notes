# A Signaling Theory of Institutional Activism: How Norway’s Sovereign Wealth Fund Investments Affect Firms’ Foreign Acquisitions

**Abstract**
Combining perspectives from institutional activism and signaling theory literatures, we suggest that an activist sovereign wealth fund (SWF) can serve as an intermediary signaler, providing cues about host countries’ institutional environment to internationalizing firms. By publicizing its investments and engaging in institutional activism, a SWF can signal the institutional quality of host countries to internationalizing firms, thus allowing them to overcome the well-known “lemons problem” in international decisionmaking. We examine the impact of a SWF’s signals on firms’ ownership choices in their foreign acquisitions. Our empirical analysis of Norway’s socially responsible SWF and firms from Norway and Sweden during 1998–2011 shows that firms are more likely to take larger commitments via full equity ownership in acquisitions in host countries where Norway’s SWF holds larger investments. The signaling effect of the SWF weakens for conational firms, suggesting that proximity to the signaler may generate alternative information channels that diminish the signaling value of foreign investments. Similarly, institutional harmonization enabled by intergovernmental organizations connecting the home and host countries weakens the signaling value of SWF investments. Our findings point to intermediary signaling by activist institutional investors and the salience of their signals for firms’ international decision-making.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether investments by an activist sovereign wealth fund can serve as intermediary signals of host-country institutional quality for internationalizing firms. It tests whether Norway’s SWF investments affect firms’ full versus partial ownership choices in foreign acquisitions, and whether that signaling effect is weakened by conationality and by home-host IGO linkages.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Larger investments by an activist SWF in a host country will increase the likelihood of full rather than partial acquisitions in that host country by observing firms.
- Hypothesis 2: The positive effect of larger investments by an activist SWF in a host country on the likelihood of full rather than partial acquisitions is weaker for conational firms compared to other observing firms.
- Hypothesis 3: The positive effect of larger investments by an activist SWF in a host country on observing firms’ likelihood of full rather than partial acquisitions is weakened when the number of joint memberships of the home and host countries in IGOs increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Norway SWF cumulative equity investments in a host country.
- DV(s): Acquiring firm ownership choice in a foreign acquisition, operationalized as full versus partial acquisition and checked with percentage of shares acquired.
- Mediators: Not directly measured; the theorized mechanism is intermediary signaling about host-country institutional quality under information asymmetry.
- Moderators: Conationality with Norway’s SWF and joint IGO membership between the acquirer’s home country and the host country.

The mechanism is that a transparent, activist, government-owned SWF can make host-country institutional quality more observable to firms evaluating foreign acquisition commitments. Larger SWF investments are interpreted as credible signals because the SWF has government-linked information access, long-term investment incentives, and institutional activism capacity. The signal is less valuable when firms have alternative information channels through conational proximity or when IGO ties already reduce institutional uncertainty and harmonize expectations.

**Data & Measures**
The study tests its hypotheses on all cross-border acquisitions undertaken by 559 Norwegian firms and 1,256 Swedish firms during 1998–2011, spanning 47 host countries in Europe, Asia, Latin America, and North America. Acquisition data were obtained from the Thomson SDC Platinum Mergers and Acquisitions database, and the unit of analysis is a firm’s cross-border acquisition; upon dropping observations with incomplete data the final sample is 4,003 firm acquisitions. Swedish firms’ acquisitions in Norway, which account for 5% of Swedish firms’ total acquisitions, were excluded. Data on Norway’s SWF cross-border equity investments came from the electronic archival records of its global equity holdings (NBIM, 2017), with Table 4 listing NBIM and the Norwegian Ministry of Finance as the source; these investments ranged from 0 to a maximum of U.S.$513 billion in a given host country.

The dependent variable, full acquisition, is coded 1 if the acquirer owns 95–100% of shares in the target and 0 otherwise (SDC Platinum; mean 0.78), an operationalization the authors say reflects the modal distribution of the data, in which 77.7% of the acquisitions are full acquisitions. The explanatory variable, log SWF investment, is the natural log of the SWF’s cumulative equity investments in a host country up to the observation year plus 1, in U.S.$ billions, so that countries with no SWF investment take a value of 0 (mean 2.06, SD 1.64). The first moderator, co-nationality, is a dummy equal to 1 if the acquirer is Norwegian and 0 if the acquirer is Swedish (mean 0.30). The second moderator, IGO joint membership, is the natural log of the count of IGOs in which the home and host countries jointly participated in the observation year plus 1, built from Pevehouse et al. (2004); the authors manually coded all 495 IGOs and identified 79 whose core mission overlapped with the ESG mandate of the Norwegian SWF (mean 3.13). Controls listed in Table 4 include total bilateral FDI and economic leverage (UNCTAD), the FDI restrictiveness index (OECD), physical distance (Google), cultural distance along Hofstede’s four dimensions, target government ownership, target private status, target regulated industry, target censored industry, SWF investment in and SWF ownership of the target (NBIM), number of bidders, related industry, acquirer private status, acquirer government ownership, acquirer regulated industry, acquirer international experience, and acquirer experience in the host country (SDC Platinum), together with year fixed effects.

Because ownership choice can only be observed conditional on entry into a host country, and because unobserved factors associated with the SWF’s investments could drive both host country selection and ownership choice, the authors used a two-stage Heckman model: a first-stage probit of host country entry (Model 1, N = 89,518) and, conditional on that choice, a second-stage probit of full versus partial acquisition (Models 2–6, N = 4,003). Choice sets were constructed for each acquisition by including countries within the host country’s geographical region. The first-stage exclusion restriction is the host country’s GDP growth rate (World Bank); the authors report a correlation of -0.55 between log SWF investment and the inverse Mill’s ratio and a first-stage pseudo-R2 of 0.18 as indicating moderate strength, and report robustness to an alternative exclusion restriction measuring host country labor market attractiveness as the proportion of university graduates in the total graduate age range. Standard errors use a robust variance estimator clustered by firm–host country dyads. Because probit coefficients and interaction coefficients are not directly interpretable, effect sizes are read off average marginal effects and simulation-based graphs (King, Tomz, & Wittenberg, 2000). Supplemental models in Table 8 re-estimate the design with the percentage of shares acquired as a Tobit dependent variable (Model 7, N = 3,654), with a manufacturing versus distribution subsidiary choice for manufacturing acquirers (Model 8, N = 1,043), with an indicator for acquisitions in the USA and the United Kingdom (Model 9, N = 4,003), with a control for time-varying foreign institutional investments in each host country from OECD Institutional Investors Statistics (Model 10, N = 2,322), on Norwegian firms only to examine acquirer government ownership (Model 11, N = 1,189), excluding Swedish firms that invested in Norway within a five-year window prior to the focal acquisition (Model 12, N = 3,656), and with prior acquirer experience in the host country (Model 13, N = 4,003).

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. Across Models 3–6 the effect of SWF investment on the probability of full versus partial acquisition is positive and significant (p < .01); a one standard deviation increase in log SWF investment to its mean value, corresponding to an increase in SWF investment from U.S.$1.5 billion to U.S.$7.8 billion, increases the probability of a firm’s full acquisition by 7.2%. In the first-stage model, greater SWF investment in a host country also increases the likelihood that the host country is selected for an acquisition: a one standard deviation increase in log SWF investment from its mean approximately doubles the probability of entry, from .023 to .049 (p < .01).

Hypothesis 2 is supported. Based on Model 3, compared to conational firms, Swedish firms are 8.9% more likely to make full acquisitions in any host country (p < .001), and Figure 2a shows consistently higher rates of full acquisitions for Swedish firms over different values of SWF investment. Based on Model 6, a one standard deviation increase in log SWF investment to its mean value yields a two-fold increase in the full acquisition rate for Swedish firms compared to Norwegian firms, and the difference line between the two groups is statistically different from 0 when the value of log SWF investment is greater than 1, or U.S.$2.7 billion, which represents 68% of the sample.

Hypothesis 3 is supported. Across Models 3 to 6 the main effect of IGO membership is positive and highly significant (p < .01), so that at any level of SWF investment firms are more likely to make full acquisitions when IGO membership is high. The difference in the predicted probability across high and low IGO levels diminishes as the level of SWF investment in a host country increases: for a one standard deviation increase in log SWF investment to its mean value, the difference in full acquisition rate reduces by 55% in host countries with high versus low IGO membership. That difference in predicted probabilities is significant for observations in which the value of log SWF investment is less than or equal to 2, or U.S.$7.8 billion, which represents 52% of the sample. Across Models 3 to 6 the inverse Mill’s ratio is positive but not significant.

The descriptive subgroup comparisons in Table 6 give what the authors call preliminary support for Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3: the rate of full acquisitions is 83% where SWF investment is high versus 73% where it is low, the increase associated with higher SWF investment is lower for conational Norwegian firms (8%) than for Swedish firms (11%), and it is greater when joint IGO memberships are low (23%) than when they are high (3%).

Supplemental analyses are consistent with the main results. With the percentage of shares acquired as a Tobit dependent variable, the coefficient for SWF investment is positive and significant, and a one standard deviation increase in log SWF investment to its mean value increases the ownership of shares by 10.8% (p < .01); for manufacturing acquirers, the same increase raises the likelihood of acquiring a manufacturing versus distribution subsidiary by 12.4 times. Model 9 reveals a significant negative interaction between SWF investment and the indicator for acquisitions in the USA and the United Kingdom, which the authors read as agreeing with the theory underpinning Hypotheses 2 and 3. Excluding Swedish firms that invested in Norway within a five-year window prior to the focal acquisition widens the differential effect of SWF investment between Norwegian and Swedish firms by 28%, which the authors treat as additional support for Hypothesis 2 (Model 12). By contrast, SWF investments in individual target firms do not have a significant effect on the ownership choices of acquiring firms, government ownership of a Norwegian acquirer does not alter the signaling effect of the SWF’s investments (Model 11), and the acquiring firm’s own host country experience does not substitute for the novel information contained in the SWF’s investment signal (Model 13). The authors note that limited data availability reduced the sample size in Model 10, whose results are weaker in significance but remain consistent with the reported findings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a signaling theory of activist SWFs as third-party intermediary signalers whose investments can unintentionally inform a broad audience about host-country institutional quality. It extends classical two-party signaling and relational-tie signaling by theorizing signals generated through an intermediary’s actions toward an entity of interest rather than through the entity’s deliberate self-presentation. It also specifies boundary conditions: proximity to the signal sender and the broader signaling environment shape when firms rely on SWF investment signals.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that managers of internationalizing firms may treat transparent SWF investments as potentially useful cues about host-country institutional environments. This is especially relevant when firms prioritize long-term objectives and institutional qualities aligned with the SWF’s goals. The implication is not that SWF investment should mechanically determine entry or ownership, but that it can become one input when assessing foreign acquisition commitment under institutional uncertainty.

**Limitations**
The article does not present a standalone limitations section. The empirical setting centers on Norway’s socially responsible and unusually transparent SWF, Norwegian and Swedish acquiring firms, and acquisitions during 1998-2011, so the evidence may not generalize cleanly to less transparent, more politically motivated, or less activist SWFs. The study also treats host-country institutional quality as inferred through SWF investment signals rather than directly observing all institutional changes activated by the SWF.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how different SWF purposes, transparency levels, asset allocations, and forms of activism affect whether their investments become observable and credible signals. Future studies could code the actual ESG policy changes activated by SWFs and examine how those changes affect subsequent foreign investment. The paper also points to research on how SWF investments can deter firms or trigger social activism and regulation when the SWF’s national image or strategic motives are viewed negatively.
