# It’s Not Easy Building Green: The Impact of Public Policy, Private Actors, and Regional Logics on Voluntary Standards Adoption

**Abstract**
Human-induced climate change is one of the most daunting challenges of the 21st century. Addressing climate change will require new sectors that create economically and ecologically sustainable products and services, often supported through voluntary certification standards. For example, the Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED) voluntary certification standard seeks to reduce the environmental impacts of buildings through stimulating growth of the “green building” sector. Both public and private actors have attempted to promote such standards; however, we know little about the relative efficacy of such efforts. We know even less about how regional culture conditions the impact of such public and private promotion. In this study, we theorize and test how regional institutional logics filter the efficacy of public and private efforts to promote adoption of standards. Our results demonstrate that, in addition to policy, private actors such as social movement organizations, market intermediaries, and environmental entrepreneurs play a role in bolstering adoption. Yet, the efficacy of such interventions is dependent upon variations in regional culture; only environmental entrepreneurs were able to bridge the cultural divide and be equally effective in regions dominated by either market or community logics. Our findings advance understanding of how new markets are socially constructed and the conditions under which efforts to promote new sectors are more effective.

**Research Question**
How do public policies and private actors affect adoption of a voluntary environmental standard, and how do regional market and community logics condition those effects? The paper asks this in the context of LEED adoption across U.S. metropolitan statistical areas.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Government economic incentive policies will have a greater impact on LEED adoption when the market logic in a region is stronger, but they will have no difference in impact on LEED adoption when the community logic in a region is stronger.
- Hypothesis 2: TSMOs will have a greater impact on LEED adoption when the community logic in a region is stronger, but they will have no difference in impact on LEED adoption when the market logic in a region is stronger.
- Hypothesis 3: Market intermediaries will have a greater impact on LEED adoption when the market logic in a region is stronger, but they will have no difference in impact on LEED adoption when the community logic in a region is stronger.
- Hypothesis 4: Environmental entrepreneurs will have a greater impact on LEED adoption when the market logic in a region is stronger, and will also have a greater impact on LEED adoption when the community logic in a region is stronger.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Economic incentive policies, TSMO activity through USGBC membership, market intermediaries measured as LEED Accredited Professionals, and environmental entrepreneur entry.
- DV(s): New LEED-certified buildings in an MSA-year.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Regional market logic and regional community logic.

The paper theorizes regional institutional logics as cultural filters that shape how potential adopters interpret public and private efforts to promote LEED. Economic incentives, TSMOs, and market intermediaries were expected or found to work especially through alignment with market-oriented interpretations of green building. Environmental entrepreneurs are theorized as hybrid actors that can make LEED meaningful under both market and community logics by combining economic opportunity with ecological benefit.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses a longitudinal MSA-year panel of LEED certification whose window of observation begins in 2000, the year LEED certification was made available, and runs through 2014, the last full year for which data were available; MSAs are geographical centers in the United States with a population of at least 50,000, the paper reports 22,135 LEED-certified projects in the United States during this period, and the panel comprises 5,110 MSA-year observations in 341 MSAs for which the authors had complete data. The dependent variable is a count of new LEED-certified buildings in an MSA-year, constructed by aggregating zip-code-level information on building locations to the MSA level with a spatial concordance file. Community logic is measured with an established measure of community social capital from the regional economics literature (Rupasingha, Goetz, & Freshwater, 2006), obtained at county level from the North East Regional Center for Rural Development at Pennsylvania State University for the years 1990, 1997, 2005, 2009, and 2014, with missing years interpolated for each county before aggregation to the MSA level. Market logic is a factor composed of six items that loaded onto a single factor in a principal component factor analysis with an eigenvalue of 2.25 and an alpha value of 0.66: the ideology of each MSA's political leaders on a liberal-conservative continuum and the political ideology of its citizens (both drawn from the Berry and colleagues Citizen and Government Ideology database, with the state-level measure weighted to each MSA by population), the percentage of voters in an MSA who voted Republican in national elections (congressional-district data from DailyKos.com aggregated to the MSA), the ratio of contributions to Republican candidates relative to total donations in each MSA (county-level Center for Responsive Politics data aggregated to the MSA), the local tax burden for businesses purchased from Moody's Analytics and reverse coded so that a lower business tax burden indicates a stronger market logic, and the density of registered labor unions (NAICS code 813930 from the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, aggregated to the MSA and normalized by population) reverse coded so that lower union density indicates a stronger market logic.

Economic incentive policies are a count variable of green building economic incentive policies by MSA-year, gathered from an online database provided by the USGBC and cross-validated with the Database of State Incentives for Renewables and Efficiency, including policies enacted at a city or county level matched to the appropriate MSAs; because the variable is strongly right-skewed, its logged value is used in the models. Market intermediaries are the number of LEED Accredited Professionals in each MSA for a given year, obtained as counts of new LEED APs by zip code and year from the Green Building Certification Institute, then aggregated to the MSA level, normalized by population, and scaled for interpretation of coefficients. TSMO activity is measured from the addresses and joining dates of USGBC members, with the count normalized by MSA population and multiplied by 100,000. Environmental entrepreneurs are the number of new companies listed for each MSA-year in the GreenSpec directory of green building product suppliers published by BuildingGreen, with entrepreneurial entrants (founded no earlier than 1994) separated from incumbents and the MSA-level count log-transformed. Time-varying MSA-level controls are population density, GDP per capita, MSA size, party legislative control, energy cost, water usage, environmental activism, pro-environmental attitudes, commercial construction area, and mimetic isomorphism (logged cumulative prior LEED adoption), together with a full set of year dummy variables.

Because the dependent variable is a non-negative and over-dispersed count, the models are negative binomial estimations, and all independent variables are lagged by one year, which the authors state enables causal interpretation. Interaction effects are interpreted through simulations that plot predicted certifications at strong (two standard deviations above the mean of 0) and weak (two standard deviations below the mean of 0) levels of each logic with other model variables held at mean values, following Zelner (2009) and King, Tomz, and Wittenberg (2000) and using the Stata intgph routine, with 90% confidence intervals around the point estimates. Endogeneity is addressed in Table 3 with an endogenous treatment-effects model for incentive policies that uses the size of local government (Census data on government payroll) as the exogenous variable, and with a control function approach for policies and private actors whose first-stage exogenous predictors are the log of local government payroll for the number of economic incentives, the number of religious adherents in an MSA for TSMO activism, employment in business associations per capita for market intermediaries, and clean energy start-ups from the i3 Cleantech database for environmental entrepreneurship. Table 3 also reports Arellano-Bond system GMM dynamic panel models with a lagged dependent variable, estimated on 4,769 MSA-year observations in 341 MSAs, treating all explanatory variables as endogenous and the year dummies as exogenous.

**Key Findings**
Entered separately in Table 2, each intervention has a positive and statistically significant direct effect on new LEED certifications: economic incentive policies (Model 3, b = 0.255, p = .015, a 3% increase in the certification rate per standard deviation), TSMOs (Model 4, b = 0.18, p < .001, a 16% increase), market intermediaries (Model 5, b = 0.06, p < .001, a 17% increase), and environmental entrepreneurs (Model 6, b = 0.277, p = .009, a 3% increase). Entered alone, both logics have statistically insignificant direct effects on adoption (Model 2, market logic b = -0.038, p = .166; community logic b = -0.024, p = .400). In the combined Model 7 the market logic coefficient is negative and significant (b = -0.051, p = .049, a 5% decrease in the rate of LEED certification per standard deviation), the impact of economic incentive policies is no longer statistically significant (b = 0.048, p = .607), and the authors report that TSMOs (b = 0.11, p < .001), market intermediaries (b = 0.04, p < .001), and environmental entrepreneurs (b = 0.17, p = .07) each retain statistical significance; a one standard deviation increase in all four independent variables combined would lead to a 25.9% increase in the rate of LEED adoption.

Three of the four hypotheses are supported. Hypothesis 1 is supported (Model 8): the marginal impact of economic incentive policies is positively moderated in regions with stronger market logics (b = 0.412, p = .001), while the coefficient on the policy-by-community-logic term is positive but insignificant (b = 0.222, p = .183); at the sample maximum of 14 policies the difference between strong and weak market logics is 1.33 LEED certifications, approximately a 35% increase over the average of 3.77 certifications per MSA-year, and the interaction is significant only after the number of policies in a region exceeds 1.03. Hypothesis 2 is not supported (Model 9): contrary to the prediction there is no evidence of a positive interaction between TSMO strength and community logics (b = -0.009, p = .704), and TSMOs are instead more effective at driving adoption as the strength of the market logic increases (b = 0.055, p = .003), with a difference of 3.56 more certifications per MSA-year (a 94% increase) at the highest TSMO value and significance once TSMO members exceed 2.6. Hypothesis 3 is supported (Model 10): the effectiveness of market intermediaries increases as market logic strengthens (b = 0.012, p = .029), with no significant interaction with community logic (b = -0.003, p = .784); at the highest count of 35 new market intermediaries the strong-versus-weak market logic difference is 4.8 more LEED certifications, a 127% increase, significant once the number of market intermediaries exceeds 19. Hypothesis 4 is supported (Model 11): environmental entrepreneurs become more effective as the market logic strengthens (b = 0.318, p = .008) and as the community logic strengthens (b = 0.469, p = .001); at the highest value of the variable (14 new environmental entrepreneurs) the market logic difference is 0.90 LEED certifications, approximately a 24% increase and significant once entrants exceed 1.7, and the community logic difference is 1.62 more LEED certifications, a 43% increase and significant once the value exceeds 1.03.

In the full Model 12, which includes all eight interaction terms, the authors note that high pairwise correlations among the interaction terms (between 0.3 and 0.7) yield lower efficiency and a lower level of statistical significance for the individual interaction coefficients while their marginal effects remain of similar magnitude. In that model the interaction between economic incentive policies and market logics remains statistically significant (p = .046), the interaction between environmental entrepreneurs and market logics is significant at the 90% confidence level (b = 0.19, p = .077), and the interaction between environmental entrepreneurs and community logics is significant (b = 0.48, p < .001), while the interactions between the logics and TSMOs and between the logics and market intermediaries show no statistically significant effects although they are directionally consistent with the previous models. The findings are robust to an alternate dependent variable weighting the level of certification achieved, a Tobit specification, a count normalized by the number of new commercial building projects, two- and three-year lags, LEED-registered rather than certified projects, state dummy variables, dummy-coded versions of the sparse independent variables, dichotomized logic measures, and population-averaged, zero-inflated, and mixed-effects negative binomial specifications, as well as to the endogenous treatment-effects, control function, and system GMM models reported in Table 3.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends voluntary standards research by shifting attention from coercive regulation and adopter-side isomorphism to the combined role of public policy, TSMOs, market intermediaries, and entrepreneurs in constructing a new market. It contributes to institutional logics research by showing that regional logics filter the effectiveness of interventions rather than simply exerting direct effects. It also links environmental entrepreneurship to voluntary standards adoption by showing how new ventures can facilitate standards diffusion.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that policy-makers and advocates promoting environmental standards should adapt interventions to regional cultural logics rather than assuming uniform policy effects. Market-oriented regions may respond especially well to incentives, professional intermediaries, and economic framing. Environmental entrepreneurs may be particularly useful because their products and services can bridge market and community interpretations of green building.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the study may generalize most directly to practices embedded in institutional complexity. They also note that LEED emergence involved co-evolution among policy, USGBC activity, LEED APs, private actors, and adoption, although they use lagged variables and simultaneous equation approaches to address endogeneity concerns. A further limitation is that some marginal effects are relatively small compared with stronger drivers such as isomorphism and environmental beliefs.

**Future Research**
Future research could test whether these findings generalize beyond environmental standards to other practices embedded in institutional complexity, such as alternative health care practices. The authors also suggest fieldwork and process-model development to better explain co-evolution among policy, private actors, and adoption. Additional work could examine actors' position within an industry, TSMO membership composition, and entrepreneurs' roles in renewable energy, local and organic foods, and other environmentally beneficial sectors.
