# Pacification or Aggravation? The Effects of Talking about Supervisor Unfairness

**Abstract**
Many employees feel a general sense of unfairness toward their supervisors. A common reaction to such unfairness is to talk about it with coworkers. The conventional wisdom is that this unfairness talk should be beneficial to the aggrieved employees. After all, talking provides employees with an opportunity to make sense of the experience and to "let off steam." We challenge this perspective, drawing on cognitive-motivational-relational theory to develop arguments that unfairness talk leads to emotions that reduce the employee's ability to move on from the unfairness. We first tested these proposals in a three-wave, two-source field study of bus drivers (Study 1), then replicated our findings in a laboratory study (Study 2). In both studies, we found that unfairness talk was positively related to anger and negatively related to hope. Those emotions went on to have direct effects on forgiveness and indirect effects on citizenship behavior. Our results also show that the detrimental effects of unfairness talk were neutralized when the listener offered suggestions that reframed the unfair situation. We discuss the implications of these results for managing unfairness in organizations.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether talking with coworkers about supervisor unfairness helps employees move on from unfairness or instead aggravates the experience by increasing anger and reducing hope. It also asks whether coworker reframing weakens those effects and whether forgiveness links unfairness talk to supervisor-directed citizenship behavior.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Unfairness talk has a positive effect on anger, controlling for perceptions of overall unfairness.
- Hypothesis 2. Unfairness talk has a negative effect on hope, controlling for perceptions of overall unfairness.
- Hypothesis 3. Unfairness talk has a negative indirect effect on forgiveness of the supervisor through anger.
- Hypothesis 4. Unfairness talk has a negative indirect effect on forgiveness of the supervisor through hope.
- Hypothesis 5. The negative indirect effect of unfairness talk on forgiveness of the supervisor through anger is moderated by the coworker's reframing response, such that the effect is weaker when reframing is high than when reframing is low.
- Hypothesis 6. The negative indirect effect of unfairness talk on forgiveness of the supervisor through hope is moderated by the coworker's reframing response, such that the effect is weaker when reframing is high than when reframing is low.
- Hypothesis 7. Forgiveness of the supervisor will have a positive effect on supervisor-directed citizenship behavior.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Unfairness talk about supervisor unfairness; in Study 2, experimentally manipulated high versus low unfairness talk.
- DV(s): Anger, hope, forgiveness of the supervisor or experimenter, and supervisor- or experimenter-directed citizenship behavior.
- Mediators: Anger and hope mediate the effect of unfairness talk on forgiveness; forgiveness further links the emotion pathway to citizenship behavior.
- Moderators: Coworker or listener reframing response is tested as a first-stage moderator of the unfairness-talk-to-emotion links; Study 2 supports the moderated indirect pathway through hope, while the anger-path difference is not significant.

The mechanism is appraisal-based. Talking about supervisor unfairness keeps the goal-hindering, blame-focused appraisal active, increasing anger, while also making the unfairness feel more solidified and less surmountable, reducing hope. Anger and lower hope make it harder to forgive the supervisor, and lower forgiveness reduces willingness to go beyond role requirements for that supervisor. Reframing responses interrupt this process by offering alternative interpretations and coping options, with the replicated conditional indirect evidence strongest for the hope pathway.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data and procedure: 170 bus drivers with complete data, employed by a large private transportation company in London, England operating under the oversight of Transport for London, drawn from seven bus depots across London. Data collection took place in three waves, each separated by six weeks, with a second source at the final wave. The Time 1 survey was distributed to 1,282 employees and 520 were returned (a response rate of 41%); the Time 2 survey went to all employees who returned Time 1 and 226 were returned (43%); the Time 3 survey went to the supervisors of the employees who returned Time 2, and all 25 supervisors identified by the general managers participated, yielding responses for 214 of the 226 employees (95%). After listwise deletion of missing data across the three waves and two sources, the authors had complete data for 170 employees. On average, each supervisor assessed the citizenship behavior of seven employees.
- Study 1 measurement timing: the Time 1 survey included overall unfairness, unfairness talk, reframing response, and employee age; the Time 2 survey included neuroticism, anger, hope, and forgiveness of the supervisor; citizenship behavior was completed by the supervisors at Time 3. Measure order on both employee surveys was counterbalanced to prevent item-context effects.
- Study 1 measures: unfairness talk = five items written for this study following Hinkin and Tracey's (1999) construct creation and validation procedure, with 115 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants rating item-definition match (mean correspondence 6.09 out of 7.00), rated from 1 = Almost never to 7 = Almost always (a = .97). Overall unfairness = Colquitt et al.'s (2015) three-item measure, 1 = To a very small extent to 5 = To a very large extent (a = .92). Anger = Watson and Clark's (1994) six-item measure (sample items "Angry" and "Hostile"), referenced to the past six weeks when thinking about or interacting with the supervisor, 1 = Very slightly/Not at all to 5 = Extremely (a = .96). Hope = Fredrickson, Tugade, Waugh, and Larkin's (2003) three-item measure ("Hopeful," "Optimistic") with anchors identical to anger (a = .95). Coworker reframing response = three items created for this study and rated by the same 115 MTurk participants (mean definitional convergence 6.05 out of 7.00), 1 = Almost never to 5 = Almost always (a = .92). Forgiveness of the supervisor = McCullough, Worthington, and Rachal's (1997) four-item Likert measure, 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree (a = .68). Supervisor-directed citizenship behavior = four items developed by Malatesta (1995) as an adaptation from Williams and Anderson (1991), completed by supervisors, 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree (a = .85). Neuroticism = Donnellan, Oswald, Baird, and Lucas's (2006) four-item measure (a = .79).
- Study 1 analysis: structural equation modeling in Mplus, with item-level indicators for overall unfairness, anger, hope, neuroticism, forgiveness of the supervisor, and citizenship behavior, and mean-centered scale scores as single indicators for unfairness talk and coworker reframing response; their product term was modeled as a single indicator of a latent product. The hypothesized eight-factor confirmatory model fit well (x2 (436) = 682.99, p < .01; CFI = .95; RMSEA = .06; SRMR = .06; average factor loadings .82), and an alternative model treating unfairness talk and overall unfairness as one factor fit significantly worse (x2 difference (7) = 304.92, p < .001). The structural model fit x2 (300) = 527.80, p < .01; CFI = .91; RMSEA = .07; SRMR = .07, with standard errors clustered by supervisor to correct for the nesting of employees within supervisors. Overall unfairness and neuroticism were controlled throughout; employee age, gender, race, tenure with the supervisor, and tenure with the organization were examined but not retained because none correlated with both the predictors and the dependent variables. Indirect effects were tested with the product-of-coefficients approach using the distribution-of-the-product method in RMediation, and conditional indirect effects with Edwards and Lambert's (2007) first-stage moderation equations plus bootstrapping. Figure 2 reports standardized path coefficients and all significance tests are one-tailed.
- Study 1 design status: the field evidence is associational across three survey waves; the authors state that a true panel design would have allowed more persuasive evidence for the presumed causality underlying their hypotheses.
- Study 2 data and design: 105 undergraduate business majors from a large university in the southeastern United States (40% male; average age 20.22, SD = 0.92), each of whom was told to bring a friend or classmate. The focal participant is termed the "talker" and the friend/classmate the "listener." The study used a 2 x 2 design in which talkers were randomly assigned to a combination of unfairness talk (high or low) and reframing response (high or low); listeners were trained by an experimenter to enact the assigned condition, received no information about the dependent variables, and each dyad was monitored by video.
- Study 2 manipulations: the experimenter created an overall sense of unfairness by ending the timed anagram task after 3.5 minutes instead of the promised 5, making a dismissive remark about undergraduates, and marking several correctly unscrambled anagrams as incorrect so only partial credit was awarded. In the high talking condition listeners asked follow-up questions to encourage further conversation; in the low talking condition they were told not to ask follow-up questions and to respond in ways that discouraged conversation (a low rather than a no talking condition, so that reframing was possible). In the high reframing condition listeners provided alternative explanations; in the low reframing condition they avoided sticking up for the experimenter or presenting the situation in a more positive way.
- Study 2 measures: anger = the six-item PANAS-X scale developed by Watson and Clark (1994) and hope = the same three-item measure as Study 1 (Fredrickson et al., 2003), both rated by the listener about the talker on a scale from 1 = Very Slightly/Not at All to 5 = Extremely (a = .96 and a = .90). Forgiveness = the Study 1 measure (McCullough et al., 1997) adjusted so that the experimenter was the target, collected through a disguised research-pool evaluation form that talkers sealed in an envelope, 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree (a = .85). Citizenship behavior = the experimenter's one-to-five rating of the degree to which the room was cleaned up after talkers were told they could clear up the art supplies as a favor. Manipulation checks used the unfairness talk and reframing response measures from Study 1, whose coefficient alphas were .93 and .92, respectively.
- Study 2 analysis: analysis of variance for the manipulation checks and structural equation modeling in Mplus for the hypothesized model, which fit x2 (109) = 209.40, p < .01; CFI = .92; RMSEA = .09; SRMR = .06. Figure 4 reports unstandardized path coefficients and all significance tests are one-tailed. Identification rests on experimental manipulation and random assignment, which the authors describe as the most powerful methods for establishing causality and controlling for alternative explanations.
- Conditional-effect table settings: in Study 1 the high and low values of reframing response were 1 (1 SD above the mean) and -1 (1 SD below the mean); in Study 2 they were 0 and 1. In both studies moderation occurred at the first stage.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 170), Hypothesis 1 supported: unfairness talk had a significant positive standardized path to anger (.24). Hypothesis 2 supported: unfairness talk had a significant negative standardized path to hope (-.25). Both effects held above and beyond overall unfairness, even when controlling for neuroticism.
- Study 1, Hypotheses 3 and 4 supported: unfairness talk had significant negative indirect effects on forgiveness of the supervisor through anger (-.05) and through hope (-.13). Table 2 also reports a direct effect of -.21 and a total effect of -.38.
- Study 1, Hypothesis 5 supported: the indirect effect through anger was nonsignificant (-.01) when reframing was high and significant (-.08) when reframing was low, and the difference between the two effects (.07) was significant. The relationship between unfairness talk and anger was near zero and nonsignificant at high reframing yet positive and significant at low reframing.
- Study 1, Hypothesis 6 supported: the indirect effect through hope was nonsignificant (-.05) when reframing was high and significant (-.20) when reframing was low, with a significant difference (.15) between the two effects.
- Study 1, Hypothesis 7 supported: forgiveness had a significant effect on supervisor-directed citizenship behavior (.23). The total indirect effect of unfairness talk on citizenship behavior through anger, hope, and forgiveness was -.09 and significant, while the direct effect of unfairness talk on citizenship (.01) was nonsignificant.
- Study 1, moderator-to-emotion paths reported in the Figure 2 note but omitted from the figure: coworker reframing response to anger = -.11 (nonsignificant) and coworker reframing response to hope = .26 (significant).
- Study 2 (n = 105), manipulation checks: talkers rated conversations in the high unfairness talk condition as having more talking than in the low unfairness talk condition (F = 8.88, p < .01; M = 3.56 vs. 3.01), and rated the high reframing response condition higher than the low reframing response condition (F = 16.77, p < .01; M = 2.94 vs. 1.99).
- Study 2, Hypotheses 1 and 2 supported: the unstandardized path coefficients from unfairness talk to anger (.51) and from unfairness talk to hope (-.78) were both significant.
- Study 2, Hypotheses 3 and 4 supported: the negative indirect effects of unfairness talk on forgiveness through anger (-.17) and through hope (-.14) were both significant. Table 4 also reports a direct effect of -.33 and a total effect of -.65.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 5 not supported: the indirect effect of unfairness talk on forgiveness through anger was significant in the low reframing condition (-.17) but not in the high reframing condition (-.07), and the difference between the two effects (.10) was not significant.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 6 supported: the indirect effect of unfairness talk on forgiveness through hope was significant in both the low reframing condition (-.14) and the high reframing response condition (.05), and the difference between the two effects (.19) was significant.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 7 supported: the effect of forgiveness on citizenship behavior (.37) was significant. The total indirect effect of unfairness talk on citizenship behavior was negative and significant (-.24), and the direct effect (.19) was nonsignificant.
- Study 2, moderator-to-emotion paths reported in the Figure 4 note but omitted from the figure: coworker reframing response to anger = .18 (nonsignificant) and coworker reframing response to hope = -.45 (significant).
- Support pattern across the two studies: Hypotheses 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 were supported in both Study 1 and Study 2; Hypothesis 5 was supported in Study 1 but not in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces unfairness talk to the organizational justice literature, shifting attention from unfair events alone to the conversational dynamics that follow unfairness. It tests and extends cognitive-motivational-relational theory by using anger and hope to explain why talking can impair forgiveness and citizenship behavior. It also identifies coworker reframing as a listener-side response that can neutralize otherwise harmful effects of unfairness talk.

**Practical Implication**
The authors caution that employees may worsen the long-term employee-supervisor relationship if they repeatedly talk about unfairness with coworkers who only validate the grievance. They suggest employees should choose conversation partners who can offer alternative perspectives and coping options, while coworkers should reframe with concern rather than dismissiveness. The paper also points to supervisor conflict-management training, forgiveness interventions, reparations, and expressive writing as possible organizational responses to supervisor unfairness.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Study 1 used two sources and three waves, but the anger, hope, and forgiveness relationships could still have been inflated because those variables were not separated into four waves. They also state that they did not capture all variables during each wave, so a true panel design would have provided stronger field evidence for causality. Another limitation is that the field sample was primarily male, although the authors argue prior evidence suggests gender may not strongly affect talking about negative experiences or forgiveness.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether benefits for the employee-coworker relationship, such as social support or strengthened coworker ties, offset the harms found for the employee-supervisor relationship. It could also compare different coworker response types, including validation and reframing, and assess whether reframing reduces future willingness to share with that coworker. The authors also call for experiments that distinguish talking about emotions from talking about thoughts, including structured conversations aimed at obtaining alternative perspectives.
