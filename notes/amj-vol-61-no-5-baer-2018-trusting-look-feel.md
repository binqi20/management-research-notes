# Trusting the “Look and Feel”: Situational Normality, Situational Aesthetics, and the Perceived Trustworthiness of Organizations

**Abstract**
We conducted two studies examining how the “look and feel” of an organization shapes newcomers’ trust in that organization. More specifically, we examined the effects of situational normality—the degree to which the work setting appears customary, with everything in proper order. We then introduced the construct of situational aesthetics—the degree to which the work setting has a pleasing and attractive appearance. A field study of new accountants revealed that situational normality and situational aesthetics had indirect effects on trust through perceived trustworthiness, with trust going on to predict coworker ratings of learning behavior. We then replicated those trustworthiness findings in a laboratory setting. Taken together, our results suggest that newcomer trust formation may be shaped by aspects of the work setting that have been heretofore ignored by trust scholars.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether the physical work setting's "look and feel" shapes newcomers' perceptions of organizational trustworthiness and trust before they have much rational or historical information about the organization. It focuses on whether situational normality and situational aesthetics affect perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity, and whether trust then predicts newcomer learning behavior.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Newcomer perceptions of situational normality are positively related to the perceived trustworthiness of the organization.
H2. Newcomer perceptions of situational aesthetics are positively related to the perceived trustworthiness of the organization.
H3. Newcomer perceptions of situational normality have a positive indirect effect on trust in the organization, through perceived trustworthiness.
H4. Newcomer perceptions of situational aesthetics have a positive indirect effect on trust in the organization, through perceived trustworthiness.
H5. Newcomer trust in the organization is positively related to newcomer learning behavior.

Study 1 tested all five hypotheses. Study 2 focused specifically on testing Hypotheses 1 and 2, and additionally examined a non-hypothesized expectation raised at the suggestion of anonymous reviewers: that psychological contract fulfillment would interact with situational normality and aesthetics, such that the latter two variables are more impactful under conditions of breach.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Situational normality and situational aesthetics; Study 2 also manipulates psychological contract fulfillment.
- DV(s): Perceived trustworthiness facets, trust in the organization, and learning behavior in Study 1; perceived trustworthiness facets in Study 2.
- Mediators: Perceived ability, perceived benevolence, perceived integrity, and trust in the organization.
- Moderators: Psychological contract fulfillment in Study 2, where breach conditions make normality and aesthetics more consequential for perceived ability.

The mechanism is a trust-formation process based on heuristic and categorical cues. Newcomers may read a setting that seems normal, customary, proper, pleasing, beautiful, or tasteful as a thin-slice cue about the organization's ability, benevolence, and integrity. Those trustworthiness judgments then support trust in the organization, which gives newcomers enough comfort to engage in learning behavior that carries interpersonal and career risk.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (field study): 165 accountants recruited by the authors on their very first day at a Big Four accounting firm in Ireland, surveyed across four waves separated by an average of 24 days over their 10-week training period. All measures used a five-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree.
- Trust propensity (Time 1): nine items from MacDonald, Kessel, and Fuller (1972); alpha = .83.
- Situational normality (Time 2): five items the authors developed for this study because the construct had not been operationalized in an organizational behavior context, content-validated with 136 undergraduates following Hinkin and Tracey (1999), mean definitional correspondence 5.72 out of 7.00; items began "That setting..." and included "Seems normal," "Comes across as customary," and "Strikes me as typical"; alpha = .87.
- Situational aesthetics (Time 2): five newly developed items validated by the same procedure, mean definitional correspondence 5.23 out of 7.00; items included "Is aesthetically pleasing," "Has parts that are beautiful," and "Is lovely"; alpha = .88.
- Psychological contract fulfillment (Time 2): Robinson and Morrison's (2000) five-item scale; alpha = .85.
- Perceived trustworthiness (Time 3): Mayer and Davis's (1999) scales, with perceived ability measured by six items (alpha = .95), perceived benevolence by five items (alpha = .91), and perceived integrity by six items (alpha = .76).
- Trust in the organization (Time 4): Mayer and Davis's (1999) four-item scale; alpha = .78.
- Learning behavior (Time 4): Edmondson's (1999) seven-item measure, reported by a coworker randomly selected from the newcomer's primary unit; alpha = .96.
- Analysis: structural equation modeling in LISREL 8.72, with indirect effects taken from LISREL's effect decomposition statistics. Study 1 is a multi-wave, multi-source field survey, so its estimates are associational rather than causally identified; the paper describes the four time periods and the coworker source as temporal and source separation against common method bias.

Study 2 (laboratory scenario experiment): 1,039 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants, asked to assume they had just started working at a professional services firm offering accounting, consulting, and financial advisory services and had been there three weeks. Situational normality, situational aesthetics, and psychological contract fulfillment were manipulated through written passages in a 3 (normality: low, medium, or high) x 3 (aesthetics: low, medium, or high) x 2 (contract fulfillment: low or high) between-subjects design with random assignment to conditions.
- Manipulation checks used the Study 1 situational normality, situational aesthetics, and psychological contract fulfillment scales; their coefficient alphas were .96, .96, and .97, respectively.
- Perceived trustworthiness: Mayer and Davis's (1999) scales again, with perceived ability alpha = .92, perceived benevolence alpha = .95, and perceived integrity alpha = .93.
- Analysis: analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the manipulation checks, the trustworthiness facets, and the interactions.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (structural equation modeling, n = 165):
- H1 partially supported: situational normality was significantly related to perceived ability (b = .17) and perceived integrity (b = .14), but not to perceived benevolence (b = -.01).
- H2 partially supported: situational aesthetics was significantly related to perceived benevolence (b = .26) and perceived integrity (b = .17), but not to perceived ability (b = .10).
- H3 not supported: situational normality's indirect effect on trust through perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity was .04 and was not statistically significant.
- H4 supported: situational aesthetics' indirect effect on trust through perceived ability, benevolence, and integrity was .17 and was statistically significant (p < .05).
- H5 supported: trust in the organization was significantly related to coworker-rated learning behavior (b = .22).
- Outside the formal hypotheses, supplemental analyses found several significant serial indirect effects on learning behavior: situational normality to perceived integrity to trust to learning behavior = .01; situational aesthetics to perceived benevolence to trust to learning behavior = .02; situational aesthetics to perceived integrity to trust to learning behavior = .02 (p < .05, one-tailed).

Study 2 (ANOVA, n = 1,039; only Hypotheses 1 and 2 were tested):
- The manipulation checks indicated that all three experimental manipulations were received as intended.
- H1 was "supported with the revised pattern": situational normality had a positive main effect on perceived ability (F = 12.26, p < .001) and an effect on perceived integrity that approached significance (F = 2.58, p < .08), while, as in Study 1, there were virtually no differences across conditions for perceived benevolence.
- H2 was fully supported: situational aesthetics had positive main effects on all three trustworthiness facets, namely perceived ability (F = 32.14, p < .001), perceived benevolence (F = 7.90, p < .001), and perceived integrity (F = 12.53, p < .001).
- Psychological contract fulfillment produced positive main effects on all three facets: perceived ability (F = 603.63, p < .001, M = 2.88 vs. 3.86), perceived benevolence (F = 1388.86, p < .001, M = 1.95 vs. 3.53), and perceived integrity (F = 1975.68, p < .001, M = 2.03 vs. 3.71).
- Two statistically significant interactions emerged, both on perceived ability: situational normality by psychological contract fulfillment (F = 9.18, p < .001) and situational aesthetics by psychological contract fulfillment (F = 5.16, p < .01). Variations in situational normality or aesthetics had little effect on perceived ability when psychological contracts were fulfilled; when contracts were breached, increases in normality or aesthetics were associated with increases in perceived ability.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges rational/historical models of organizational trust formation by showing that newcomers' trustworthiness judgments can be shaped by physical-setting cues before extensive direct evidence is available. It extends McKnight et al.'s situational normality idea from initial interpersonal trust to trust in an organization and introduces situational aesthetics as a new construct in the trust-formation literature. It also reconnects research on physical work settings with organizational behavior outcomes by showing that workplace appearance can matter for perceived trustworthiness and learning-related behavior.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should treat physical-setting decisions as part of newcomer trust formation, not only as facilities or branding choices. The findings suggest that avoiding atypical or disorderly workplace cues may be a basic step, while aesthetic choices may require attention to employee preferences around lighting, colors, textures, spatial forms, and internal organization. The paper cautions that aesthetics are subjective and can be overdone, but argues that construction, remodeling, and redecorating choices can be managed with trustworthiness in mind.

**Limitations**
The field study did not temporally separate trust in the organization from coworker-rated learning behavior, so that link lacks the temporal precedence normally desired in hypothesis tests. Attrition across waves and sources limited the Study 1 sample size, and situational normality and aesthetics were measured about three weeks after newcomers' first day rather than during their earliest hours at work. The authors state that they addressed the Study 1 sample-size issue in Study 2 by ensuring the laboratory design had strong statistical power, and that Study 2's results, which measured perceptions of trustworthiness only minutes after exposure to the organization's situational normality and situational aesthetics, were very similar to Study 1's.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether situational normality and situational aesthetics operate through fully heuristic processes or whether newcomers can articulate rational reasons for the work setting's influence. The paper also calls for work on interactions between rational and heuristic antecedents of trustworthiness, differences across ability, benevolence, and integrity, and other early trust heuristics such as reception-staff friendliness or elevator courtesy. It further suggests studying whether managers intentionally use physical settings for impression management and where efforts to look typical or beautiful become excessive or misaligned with employees.
