# Quitting When the Going Gets Tough: A Downside of High Performance Expectations

**Abstract**
High performance expectations often improve performance. When individuals with high external performance expectations encounter early setbacks, however, they face impression management concerns and the prospect of embarrassment. As a result, when the going gets tough, individuals facing high external expectations may be less likely to persist than people facing low external expectations. In a field study of 328,515 men’s professional tennis matches (Study 1), we employ a regression discontinuity design to demonstrate that, after losing the first set of a match, players who are expected to win (favorites) are significantly more likely to quit than players who are expected to lose (underdogs). We replicate this pattern of results in a laboratory experiment (Study 2) and provide evidence for our proposed mechanism: compared to individuals facing low external expectations, those facing high expectations are more easily embarrassed by poor performance and consequently less persistent following early setbacks.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how external performance expectations affect persistence after poor initial performance. It specifically examines whether individuals expected to perform well become less persistent than those expected to perform poorly when early outcomes threaten their public image.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Individuals facing higher external performance expectations will be less persistent than those facing lower external performance expectations following poor initial performance.
- Hypothesis 2: Individuals facing higher external expectations will experience more embarrassment than those facing lower external expectations following poor initial performance.
- Hypothesis 3: Embarrassment will mediate the relationship between external expectations and persistence decisions following poor initial performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): External performance expectations, operationalized as favorite versus underdog status in Study 1 and middle-school versus expert difficulty expectations in Study 2.
- DV(s): Persistence, operationalized as quitting mid-match in Study 1 and the number of original-topic trivia questions attempted before switching topics in Study 2.
- Mediators: Embarrassment; impression management concerns provide the underlying image-management motive.
- Boundary condition tested: Poor initial performance, captured by losing the first set in Study 1 and poor early trivia performance in Study 2.

The proposed mechanism is that high external expectations create a stricter public reference point. When individuals underperform early, this expectation violation produces embarrassment and impression management concerns, making quitting or switching tasks attractive because it reduces exposure to further public failure and can preserve an alternative explanation for poor performance.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (archival field study) draws on the online data archive of the Association of Tennis Professionals (ATP) World Tour and covers 328,515 men's professional singles tennis matches played between 1973 and 2011 that had information about both players' world rankings; descriptive statistics are reported for 657,030 player-match observations from those 328,515 matches, and for 648,594 player-match observations from the 324,297 matches in which players completed the first set. The dependent variable, quit, is a binary variable equal to 1 when a player quit mid-match and 0 otherwise. External expectations are captured by favorite, an indicator equal to 1 if a player had a better ATP ranking than his opponent and 0 otherwise, and poor initial performance is operationalized as losing the first set. The continuous assignment variable is log rank ratio, the logarithm of the target player's opponent's ranking divided by the target player's own ranking, with the rank-equality threshold (log rank ratio = 0) separating slight favorites from slight underdogs. The paper claims causal identification through a quasi-experimental sharp regression discontinuity design, estimated with OLS models that include a fourth-degree polynomial of log rank ratio, the player's rank and his opponent's rank and their interactions with each polynomial term, age and its squared term, prize money, and fixed effects for first-set score, court surface, tour, tournament round, tour x round, and year, with standard errors clustered at the player level. Models 1 and 2 use 323,683 observations; Model 3 reports the mean and standard deviation of coefficients across 100,000 bootstrap resamples (310,844-340,739 observations across samples). The authors excluded 4,218 matches in which players did not finish the first set and 628 matches in which both players had the same recorded rank.

Study 2 (laboratory experiment) was run at a Midwestern university in the United States: 305 participants showed up across 29 sessions and 304 (161 females, average age of 19 years) completed the study. A coin flip assigned each participant to a "middle-school difficulty" trivia challenge (questions most people are exposed to by the end of middle school, with good performers answering 70% correctly) or an "expert difficulty" challenge (questions most people cannot answer, with good performers answering 20% correctly), manipulating external expectations to perform at a relatively high or low level respectively; unbeknownst to participants the questions were identical across conditions, and performance was to be ranked and posted publicly. The dependent measure of persistence is the number of questions from the original topic set that a participant attempted before electing to switch topics, ranging from 20 to 45. Embarrassment is a composite of four seven-point items adapted from Mosher and White (1981) ("embarrassed," "self-conscious," "ashamed," and "disgraced"; Cronbach's alpha = .89). Measures addressing alternative explanations are unfairness (three items, Cronbach's alpha = .61, with the reported analyses focused on the "unfair" item), negative affect (angry, upset, frustrated, and disappointed; Cronbach's alpha = .89), and change in confidence (the difference between participants' two predictions of the percentage of questions they would answer correctly, made before the challenge and again after the 20th question, with negative values indicating that participants became less confident). Analyses use t-tests, OLS regression, and a 5,000-sample bootstrap with 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals for the mediation test.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supports Hypothesis 1. Among players who lost the first set, the coefficient on favorite is positive and significant (b = 0.0037, p < .001), so being a slight favorite is associated with a 0.37-percentage-point increase in the probability of quitting mid-match, an increase of 26.62% relative to the average quitting rate observed in the data. In the raw data, slight underdogs whose log rank ratio falls between -0.10 and 0 (n = 14,072) retire 1.72% of the time, whereas slight favorites whose log rank ratio is between 0 and 0.10 (n = 13,575) retire at a significantly higher rate of 2.04%, χ²(1) = 3.86, p < .05. The discontinuity is specific to poor initial performance: among players who won the first set the favorite coefficient is not significant (b = 0.0002, p = .72), and the first-set-loser x favorite interaction is positive and significant (b = 0.0053, p < .001). In placebo tests that re-ran the model at 21 threshold values of log rank ratio from -1.0 to 1.0, the largest and most significant favorite coefficient arises at the rank-equality threshold of theoretical interest, significant discontinuities also appear at the adjacent -0.1 and 0.1 values, and no other placebo threshold generates a significant discontinuity. Hypothesis 1 also remains supported under local linear models estimated within an optimal bandwidth around the threshold.

Study 2 replicates Hypothesis 1 and supports Hypotheses 2 and 3. Across the first 20 questions, participants in the middle-school difficulty and expert difficulty conditions both performed poorly (answering 22.76% vs. 23.07% of questions correctly, respectively, p = .80). Consistent with Hypothesis 1, participants in the middle-school difficulty (high expectations) condition switched topics sooner (M = 22.44, SD = 6.34) than participants in the expert difficulty (low expectations) condition (M = 29.89, SD = 11.38), t(302) = 7.07, p < .0001. Consistent with Hypothesis 2, the paper reports that participants in the middle-school difficulty condition felt more embarrassed than participants in the expert difficulty condition, t(302) = 2.64, p = .009. Supporting Hypothesis 3, embarrassment was a significant and negative predictor of persistence (b = -1.16, p < .001), the effect of facing high (vs. low) external expectations on persistence was significantly reduced from b = -7.47 (p < .001) to b = -6.95 (p < .001) when embarrassment was included, and the bootstrapped indirect effect via embarrassment was -0.50 (bootstrapped SE = 0.26) with a 95% bias-corrected confidence interval of [-1.17, -0.11] that did not include 0. The indirect effect remained significant when unfairness, negative affect, and reduced confidence were controlled (indirect effect = -0.84; bootstrapped SE = 0.39; 95% CI [-1.79, -0.22]), and none of those three variables significantly mediated the effect of external expectations on persistence. The manipulation of performance expectations did not produce a link between expectations and performance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the dominant view that high external expectations mainly improve effort, persistence, and performance. It contributes to persistence research by showing that image goals can undermine performance goals, and it extends expectation research by identifying a downside of positive expectations under public setbacks.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should treat positive expectations and ranking systems as potentially costly when employees face visible early setbacks. The paper suggests that managers can reduce premature withdrawal by supporting high-expectation employees after setbacks and by fostering a culture that accepts and learns from failure.

**Limitations**
The field evidence comes from professional men's tennis, a highly visible competitive setting, so the authors call for research in more traditional organizational environments. Both studies involve high performance visibility, leaving uncertainty about whether the same expectation-persistence relationship holds when interim or final performance is not publicly observable.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on whether the pattern reflects a burden of high expectations or a benefit of low expectations. It also suggests examining contexts with less visible performance, the role of internal expectations, and how the perceived legitimacy of quitting excuses moderates the relationship between external expectations and persistence.
