# Strategizing and the Initiation of Interorganizational Collaboration through Prospective Resourcing

**Abstract**
In this paper, we explain how managers establish resource complementarity during their strategizing efforts for interorganizational collaboration. Based on a longitudinal field study at an automotive company, we show that resource complementarity is not given but jointly constructed in interactions with multiple potential partners through recursive cycles of what we refer to as “prospective resourcing.” Prospective resourcing mediates the interplay of strategizing and collaboration, thereby reversing the prevailing logic that strategy precedes and determines collaboration. Our findings offer insight into resourcing as a mechanism for developing strategic initiatives and shows how external actors may influence strategizing.

**Research Question**
How do actors establish resource complementarity when initiating interorganizational collaboration for an innovative strategic initiative? The paper asks how resource complementarity is constructed during strategizing rather than known ex ante as a preexisting partner-selection condition.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: resource complementarity, strategizing, interorganizational collaboration, prospective resourcing, emergent strategy content, partner preferences, and external actors' strategic influence.
- Process / phases: AutoCo moved through recursive cycles of resource exploration, envisioning resource use, and configuring resources across six periods with associated strategic configurations for the Connect initiative.
- Resource exploration: managers and potential partners interacted to understand what external resources could do and which outcomes could be generated from their use.
- Envisioning resource use: managers used growing familiarity with external resources to specify resource needs and imagine how resources could support elements of the strategic initiative.
- Configuring resources: managers aligned internal resources, external resources, strategic objectives, initiative elements, and preferred partners into increasingly complementary resource combinations.
- Emergent outcomes: resource complementarity increased over time, but repeated reconfiguration also shifted partner preferences and frustrated some potential partners.

The process is not a variable-based causal model. The paper theorizes prospective resourcing as the recursive process through which firms turn external and internal resources into complementary combinations while strategy content and collaboration preferences co-evolve. Potential partners do not simply fill known resource gaps; they help reveal, frame, and sometimes redirect what the focal firm comes to see as strategically valuable.

**Data & Measures**
The study is an inductive longitudinal process study of the initiation of collaborations for the "Connect" strategic initiative at AutoCo, an international automotive firm; the first author followed AutoCo's strategy team from June 2010 for 17 months, spending between one and five days per week either on site at AutoCo or at meetings with potential collaboration partners, and the authors completed the field study at the end of February 2012. Observation data (Table 1) total 102 observed meetings, comprising 35 strategy team meetings (weekly recurring starting October 2010), 33 other intraorganizational meetings and workshops (of which 7 half-day or day-long workshops), and 34 interorganizational meetings (of which 15 half-day or day-long workshops), broken down per potential partner firm in Table 1 (the largest counts being TechCom 8 and ITCorp 6); the text reports the same corpus as 68 internal meetings and 34 meetings with potential collaboration partners. The first author took notes of what participants discussed and captured part of the communication verbatim, recorded meetings and transcribed parts of the conversation where possible, participated in informal events such as lunches and dinners, and expanded her notes into more elaborate field notes after each day in the field. Interview data (Table 2) comprise 47 formal, semi-structured interviews and 65 informal interviews, 112 in total, with interviewees sampled from different managerial levels — executive directors, non-executive directors, senior managers, and middle managers; formal interviews addressed the objectives for the initiative, which aspects of the initiative were new or challenging for AutoCo managers, their actions, their interactions with potential partners, and their expectations of the potential collaborations, while informal interviews were used mainly to clarify what had been observed and later interviews became more focused as themes emerged. Members of the potential partner firms were interviewed informally before and after formal meetings and during social events, and after February 2012 the authors held retrospective interviews with ten members of three potential partner firms using timelines to help them recall specific events; all formal and a number of the informal interviews were transcribed verbatim. To further triangulate, the authors created a document database comprising 2,693 documents — the strategy team's email communications plus all sent and received emails about the Connect initiative from two members of two different departments, PowerPoint presentations, memos, technical documents, meeting minutes, published news articles, company announcements, and Intranet sites — imported into a database system that facilitated sorting and querying their content. There are no quantitative variables or estimation; constructs were built through process-analysis methods (Langley, 1999) — an event list, a narrative strategy, a grounded theory strategy, and visual mapping — applied in three steps. Step 1 created a chronological event list (Poole et al., 2000) in which events were defined as actions to advance the strategic initiative, with the document database queried to identify the occurrence and timing of events; the final list contained 273 events, a log of underlying data sources per event created a chain of evidence (Yin, 2009), and descriptive narratives drawing on verbatim quotes were written, with interviewees checking the emerging event list and two key informants checking the case narrative for accuracy. Step 2 inductively clustered events by element — an element being a set of related actions regarding a specific part of the strategic initiative, defined from how informants provisionally split up their work in loosely defined work packages — and used the moments when strategic configurations shifted significantly to define six periods with associated strategic configurations, where a strategic configuration is a combination of specific internal resources, external resources, and partners across the elements of the initiative. Step 3 analyzed progress by tracing how the configurations that defined the six periods came about, comparing cycles of prospective resourcing and differences in progress across periods to arrive at a process model. The design is inductive and interpretive and the paper develops a process model rather than claiming causal identification.

**Key Findings**
Across all six periods the authors identify a process they label prospective resourcing: actions that turn external and internal resources into complementary combinations for future use, consisting of three sub-processes — resource exploration (interacting with potential partners to identify external resources that can potentially advance a strategic initiative, enabling understanding of precisely what such resources entail and of possible outcomes from their use), envisioning resource use (thinking through options for external resource deployment to further specify the resources a particular element may need), and configuring resources (aligning and integrating the developments of separate elements into synergistic combinations across elements). In the process model (Figure 2), resource exploration and envisioning resource use may occur in parallel for the various elements and utilize different potential partners, while configuring integrates these parallel streams and feeds back into strategizing and the initiation of collaboration. The Connect initiative was developed in interaction with over 30 potential partners, and the six periods carry strategic configurations labeled C1–C8 (Figure 1, Table 4), ranging from a strategy to fully outsource Connect's development, to taking a central role in orchestrating different partners' input, to a strategy to develop parts in house. The first three configurations (C1–C3) were discarded altogether, which required starting anew, whereas later configurations (C4–C8) were adapted more incrementally; only after six iterative cycles of prospective resourcing was AutoCo able to develop a strategic configuration where the resource combinations were sufficiently complementary, and this more fully developed strategy allowed for the formal initiation of collaboration. Configuring involved (re)prioritizing, changing, adding, or discontinuing elements: some elements were subsumed under others (in the service integration configuration C4, "IT platform" became part of "service development"), new elements were created (e.g., the field validation), and some elements were foregrounded over others. Potential partners actively steered the focal firm's strategizing — ITCorp proactively demonstrated its data analytics tools, which AutoCo adopted as an integral part of its initiative and made a central resource in strategic configurations C4–8, and ITCorp's Software Development Kit was incorporated into the plans for the open services platform, while TechCom alerted AutoCo managers to the importance of device management, signaling interdependencies between the connectivity hardware and service integration elements of which the managers had been unaware. Partner preferences shifted accordingly: the preferred partner for service development changed from ITCorp to TechCom to ServeIT because of the changing emphasis from data analytics to device management to wireless data communication management, and ServeIT — initially treated as a mere benchmark generator and the least attractive of the three potential partners — was eventually selected as the actual service integrator partner. The amplitude of change was conditioned by novelty: for novel elements such as service development, more radical and sudden changes in strategy emerged compared with the relatively straightforward specification for the more familiar connectivity hardware, and over time, as novelty decreased, changes became less abrupt and more incremental, a decrease the authors attribute partly to the managers' approach of tackling the more novel elements first. The authors also report a relational cost: rising expectations about collaboration proved inappropriate as new configurations emerged, resulting in tensions and unfulfilled expectations — a senior manager from ITCorp reflected that they "certainly won't be doing a project with AutoCo any time soon," and TechCom's senior managers were disappointed and angry after having spent significant time and money — and the risk of frustrating potential partners was higher for partners who contributed resources for the more novel elements, because those elements were the most prone to volatile changes. The key insight the authors draw is that resource complementarity is interactively created through prospective resourcing instead of being a pre-existing condition for collaboration, so that the process model reverses the prevailing logic that strategy is an initial condition that precedes and determines collaboration.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to interorganizational collaboration research by showing that resource complementarity can be interactively created through prospective resourcing rather than identified before collaboration begins. It contributes to strategizing research by explaining how strategy content emerges relationally through interactions with external actors under novelty and complexity. It also extends resourcing and resource-based perspectives by showing how resource bundles gain strategic value through distributed, joint configuration rather than through the simple orchestration of preexisting resource pieces.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that managers initiating innovative collaborations should not expect to know all resource needs or partner-selection criteria upfront. They need to keep multiple partner options open long enough to explore possible synergies, while also recognizing that prolonged informal exploration can create unmet expectations and burn social capital. Managers should pay close attention to how potential partners frame the initiative around their own resources.

**Limitations**
The authors identify novelty and complexity as boundary conditions for the model. The study focuses on one innovative strategic initiative at an international automotive firm, where required resources were distant and unfamiliar and where multiple interdependent elements required several types of partners. Less novel and less complex initiatives may require fewer cycles of prospective resourcing.

**Future Research**
The authors call for more research into the connections between interorganizational and intraorganizational processes and further cross-fertilization between research on strategizing and interorganizational collaboration. Future research could examine prospective resourcing in initiatives with lower novelty or complexity, in other kinds of innovative strategic objectives, and in settings where needed resources such as financial resources or distribution channels are more familiar.
