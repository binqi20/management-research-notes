# The Pivot: How Founders Respond to Feedback through Idea and Identity Work

**Abstract**
Efforts to incorporate external feedback are central to the process of entrepreneurship and to that of creative work more broadly, yet, because individuals may view aspects of their creative ideas as linked to their self-concepts, this can trigger resistance toward revision. Thus, feedback-induced change, while likely intended to increase the viability of creative ideas, might paradoxically undermine that viability by compromising creative workers’ associated identity-based relationships with their creative endeavors. While existing scholarship has established the importance of creative revision, research has largely overlooked how this vital process intersects with creative workers’ identities. Through a field study of 59 founders and their entrepreneurial ideas, I present an identity-based process model of creative revision that highlights differences in founders’ psychological ownership of their ideas and how those differences affect subsequent revision efforts. The emerging findings contribute to existing theory by revealing that the capacity to extend the novelty and usefulness of one’s ideas is not merely subject to informational constraints but also to identity-based constraints.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how founders respond to external feedback when that feedback requires changes to entrepreneurial ideas that are linked to their self-concepts. More specifically, it examines how psychological ownership of ideas shapes founders' idea work, identity work, and ability to balance differentiation and assimilation within an entrepreneurial community.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: creative revision, identity-sharpening feedback, psychological ownership reappraisal, idea work, identity work, collective sensemaking, breadth of prior creative work experience, and optimal distinctiveness.
- Process / phases: identity-sharpening feedback triggers founders to reappraise psychological ownership of their ideas by reaffirming, abstracting, or relinquishing that ownership.
- Idea work practices: reaffirming ownership is linked to defending; abstracting ownership is linked to repairing; relinquishing ownership is linked to re-engineering.
- Identity work practices: defending supports transcending, repairing supports decoupling, and re-engineering supports professionalizing.
- Conditioning factors: collective sensemaking and broader prior creative work experience make founders more willing to reconsider feedback and loosen psychological ownership.
- Emergent outcome: decoupling is the path most clearly associated with optimal distinctiveness because it lets founders keep commitment to a broader creative vision while revising specific assumptions.

The central process is identity-based rather than only informational. Feedback changes founders' attention and intentions, but those effects depend on whether founders experience ideas as extensions of the self. When founders keep ownership tightly attached to the original idea, feedback is treated as a threat and revision narrows into defense. When they abstract or relinquish ownership, they can repair or re-engineer the idea and reposition the self in relation to competing founder roles.

**Data & Measures**
The study is an inductive, grounded theory field study (Fendt & Sachs, 2008; Locke, 2001; Strauss & Corbin, 1990) of 59 founders and their feedback providers, conducted over eight months and drawing on serial interviews, non-participant observations, and archival documentation. Site and participant selection used purposive sampling (Patton, 2002; Yin, 2009): the author negotiated access to two entrepreneurial incubators, both in the same region of the United States, with concentrated pools of resource-constrained founders gathering feedback while seeking resources to commercialize their ideas, and within those contexts sought founders who were actively pursuing feedback and capital investment opportunities, who were early enough in the commercialization process (e.g., pre-revenue, pre-capital investment) that feedback was particularly meaningful, and who had been directly involved in generating the ideas and launching the ventures they were pursuing. For ventures with founding teams each founder was treated as a separate case, and the analytical unit of analysis was the founder and his or her practices. The primary sample comprised 26 ongoing incubator residents whom the author observed and interviewed frequently; when the study started, 15 of these participants had just been accepted into a 14-week program designed to accelerate customer development and capital acquisition. Using the incubators' documentation the author selected another 28 founders affiliated with (but who had not applied to be residents of) the incubators, whose interactions were limited to irregular mentorship sessions with domain experts, and convenience sampling added five more founders with no exposure to the incubators; across the sample, 26 participants were incubator residents and 29 were part of cofounding teams. Data collection was triangulated (Denzin & Lincoln, 1994; Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2009) across three sources — semi-structured interviews, observations, and documentation. The author conducted 103 interviews with an average duration of 30 minutes, focused on surfacing participants' experiences related to idea development, feedback interactions, subsequent changes to their ideas, and their ongoing relationships to their creative work; Table 1 attributes 69 of these interviews to the primary sample and 34 to the secondary sample, and adds 12 stakeholder interviews with two incubator directors and 10 business mentors. Most direct observation involved the primary sample and covered 17 pitch events, 10 private (i.e., closed-door) and five semi-private (i.e., within a public meeting space) mentoring sessions, producing more than three months' worth of field notes and 36 hours of recorded and transcribed audio files. Documentation comprised notes taken by mentors during their feedback interactions with founders from both samples, 20 pages of peer evaluations, six business plans, eight pitch decks, sundry marketing collateral and business modeling artifacts, and the "Business Model Canvas" (Osterwalder & Pigneur, 2010) on which primary-sample founders documented their assumptions about how their entrepreneurial ideas would create value, which allowed the author to capture data on the evolution of the founders' ideas and underlying assumptions. There are no quantitative variables or estimation. Analysis used the constant comparative method, consistent with Strauss and Corbin (1990) and Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013), in three steps. First-order codes were built from the terms frequently employed by participants during observations and interviews — for changes in the ideas these included words and phrases conveying both the degree of change and the specific aspects requiring revision (e.g., "overhaul the model" vs. "tweak the cost structure"), alongside codes for the feedback context such as rhetorical differences in the feedback, forum type (public or private), the number of people in attendance, and whether founders discussed the feedback with peers afterwards; data saturation was declared once first-order coding failed to reveal additional unique terms, and each first-order code carried into later stages was present in at least five of the 59 observed cases. Second, axial coding compared similar first-order codes (e.g., references to "my idea", and personal narratives conveying a sense of control over an idea or investments of time, energy, and values into it) to surface second-order themes, each of which was present in at least 11 of the 59 observed cases. Third, selective coding refined the categories and themes into a model of creative revision, using "temporal bracketing" (Langley, 1999) to segment feedback episodes — each comprising the initial feedback, subsequent efforts to process and make sense of it, and practices aimed at revising the founder's idea and identity — and cluster them by founder so that patterns could be perceived within and across cases. Figure 1 presents the resulting data structure of first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions; Table 2 reports case-level patterns for all 59 founders; Table 3 gives exemplary quotes per theme. The design is inductive and interpretive: the paper builds a process model from observed patterns rather than testing hypotheses or claiming causal identification.

**Key Findings**
The study's emergent finding is an identity-based process model of creative revision (Figure 2) in which identity-sharpening feedback triggers psychological ownership reappraisal, which in turn conditions idea work, identity work, and the ability to achieve optimal distinctiveness. Identity-sharpening feedback practices discounted founders' creative ideas as either too different from creative standards or too similar to ideas that had already been tested or established; although such practices exclusively targeted the creative ideas rather than the entrepreneurs, they primed founders' identity-based connections to their ideas by introducing reflected appraisals that diverged from their own self-concepts, and they contrasted with practices such as dialogic problem solving, which were less likely to trigger an identity-informed revision process. Following such feedback, entrepreneurs reappraised their psychological ownership in three ways: reaffirming it (post-feedback references to maintaining control or the integrity of the original idea), abstracting it (references to maintaining control or the integrity of one's larger vision or mission), or relinquishing it (references to the unimportance of ideas, treating one's ideas as hypotheses). Each reappraisal allowed a different combination of idea work, and founders who engaged in more pronounced types of idea work also engaged in the less pronounced types: reaffirming led to defending (clarifying, justifying, and broadening any or all aspects of the creative model); abstracting led to repairing plus defending (questioning aspects of the model, narrowing the original assumptions, and replacing peripheral aspects); relinquishing led to re-engineering plus repairing and defending (criticizing any or all aspects of the model and replacing the key assumptions underpinning the idea). Entrepreneurs confronted two competing, culturally based conceptions of the founder role — a "scientific founder" role explicitly championed by all of the resource providers, mentors, and incubator administrators and reinforced by circulated literature including The Lean Startup, Business Model Generation, and The Four Steps to the Epiphany, and an equally prominent "visionary founder" role whose expectations explicitly contradict those of the scientific role — and each idea work pattern encouraged a distinct identity work practice: defending encouraged transcending (increasing identification with roles that portray creative work as non-formulaic, e.g., the visionary founder role), repairing encouraged decoupling (dis-identifying from any socially prescribed creative role, treating the founder role as a hat to be put on when useful), and re-engineering encouraged professionalizing (increasing identification with roles that portray creative work as formulaic, e.g., the scientific founder role). The three identity work practices differed in their effects on optimal distinctiveness within the local entrepreneurial community: transcending enhanced founders' sense of differentiation yet impeded their assimilation, leading them to distance themselves from feedback providers they perceived as misinformed, in a number of cases to stop going to social events attended by peers and feedback providers, and to seek membership in other expert communities that might provide a stronger fit — an isolation the author reports as deleterious to their ability to attract resources; professionalizing strengthened founders' affiliations with the local entrepreneurial community but diminished the strength of their sense of distinctiveness, rendering them more susceptible to new entrepreneurial or career opportunities that might better affirm their unique creativity; and only decoupling provided entrepreneurs with a sense of optimal distinctiveness, the ability to both fit in with and stand out from the local start-up community. Two biographical and experiential differences conditioned which path founders took. Collective sensemaking — discussing feedback with cofounders and incubator peers — increased dialogue about the ideas and the feedback, shifted founders' attention and intentions toward a pragmatic (outcome-oriented) rather than idealistic (values-oriented) and typically defensive response, conferred a degree of legitimacy on challenging feedback, and made founders more willing to alter or relinquish their psychological ownership; it also clarified the scientific and visionary founder roles, compressing the time between idea work and identity work, and encouraged mimetic adoption and validation of similar idea and identity work practices among peers who identified similarly. Breadth of creative work experience operated in a parallel way: founders with more diversified prior experience tended to be more receptive to identity-sharpening feedback practices, perceived outsiders' creative inputs to have more value, and rarely took idea- or task-based feedback personally, whereas founders with specialized or limited creative work experience tended to perceive outsiders as challenging their creative authority or domain expertise and thus their self-concepts. The author reports being surprised to find that the founders most likely to incorporate extensive and dramatic change were those most extensively embedded within peer groups and conversant with their peers: among the 26 incubator residents in the sample, 17 had cofounders, and in all 17 cases the entrepreneurs engaged in more extensive forms of idea and identity work, which the paper reads as a striking reinforcing effect between incubator peers and cofounders in encouraging founders to relinquish at least some psychological ownership of their ideas; peer group conversations created a context in which founders could "depersonalize" external feedback and view idea-based feedback as a standard part of the creative revision process rather than as personal criticism. Overall, entrepreneurs who reaffirmed their psychological ownership were more highly restricted in their idea and identity work than those who abstracted or relinquished it, a relationship operating through the mechanism of constrained attention and intentions that led them to overlook or dismiss much of the advice and criticism they received.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends creative revision theory by showing that revision is constrained not only by information deficits but also by identity-based constraints rooted in psychological ownership. It bridges research on psychological ownership and founder identities by introducing psychological ownership reappraisal and showing how founder role identities shift during feedback encounters. It also contributes to identity scholarship by showing how competing visionary and scientific founder roles create role equivocality and make optimal distinctiveness central to entrepreneurial revision.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that founders, mentors, and resource providers should treat feedback as both idea work and identity work. Feedback processes are more likely to support viable revision when they help founders preserve a coherent broader purpose while questioning specific business-model assumptions. Incubators and founder teams may also improve revision by enabling collective sensemaking that depersonalizes criticism and helps founders evaluate feedback pragmatically.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on idea-stage founders who were pre-revenue, pre-customer, and pre-funding, which allowed the author to isolate idea and identity work before later accountabilities and dependencies entered the venture. The founders were all highly involved in generating their ideas and were pursuing capital, so the findings are especially suited to contexts where ideas feel novel, personal, and exposed to resource-provider judgment. The paper also notes that equity-based financing contexts represent only a subset of entrepreneurial settings.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how later-stage accountability and resource dependencies condition the identity and idea work that follow feedback. The paper also calls for work on the long-term effects of early idea and identity work, including imprinting on organizational culture, founder resilience, and venture success. Additional studies could test the model with different founder types, such as necessity entrepreneurs, franchise operators, later-recruited founders, and other creative workers such as artists, designers, and scholars.
