# Safe Bets or Hot Hands? How Status and Celebrity Influence Strategic Alliance Formations by Newly Public Firms

**Abstract**
Social approval assets derive their value from favorable stakeholder perceptions. Past research has focused primarily on their role as signals that reduce stakeholders’ perceived uncertainty about the firm. However, social approval assets can also serve as frames that influence how other information is interpreted. We theorize how the frames associated with two social approval assets—status and celebrity—influence the interpretation of equivocal information about newly public firms. Specifically, we examine how each frame influences the way underpricing is interpreted, and how these interpretations, as well as the joint effects of possessing status and celebrity, influence newly public firms’ strategic alliance formations. We explore these ideas in the ambiguity-ridden context of “Dot-Com” firms during the commercial dawn of the Internet. Our findings generally support our arguments, providing new theory and evidence about the framing effects of social approval assets with different sociocognitive content, and the dynamics of information and frame (in)congruence.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how status and celebrity, as social approval assets with different sociocognitive content, shape stakeholders' interpretation of equivocal IPO underpricing information. It specifically examines how those frames, separately and jointly, influence newly public firms' strategic alliance formations in the Dot-Com context.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Status will have a stronger positive effect on the relationship between underpricing and alliance formations by newly public firms when underpricing is low than when underpricing is high.
Hypothesis 2. Celebrity will have a stronger positive effect on the relationship between underpricing and alliance formations by newly public firms when underpricing is high than when underpricing is low.
Hypothesis 3. Newly public firms will form fewer strategic alliances when they possess both celebrity and high-status affiliations than when they possess one or the other.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Status affiliations, celebrity, IPO underpricing, and the interactions among status, celebrity, and underpricing.
- DV(s): Number of post-IPO strategic alliances formed during the first year after the IPO.
- Mediators: Not directly modeled; the theory centers on interpretive framing of underpricing as low uncertainty or market excitement.
- Moderators: Status moderating the underpricing-alliance relationship, celebrity moderating the underpricing-alliance relationship, and the joint possession of status and celebrity.

The mechanism is that status and celebrity act as interpretive frames rather than only as signals. Status draws attention to analytical assessments of vetted quality and affiliation, whereas celebrity draws attention to affective excitement and future potential. Because underpricing is equivocal, celebrity amplifies the positive interpretation of high underpricing, while joint status and celebrity can create interpretative uncertainty that reduces the value of possessing status alone.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: Compustat, firm proxy statements, the Compact Disclosure SEC database, the Securities Data Corporation (SDC) Joint Ventures database, the SDC New Issues database, IPO prospectuses, and LexisNexis; venture capitalist status came from the Thomson Banker One Private Equity data used by Pollock and colleagues. The initial sample was 359 U.S.-based Internet start-up firms that conducted their IPOs between 1995 and 2000, and after accounting for missing data the final sample included 347 firms; t-tests confirmed no differences between the initial and final samples on celebrity, status, underpricing, and the number of strategic alliances formed. Table 1 reports summary statistics and correlations with n = 359, and the negative binomial models in Table 2 are estimated on n = 347.
- DV: Post-IPO strategic alliances = the number of alliances a firm entered into during the first year after its IPO, counted from the SDC Joint Ventures database and including all forms of strategic alliance represented there (marketing agreements, R&D alliances, product licensing agreements, and equity joint ventures).
- IV: Status = the sum of two high-status affiliation indicators, ranging from 0 (no high-status affiliations) to 2 (affiliations with both a high-status underwriter and a high-status VC). Lead-VC status was coded 1 if the VC was in the top quartile of a VC status index based on Bonacich (1987) beta centrality in co-investment syndication networks in the year the firm went public, and 0 otherwise. Underwriter status used Jay Ritter's modified version of the Carter and colleagues index, which ranges from 0 (low status) to 9 (high status), coded 1 when the value was greater than 8.75 and 0 otherwise.
- IV: Celebrity = a binary indicator coded 1 if a firm scored 1 on all three of high media coverage (top quartile of the number of articles about the firm in a year), high positive affect (mean positivity of the firm's coverage greater than 75%, where positivity is the LIWC 2007 ratio of positive affective words to total affective words), and nonconforming language (share of nonconforming words above the 25th percentile of the sample), and 0 otherwise; firms were coded as celebrities if they met the criteria in either the year of their IPO or the year prior. The corpus was 6,006 Red Herring articles published between 1995 and 2000 and retrieved via LexisNexis, and the custom nonconforming-language dictionary was built from 94 candidate words rated by five expert raters (ICC = 0.86), of which 29 were retained. This produced 72 celebrity firms, representing 20% of the sample.
- IV: Underpricing = the percentage change in stock price on the first day the stock was traded on a national exchange multiplied by 100, natural-logged to normalize the distribution (after adding .01 to the positive counterpart of the lowest observed underpricing value) and mean-centered.
- Controls: founder-CEO, firm age, board size, business type (business-to-business and business-to-consumer dummies with infrastructure omitted), IPO year dummies for 1999 and 2000 (IPOs before 1999 omitted), California-based, cash before IPO, IPO free cash flow (winsorized at the 1% level and z-standardized), number of VC firms, and pre-IPO alliances.

Estimation: negative binomial regression with robust standard errors for the count dependent variable, followed by comparisons of predicted marginal estimates using the mlincom command in the spost13 package of Stata 14, with all other variables held at their means (continuous measures) or modes (discrete measures). Because the hypotheses concern the predictors' relative effect sizes, the regression coefficients alone were treated as insufficient for hypothesis testing, and the analyses testing Hypotheses 1 and 2 assumed that firms did not possess both high status and celebrity simultaneously. Multicollinearity diagnostics gave a mean VIF of 1.39, no individual VIF greater than 3.0, and condition numbers less than 9. The design is archival and associational; the authors address endogeneity concerns by re-testing the hypotheses with residualized versions of status, celebrity, and underpricing constructed from OLS predictions, rather than with a causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was not supported. In Table 3, moving from low to high status raised predicted alliances by 1.58 (1.52 to 3.10) at low underpricing (-1 SD), a marginally significant change (p < 0.10), and by 3.14 (1.75 to 4.89) at high underpricing (+1 SD), a significant change (p < 0.01); the difference in changes that tests H1 was 1.56 alliances and was not statistically significant. The authors read this as status having a significant main effect on alliance formations but no significant moderating effect on the relationship between underpricing and alliance formations.
Hypothesis 2 was supported. Celebrity was associated with a nonsignificant decrease of 0.07 alliances (1.45 minus 1.52) at low underpricing and with 2.25 more alliances (4.00 minus 1.75) at high underpricing (p < 0.05); the difference between these effects, 2.32 alliances, was statistically significant (p < 0.05).
Hypothesis 3 was supported for the effect of celebrity on status, but not for the effect of status on celebrity. In Table 4, for high-status firms also being a celebrity resulted in 1.65 fewer alliances (2.26 versus 3.91), a statistically significant difference (p < 0.05), whereas for low-status firms being a celebrity made no significant difference (0.81). The difference between low- and high-status firms that are not celebrities was 2.28 alliances (p < 0.01), confirming the main effect of status, but among celebrity firms the possession of high status was not associated with a significant difference (-0.18).
The baseline effect of underpricing when status and celebrity are both low was 1.52 alliances at low underpricing and 1.75 alliances at high underpricing, and the difference between these two values was not significant.
Robustness checks: repeating the analyses with analyst coverage as the dependent variable (Appendix B) showed the same pattern of support - H1 not supported, H2 supported, and H3 supported for the effect of celebrity on status with no significant effect of status on celebrity. Substituting Fortune for Red Herring (4,131 articles; celebrity firms reduced from 72 to 50) retained support for H3 but lost support for H2. Using the top-50 major U.S. newspapers (1,269 feature articles; 93 celebrities) produced support for none of the hypotheses, and restricting the celebrity set to the 26 firms that were celebrities in both the industry and mass-media corpora supported H3 but not H2 (results in a consistent direction, significance dropping to p = 0.13). Lowering the positive-tenor cut-off to 70% gave substantively similar results; using the top quartile of media tenor in a given year dropped 38 celebrity firms (53% of those originally identified) and affected support for H3; raising the cut-off to 80% removed support for all hypotheses. Results based on the residualized independent variables were consistent with those reported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends research on social approval assets by theorizing and testing their framing effects, not just their signaling effects. It shows that status and celebrity have different sociocognitive content, that celebrity reinforces the affective interpretation of high underpricing, and that incongruent social approval frames can weaken rather than accumulate each other's benefits.

**Practical Implication**
For managers of newly public firms, the findings suggest that high-status affiliations are generally more productive for gaining alliance partners than pursuing media celebrity. The paper also warns that firms with high-status affiliations should be cautious about courting celebrity because the combination may create interpretative uncertainty, although celebrity can help when market sentiment is already strongly positive.

**Limitations**
The empirical setting is limited to high-tech Dot-Com firms in an ambiguity-ridden historical context, so the authors cannot establish that the findings generalize to other time periods or industries. The data are cross-sectional and archival, which means changes in status and celebrity over time and stakeholders' underlying psychological reactions are measured indirectly rather than observed directly.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for additional investigation of interpretive frames in other time periods, contexts, stakeholder audiences, and types of social approval assets. It also suggests using methods such as laboratory studies or policy-capturing designs to test stakeholders' psychological reactions to status, celebrity, and other frames under high uncertainty.
