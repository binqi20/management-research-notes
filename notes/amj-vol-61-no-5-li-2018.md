# From Employee-experienced High-involvement Work System to Innovation: An Emergence-based Human Resource Management Framework

**Abstract**
The influence of human resource management on innovation has attracted considerable research attention over the last decade. However, existing studies have primarily focused on the macro-level human resource management architecture, limiting our understanding about the cross-level origin of innovation. Developing an emergence-based human resource management framework, we propose that an employee-experienced high-involvement work system (HIWS) promotes innovation by eliciting collective interactions for knowledge exchange and aggregation. Further, we investigate the emergence-enabling process that facilitates an employee-experienced HIWS to give rise to organization-level innovation. Specifically, we probe three distinct emergence enablers that amplify the positive influence of HIWS on innovation by shaping the concertedness, direction, and adaptability of collective interactions: (1) the homogeneity of HIWS experiences as the internal mechanism, (2) the strategic importance of innovation as the external mechanism, and (3) the churn in human resources as the temporal mechanism. We tested our theoretical model using data from a nationally representative sample of workplaces in Canada (n 5 2,639). Our results suggest that an employeeexperienced HIWS was positively related to innovation. In addition, this positive effect was amplified by all three emergence enablers (i.e., the homogeneity of HIWS experiences, the strategic importance of innovation, and the churn in human resources).

**Research Question**
The paper asks how employee-experienced high-involvement work systems give rise to organization-level innovation. It focuses on whether collective interactions for knowledge exchange and aggregation are amplified by three emergence enablers: homogeneity of HIWS experiences, strategic importance of innovation, and churn in human resources.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. An employee-experienced HIWS is positively related to innovation.
- Hypothesis 2. The homogeneity of HIWS experiences moderates the relation between an employee-experienced HIWS and innovation, such that this positive relation is more (vs. less) pronounced when HIWS experiences are more (vs. less) homogeneous.
- Hypothesis 3. The strategic importance of innovation moderates the relation between an employee-experienced HIWS and innovation, such that this positive relation is more (vs. less) pronounced when the strategic importance of innovation is high (vs. low).
- Hypothesis 4. The churn in human resources moderates the relation between an employee-experienced HIWS and innovation, such that this positive relation is more (vs. less) pronounced when the churn in human resources is high (vs. low).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employee-experienced high-involvement work system, measured from employee reports of flexible job design, information sharing, problem-solving teams, self-directed teams, gainsharing, and training.
- DV(s): Workplace innovation in 2006, measured as an ordinal product and process innovation launch outcome.
- Mediators: No mediator is directly tested; knowledge exchange and aggregation through collective interactions are the theorized emergence process.
- Moderators: Homogeneity of HIWS experiences, strategic importance of innovation, and churn in human resources.

The theorized mechanism is bottom-up: employee-experienced HIWS creates opportunities for collective interactions, through which employee knowledge is exchanged and aggregated into organization-level innovation. Homogeneous HIWS experiences increase concertedness, strategic importance of innovation directs collective effort toward innovation, and human resource churn increases adaptability by refreshing knowledge and reducing stagnation. The results support the positive HIWS-innovation relationship and show that all three emergence enablers strengthen it.

**Data & Measures**
The study used the Workplace and Employee Survey (WES) collected by Statistics Canada, a data set that links employees to workplaces, where workplaces are akin to establishments. Statistics Canada built the Workplace Survey through stratified random sampling by industry, region, and size, and sampled a maximum of 12 employees at each workplace for the Employee Survey using a probability mechanism. From the multi-wave 1999-2006 WES data set the authors selected the 2005-2006 waves, because those waves included the most recent and complete set of variables used in the study and had the largest sample size across all surveyed years; the independent variables and control variables were measured in 2005 and the dependent variable, innovation, was measured in 2006. The authors excluded employees who did not answer questions about HRM practices (16.28%), Employee Survey responses from managerial employees (10.48%), workplaces with fewer than three employee reports (5.37%), and non-profit workplaces (14.01%), producing a research sample of 2,753 workplaces and 13,032 employees; after deleting observations with missing values in variables of interest, the final sample consisted of 2,639 workplaces and 12,519 employees, with a mean workplace size of approximately 49 employees (SD = 100.09) and an average of 19.32 years (SD = 17.56) at the current address. DV: innovation (Workplace Survey in 2006) was measured with two innovation-launch items, product innovation and process innovation, with workplaces reporting no product or process innovation coded 0, those reporting either coded 1, and those reporting both coded 2. IV: the employee-experienced HIWS (Employee Survey in 2005) was measured with six HRM practices commonly used to involve employees - flexible job design, information sharing, problem-solving teams, self-directed teams, gainsharing, and training (1 = yes, 0 = no) - taken from the Employee Survey rather than from managerial reports and aggregated from employees' individual ratings to the workplace level; a categorical indicator-based confirmatory factor analysis supported a one-factor model (chi-square = 210.38, df = 9, p < .01; CFI = .97; TLI = .95; RMSEA = .04), Cronbach's alpha was .71, the mean rWG(J) across workplaces was .57, and ICC(1) = .24 with ICC(2) = .61. Internal moderator: the homogeneity of HIWS experiences (Employee Survey in 2005) was derived from employee HIWS responses using a dispersion model and measured by both the standard deviation and rWG(J); for the standard deviation measure the authors multiplied it by -1 so that higher values represent higher homogeneity, results were virtually the same for the two measures, and only the standard deviation findings are presented, with the HIWS and the homogeneity measure modeled simultaneously to address range restriction. External moderator: the strategic importance of innovation (Workplace Survey in 2005) was measured with three manager-rated items on the importance (from 1, of no importance, to 6, crucial) of undertaking research and development, developing new products/services, and developing new production/operating techniques; Cronbach's alpha was .81. Temporal moderator: the churn in human resources (Workplace Survey in 2005) was measured as the total number of joiners and leavers scaled by the total number of employees in the workplace. Control variables from the 2005 Workplace Survey were workplace age, workplace size, profitability, union density, and 14 dichotomous industry sectors; Table 2 additionally reports multi-plant firm, percentage of male employees, percentage of full-time employees, percentage of on-site employees, and average employee wage. Because the dependent variable is ordinal, the authors conducted ordered probit regressions, using the workplace survey weights provided by Statistics Canada for representativeness and testing the moderating effects with three-step hierarchical regressions in which control and independent variables were grand mean-centered and interaction terms were products of the grand mean-centered independent variables; analyses were conducted in Mplus 7.11. The design is associational; the paper reports no causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported. In Model 2, the employee-experienced HIWS was positively and significantly related to innovation (b = 1.55, p < .01); a one standard deviation increase from its mean resulted in a 2.2-percentage-point increase in the probability of having either product or process innovation and a 7.5-percentage-point increase in the probability of having both product and process innovation. Model 2 also showed that the strategic importance of innovation was positively and significantly related to innovation (b = .20, p < .01), where a one standard deviation increase from its mean raised the probability of having either product or process innovation by 2.4 percentage points and the probability of having both by 8.5 percentage points, and that the churn in human resources was positively and significantly related to innovation (b = .06, p < .01), where a one standard deviation increase from its mean raised the probability of having either product or process innovation by 0.8 percentage points and the probability of having both by 2.1 percentage points. Together, the employee-experienced HIWS, the homogeneity of HIWS experiences, the strategic importance of innovation, and the churn in human resources accounted for 10.8% of the variance in predicting innovation above and beyond the control variables.

The three moderation hypotheses were also supported, with the discussed coefficients taken from Model 3.2, which adds the three interaction terms among the moderators to the three hypothesized interactions of Model 3.1; the authors report that their main findings were virtually the same for the two models. For Hypothesis 2, the interaction between the employee-experienced HIWS and the homogeneity of HIWS experiences was positive and significant (b = 7.95, p < .01), and the plotted pattern showed that the positive effect of HIWS on the probability of innovation was stronger when the homogeneity of HIWS experience was higher. For Hypothesis 3, the interaction between HIWS and the strategic importance of innovation was positive and significant (b = .45, p < .01), and the positive effect of HIWS on the probability of having both product and process innovation was stronger when the strategic importance of innovation was higher; the authors state that they found general support for Hypothesis 3. For Hypothesis 4, the interaction between HIWS and the churn in human resources was positive and significant (b = .57, p < .01), and the positive effect of HIWS on the probability of innovation was stronger when the churn in human resources was higher.

The authors report several robustness and sensitivity checks whose result patterns were virtually the same as or similar to the reported ones: a probit regression using a dichotomous innovation measure, probit regressions treating product innovation and process innovation as two separate outcomes, an analysis controlling for the 2005 innovation baseline, and marginal-effects plots of the interactions following Hoetker (2007).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to strategic HRM by shifting attention from macro-level HRM architecture to the bottom-up emergence process through which employee HRM experiences produce organization-level innovation. It integrates the human capital resource emergence perspective with complex adaptive system theory by specifying internal, external, and temporal emergence-enabling mechanisms. It also extends contingency HRM research by showing that the value of HIWS for innovation depends on homogeneity of employee experiences, strategic innovation priority, and human resource flow.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that organizations competing through innovation should not focus only on adopting HIWS practices. They also need to implement HIWS uniformly across employees, communicate innovation as a clear strategic priority, and actively manage employee inflow and outflow so collective interactions remain adaptive and knowledge-rich.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the archival WES data constrained their measures, including dichotomous innovation items and limited detail on how each HRM practice was implemented across employees. They could measure the quantity of employee mobility but not the quality of mobility. They also could not directly test the mediating processes through which the emergence enablers shape the HIWS-innovation relationship.

**Future Research**
Future research could use field studies to test mediating mechanisms such as transactive memory systems, interpersonal communication, and collective cohesion. The authors also call for work distinguishing exploitative and explorative innovation and for studies of other organizational factors, such as developmental stages and life cycles, that may facilitate or inhibit human resource emergence.
