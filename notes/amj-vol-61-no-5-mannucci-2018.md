# The Differential Impact of Knowledge Depth and Knowledge Breadth on Creativity over Individual Careers

**Abstract**
While usually argued to be fostering creativity, the effect of knowledge depth and breadth on creativity is actually mixed. We take a dynamic approach to the knowledge–creativity relationship and argue that the effect of knowledge depth and knowledge breadth is likely to be contingent on career age. We propose that individuals’ knowledge structures become increasingly rigid as career age grows and that, because of this, knowledge depth and breadth have different effects on creativity at different points of the career. More specifically, we hypothesize that knowledge depth is more beneficial for creativity in earlier stages of one’s career, when creators need to increase the complexity of knowledge structures, while knowledge breadth is more beneficial in later stages, when flexibility is most needed. We test and find support for our hypotheses in a longitudinal study set in the context of the Hollywood animation industry, a setting characterized by the presence of a variety of creators involved in knowledge-intensive activities. Theoretical and practical implications of the results are discussed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when knowledge depth and knowledge breadth are more or less conducive to individual creativity over the course of a career. It examines whether career age changes the creativity effects of depth and breadth because individual knowledge structures become increasingly rigid over time.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: As career age increases, the effect of knowledge depth on creativity becomes less positive.
- H2: As career age increases, the effect of knowledge breadth on creativity becomes more positive.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Knowledge depth, measured as the number of prior movies a creator worked on in the focal movie's main genre; knowledge breadth, measured as the number of different genres a creator had worked on up to the focal year.
- DV(s): Individual creativity, measured as the number of awards and nominations received by a creator in a given year.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper; the theory centers on knowledge-structure complexity, flexibility, and rigidity as cognitive mechanisms.
- Moderators: Career age, measured as time elapsed between the creator's first contribution to the Hollywood animation industry and the observation year.

The mechanism is temporal and cognitive. Early-career creators have relatively flexible but less complex knowledge structures, so depth helps them build richer domain schemas and generate creative outcomes. As career age increases, linkages within and between knowledge domains become more rigid; depth then becomes less beneficial and can intensify entrenchment, whereas breadth helps loosen knowledge structures by exposing creators to different domains.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses archival, longitudinal data on the entire population of core crew members who worked on at least one of the 231 feature-length animated movies produced in the United States and released in movie theaters from 1978 through 2013, with the movie-creator dyad as the unit of analysis. Movies, genres, and core crew were identified from the Internet Movie Database (IMDB) and cross-checked against the American Film Institute Catalog of Motion Pictures (AFI), company websites, and other online sources; because IMDB lists genres alphabetically, the AFI catalog was used to determine each movie's main genre. The core crew comprises the producer, director, writer, editor, cinematographer, art director, production designer, and composer of the original music score. After cleaning, the final sample is an unbalanced panel of 3,409 observations for 2,070 creators, who on average worked on two movies during the observation period (M = 1.95, SD = 1.71). The dependent variable, creativity, is the number of awards and nominations a creator received in a given year from the professional critics' and peers' societies listed in Table 2 (mean 0.71, SD 1.26, range 0 to 13). Convergent validity was assessed with seven expert judges, one for each core-crew role, who rated individual creativity: the 189 ratings collected amounted to 383 observations (340 creators), equal to 11.23% (16.43%) of the sample, of which 87 (46%) were above the mean and 102 (54%) below the mean; creators with an above-the-mean rating received eight times more awards and/or nominations than those with a below-the-mean rating (t = 7.75, p < .001, two-tailed). Each movie genre is treated as a knowledge domain, and the domain of a movie is identified with its main genre. Knowledge depth is measured for a creator at year t as the number of movies the creator has worked on that include the main genre of the movie he or she realized in year t (mean 1.75, SD 1.41, range 1 to 16). Knowledge breadth is the number of different genres an individual has worked on up to the focal year (mean 4.49, SD 1.86, range 1 to 12). The moderator, career age, is the time elapsed between the year of the creator's first contribution to the Hollywood animation industry, which could be of any nature and in any core or noncore role, and the observation year (mean 6.33, SD 7.82, range 0 to 62). Controls are a dummy coded 1 when a creator makes a first contribution in a core role; individual quality, the number of awards received by the creator prior to the focal year; mobility, coded 1 if the creator moved to a different production studio since the previous movie; repeated collaboration, the average strength of all prior collaborations with team members; a sequel dummy; team quality, the sum of awards received for the movie by team members other than the focal creator; the logarithmic transformation of movie budget, taken from IMDB and cross-checked with sources such as Box Office Mojo; critical reception, the average of critics' ratings from rottentomatoes.com for the movie(s) the creator worked on in the focal year; and dummies for observation year, production studios, and roles, with a studio dummy included for each company that produced seven or more movies in the observation period. Because the count dependent variable is overdispersed, the authors estimate a panel negative binomial regression using generalized estimating equations, with an exchangeable correlation structure selected by the QIC statistic, Huber-White robust standard errors equivalent to clustering on the creator, predictors centered before the interaction terms were calculated, and the STATA 14.0 xtgee command. The design is archival and associational rather than causally identified; sample selection is addressed as a robustness check using Lee's (1983) modified version of the Heckman model, with an accelerated failure time model with an exponential distribution estimating the likelihood that a creator leaves the industry.

**Key Findings**
The authors found support for both hypotheses. In the full model (Model 3; 3,409 observations, 2,070 creators), the interaction between knowledge depth and career age is negative and significant (b = -0.005, p < .01), which the authors report as consistent with Hypothesis 1, and the interaction between knowledge breadth and career age is positive and significant (b = 0.004, p < .01), in line with Hypothesis 2; the chi-square test for two degrees of freedom shows that Model 3 improves significantly on Model 2. In Model 2, career age has a positive, marginally significant effect (b = 0.007, p < .10) while knowledge depth (b = 0.030) and knowledge breadth (b = 0.015) have no significant effect on creativity; in Model 3, knowledge depth is positive and significant (b = 0.075, p < .05) while knowledge breadth (b = -0.002) and career age (b = 0.006) are not significant. The marginal-effects analysis shows that knowledge depth has a positive and significant effect (p < .05) in the first 10 years of the career that turns progressively less positive as career age increases, becoming negative and significant in later stages (p < .05), whereas knowledge breadth has no significant effect in the early years of the career and then a positive and significant effect (p < .01). Expressed as incidence rate ratios, each additional level of knowledge depth is associated with an increase in creativity that starts at 8% and then declines toward 4% early in the career, and with a decrease in creativity of between 7% and 24% in later stages, while knowledge breadth does not affect creativity early in the career but is associated with an increase in creativity of between 3% and 27% later in the career. A split-sample estimation of Model 2 reproduces the pattern: at below-median career age the effect of knowledge depth is positive and significant (b = .37, IRR = 1.45, p < .01) while the effect of breadth is nonsignificant, and at above-median career age the effect of knowledge breadth is positive and significant (b = .04, IRR = 1.04, p < .05) while the effect of depth is nonsignificant. Robustness checks are consistent with these results: the inverse Mills ratio has no significant effect on creativity and Model 4 reproduces Model 3; a logistic model with creativity dichotomized between high and low reproduces the negative moderation of depth (p < .01) and the positive moderation of breadth (p < .01); replacing the continuous career age measure with a categorical career-stage variable yields the same directions (depth: b mid career = -.13, p < .10; b late career = -.13, p < .05; breadth: b mid career = .07, p < .05; b late career = .08, p < .05); the linear and squared terms for depth and career age were nonsignificant, which the authors read as showing that curvilinear effects were absent; overall experience had a positive and significant effect (p < .05) but no significant interaction with career age; and the interaction between the two knowledge dimensions was never significant (b = .006, n.s.). In subsamples split by mobility rate, the pattern for knowledge breadth did not differ between independent freelance professionals and permanent hires, but the pattern for knowledge depth did: for independent professionals the effect of depth was positive and significant over the first 10 years (p < .05), decreased over time and became nonsignificant, and turned negative after 30 years of career (p < .10), whereas for permanent hires the effect also turned negative over time but was always nonsignificant. In a subsample of creators in the mid-to-late stages of their career, early knowledge depth had no significant effect on later creativity while early knowledge breadth had a positive and significant effect (b = .06, p < .01). Among the controls, individual quality, team quality, critical reception, and budget are positive and significant (p < .01).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconciles mixed findings about knowledge depth, knowledge breadth, and creativity by showing that their effects depend on career age. It contributes to research on career age and creativity by specifying why different knowledge configurations are useful at different career stages. It also adds a temporal microfoundation to creativity theory by connecting changes in knowledge structures to variation in creative output over individual careers.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that learning and development programs should vary by career stage. Newer employees may benefit more from deepening domain knowledge and learning the rules, norms, and procedures of their field. Later-career employees may need exposure to diverse domains and tasks that broaden their knowledge base and help refresh creative cognition.

**Limitations**
The archival design does not directly observe the cognitive mechanisms by which career age changes knowledge structures, so alternative mechanisms such as skill-building dynamics, intrinsic motivation, or job stability cannot be fully ruled out. The setting is a creative, project-based industry, which may limit generalizability to tasks or industries where creativity is less central. Some robustness checks, including the analysis of job stability among permanent hires, had limited sample size.

**Future Research**
The authors call for future research that directly studies intra-individual creative cognition and the joint role of knowledge and cognition in shaping creativity. They also suggest examining linkages between knowledge domains, not only within-domain schemas, and testing whether other creativity antecedents vary in effect across career stages. Further studies should examine the knowledge-depth, knowledge-breadth, and career-age relationship in settings beyond the animation industry.
