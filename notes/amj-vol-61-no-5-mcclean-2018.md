# The Social Consequences of Voice: An Examination of Voice Type and Gender on Status and Subsequent Leader Emergence

**Abstract**
This paper explores the impact of two types of voice and gender on peer-rated social status and subsequent leader emergence. Across two studies—a three-wave field study and an experiment—we find that speaking up promotively, but not prohibitively, is positively and indirectly related to leader emergence via status, and that this relationship is conditional on the gender of the speaker. Specifically, men who spoke up promotively benefited the most in terms of status and leader emergence, not only compared to men who spoke up prohibitively, but also compared to women who spoke up promotively. This research extends our understanding of the outcomes of voice by articulating how it impacts one’s place in his or her group’s social structure, and ultimately whether he or she is seen as a leader. We also add to our understanding of leader emergence by suggesting that talking a lot or participating at a high level in a group may not be enough to emerge as a leader—it also depends how you do it and who you are.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how peers respond to promotive versus prohibitive voice and whether these voice types shape peer-rated status and subsequent leader emergence. It further asks whether the social consequences of voice differ by speaker gender.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Promotive voice is positively related to status.
- Hypothesis 1b. Prohibitive voice is positively related to status.
- Hypothesis 1c. The positive relationship between promotive voice and status is stronger than the positive relationship between prohibitive voice and status.
- Hypothesis 2. Status mediates the relationship between promotive voice and leader emergence.
- Hypothesis 3. Status mediates the relationship between prohibitive voice and leader emergence.
- Hypothesis 4. Gender moderates the indirect effect of promotive voice on leader emergence via status, such that the relationship is stronger for men than for women.
- Hypothesis 5. Gender moderates the indirect effect of prohibitive voice on leader emergence via status, such that the relationship is stronger for men than for women.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Promotive voice and prohibitive voice; in Study 2, voice type was experimentally manipulated as promotive versus prohibitive.
- DV(s): Leader emergence, measured as peer rankings in Study 1 and perceived leader emergence ratings in Study 2.
- Mediators: Peer-rated status.
- Moderators: Speaker gender.

The proposed mechanism is that voice can function as a status-claiming behavior, but peers grant status only when the behavior is seen as valuable and legitimate. Promotive voice is theorized to be more status-enhancing than prohibitive voice because it emphasizes future-oriented improvement and positive movement toward an ideal state, whereas prohibitive voice highlights problems and potential harm. Expectation states theory further implies that gender shapes legitimacy judgments, so men receive greater status and leader-emergence benefits from promotive voice than women do.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 design and data: a three-wave lagged survey of all 36 United States Military Academy teams competing in the annual Sandhurst competition, administered three months before the competition (Time 1), 10 days before it (Time 2), and immediately following it (Time 3). Each team had 10 competitors including one formal leader, who was excluded from analysis, and 174 of the 324 eligible competing cadets (54%) completed all three surveys.
- Study 1 measures: promotive and prohibitive voice were self-rated at Time 1 as the frequency with which the respondent spoke up in each way, using three items each from Liang et al. (2012) on a 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always) scale; the authors dropped one inapplicable and one redundant item per voice type from Liang et al.'s five-item scales, and a validation study with 284 Master MTurk workers found the three-item measures of promotive and prohibitive voice correlated with the five-item measures at r = .98 and r = .97, respectively. Status was measured at Time 2 by round-robin peer ratings on two items adapted from Anderson, Kraus, Galinsky, and Keltner (2012) — "This person has a high level of respect in others' eyes" and "This person is held in high regard by others" — on a 1 (disagree) to 5 (agree) scale, aggregated over an average of 6.61 peer raters (ICC1 = .39, ICC2 = .83, median rwg = .87). Leader emergence was measured at Time 3 following Smith and Foti's (1998) procedure, with peers ranking all team members in order of preference for who they would want as the future team leader, averaged over an average of 5.52 peer raters and excluding the formal leader's rankings; lower ranks indicate greater preference (a "1" is a respondent's top choice). Gender information was provided by the USMA. Controls were athletic ability and SAT score supplied by the USMA, proactive personality (Crant, 1995), the Big 5 measured with the Ten Item Personality Inventory, and psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) measured at Time 2.
- Study 1 analysis: OLS regressions, a bootstrap relative importance analysis producing partitioned lmg statistics (Tonidandel & LeBreton, 2011), and bootstrap mediation and moderated mediation with 1,000 samples with replacement and percentile confidence intervals. Multilevel tests found no significant between-group variance in leader emergence, so the data were not nested for subsequent analyses. The design is a lagged field survey, so the Study 1 evidence is associational.
- Study 2 design and data: a 2 x 2 between-subjects randomized vignette experiment in which 196 adults across a variety of industries, recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk, evaluated a peer on an insurance sales team at a fictitious company, InsureCo. The average participant was 39 years old, 87% were employed full time, participants averaged 15.6 years of full-time work experience, 55% had a four-year college degree, and 38% were female. The insurance industry was chosen because it is gender balanced.
- Study 2 measures: voice type (promotive vs. prohibitive) and speaker gender (male vs. female) were manipulated by having participants listen to an audio recording of a teammate — "Eric" or "Erica" — delivering a scripted statement that either proposed a new script or called for getting rid of the existing one. Status used the same two-item measure as Study 1 (alpha = .71). Leader emergence used seven items (alpha = .85) from Lanaj and Hollenbeck (2015), rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher ratings indicating greater emergence. Manipulation checks assessed perceived promotive voice, prohibitive voice, collective focus, size of change, riskiness, and complaining. Analysis used analyses of variance with Tukey HSD contrasts and bootstrap mediation with 1,000 random samples with replacement and percentile confidence intervals; the authors describe this randomized design as providing evidence of causality.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 174), voice and status: Hypothesis 1a was supported — promotive voice was significantly related to status when entered independently (Model 1: b = .17 (.06), p < .01) and remained significant when both voice types were entered together (Model 3: b = .20 (.08), p = .02). Hypothesis 1b was not supported — prohibitive voice was only marginally significant when entered independently (Model 2: b = .10 (.05), p = .07) and was not significant when the two voice types were entered together. Hypothesis 1c was supported — the relative importance analysis produced lmg values of .05 for promotive and .02 for prohibitive voice, and the 95% confidence interval around the difference excluded zero (difference = .03 [.0002, .08]).
- Study 1, mediation: Hypothesis 2 was supported — the indirect effect of promotive voice on leader emergence via status was significant (effect = -0.29; 95% CI [-0.61, -0.03]); because leader emergence is a rank in which 1 is the first choice for leader, a negative effect indicates greater emergence. The direct effect of promotive voice was also significant (effect = -1.05; 95% CI [-1.82, -0.24]), which the authors read as complementary mediation. Hypothesis 3 was not supported — the confidence interval around the indirect effect of prohibitive voice included zero (effect = 0.04; 95% CI [-0.25, 0.32]).
- Study 1, moderated mediation: Hypothesis 4 was supported — the promotive voice by gender interaction was significantly related to status when considered independently (Model 1: b = -.31 (.14), p = .02) and when entered jointly with the prohibitive voice interaction (Model 3: b = -.41 (.21), p = .055); the simple slope of promotive voice on status was significant for men (b = .47 (.06), p < .01) but not for women (b = -0.08 (.12), n.s.); and the indirect effect of promotive voice on leader emergence via status was significant for men (effect = -0.46; 95% CI [-0.86, -0.14]) but not for women (effect = 0.24; 95% CI [-0.43, 0.91]). The difference between the men's and women's indirect effects was significant at the 90% confidence level (difference = -0.69; 90% CI [-1.33, -0.07]) but not at the 95% level (95% CI [-1.57, .09]). Hypothesis 5 was not supported — the prohibitive voice by gender interaction was not significant, and the indirect effect of prohibitive voice via status was not significant for men or for women.
- Study 2 (n = 196) replicated the conditional process model rather than re-testing the numbered hypotheses: the omnibus test was significant (F(3, 192) = 3.30, p = .02), and planned contrasts showed significantly higher status in the promotive-man condition (M = 3.95, SD = .57) than in the promotive-woman condition (M = 3.58, SD = .75; t(97) = 2.82, p = .01), with no significant difference between the prohibitive-man (M = 3.67, SD = .69) and prohibitive-woman (M = 3.71, SD = .47; t(95) = .74, n.s.) conditions. The voice by gender interaction was significantly related to status (b = .42 (.18), t(193) = 2.32, p = .02) and status was significantly related to leader emergence (b = .47 (.06), t(194) = 7.99, p < .01), while the voice and gender main effects were not significant. The indirect effect of voice type on leader emergence via status was significant for men (effect = .13 (.07), 95% CI [.01, .27]) but not for women (effect = -0.06 (.06), 95% CI [-.19, .05]). Including riskiness of voice as a covariate produced no notable changes to the results, and the rater's own gender was not a significant factor in how targets were rated.
- Overall: promotive voice, but not prohibitive voice, was positively and indirectly related to leader emergence through status, and in both studies that benefit accrued to men and not to women; the authors conclude that women received no benefit from speaking up promotively in terms of status or leader emergence.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends voice research by shifting attention from managers' reactions to peers' social evaluations of speakers. It links voice, status, and leader emergence by showing that promotive voice can increase peer-granted status and leadership emergence, while also showing that these benefits depend on gender. It also contributes to expectation states theory by distinguishing between types of assertive behavior rather than treating speaking up as uniformly status-enhancing.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that employees may gain respect and influence by speaking up in ways that focus on ideas for moving the group toward an ideal state. It also warns that these benefits are not evenly distributed: women may speak up without receiving the same status and leadership credit as men. The authors recommend managerial practices such as documenting, recognizing, and amplifying women's voice to counter unequal status-conferral processes.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Study 1 took place in a male-dominated and distinctive military academy competition setting, even though Study 2 used a more gender-balanced context. They also note that Study 1 relied on self-ratings of voice and that the two studies used different measures of leader emergence. The paper did not directly examine the substantive content of specific voice episodes or the role of formal leaders in shaping the voice-status-emergence process.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research in settings with different gender compositions, including teams with mostly women, and for more objective measures of employee voice such as direct observation. It also calls for work on how the content of discrete voice events affects peer perceptions of respect and influence. Additional future research should examine other change-oriented behaviors, clarify leader-emergence measurement, and investigate how formal leaders affect whether voice accrues status.
