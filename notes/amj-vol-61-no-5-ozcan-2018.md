# Playing Cat and Mouse: Contests over Regulatory Categorization of Dietary Supplements in the United States

**Abstract**
Regulatory categorization can be a matter of life and death to firms, as it sets legal limitations on the production and sales of their product. In this paper we set out to uncover this critical process, for which there is only anecdotal information in extant literature, by asking how regulatory categories are determined through the strategies and interaction of firms, regulators, and other category audiences. We use extensive archival data to examine how U.S. dietary supplement makers first moved from the drug to the food category in 1976, and, when faced with resistance from the Food and Drug Administration, created an entirely new category in 1994, which then fueled their explosive market growth. Our findings show that regulatory categorization is a contest between firms and the regulator, where firms try to disrupt unfavorable regulation by overpowering the regulator through hard power imposed by other state actors, which is achieved through pressure from another category audience—consumers—who are themselves won over through soft power.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how regulatory categories are determined through the strategies and interaction of firms, regulators, and other category audiences. Empirically, it examines how U.S. dietary supplement makers moved first from the drug category to the food category in 1976 and then into a newly created dietary supplement category in 1994.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key actors / audiences: supplement makers, the FDA, Congress and other state actors, consumers, health stores, pharmaceutical companies, food corporations, public-interest groups, and category incumbents.
- Process / phases: movement between extant categories through the Proxmire Amendment of 1976, followed by creation of a new dietary supplement category through DSHEA in 1994.
- Firms' category work: dissociation from unfavorable categories and category evaluators, association with target categories, and selective representation through less controversial labels such as vitamins.
- Firms' advocacy work: lobbying legislators directly, hooking consumers through cultural meta-narratives, activating them through dramatization and urgency, and coordinating action through health stores and the value chain.
- Regulator work: FDA category work associated supplements with drug or food-additive categories, while maintenance work relied on educating, policing, deterrence, raids, product seizures, labeling restrictions, and scientific authority.
- Emergent outcome: firms used soft power over consumers to activate hard power from Congress, thereby overpowering FDA categorization decisions and changing the legal category governing the industry.

The process is a multi-actor political contest rather than a purely cognitive categorization process. Supplement makers first cognitively prepared audiences for category change by reframing what supplements were and who should evaluate them. They then mobilized consumers and legislators so that Congress could impose legal changes on the FDA. The regulator, by contrast, relied heavily on scientific expertise, education, and coercive enforcement, which made it vulnerable to firms' freedom-of-choice framing and public mobilization.

**Data & Measures**
Design and corpus: an inductive single-case study of the U.S. dietary supplement industry, tracing regulatory change from the 1940s with particular focus on the categorization of dietary supplements between 1973 and 1994, when the majority of categorization-related actions took place. Table 1 reports the data as archival documents plus interviews: 375 articles from major industry journals (Food Labeling News, Food Chemical News) covering 1977-1994; 138 newspaper articles obtained from the Factiva database with the keyword "dietary supplements" for 1988-1994 (Table 1 reports this row as 115 newspaper articles); 80 articles from academic law journals (1972-2006, with "dietary supplements" as the search keyword); 11 books written on the topic; and transcripts from six congressional hearings (350 pages on average) obtained from Casewatch. Interview material comprises 10 interviews with dietary supplement executives conducted by leading industry journals (New Hope Natural Media, Natural Products Insider); 35 relevant transcripts identified and further analyzed out of 238 FDA oral-history transcripts from FDA commissioners and regional directors covering 1968-2013; and two retrospective interviews with prominent actors in the passage of the DSHEA act, conducted as a final validity check. The data were uploaded into NVivo for further analysis.

Coding: a four-stage iterative process of moving back and forth between theory and data. Stage 1 compiled a 50-page case history, each researcher revising the data separately, and placed key events into a timetable (Table 2), identifying two phases — change between extant categories and new category creation. Stage 2 revised theory and prior evidence to compare the phases against corporate political strategy, institutional work, and categorization processes, surfacing first-order concepts (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013) such as "cultural meta-narratives," "redefining relevant attributes for categorizing," "food as a label," and "regulator bias and incompetence arguments." Stage 3 reexamined the codes to theorize, relating each first-order concept to second-order themes (mechanisms) in Appendixes A and B and Figure 1, and arriving at two aggregate dimensions for firms — advocacy work and category work — with a parallel process for the FDA's actions yielding the aggregate dimensions of category work and maintenance work. Stage 4 analyzed the actions of other key actors (Congress, health stores, pharmaceuticals) and sharpened the emergent theory by iterating between theory and data. The design is inductive and process-based; the paper makes no causal-identification claim.

**Key Findings**
- Core finding: unlike product categorization, regulatory categorization is a contest between firms and the regulator, where firms try to disrupt unfavorable regulation by overpowering the regulator through hard power imposed by other state actors in the form of new laws and rulings, and where such hard power is itself assembled through the indirect influence of another category audience — consumers — who are won over through soft power.
- Firms' work resolves into two aggregate dimensions. Category work cognitively prepares critical audiences through dissociation from the current category (redefining relevant attributes for categorizing), association with the target category (labels and category attributes), and selective representation through the least controversial category member (the vitamin label applied to the whole category). Advocacy work activates state actors directly through lobbying and indirectly through consumers, by hooking them with cultural meta-narratives of freedom of choice, activating them with rhetorical tools of dramatization and urgency, and coordinating consumer action through the product's value chain (health stores). The paper argues the sequence of these advocacy mechanisms is critical: attention must be caught before actors are motivated to act, and coordination directs the action at the right target.
- The regulator's work resolves into category work (association with the target category by redefining relevant attributes to emphasize function, i.e., that supplements' intended use was therapeutic) and maintenance work (educating; policing and deterrence through raids, product seizures, and restrictions of use). The FDA's scientific approach, its focus on educating the public rather than generating advocacy, and its policing and deterrence tactics allowed supplement makers to portray the regulator as arrogant and aggressive and themselves as victims. The paper also finds that supplement makers used goal-based categorization while the regulator used causal-based categorization relying on professional knowledge.
- The two phases differ in what they require. Movement between extant categories (the 1976 Proxmire Amendment, establishing that dietary supplements are not drugs) enables operations under less strict conditions but carries the risk of unfavorable recategorization under the same regulator. New category creation (the 1994 DSHEA) additionally requires dissociation from the category evaluator itself through a frame of incompetence and bias against innovation in the public interest, and allows firms to reset category evaluators. Labels help association with extant, well-known categories such as food, but are less helpful in new regulatory category creation because the new category's name is not yet resonant.
- Outcome of the contest: DSHEA mandated that the FDA not require premarket approval of supplements, explicitly excluded supplements from the definition of a food additive, and took away the FDA's sole authority by appointing an NIH Office of Dietary Supplements and a Commission on Dietary Supplement Labels as regulatory authorities. FDA Commissioner David Kessler called it his "greatest failure as the head of the FDA," and in the subsequent four years annual sales of dietary supplements jumped from $4 billion to $12 billion.
- On the other players: regulatory categories are neither decided solely by a regulator nor shaped by regulated firms without resistance, but are the outcome of a contest between the two involving multiple other category audiences, in which firms and regulators attempt to disrupt or maintain the regulatory category, respectively. Category incumbents may not all respond alike, as they may be split over whether to diversify into the new sectors or resist their growth, which the paper reports as an important opportunity for entrepreneurial market entrants to diffuse opposition power and shape regulatory categorization.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional work by showing how advocacy work can combine soft power and hard power to produce regulatory change, especially when less powerful firms mobilize other audiences that have leverage over state actors. It contributes to categorization research by defining category work as efforts to influence category attributes, boundaries, and membership, and by distinguishing movement between extant regulatory categories from new regulatory category creation. It also links institutional work and corporate political strategy by treating regulatory categorization as a multi-player contest among firms, regulators, legislators, consumers, value-chain actors, and incumbents.

**Practical Implication**
For firms in regulated markets, the study implies that changing regulation may require more than direct lobbying: firms may need to cognitively prepare audiences for category change and mobilize actors who can influence legislators. For regulators, the case shows the limits of relying only on scientific authority, education, and enforcement when firms can frame regulation as a threat to public values. For entrants facing powerful regulators, the paper suggests that category incumbents' divisions and value-chain access to consumers can create openings for regulatory change.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the study is set in a single industry, where the broader political and cultural climate may have contributed to the outcome. They also note that the alternative medicine movement in the 1970s and 1980s may have widened political support for the dietary supplement movement. A further boundary condition is that the setting involved individuals' health and well-being, making the public especially susceptible to heightened emotions.

**Future Research**
The authors call for studies of regulatory categorization in other markets, such as energy or broadcasting, where the issues may have less dramatic implications for society at large. They also suggest examining newer settings such as peer-to-peer platforms and e-cigarettes to understand when firms can or cannot mobilize public support for favorable categorization. Future work should further investigate hard versus soft power tactics and how power is distributed among different category audiences across regulatory settings.
