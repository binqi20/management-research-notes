# The Short-lived Benefits of Abusive Supervisory Behavior for Actors: An Investigation of Recovery and Work Engagement

**Abstract**
Although empirical evidence has accumulated showing that abusive supervision has devastating effects on subordinates’ work attitudes and outcomes, knowledge about how such behavior impacts supervisors who exhibit it is limited. Drawing upon conservation of resources theory, we develop and test a model that specifies how and when engaging in abusive supervisory behavior has immediate benefits for supervisors. Via two experiments and a multi-wave diary study across 10 consecutive workdays, we found that engaging in abusive supervisory behavior was associated with improved recovery level. Moreover, abusive supervisory behavior had a positive indirect effect on work engagement through recovery level. Interestingly, supplemental analyses suggested that these beneficial effects were short-lived because, over longer periods of time (i.e., one week and beyond), abusive supervisory behavior was negatively related to supervisors’ recovery level and engagement. The strength of these short-lived beneficial effects was also bound by personal and contextual factors. Empathic concern––a personal factor––and job demands––a contextual factor––moderated the observed effects. Specifically, supervisors with high empathic concern or low job demands experienced fewer benefits after engaging in abusive supervisory behavior. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings, and propose future research directions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether, why, and when engaging in abusive supervisory behavior has proximal benefits for supervisors themselves. It focuses on whether abusive supervisory behavior can improve supervisors' recovery level and work engagement, and whether empathic concern and job demands condition those effects.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Engaging in abusive supervisory behavior will be positively related to supervisors' own recovery level.
- H2: Abusive supervisory behavior will have a positive indirect effect on work engagement via recovery level.
- H3a: Empathic concern will moderate the relationship of engaging in abusive supervisory behavior with recovery level, such that the strength of this relationship will be negatively related to empathic concern.
- H3b: Empathic concern will moderate the positive indirect effect of engaging in abusive supervisory behavior on work engagement via recovery level, such that the strength of this indirect effect will be negatively related to empathic concern.
- H4a: Job demands will moderate the relationship of engaging in abusive supervisory behavior with recovery level, such that the strength of this relationship will be positively related to job demands.
- H4b: Job demands will moderate the positive indirect effect of engaging in abusive supervisory behavior on work engagement via recovery level, such that the strength of this indirect effect will be positively related to job demands.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Engaging in abusive supervisory behavior, measured or manipulated as supervisors' verbal abuse or daily abusive behavior toward subordinates.
- DV(s): Supervisors' recovery level and work engagement, with supplementary longer-term analyses of recovery, work engagement, and job satisfaction.
- Mediators: Recovery level mediates the relationship between abusive supervisory behavior and work engagement.
- Moderators: Empathic concern weakens the short-term abusive behavior to recovery relationship; job demands strengthen it.

The mechanism is resource-based. The authors argue that abusive supervisory behavior can conserve supervisors' resources by releasing them from the effortful inhibition of aggressive impulses and can generate resources by increasing a sense of control. This resource recovery then supports same-day work engagement. The benefits are bounded: high empathic concern creates additional stress after abuse, low job demands make resource gains less salient, and cumulative abuse becomes detrimental over longer time windows.

**Data & Measures**
The model is tested in three studies. Study 1a is a laboratory experiment with 64 undergraduate and graduate students from a large university in Northern China, randomly assigned to an abusive supervision condition (n = 33) or a control condition (n = 31); all participants were given the supervisor role in a six-round visual perception task adapted from Gino, Norton, and Ariely (2010) with three confederates acting as subordinates, one of whom was made to perform poorly, and abusive supervision was manipulated after the fifth round by instructing participants to comment on that subordinate as a "harsh" versus a "neutral" supervisor in WeChat voice messages. Recovery level was measured with Sonnentag's (2003) three-item scale (α = .74) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = "Strongly disagree," 5 = "Strongly agree"), with the sample item "I feel relaxed"; the manipulation check used Mitchell and Ambrose's (2007) five-item version of Tepper's (2000) abusive supervision scale (α = .85), supplemented by two independent research assistants blind to the hypotheses and conditions who coded the voice messages with the same scale (α = .93 and .91; r = .93, p < .001). Study 1b is an online experiment with 100 supervisors employed in the United States and recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk, randomly assigned to the abusive supervision condition (n = 51) or the control condition (n = 49); the task was the same as in Study 1a except that participants typed text messages to subordinates who did not in fact exist, and the same recovery measure (α = .89) and manipulation check (α = .95) were used, again with two independent coders (α = .90 and .92; r = .92, p < .001). Study 2 is a multilevel, multiday diary field study of supervisors recruited through alumni networks of several large universities in China: 89 supervisors with at least one subordinate were invited and 72 responded with valid data (response rate 80.9%), producing 565 complete daily observations out of a possible 720 (response rate 78.5%); the 72 supervisors did not differ significantly on gender, age, education, and tenure from the 17 who did not provide complete data. Data were collected over a period of four weeks in three phases: an initial one-time survey measuring demographic information and job demands; two surveys per day for 10 consecutive workdays, a morning survey (T1) sent at 11 AM that assessed abusive supervisory behavior, recovery level, negative affect, and positive affect, and an afternoon survey (T2) sent at 4:30 PM that assessed recovery level and work engagement, with an average lapsed time of 6.1 hours between them; and a final one-time survey one week after the daily surveys that measured empathic concern. Daily abusive supervisory behavior (T1) was self-reported by supervisors on the same Mitchell and Ambrose (2007) five-item abusive supervision scale used in Studies 1a and 1b, with items such as "I ridiculed my subordinates" answered for behavior since arriving at work that day (1 = "Never," 5 = "Always"; average α across days .93); recovery level (T2) used Sonnentag's (2003) three-item scale (average α .96); work engagement (T2) used a three-item scale adapted by Lanaj, Johnson, and Barnes (2014) from the short-form Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (average α .94); empathic concern used a seven-item subscale of Davis' (1980) shortened version of the Empathy Scale (α = .92); and job demands used eight items developed by Van Veldhoven and Meijman (1994) (α = .81). Morning recovery level (T1), measured with the same three-item scale (average α .94), was included as a control so that the model predicts change in recovery level. Study 1a's post-experiment questionnaire and all Study 2 surveys were administered in Mandarin Chinese, with scales translated using Brislin's (1980) translation–back translation procedure. Study 2 hypotheses were tested with random coefficient modeling in HLM, with the within-person variables (abusive supervisory behavior, recovery level, work engagement) modeled at Level 1 and group-mean centered and the between-person moderators (empathic concern, job demands) modeled at Level 2 and grand-mean centered; RMediation (Tofighi & MacKinnon, 2011) was used to test the indirect effect, and Edwards and Lambert's (2007) moderated path analysis was used to calculate conditional indirect effects at high (+1 SD) and low (–1 SD) levels of the moderators. A multilevel confirmatory factor analysis of the four within-person and two between-person variables showed that the six-factor model had acceptable fit (χ²(155) = 483.54, p < .001; SRMR = .07, RMSEA = .06, CFI = .90) and fit better than alternative five-factor models. The two experiments use random assignment and were, in the authors' words, designed to establish the causal effect of engaging in abusive supervisory behavior on recovery level; Study 2 is an observational within-person diary design in which the authors state that the morning-to-afternoon time lag allowed them to examine change in recovery level and to establish temporal separation and causal precedence between the predictor and outcome variables. Supplementary long-term analyses measured participants' average abusive supervisory behavior during the past three months on the initial one-time survey with the same five-item scale (α = .86), averaged daily abusive behavior across the first five workdays, and used HLM and OLS regression controlling for supervisor gender, age, education, managerial tenure, empathic concern, and job demands; job satisfaction was measured with a single item ("How satisfied are you with your job in general?") on the second one-time survey, and supervisors' power distance orientation was also controlled in the daily field study.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses were supported. Hypothesis 1 was supported in each of the three studies. In Study 1a, ANOVA results showed that participants in the abusive supervision condition experienced significantly higher recovery (M = 3.53, SD = .69) than those in the control condition (M = 3.11, SD = .61), F(1, 62) = 6.52, p < .05, η² = .10. In Study 1b, supervisors in the abusive supervision condition again reported higher recovery (M = 3.69, SD = .99) than those in the control condition (M = 3.14, SD = 1.14), F(1, 98) = 6.56, p < .05, η² = .06. Manipulation checks were successful in both experiments, on both self-reports and blind coder ratings. In Study 2, null models indicated that 32% of the variance in abusive supervisory behavior resided at the within-person level, with within-person proportions of 50% and 47% for recovery level at T1 and T2 respectively and 49% for work engagement. Daily abusive supervisory behavior was positively related to daily recovery level (b = .27, p < .05), and remained so in the model that added the moderators (b = .21, p < .05). Hypothesis 2 was supported: daily recovery level was significantly related to daily work engagement (b = .44, p < .001), and the indirect effect of abusive supervisory behavior on work engagement via recovery level was significant (estimate = .12, 95% CI [.02, .22]); the authors report comparable coefficients and significance levels when work engagement from the next day was used instead. Hypothesis 3a was supported: the abusive supervisory behavior × empathic concern interaction predicting recovery level was significant (b = –.65, p < .01), and the relationship of abusive supervision with recovery level was significant and positive when empathic concern was low (b = .56, p < .001) but nonsignificant when empathic concern was high (b = –.14, n.s.). Hypothesis 3b was supported: the conditional indirect effect was significant when empathic concern was low (b = .20, p < .01) but not when it was high (b = –.06, n.s.), and the difference between the two indirect effects was significant (Δb = –.27, p < .05). Hypothesis 4a was supported: the abusive supervisory behavior × job demands interaction predicting recovery level was significant (b = .72, p < .01), and the relationship of abusive behavior with recovery level was significant and positive when job demands were high (b = .58, p < .001) but not when job demands were low (b = –.16, n.s.). Hypothesis 4b was supported: the indirect effect was significant and positive when job demands were high (b = .25, p < .01) but not when they were low (b = –.07, n.s.), and the difference between the indirect effects was significant (Δb = .32, p < .05). In the models predicting work engagement, the path from abusive supervisory behavior to work engagement was 0.10 (t = 1.02) in the total effects model and –0.00 (t = –0.06) once recovery level (T2) was included; empathic concern was positively related to recovery level (b = .31, p < .01) and to work engagement (b = .37, p < .001), job demands was not significantly related to either outcome (b = –.14 and –.06, n.s.), the abusive supervisory behavior × empathic concern interaction also predicted work engagement (b = –.79, p < .001; b = –.50, p < .05 with recovery level (T2) in the model), and the abusive supervisory behavior × job demands interaction predicting work engagement was marginal (b = .41, p < .10) and nonsignificant with recovery level included (b = .12, n.s.). Results were comparable when gender, age, education, and managerial tenure were controlled. The supplementary analyses report the opposite sign over longer windows: average abusive supervisory behavior over the past three months, reported on the initial one-time survey, was negatively related to afternoon recovery level (b = –.31, p < .05) and had a significant negative indirect effect on afternoon work engagement via afternoon recovery level (estimate = –.14, 95% CI [–.26, –.02]), with predictor–outcome time lags ranging from seven to 19 days; the same measure was negatively related to Day 10 recovery (b = –.57, p < .01) and to Day 10 work engagement via Day 10 recovery (estimate = –.34, 95% CI [–.66, –.09]). Average daily abusive supervisory behavior during the first workweek had a negative impact on Day 10 recovery level (b = –.41, p < .05) and a significant negative indirect effect on Day 10 work engagement via Day 10 recovery level (estimate = –.26, 95% CI [–.53, –.05]). A footnote reports significant negative indirect effects on job satisfaction via Day 10 recovery level for average abusive supervisory behavior assessed on the initial one-time survey (estimate = –.25, 95% CI [–.53, –.05]) and for average daily abusive supervisory behavior across the first five workdays (estimate = –.14, 95% CI [–.33, –.01]). The authors also report that supervisor power distance orientation did not moderate the relationship of abusive supervisory behavior with recovery level and that controlling for it did not alter the results.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends abusive supervision research by shifting the focal actor from subordinates as recipients to supervisors as perpetrators, showing that abusive behavior can have short-term benefits for actors even while remaining harmful overall. It contributes to conservation of resources theory by treating a "bad" behavior as a possible short-term resource-conservation and resource-generation event, while showing that the same behavior becomes resource-consuming over longer time periods. It also identifies empathic concern and job demands as personal and contextual boundary conditions on actor-centric abusive supervision effects.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that organizations should not treat abusive supervision only as irrational or purely destructive from the supervisor's perspective; short-term recovery and engagement benefits may reinforce the behavior. The authors point to less harmful recovery alternatives, including workday breaks, coworker communication, cultivating empathic concern, and reducing or managing supervisors' job demands. Interventions should target the resource pressures and perceived control deficits that make abusive behavior temporarily rewarding.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Study 2 relies on supervisor self-reports of daily abusive behavior, recovery, and engagement, creating potential common method variance despite temporal separation, controls, and supporting experiments. The tested outcomes are limited mainly to recovery and engagement, and the moderators focus on empathic concern and job demands rather than other organizational or subordinate factors. The same-day design captures proximal effects, while longer-term consequences are explored through supplementary analyses rather than a dedicated long-horizon longitudinal design.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on broader actor-based outcomes of abusive supervision, including negative affect, burnout, productivity, promotion potential, and social sanctions from subordinates. They suggest examining additional moderators such as organizational context and subordinate status, and testing whether supervisors are affected differently depending on whom they abuse. They also recommend longitudinal studies with different time frames and further investigation of cultural contingencies in the consequences of abusive supervisory behavior for actors.
