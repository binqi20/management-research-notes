# Short Seller Influence on Firm Growth: A Threat Rigidity Perspective

**Abstract**
Whereas most investors seek to maximize their investment, there are some who profit from downward stock price movement. Engaging in such behavior is called “short selling,” and it has increased considerably among publicly traded North American firms in recent years. This study builds on threat rigidity theory to develop arguments about the behavioral consequences of short sellers. We theorize that, when firms have a high level of short interest, managers adopt a defensive stance by halting new growth initiatives and limiting the range of growth modes in which they participate. We also suggest that managers’ ability to process information and exercise control moderate these main effects. Specifically, we argue that managers will be less reactionary when firms have high absorptive capacity, possess high board centrality, maintain sufficient financial slack, and are well regarded by the media. Results largely support our theory, as we show that short sellers can have a profoundly deleterious effect on firm growth prospects.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how high levels of short interest influence firm growth decisions. Specifically, it examines whether short sellers, treated as an indirect external threat, lead managers to undertake fewer growth actions and to use a less diverse mix of growth modes.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. When a firm's level of short interest rises, managers will tend to engage in fewer growth actions.
- Hypothesis 1b. When a firm's level of short interest rises, managers will tend to engage in a less diverse mix of growth actions.
- Hypothesis 2. Absorptive capacity mitigates the negative relationship between short interest and the (a) number, and (b) diversity of growth actions. The relationships are weaker when the firm has a high level of absorptive capacity.
- Hypothesis 3. Board centrality mitigates the negative relationship between short interest and the (a) number, and (b) diversity of growth actions. The relationships are weaker when the firm is more central in the interlocking directorate.
- Hypothesis 4. Financial slack mitigates the negative relationship between short interest and the (a) number, and (b) diversity of growth actions. The relationships are weaker when the firm has a high level of financial slack.
- Hypothesis 5. News sentiment mitigates the negative relationship between short interest and the (a) number, and (b) diversity of growth actions. The relationships are weaker when news sentiment is positive.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Level of short interest, measured as short shares as a percentage of total shareholdings; supplementary tests also use a high-short-interest dummy.
- DV(s): Number of growth actions and diversity of growth actions, with supplementary outcomes for consolidation actions, growth ratio, growth complexity, and broader competitive actions.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Absorptive capacity, board centrality, financial slack, and news sentiment.

The mechanism is threat rigidity. The authors argue that short sellers threaten managers by publicly betting against and denigrating the firm, even though they do not directly control it. Under high short interest, managers restrict information processing and narrow their span of control, which makes risky and cognitively demanding growth actions less attractive. Absorptive capacity, board centrality, financial slack, and positive media sentiment are expected to dampen this response because they improve information processing, control, resources, or external support.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: level of short interest from the Compustat Short Interest database; firm growth actions from the Capital IQ Key Development database; financial data from Compustat; stock price data from CRSP; institutional ownership from Thomson Reuter 13(F); analyst data from IBES; news sentiment from RavenPack; CEO and board data from BoardEx. The longitudinal dataset spans 2000 to 2014 and the sample begins with all firms listed in the Standard & Poor's (S&P) 1500 Index.
- Growth actions: announcements listed and described in Capital IQ Key Development, covering new product introductions, mergers and acquisitions, strategic alliances, and actions in the business expansion category. The authors report a total of 126,273 growth actions for the sample: 72,725 new product introductions, 11,353 M&As, 12,945 strategic alliances, and 29,250 business expansions, which averages over five actions per firm per year. Because Capital IQ groups all product-related announcements into one category, the authors read through each announcement to retain only new product announcements.
- Dependent variable 1, number of growth actions: measured at the end of the year, counting the total number of actions a firm announced throughout the year. The natural logarithm of the count is taken to address skewness.
- Dependent variable 2, diversity of growth actions: one minus a Herfindahl index of action types, capturing how many different types of growth actions a firm uses and how (un)evenly it uses them, so that higher values indicate a more complex mix of growth actions and lower values a simpler mix.
- Independent variable, level of short interest: measured as a percentage of the firm's total shareholdings. The monthly ratio of short shares to total shares outstanding is calculated and then averaged to obtain the firm's annual level of short interest. An alternative Short interest dummy classifies a firm-year as high or low short interest at a 5% cutoff, the threshold commonly used in governance research to identify blockholders.
- Moderators: absorptive capacity is captured by R&D intensity, the ratio of R&D expenditure to annual sales revenues; board centrality counts each board's directors' total connections in BoardEx (through directorship interlock, employment, education, and social activities), normalized by the number of directors on the board; financial slack is the additive inverse of the Kaplan and Zingales (1997) KZ index of financial constraints, which encompasses a combination of (1) cash flow-to-capital, (2) Tobin's Q, (3) debt-to-capital, (4) dividends-to-capital, and (5) cash holdings-to-capital; news sentiment is the average tone of all news about a firm appearing throughout a year, scored by RavenPack's Expert Consensus Methodology and scaled so that the mean is 0 (neutral), positive numbers reflect positive sentiment, and negative numbers reflect negative sentiment.
- Controls: firm size (natural log of total assets), ROE, annual stock returns, stock return volatility (the standard deviation of monthly stock returns in a firm year), total diversification (an entropy measure of business diversification), total institutional ownership, analyst coverage, percentage of CEO-appointed directors, CEO duality, CEO tenure, CEO gender, CEO equity ownership (the CEO's shares over total shares outstanding), the number of consolidation actions a firm has undertaken in a year, and year fixed effects.
- Estimation: Hausman tests reject random-effects regressions, so firm fixed-effects models are used to control for bias from time-invariant firm heterogeneity. Firm fixed-effects OLS regressions are the primary test for both dependent variables; the authors prefer OLS over fixed-effects Poisson for the hypothesis tests because Poisson models are nonlinear and the coefficients of their interaction terms may not accurately reflect the actual interaction effects, which matters given four moderators. In all regressions the dependent variable is measured at t+1 and all other variables at t to help mitigate simultaneity bias, standard errors are clustered by firm, and firm and year fixed effects are included. Table 3's main-effect models report 23,509 observations, with 22,924 for the fixed-effects Poisson model and 23,474 for the instrumental-variable models. Table 4's moderator models report 23,509 observations for absorptive capacity, 22,292 for board centrality, 21,255 for financial slack, 21,174 for news sentiment, and 18,396 for the saturated models.
- Identification and robustness: firm fixed-effects instrumental-variable regressions instrument the percentage of short interest with the average percentage of short interest for all firms in the focal firm's two-digit SIC industry excluding the focal firm, and with exchange traded fund (ETF) ownership; the reported Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic is 298 against the Stock and Yogo (2005) critical value of 19.93, with a Hansen J statistic of 0.123 (p = 0.73) for the number of growth actions and 0.686 (p = 0.41) for diversity. A Regulation SHO natural experiment supplies a difference-in-difference test over a 2000-2010 sample period with a 2000-2003 baseline, a 2005-2007 during-treatment period, and a 2008-2010 post-treatment period; 2004 is excluded because the SEC did not identify the pilot stocks until halfway through that year, and the models report 13,372 observations. Arellano-Bond dynamic GMM regressions include the number (diversity) of growth actions at t and t-1 as predictors and set short interest as a predetermined variable.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1a supported: the coefficient estimate of short interest on the number of growth actions is negative and statistically significant (b = -0.734, p < .01, Model 1 of Table 3). In terms of economic magnitude, a 1 SD increase in short interest (.05) is associated with a 4% decrease in the number of growth actions.
- Hypothesis 1b supported: the coefficient estimate of short interest on the diversity of growth actions is negative and statistically significant (b = -0.169, p < .01, Model 2 of Table 3). When short interest increases from 0 to its mean + 1 SD, the diversity of growth actions decreases around 10%.
- The main effects hold under alternative specifications: the fixed-effects Poisson estimate of short interest is negative and significant (b = -1.290, p < .01), and the Short interest dummy is negative and significant for both the number (b = -0.061, p < .01) and the diversity (b = -0.014, p < .01) of growth actions.
- Instrumental-variable second-stage estimates support both main hypotheses: short interest is negative and statistically significant for the number of growth actions (b = -2.422, p < .01) and for the diversity of growth actions (b = -0.684, p < .01). In the first stage, average industry short interest (b = 0.460, p < .01) and ETF ownership (b = 0.501, p < .01) are positive and statistically significant.
- Regulation SHO natural experiment: Treatment x During is negative and significant for the number of growth actions (b = -0.062, p < .10, two-tailed test), so treatment firms' growth actions decreased by 6% relative to control firms from the pre-treatment to the during-treatment period. For the diversity of growth actions Treatment x During is negative but statistically not significant (b = -0.008, n.s.).
- Hypothesis 2a supported, Hypothesis 2b not supported: the interaction between short interest and absorptive capacity is positive and significant for the number of growth actions (b = 6.226, p < .01) — increasing short interest by 1 SD results in a 6% decrease in growth actions when absorptive capacity is low (mean - 1 SD) and a 2% decrease when absorptive capacity is high (mean + 1 SD) — but negative and statistically insignificant for the diversity of growth actions (b = -0.297, n.s.).
- Hypotheses 3a and 3b supported: the interaction between short interest and board centrality is positive and significant for the number of growth actions (b = 0.735, p < .01) and for the diversity of growth actions (b = 0.140, p < .05). Increasing short interest by 1 SD results in a 5% decrease in the number of growth actions when board centrality is low and a 1% decrease when it is high. The authors note that in both plots the two lines cross, so their description of the attenuating effect of board centrality holds for levels of short interest above the mean but is less applicable at low levels of short interest, where the least central firms engage in the most numerous and most diverse growth actions.
- Hypotheses 4a and 4b supported: the interaction between short interest and financial slack is positive and significant for the number of growth actions (b = 0.034, p < .05) and for the diversity of growth actions (b = 0.006, p < .10, two-tailed test). Increasing short interest from 0 to its mean + 1 SD results in a 12% decrease in the diversity of growth actions when financial slack is low and a 5% decrease when financial slack is high.
- Hypothesis 5a supported, Hypothesis 5b not supported: the interaction between short interest and news sentiment is positive and significant for the number of growth actions (b = 0.220, p < .05), but positive and statistically not significant for the diversity of growth actions.
- In the saturated models carrying all interaction terms, many interaction terms keep the significance levels they showed when investigated alone but some become insignificant. The authors also report that although the hypothesized moderators change the nature of the main relationship, they do not cause the main results to disappear.
- Supplementary analyses: short interest is positively and significantly related to the number of consolidation actions (b = 0.515, p < .01) and to the diversity of consolidation actions (b = 0.076), and negatively and significantly related to the ratio of growth actions to total growth and consolidation actions (b = -0.373, p < .01), which the authors read as managers favoring active consolidation over merely slowing growth. Short interest is also negatively and significantly related to a composite measure of growth-action repertoire complexity (b = -1.809, p < .01) and, in unreported results, to the number (b = -0.730, p < .01) and the diversity (b = -1.269, p < .01) of a broader set of seven competitive action types. Unreported threshold tests indicate the findings become most significant when short interest rises above roughly 5%. Arellano-Bond dynamic GMM estimates remain negative and significant for the number (b = -0.530, p < .01) and the diversity (b = -0.153, p < .05) of growth actions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends threat rigidity theory by applying it to an indirect threat: short sellers who cannot directly impose organizational harm but can unsettle managers through capital-market pressure and public criticism. It also contributes to investor-influence research by studying short sellers as a hostile investor type rather than as monitors or activists seeking firm improvement. Finally, it adds to managerial risk-taking research by showing that capital-market signals about a firm's prospects can shape growth decisions.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that managers should recognize how short-seller pressure may bias them toward defensive, internally focused decisions. Awareness of threat-rigidity tendencies may help managers develop more reasoned responses to short sellers rather than automatically reducing growth activity. The findings also matter for policy makers because short selling may have organizational consequences beyond market-price effects.

**Limitations**
The authors state that they do not directly measure the psychological mechanism of threat rigidity and instead infer it through moderator analyses. They also examine managerial actions rather than managers' public statements or counter-campaigns. In addition, the sample focuses on S&P 1500 companies, so the effects may differ for smaller and younger firms.

**Future Research**
Future research could directly measure the psychological and cognitive processes triggered by short-interest pressure, for example through survey instruments. It could also study how managers publicly respond to short sellers and whether those responses affect strategic decision-making. The authors further suggest expanding samples to smaller and younger firms and parsing competitive actions and competitor countermoves in more detail.
