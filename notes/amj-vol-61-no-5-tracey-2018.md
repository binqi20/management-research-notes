# Fish out of Water: Translation, Legitimation, and New Venture Creation

**Abstract**
We draw on institutional theory to study a common type of new venture creation that has been neglected in the literature: the translation of an existing organizational form from a different—and misaligned—institutional context. To do so, we conducted an indepth case study of H-Farm, an Italian venture that was founded as a business incubator, a type of organization that first emerged in Silicon Valley and other U.S. technology regions. Our study illuminates the specific configuration of legitimacy pressures inherent in this type of entrepreneurship, and theorizes the strategies that entrepreneurs can enact to address them: local authentication work, category authentication work, and dual optimal distinctiveness work. We also show that the legitimacy pressures experienced by entrepreneurs may vary significantly as ventures mature, and challenge the notion of a specific “legitimacy threshold” that new ventures are required to reach. Finally, our model conceptualizes translation as an iterative, dynamic, and ongoing accomplishment rather than a “one off” activity with clear beginning and end points.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how entrepreneurs create and legitimate a new venture by translating an existing organizational form from a different and misaligned institutional context. Empirically, it examines how H-Farm translated the business incubator/accelerator form from Silicon Valley and other U.S. technology regions into Veneto, Italy.

- Triggering conditions: perceived shifts in the target organizational form and reflection on the limits of H-Farm's current model.
- Emergent outcomes: a model of entrepreneurship through misaligned translation in which legitimacy pressures vary over time rather than being resolved at a single threshold.
- Boundary conditions: stakeholder diversity and the rapid evolution of the organizational form may shape whether the process is generative or creates muddled meaning.

The process begins with improvising, where entrepreneurs loosely emulate a poorly aligned target form and work to make the venture locally understandable. It then shifts to converging, where entrepreneurs seek category-level acceptance from stakeholders associated with the target form while continuing local authentication. In the optimizing phase, growing local and international competition makes the venture pursue dual optimal distinctiveness: what once authenticated the venture locally helps it stand out at the category level, while category-level authentication helps distinguish it locally.

**Data & Measures**
The study is an in-depth, inductive single-case study of H-Farm built on three data sources summarized in Table 3. The main source is 53 semi-structured interviews conducted in three rounds, between 15 minutes and two hours long, face to face or by Skype or phone: H-Farm's founders (4), partners (7), top and middle managers including the CFO, communication manager, associate, and heads of division (9), and startuppers (7), which the authors describe as the main source for understanding how H-Farm was created and changed and how members constructed its goals, practices, and legitimacy pressures; 13 interviews with local industrialist associations, research centers, a policy maker, VC managers, an incubator's founder, and startups, used to triangulate facts about the local context; and 13 interviews with international VCs (seed and round A), Italian VCs, angel investors, the industry association AIFI, and accelerators, used to triangulate H-Farm's perception of legitimacy pressures. The archive comprises 1,316 items, about 5,708 pages excluding non-printed material: 250 media articles mentioning H-Farm (2005–December 2017); one TV event; 1,010 H-Farm materials including the 2015 IPO prospectus, analysts' and H-Farm's 2016 reports, PowerPoint presentations on acceleration programs, material distributed at Demo Night (2017), YouTube videos, Donadon's tweets from when he joined Twitter in 2009 to 2017, and website captures (2009–2017); the founder's book; 10 items on the Italian incubator and VC market; 20 on entrepreneurship in Veneto since 2005; 22 on business incubators; and 2 publications on the global and European VC market. Six field-note observations were written up from site visits to H-Farm in 2012, 2013, and 2017 and from a 2017 meeting in Milan between Italian investors and accelerators. Analysis ran in six stages: building a database and timeline of H-Farm's history from its founding in 2005 to 2017 and integrating it with data on the regional and national context and on the evolution of the incubator model; a first-order analysis using thorough open coding of the interviews and field notes, with sentences or paragraphs as coding units labeled with in vivo codes or descriptive sentences and compared using the constant comparative method; a historical case analysis that looked for "critical junctures", identified three of them, and provisionally bracketed the timeframe into three discrete periods used as embedded units of analysis; axial coding that established links among the first-order concepts in each period and yielded seven second-order themes, which the authors treated as working hypotheses tested against the available data; collapsing those themes into three aggregate dimensions; and drawing multiple representations of the emerging relationships among constructs to build the theoretical model. The resulting data structure is reported in Figure 2 following Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013); the design is interpretive and grounded-theory-based, with constructs emerging inductively rather than being measured or causally identified.

**Key Findings**
The case yields a model of new venture creation through misaligned translation (Figure 3) organized around three translation phases. In Phase 1, improvising (2005–2009), the founders had only a generalized idea of what U.S. incubation entailed and there was no "standard" model to emulate; perceived pressures for category-level legitimacy were minimal while local-level legitimacy was the core challenge, and H-Farm responded with local-level authentication work, defined as constructing resonance between the venture and its local context. This comprised practice work — improvised practices with little in common with U.S. incubators, including initially owning equity stakes of 90% of incubated startups, an intended tenure spanning several years with no clearly proscribed incubation period, and regular networking events for startuppers and outsiders — and meaning work, including claiming a humanistic approach to innovation, using symbols from the local peasant tradition, telling stories of local innovators past and present, and explaining what H-Farm was to government, local banks, and entrepreneurs. In Phase 2, converging (2010–2014), the belief that the requisite entrepreneurs and investment could not be obtained from Veneto, combined with a perceived shift in the target form toward Y Combinator, Techstars, and the Global Accelerator Network, made category-level legitimacy the central pressure; H-Farm engaged in category-level authentication work through systematic benchmarking against established accelerators, a shift from majority to 25–30% stakes, the launch of short timed programs (the Seed Program and H-Camp), a move to a mentor-like structure, splitting into "Accelerator" and "H-Farm Venture" in 2010, applying for GAN membership in 2012 and being admitted in 2013, and hosting GAN meetings in 2012 and 2013. Local-level authentication work continued in parallel during this phase rather than being displaced. In Phase 3, optimizing (2015–2017), the reference point shifted again to the European Accelerator Network, and the rapid expansion of private incubators in Italy — from a handful at the beginning of Phase 2 to 40 by the beginning of Phase 3 — and worldwide meant the venture needed not only acceptance but also differentiation at both levels. The authors report this as a "double optimal distinctiveness hurdle" that has not been identified or examined in the literature, and find that H-Farm addressed it through "dual optimal distinctiveness work": an inversion in which the actions previously underpinning local-level authentication were used to legitimate and distinguish the venture at the category level, while the actions underpinning category-level authentication were used at the local level. Two findings cut across the phases. First, the analysis does not support the idea of a clear legitimacy threshold or set of thresholds; the authors move from a binary conception to a gradated one in which ventures become more or less legitimate as the expectations and relative salience of different stakeholders evolve over time. Second, translation here involved a "moving target" transposed by a "moving object", making it iterative, dynamic, and ongoing rather than a "one off" activity; contrary to Ansari and colleagues' (2010) argument that poorly fitting practices are implemented with high fidelity by early adopters, H-Farm was a first mover in Italian business incubation yet adopted a model that differed in key respects from the form on which it was based, which the authors explain by entrepreneurs' need to adapt to local circumstances outweighing the perceived risks of experimentation. The authors note that dual optimal distinctiveness work was enacted successfully in this case but is challenging to execute, and that if such a strategy backfires the risk is "muddled meaning" in the eyes of local- and category-level stakeholders.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional entrepreneurship research by identifying the distinct legitimacy pressures faced by ventures created through misaligned translation: local-level legitimacy, category-level legitimacy, and later a dual optimal-distinctiveness challenge. It also theorizes legitimation as gradated and temporal rather than as crossing a single legitimacy threshold. Finally, it extends translation theory by showing that entrepreneurial translation involves a moving target being transposed by a moving object, making translation iterative, dynamic, and ongoing rather than a one-off act of faithful emulation.

**Practical Implication**
Entrepreneurs translating an organizational form into a misaligned context should not treat faithful imitation as sufficient. The H-Farm case suggests that they need to build local resonance first, then seek category-level acceptance when growth requires resources outside the local context, and later manage similarity and differentiation at both levels. The paper also cautions that if dual optimal distinctiveness work backfires, entrepreneurs may create muddled meaning for local and category-level stakeholders.

**Limitations**
The authors note that their case involved local and category-level stakeholders who were mainly entrepreneurs and investors, and that these pressures were sufficiently homogeneous for H-Farm to harness them productively. They caution that translating forms with more diverse stakeholders, such as social enterprises moving between the global north and global south, may create more incompatible legitimacy pressures. They also indicate that the dynamics of dual optimal distinctiveness may differ when the target organizational form is more static and has crisper boundaries.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether the differences between local-level and category-level legitimacy pressures remain or erode as a translated organizational form matures. It could also study misaligned translation in settings with more diverse stakeholder expectations, where local and category-level pressures may become competing tensions. The conclusion further invites work that builds on, extends, and refines the model for other digital ventures and other forms of new venture creation through misaligned translation.
