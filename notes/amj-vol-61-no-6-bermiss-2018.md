# Ideological Misfit? Political Affiliation and Employee Departure in the Private-equity Industry

**Abstract**
Though organizations are increasingly active participants in the political realm, little research has investigated how an organization’s heightened focus on political ideology impacts employees. We address this gap by exploring how an individual’s political ideological misfit with an organization’s prevailing ideology impacts departure. By tracking the movements of over 40,000 investment professionals in the U.S. private equity industry over 10 years, we investigate the consequences of the ideological misfit that arises when individuals’ political ideologies diverge substantially from the prevailing ideology of their firms. We hypothesize that ideological misfit increases the likelihood of departure, though the effects vary with liberal or conservative ideological orientation. Polynomial regression analyses reveal that the fit relationship between individual and organizational political ideology deviates from the idealized congruence relationship that underlies prevailing theory. Specifically, we find that departure is more likely for employees experiencing conservative misfit (a personal ideology more conservative than the firm’s) versus liberal misfit (a personal ideology more liberal than the firm’s). Conservatives are more likely to depart than liberals regardless of fit, and departing misfits tend to join new organizations that are closer to their own ideology.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how ideological misfit between an employee's political ideology and the prevailing political ideology of the employing organization affects departure. It also asks whether the departure effect differs for conservative misfit versus liberal misfit.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The more an individual's political ideology (i.e., liberal or conservative) differs from the prevailing ideology within his or her organization, the greater the likelihood of his or her departure.
Hypothesis 2. Departure likelihood associated with ideological misfit will be higher for conservative individuals than for liberal individuals.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Individual-level liberal ideology, firm-level liberal ideology, their squared terms, and the interaction between individual- and firm-level ideology.
- DV(s): Employee departure from a private equity firm.
- Mediators: The paper theorizes hostile climate, alienation, detachment, reduced organizational identification, and lower social cohesion as mechanisms, but it does not estimate them as formal mediators in the main archival models.
- Moderators: Direction of ideological misfit, distinguishing conservative misfit from liberal misfit; additional analyses examine destination-firm ideology and employee satisfaction robustness.

The central mechanism is that political ideology functions as a person–organization fit dimension. When employees' political ideology diverges from the organization's prevailing ideology, the paper theorizes that they experience discomfort, hostile climate, detachment, and weaker identification, making departure more likely. The evidence is asymmetric: conservative misfits are more likely to depart, whereas liberal misfits are less likely to depart in some conditions, which the authors interpret through conservative sensitivity to misfit and through possible misfit strategies such as missionary action or discreet dissent.

**Data & Measures**
Primary data are archival. Employment data on all investment professionals in the industry between 1999 and 2008 come from Galante's Venture Capital and Private Equity Directory, published annually by Asset Alternative, a Dow Jones company; the data set captures 40,733 individuals employed at 4,641 private equity firms over 10 years, and constructing each individual's career history from 1999 to 2008 yielded 143,455 individual-year observations. Table 1 reports n = 143,454 with the individual-year as the unit of analysis, and Table 2 reports 143,454 observations and 40,732 individuals. Political contribution data come from the U.S. Federal Election Commission, which records all individual contributions over $200 to candidates, campaigns, political action committees, and parties. The archival work is augmented by 25 semistructured interviews with private equity investment professionals identified through a snowball technique and employed at multiple hierarchical levels (associate, principal, vice president, and partner).
- DV: employee departure from a private equity firm, coded from an individual's final listing at a particular firm in the directory; a single-year gap is not coded as a departure if the individual was employed at the same firm before and after the gap.
- IV: individual-level liberal ideology, set equal to the dollar amount of an individual's contributions to the Democratic Party (including contributions to both candidates and political action committees) in a given year, divided by the dollar amount of that individual's total political contributions. Individuals who have not met the $200 contributions threshold in a given year are modeled as making an explicit choice not to contribute rather than as missing values, by adding 0.10 to all numerators and 0.20 to all denominators; this sets a minimum of approximately 0, a maximum of 1, and a median of 0.50. Individuals with no recorded donations score 0.50 and are ideologically neutral, values below 0.50 indicate a conservative ideology, and values above 0.50 indicate a liberal ideology.
- IV: firm-level liberal ideology, equal to the mean value of the individual-level liberal ideology of a firm's employees in a specific year. Ideological misfit is measured through these two variables, which enter the model as each variable, its square, and the individual-by-firm interaction.
- Controls: total capital under management, number of firm employees, top 50 in USNWR ranking, female, top manager, middle manager, associate, firm political donations, and presidential election year (2000, 2004, and 2008).

Estimation uses the polynomial regression procedure of Edwards and Parry (1993), Z = b0 + b1(X) + b2(Y) + b3X2 + b4(XY) + b5Y2, where X is the individual-level and Y the firm-level political ideology score. The polynomial model was compared with a Euclidean distance model and explained a larger amount of variance (change in -2 log likelihood = 86.46, p < 0.01). Hypotheses are tested with a random-effects discrete-time logistic regression model that includes fixed effects for each state represented in the sample, and the log-odds coefficients are transformed into predicted probabilities for the response surface. The surface analysis examines the Y = X ("perfect fit") and Y = (1-X) ("perfect misfit") lines and the two principal axes, with jackknife standard errors for the second principal axis components computed over 1,000 iterations. The design is archival and associational; no causal identification strategy is named.

Three supplemental analyses accompany the main models. A simulated sample randomly reassigns observed political donations across all individuals within each observed year, recalculates all misfit scores, and repeats the procedure for 500 iterations. A destination analysis uses a subsample of individuals who departed one observed firm and relocated to another observed firm, pared down to firms that were politically active (total firm donations above the sample mean) and excluding firms under 20 employees; the resulting subsample consisted of 2,872 total departures, with the destination firm identified for 1,795 departures (63% of the total departures), of which 715 (40%) were included in the sample. Its dependent variable, firm-level liberal ideology difference, is the numerical difference between the firm-level liberal ideology of the old firm subtracted by the firm-level liberal ideology of the new firm, so a positive value means a move to a more conservative firm and a negative value a move to a more liberal firm; it is estimated by ordinary least squares with the primary analysis controls and year fixed effects (Table 3 reports 713 observations and R2 = 0.212). A third-party job satisfaction check uses the mean Glassdoor company rating as a proxy for employee satisfaction, drawn from 152,000 total reviews that equated to firm-level job satisfaction scores for 1,152 firms, capturing 12,700 employees and 45,134 individual-year observations.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is not supported. The surface graph in Figure 1 does not conform to the trough shape the hypothesis predicts, and the jackknife test returns p20 = 0 and p21 = -0.33 (p < .05), so the second principal axis does not run along the Y = X line and departure is not minimized by "perfect" ideological fit. The authors conclude that although ideological misfit affects the likelihood of departure, it does not do so in a symmetrical manner.
The effect depends on the direction of misfit. Compared with "perfect" fit situations, departure is more likely for individuals who experience conservative misfit and less likely for those who experience liberal misfit: conservative misfits scoring one standard deviation below the mean are 15% more likely to depart a firm than individuals at the mean, whereas liberal misfits scoring one standard deviation above the mean are 25% less likely to depart than individuals at the mean. Only conservative misfit aligned with the argument for Hypothesis 1; the effect of liberal misfit runs counter to the hypothesis, because increases in liberal misfit lead to a lower likelihood of departure. The trend holds at both conservative- and liberal-leaning firms, with the exception, reported in a footnote, that at the extreme end of the conservative-misfit spectrum (individual scores below 0.2 at firms with scores above 0.8) increased conservative misfit is associated with a decreased likelihood of departure.
Hypothesis 2 is supported. Along the Y = (1-X) "perfect misfit" line the slope is convex with a curvature of ax2 = -5.23 (p < .05), significantly larger than the plot for perfect fit, and the authors read this sharp negative curvature as showing that conservative misfits are significantly more likely to depart their firms than liberal misfits.
There is also a strong main effect of political ideology on departure regardless of fit: liberal individuals are significantly less likely to depart their firms than conservatives. Along the Y = X "perfect fit" line the slope is convex (ax2 = -0.76, p < .05), implying that the likelihood of departure decreases as individual and firm ideologies become more liberal-leaning, and the predicted probability of departure for individuals with a strong conservative ideology is approximately twice as large as that for individuals with a strong liberal ideology.
In the Table 2 random-effects discrete-time logistic model, the polynomial ideology terms are individual-level liberal ideology squared = -3.00 (p < 0.01) and individual-level by firm-level liberal ideology = 2.23 (p < 0.1), while individual-level liberal ideology (-0.75), firm-level liberal ideology (-0.12), and firm-level liberal ideology squared (-0.39) carry no significance stars. Adding the five polynomial ideology variables significantly improves the fit of the model (change in -2 log likelihood = 182.45, p < 0.01). The authors note that the polynomial method places less emphasis on the regression coefficients themselves than on the variance explained and the surface pattern of predicted values.
In the destination analysis (Table 3), the coefficient for departing individual with liberal misfit is negative and significant (b = -0.04, p < 0.01) and the coefficient for departing individual with conservative misfit is positive and significant (b = 0.03, p < 0.01), so individuals who have experienced liberal (conservative) misfit and departed tend to join firms that are more liberal (conservative) than their previous employer.
Robustness checks are consistent with the main results. The simulation found no significant evidence of self-selection, with the simulated samples producing a mean (0.49) and standard deviation (0.05) similar to the original sample. Rerunning the regression models on the Glassdoor subsample with the employee satisfaction variable produced no material change in the support of the hypotheses, and running the models on the subsample of professionals who departed and subsequently joined another private equity firm yielded the same results as those presented in the paper.
The interview evidence was generally consistent with the quantitative finding that the direction of misfit was particularly decisive: both conservative and liberal misfits reported being marginalized and offered similar accounts of inhospitable organizational climates, discomfort, and feelings of detachment, but conservatives reported greater sensitivity (and aversion) to misfit and expressed more rigid, negative attitudes toward their organizations. The interviews also produced two interpretations of the lower departure likelihood of some misfits that the archival models do not test: misfits as a kind of workplace missionary who values the chance to persuade others, and a decoupling of misfit voice and action in which misfits stay quiet at work but register dissent through monetary contributions. The decoupling interpretation was borne out in the qualitative data more frequently than the missionary scenario.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to employee mobility research by theorizing departure as a sociocultural process shaped by political ideology rather than only as an economic or vacancy-driven process. It extends person–organization fit theory by introducing political ideology as a fit dimension and showing that misfit effects need not be symmetrical or uniformly detrimental. It also broadens research on political ideology in organizations beyond senior executives by studying rank-and-file professionals.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that managers should treat political ideology as a potentially consequential but difficult-to-observe dimension of fit. It cautions against assuming that ideological homogeneity is always desirable, because some ideological misfits may become sources of productive dissent or learning if the organization provides enough security for minority viewpoints. Managers in small, human-capital-intensive firms should pay attention to whether political discourse creates silence, alienation, or avoidable departures.

**Limitations**
The authors note that political contributions are an imperfect measure of political ideology and that the archival data cannot fully distinguish voluntary from involuntary departure. They also acknowledge that the study does not account for all turnover antecedents, including compensation, leadership, group cohesion, stress, and other demographic predictors. The empirical context is U.S. private equity, so the pattern may not generalize to other industries or to other salient ideologies.

**Future Research**
The authors call for future research integrating ideology with other antecedents of turnover, including job dissatisfaction, job search, and quit intentions. They also suggest studying whether misfit works similarly for other salient workplace ideologies, such as religious or philosophical ideologies. Further work could test the causal mechanisms behind the conservative-versus-liberal asymmetry and examine whether the findings replicate in other industry contexts.
