# Becoming Who We Serve: A Model of Multi-Layered Employee–Customer Identification

**Abstract**
Though employee identification with customers has received little explicit attention in organizational scholarship, and has only recently emerged as an area of focus in marketing, research suggests that customers should play a central role in the identity dynamics of employees. What makes employee–customer identification different from other forms of identification (e.g., employee–organization and customer–organization identification) is that although the organization may be the conduit for identification to occur, it may not be the focus of identification. Indeed, in the organization we explore here, organizational practices lead employees to identify with customers, but to do so in such a way that employees become—at least in part—more like “who they served” than vice versa. Moreover, the bonds we find between employees and customers are multilayered, incorporating nonwork identities, as well as identities at different levels of inclusiveness (e.g., collective and role). This paper explores and models the development and consequences of multilayered employee–customer identification, argues for its theoretical and practical implications, and suggests avenues for future research.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what the nature of employee–customer identification is, what role organizations may have in cultivating such bonds, and what perceived implications employee–customer identification has for employees and the employing organization. It specifically examines an alternative to the assumption that ECID is anchored in customers becoming "like us" through shared organizational values.

**Mechanism Process**
- Organizational practices: Bridging practices helped COP-CU employees understand, appreciate, and become involved with police-officer customers; valuing practices clarified and reinforced why serving police officers mattered.
- Identity process: These practices supported collective-level and role-level self-expansion, producing multilayered employee–customer identification.
- Collective-level expressions: Employees came to see themselves and police officers as part of the same family and as part of the police social collective.
- Role-level expressions: Employees came to see their work as familial emotional support and as sharing elements of policing, especially protecting and serving.
- Perceived implications: Occupational self-expansion contributed to work meaningfulness, familial self-expansion contributed to perceived service effectiveness, and these were linked to organizational identification, growth, and profitability.
- Comparison case: POST-CU showed little evidence of bridging and valuing practices, self-expansion, meaningful work, or distinctive service effectiveness, weakening alternative explanations based only on credit-union form, small size, defined membership, service mission, or personal ties to the customer occupation.

The process is not presented as a statistical causal test; the authors use an inductive comparative design to build a model. COP-CU's bridging practices blurred boundaries between bankers and police officers, while valuing practices promoted social agency by motivating employees to form strong reciprocal connections with police officers. Those connections enabled employees to become more like the customers they served rather than only seeing customers as sharing organizational values. The resulting M-LECID operated through multiple identity layers and was associated with perceived task significance, organizational instrumentality, and identification-based trust.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from two credit unions located in the same large U.S. city: the focal Police Officer's Credit Union ("COP-CU," a pseudonym) and the comparison Postal Worker's Credit Union ("POST-CU"), the latter sought as a theoretical replication by sampling two credit unions matched on organizational form, size, services, and service to a clearly defined population. Both are relatively small, with fewer than 50 employees; POST-CU is smaller than COP-CU. At the focal organization the authors used two primary data sources, semi-structured interviews and analysis of company documents, and triangulated them to improve the validity of the theory developed. They interviewed 41 COP-CU employees from various branch locations: frontline employees such as tellers and loan officers (34.1%), managers (41.5%), executives (12.2%), and "back office" employees such as accounting and legal services (12.2%). Informants were 63% female with an average organizational tenure of 5.3 years (range 1–15 years), and the sample represented approximately four-fifths of credit union employees. Sampling began as stratified purposeful sampling on functional position and tenure, then shifted to include the CEO and other executive officers once it became clear the dynamics with police officers began when the new CEO and his leadership team arrived. Interviews were conducted face-to-face or over the phone, lasted one hour on average, and were recorded and transcribed verbatim; they were collected in three rounds (nine in round 1, 10 in round 2, and 22 in round 3), with the protocol slightly modified between rounds, and collection ceased after the third round at theoretical saturation. The document corpus is five years' worth of COP-CU quarterly newsletters, approximately four pages each. After the theoretical model was developed, the authors conducted member-check interviews with five COP-CU customers, each lasting approximately 30 minutes, to verify employee interpretations of their relationships with customers. For the comparison organization they interviewed 12 POST-CU employees (100% of employees) from branch locations and the executive offices, asking a similar set of questions plus additional questions about themes that emerged from the initial COP-CU analysis (for example, whether POST-CU informants felt their work was similar to, or facilitative of, the work of postal workers, or whether they treated postal workers like family, as indicators of multilayered ECID); because POST-CU published no newsletter, only interview data were analyzed, collected in two rounds of six employees each. Both interview and document data were analyzed using grounded theory in an iterative pattern linking data to the emerging theoretical framework, in three main steps: open coding of descriptive codes tied directly to the words of informants and newsletter text (e.g., "using police language"); axial coding, in which open codes were bundled into more abstract groupings (e.g., "service tailoring," "purpose building"); and a third step exploring the relationships among the more abstracted codes to develop a model, after which the data were re-examined for fit with the theoretical understanding. The authors note the steps did not unfold sequentially but moved back and forth between data and theory. The first author conducted all interviews, collected the documents, and initially read and coded each transcript and document, creating a summary sheet and a hierarchical listing of all codes and their precise meanings; the second author debriefed the interview sessions, read the code lists and contact summary sheets, and served as a second source of analysis. The design is interpretive and comparative rather than causally identified: the authors state that they cannot make causal statements with their data.

**Key Findings**
At COP-CU, informants identified strongly with their customer group, experiencing a form of "oneness" with those they served, but that sense of oneness did not appear to revolve exclusively around the employees' organization; it revolved around perceptions of family and the customers' occupation, so identification was less about customers becoming "like us" and more about employees becoming "like them." The basis for employee–customer identification in this context was two interdependent forms of self-expansion, collective-level and role-level, which the authors label multilayered employee–customer identification (M-LECID). Two types of organizational practices facilitated M-LECID: bridging practices (involvement in police officer affairs and service tailoring), which helped employees better understand, appreciate, and be involved with their service group; and valuing practices (purpose-building and incentivizing), which were geared internally, often preceded actual contact, and were used as motivation to spur the bridging practices. Document data corroborated the valuing language: over a five-year period, 94% of the "Message from the CEO" newsletter columns ended in a reference to "service," and also service in relation to "duty" and "discipline" as well as "honor" and "pride." Collective-level self-expansion was expressed in two ways, being part of the same "family" and being "part of the cops," while role-level self-expansion involved providing emotional support to customers (familial) and employees feeling their work took on some of the same features as policing, protecting and serving (occupational); occupational and familial forms of collective-level and role-level self-expansion were analytically distinct and created the foundation for employees to experience M-LECID. Member checks with the five COP-CU police-officer customers echoed the employee accounts: all five confirmed the credit union was viewed as heavily involved in police officer events and associations, the officers associated the same policing values with the credit union, they described credit union employees as playing a caring, almost familial role, and they supported the employees' view that the banking role had features similar to policing and helped officers do their jobs better by removing a source of stress. The perceived implications differed by expression: collective- and role-level occupational expressions of self-expansion in particular contributed to a heightened sense of work meaningfulness, which was in turn associated with a stronger sense of identification with the COP-CU organization, whereas the familial expressions enhanced the organization's perceived service effectiveness, which informants attributed to the credit union's growth and profitability. Archival data on growth matched what informants reported: over the past several years the organization had nearly tripled in size and number of branches and increased its assets by a factor of five, growth many employees attributed to the leadership of the current CEO, who had a six-year tenure at the time of the interviews. The comparison organization produced counter-examples rather than parallel findings: at POST-CU the authors saw no evidence of self-expansion of any type, as employees did not see themselves and postal workers as part of the same family, did not see themselves as "one of them," and described no self-expansion in terms of the roles they enacted; there was also a lack of evidence of bridging or valuing practices, work was described as "just a job" or in terms of "efficiency," and employees did not describe going "above and beyond" for customers, so work meaningfulness and service effectiveness were not clearly evident. These counter-examples provide some evidence that the identity dynamics at COP-CU are not simply a function of (a) small size, (b) referring to customers as "members," (c) having a well-defined customer group, (d) a service-oriented mission, or (e) personal connection to the customer group; on the last point, more members of the POST-CU than the COP-CU had some sort of personal connection with the occupation of their service group (80% vs. 12%), statistics the authors describe as admittedly based on small sample sizes.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to workplace identification research by challenging the assumption that employee identification is necessarily anchored in the organization. It shows that employee–customer identification can involve employees taking on customer-linked identity content, so part of "who we are" as organizational employees becomes anchored in customers. It also extends identification and self-expansion theory by showing that identification can be multilayered, with collective and role-level self-expansion occurring in the same employee–customer relationship.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations that rely on repeated service relationships may foster productive ECID by creating bridging practices that help employees understand and interact with the service group, and valuing practices that make the mission of serving that group clear and rewarded. The paper also warns that closeness to customers may create risks: employees may become too customer-anchored, organizational flexibility may be constrained, and practices that encourage closeness may frustrate employees or customers if the organization does not enable or reward the connection.

**Limitations**
The study is inductive, so it supports analytical and naturalistic generalizability rather than population-level statistical generalizability. The comparison design does not directly replicate two credit unions serving the same customer population, so police-officer customer characteristics may still have influenced the identity dynamics observed at COP-CU. The authors also note that they did not collect longitudinal data, leaving the temporal ordering between organizational practices, self-expansion, and identification open to further testing.

**Future Research**
Future research should test multilayered employee–customer identification in other service contexts, including organizations with different customer groups, more heterogeneous customer bases, or other stakeholder targets. Studies could examine when customer categorization and organizational practices foster self-expansion, whether multilayered identifications differ from unidimensional identifications in resilience or behavioral consequences, and how employees respond when the customer group becomes threatened or morally contested. Longitudinal designs could also examine how bridging and valuing practices contribute to self-expansion and M-LECID over time.
