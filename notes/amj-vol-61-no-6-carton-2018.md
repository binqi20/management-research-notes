# How Can Leaders Overcome the Blurry Vision Bias? Identifying an Antidote to the Paradox of Vision Communication

**Abstract**
Evidence suggests that organizational leaders can inspire employees by communicating a vision of the future with image-based rhetoric—words and phrases that are readily envisioned in the mind's eye (e.g., "our vision is to make moviegoers laugh"). Yet research has demonstrated that most leaders do not craft visions with image-based rhetoric, instead favoring abstract language that cannot easily be visualized. We integrate theory on leadership and dual cognitive processing to argue that this problem is exacerbated when leaders focus on word selection when crafting visions because they overemphasize the meaning-based cognitive system (in which they consider the abstract meaning of words) and underemphasize the experience-based cognitive system (in which they can generate vivid mental images of what the future could look like). We introduce a novel tactic to help leaders activate the experience-based system and, in turn, generate and communicate more impactful visions. We also investigate boundary conditions. We test our predictions with three experiments featuring three distinct samples, including one with senior corporate executives and one in which members of the British government crafted visions on the day Britain announced it would exit the European Union ("Brexit").

**Research Question**
The paper asks why leaders continue to communicate abstract, hard-to-visualize visions even though image-based rhetoric is more inspiring, and whether temporal projection can help them overcome this "blurry vision bias." It also asks when this intervention works best, focusing on whether efficient versus analytical cognitive style changes the effect of mental imagery on image-laden vision communication.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Leaders who mentally project themselves to the distant future (i.e., temporal projection) will use more image-laden rhetoric in vision communication compared to leaders who emphasize word selection.
- Hypothesis 2. Individual cognitive style (efficient versus abstract thinking) and prescription type (temporal projection versus language-based prescriptions) will interact, such that temporal projection will boost the use of image-laden rhetoric in vision communication more for efficient thinkers than for analytical thinkers.
- Hypothesis 3. The role of mental imagery as a mediator between prescription type (temporal projection versus language-based) and image-laden rhetoric will be moderated by cognitive style, such that the mediated relationship will hold more for efficient than for analytical thinkers.
- The paper states that it is outside the scope of the paper to construct hypotheses about the impact of temporal projection on the other three dimensions of vision quality (specificity, achievability, and values), although it assesses its effect on all dimensions of vision quality.
- Studies 1 and 2 tested all three hypotheses. In Study 3 the only part of the experiment administered for all cases was the experimental manipulation, so the mediator and moderator items were not collected there; the authors describe this as mitigated by their testing of the mediator and moderator in Studies 1 and 2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Prescription type, comparing temporal projection or mental time travel against language-based prescriptions and control conditions.
- DV(s): Image-based rhetoric in vision communication, broader vision quality, and in Study 3 employee inspiration.
- Mediators: Mental imagery generated by temporal projection; Study 3 also tests image-laden rhetoric as a mediator between temporal projection and employee inspiration.
- Moderators: Cognitive style, contrasting efficient thinkers with analytical thinkers.

The mechanism is grounded in dual cognitive processing. A direct focus on word selection keeps leaders in the meaning-based system, where they process abstract concepts and therefore continue to craft abstract visions. Temporal projection instead asks leaders to imagine a future scenario as if they were observing it firsthand; this activates the experience-based system, creates mental imagery, and makes image-based rhetoric the natural language for communicating what they have imagined. The effect is stronger for efficient thinkers because they are less likely than analytical thinkers to override imagery with abstract semantic processing.

**Data & Measures**
All three studies were randomized experiments in which participants wrote vision statements; Studies 1 and 2 were between-subjects, and Study 3 compared two conditions between subjects while also using a vision each executive crafted before the manipulation as a within-subjects control. Study 1 (the Brexit experiment) was launched on June 24, 2016 with 166 government officials from the United Kingdom identified via an online database (prolific.ac); average age 37, average 11.3 years of experience, 66% female, and roles including council members from provincial governments, heads of department, executive officers, education officers, national parks managers, heads of communication systems, and administrators in the National Health Service. It used six conditions: a no-instruction control; four language-based prescriptions (imagery/vividness, specificity, achievability, values) whose wording reproduced the most frequently invoked words from the authors' review of 180 sources on vision construction; and a mental time travel (temporal projection) condition that told participants "Imagine you enter a time machine and emerge in the future just after your company has achieved its vision." Nine participants who failed an attention check were retained because the results remained substantively the same. Study 2 recruited 700 full-time employees through a Mechanical Turk filter across seven conditions, the six from Study 1 plus a seventh giving all four language-based prescriptions at once; 44 participants who did not complete the task and 4 who failed an attention check were excluded as specified in the preregistration, leaving a final sample of 652 (average age 35, 48% women; participants came from various countries including China, Honduras, India, the Netherlands, Philippines, Singapore, Ukraine, and the United States), each crafting a vision for either financial services or health services. Study 3 sampled 123 senior corporate executives at major firms, recruited over a five-month period (CEOs, COOs, CTOs, vice presidents, heads of department, partners, regional managers, and firm owners; average age 37, 42% female; average company size 10,610 employees; average of 28 direct reports), comparing the time machine condition against a condition that explicitly instructed participants to include imagery; 100 coworkers (average age 38, 43% female) rated the visions' inspirational power. Measures: the primary dependent variable, image-based rhetoric, used the Coh-Metrix dictionary, an online database of 4,825 words coded by adult raters from 100 (weak imagery) to 700 (strong imagery), scored as the average amount of imagery per word in each vision. The mediator, mental imagery, was measured after the manipulations but before participants crafted visions, using items adapted from Babin and Burns (1998) (1 = disagree to 7 = agree; alpha = .90 in Study 1, .94 in Study 2). The moderator, cognitive style (analytical versus efficient thinking), used the scale created by Epstein et al. (1996) (1 = disagree to 5 = agree; alpha = .66 in Study 1, .83 in Study 2). The three other vision-quality dimensions were specificity (the Coh-Metrix dimension hypernymy), achievability (two coders blind to the study hypotheses, scale from 100 to 700; alpha = .65 in Studies 1 and 2, .70 in Study 3), and values (a count of how often participants invoked the nine values practitioners most frequently use). Employee inspiration in Study 3 used the inspiration scale from Thrash and Elliot (2003): the full four-item scale in 65 cases (alpha = .928) and, because of participants' time constraints, the first item alone in the remaining cases. Controls in Studies 1 and 2 were intrinsic motivation (a four-item scale from Grant, 2008), time on task, cognitive fluency (four items), level in the organizational hierarchy, and number of direct reports as a measure of power. Studies 1 and 2 were analyzed with analysis of covariance and OLS regression, with the conditional indirect effect tested via bootstrapped confidence intervals in Hayes' (2013) PROCESS at the 25th and 75th percentiles of the moderator, truncated regression to account for a censored mediator, and tobit regression in STATA as a robustness check; Study 3 used linear mixed models accounting for random effects of each participant. Studies 2 and 3 were preregistered at aspredicted.org. A supplementary alternate measure of the dependent variable had two raters blind to the study hypotheses independently code a randomly selected subsample of 50 participants' visions at the clause level and at the level of the entire vision statement.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported in Studies 1 and 2, and Study 3 replicated the effect with senior executives. In Study 1 the mental time travel condition (M = 406.902, SD = 28.398) boosted image-laden rhetoric in vision communication more than did any other condition (M = 395.383, SD = 24.259), F(1,159) = 5.663, p = .019, partial eta-squared = .034, with R-squared = .062; with separate fixed effects, mental time travel significantly increased imagery compared to all four language-based conditions, F(1,158) = 5.19, p = .024, partial eta-squared = .032, and compared to the no-instruction control, F(1,158) = 3.91, p = .049, partial eta-squared = .024, and it was the only one of the five experimental interventions that induced significantly more imagery than the control. Study 1 found no differences on specificity, achievability, or values (p > .1 for all). In Study 2, participants in the mental time travel condition (M = 409.269, SE = 33.25) communicated visions with significantly more image-laden rhetoric than those in the other conditions (M = 391.611, SD = 26.80), F(1,645) = 33.219, p = .00000001, partial eta-squared = .049, with R-squared = .051; the time machine condition also beat the no-instruction control, F(1,643) = 22.775, p = .000002, partial eta-squared = .034, the four language-based conditions taken independently, F(1,643) = 28.999, p = .0000001, partial eta-squared = .043, and the condition receiving all four language-based prescriptions at once, F(1,643) = 20.628, p = .000007, partial eta-squared = .031, and it was the only one of the six experimental interventions that induced significantly more imagery than the control. Unlike Study 1, Study 2 also found that leaders in the mental time travel condition crafted more achievable visions (p = .0000000000001), with no differences for specificity or values. In Study 3, with the mental time travel condition as the reference category, executives who employed mental time travel communicated visions with more imagery than visions created by executives who were explicitly told to include imagery (b = -19.34, t = -3.66, p = .0003) and than visions created in the within-subjects control condition (b = -21.08, t = -4.65, p = .00001); the effect of mental time travel versus the other conditions combined was also significant (b = 20.51, t = 4.80, p = .000003). Study 3 further found that temporal projection caused leaders to craft visions with fewer values (p = .041), which the authors note is associated with greater vision quality, and marginally increased achievability (p = .08).

Hypotheses 2 and 3 were tested in Studies 1 and 2 only, and both were supported in both studies. For Hypothesis 2, Study 1 found a significant interaction between prescription type and cognitive style, F(1,157) = 12.45, p = .0005, with a significant simple slope for efficient thinkers (b = 16.72, SE = 5.24, p = .001) but not for analytical thinkers (p = .42); Study 2 found a significant interaction, F(1,643) = 4.981, p = .026, with efficient thinkers' increase in image-laden rhetoric in the time machine condition relative to the language-based conditions (b = 23.13, SE = 3.94, p = .00000001) greater than that of analytical thinkers (b = 9.83, SE = 4.71, p = .037). For Hypothesis 3, the indirect effect of mental time travel on imagery in vision communication via the activation of mental imagery excluded zero for efficient thinkers but not for analytical thinkers in Study 1 (5.60, 95% CI: 1.60, 12.88 versus 5.36, 95% CI: -.17, 12.35) and in Study 2 (1.83, 95% CI: .16, 4.16 versus 1.14, 95% CI: -.35, 2.88); in both studies a tobit robustness check correcting for censoring of the mediator left the results substantively the same.

On downstream and boundary questions, Study 2 found that span of control did not significantly moderate the effect of temporal projection on image-based rhetoric (p = .90), and temporal projection significantly increased imagery relative to the other conditions for leaders with 10 or more (n = 95, p = .004), 20 or more (n = 41, p = .048), 30 or more (n = 22, p = .005), 40 or more (n = 15, p = .035), and 50 or more (n = 11, p = .045) direct reports. Also in Study 2, a health care employee rated the inspirational power of visions created by leaders of the 25 largest units among participants who crafted visions for health care services, and inspiration was predicted by leaders' use of imagery in vision communication (r = .513, p = .012). In Study 3, the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval of the indirect effect (b = .10) running from leaders' use of mental time travel through image-laden rhetoric to employees' level of inspiration excluded 0 [95% CI: .01, .29], and continued to exclude zero when the three other aspects of vision quality were included as covariates; when all four elements of vision quality were tested simultaneously as mediators, imagery was the strongest mediator and the 95% confidence interval of the overall indirect effect (b = .14) excluded 0 [95% CI: .01, .36]. The supplementary human-coded measure showed that mental time travel boosted the overall image quality of leaders' visions for the first rater at both the clause level, F(1,43) = 4.717, p = .036, and the statement level, F(1,43) = 5.209, p = .028, and for the second rater at both the clause level, F(1,43) = 9.420, p = .004, and the statement level, F(1,43) = 7.323, p = .010. A supplementary experiment using the same design as Study 3, with daytime and executive MBA students (n = 121) crafting visions for a team they oversaw, found via linear mixed models that those in the mental time travel condition communicated visions with more imagery (b = 10.03, t = 2.49, p = .01).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to leadership and vision communication theory by explaining the descriptive-prescriptive gap in which leaders are advised to choose better words but still produce blurry, abstract visions. It integrates leadership, linguistics, and dual cognitive processing to specify why temporal projection improves vision rhetoric, how mental imagery mediates the effect, and when cognitive style constrains it. It also redirects theory from wordsmithing toward the cognitive system leaders activate before choosing words.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that leaders should not rely only on advice to use vivid words. A more useful intervention is to mentally project themselves to a future moment when the organization's vision has been realized, imagine what people would see and experience, and then translate that scene into words. The findings also imply that analytically oriented leaders may need additional support or pairing with more intuitive visionary communicators when crafting inspiring visions.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that the moderated mediation evidence should be interpreted carefully because the mediator and moderator were nonnormally distributed and the conditional indirect effects were driven partly by a very strong independent-variable-to-mediator relationship. They also note that the word "company" in the mental time travel manipulation may have been less appropriate for some Brexit-study participants who worked in agencies. A further limitation is that some Brexit-study participants did not occupy high-ranking roles, even though Studies 2 and 3 included leaders with larger spans of control and senior executives.

**Future Research**
The authors suggest examining whether temporal projection generalizes to team leaders, including evidence from a supplementary MBA-team experiment. They also call for research on delivery, cadence, follower characteristics, and whether leaders can sustain high-quality vision communication after temporal projection. Future work could also study how leaders refine visions after converting mental imagery into words.
