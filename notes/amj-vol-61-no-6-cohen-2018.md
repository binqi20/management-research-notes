# Managing Technological Transitions by Building Bridges

**Abstract**
While much research has demonstrated that radical technological transitions challenge incumbents, surprisingly little empirical work has examined which factors drive variation in their ability to invent in the new domain. Through a longitudinal study of photography firms transitioning from analog to digital technologies, we examine a key source of heterogeneity: whether and how incumbents integrate knowledge related to both old and new technologies to bridge generations. Specifically, we explore how the use of three types of intergenerational knowledge bridges—(1) inventor bridges, (2) technology bridges, and (3) hybrid product bridges—influences inventive performance in the new generation. Consistent with theories of inertia, we find that, on average, inventor and technology bridges are associated with lower performance in the new generation. However, the strength of a firm’s inventive performance in the old generation positively moderates this effect. When performance in the old technology is strong, both technology and hybrid product bridges are associated with higher inventive performance in the new generation. This finding suggests that, when combined with a strong R&D program, old-technology knowledge forms a foundation that incumbents can leverage to learn new technologies as they navigate transitions.

**Research Question**
Which factors explain variation in incumbents' inventive performance as they transition from an old technological generation to a new one? The paper asks whether and how firms' use of intergenerational knowledge bridges between analog and digital photography affects digital inventive performance, and whether old-technology inventive performance changes the value of those bridges.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater the presence of inventor bridges, the lower a firm's inventive performance in the new generation.
- H2a: The greater a firm's use of technology bridges, the lower the firm's inventive performance in the new generation.
- H2b: The greater a firm's use of technology bridges, the higher the firm's inventive performance in the new generation.
- H3a: The greater a firm's use of hybrid product bridges, the lower the firm's inventive performance in the new generation.
- H3b: The greater a firm's use of hybrid product bridges, the higher the firm's inventive performance in the new generation.
- H4: A firm's inventive performance in the old technology will positively moderate the relationship between using bridges and inventive performance in the new generation of technology.
- The authors present H2a/H2b and H3a/H3b as competing hypotheses, because theory offers opposing predictions about technology bridges and hybrid product bridges.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): inventor bridges, technology bridges, hybrid product bridges, and their interactions with analog inventive performance.
- DV(s): digital inventive performance, measured as forward citations to a firm's digital imaging patent portfolio by application year.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: analog inventive performance, interpreted as the strength of the firm's old-technology R&D capability.
- Controls: percent analog patents, digital exploration, number of digital patents, firm size, digital camera models, new digital inventors, firm fixed effects, and year fixed effects.

The paper theorizes intergenerational knowledge bridges as recombination across old and new technological generations. Inventor bridges can carry old capabilities, routines, and cognitive frames into the new domain, lowering inventive performance on average. Technology and hybrid product bridges can also reinforce old knowledge, but when a firm has strong analog inventive performance, old-technology knowledge becomes a usable foundation for learning digital technology rather than only an inertial constraint.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses a hand-collected longitudinal data set of firm, product, and patent data for research-active photography firms. General patent data come from the NBER Patent Data Project and inventor-level data from the Patent Network Dataverse; data on digital and Advanced Photography System (APS) camera introductions were hand-collected from trade publications, research reports, company website archives, photography industry websites, and press coverage, with shipment dates cross-checked against two or more sources; firm-level sales data come from Compustat. The authors examined the patent portfolio of each of the 25 photography firms that produced analog consumer-oriented point-and-shoot or high-end SLR cameras and treated a firm as research active if it held at least two patents; 14 firms had an active research presence in analog technology, and all of them developed digital technology and filed digital photography patents. The patent observation period begins with patents applied for in 1974 and ends in 2010; after excluding patents not related to photography, the final sample was 84,861 patents held by the focal firms between the years 1974 and 2004, together with 32,585 individual inventors identified using the lower bound of the Patent Network Dataverse disambiguation algorithm. Table 1 reports per-firm counts totaling 20,973 analog photography patents, 38,959 digital photography patents, 183 APS cameras, and 574 new digital cameras. The regressions in Table 3 use 293 observations across 14 firms. The dependent variable, digital inventive performance, is constructed by summing the number of citations made to a firm's digital imaging patent portfolio for each application year; total citations for each patent were adjusted using the NBER truncation factor based on grant year, year fixed effects were included, and the analysis was stopped at the end of 2004 so that forward citations could accumulate for an additional five years. Inventor bridges is a count, for each year, of how many inventors currently at each firm have patented in both analog and digital classes, with each individual labeled a bridge inventor two years before their first bridging patent and assumed to have left the firm two years after their last patent application. Technology bridges is the percentage of each digital patent's citations that were made to analog patents, averaged as the average percentage of analog citations for each firm's digital patents applied for each year. Hybrid product bridges is the sum of the number of new APS cameras shipped by each firm in each year. The moderator, analog inventive performance, divides the sum of citations made to a firm's analog photography patents applied for each year by the total number of analog photography patents applied for that year, using the same NBER truncation adjustment. Controls are percent analog patents (analog photography patents in a given year divided by total photography patents in the same year), digital exploration (the average percentage of cited patent class-subclasses that the firm had not cited previously), number of digital patents, firm size (the firm's prior-year total revenues), the number of digital cameras introduced by the firm in the prior year, and the number of new digital inventors who joined the firm each year (smoothed over the prior three years); number of digital patents, firm size, number of digital cameras, and number of new digital inventors are logged, and firm and year fixed effects are included. Estimation uses conditional fixed effects Poisson quasi-maximum likelihood with Huber-White robust standard errors. The design is associational: the authors state that they lacked suitable instruments to address endogeneity, relied on control variables and conditional fixed effects with robust standard errors, and that their results should be interpreted as indicating correlation and not necessarily causation.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: in Model 2 the coefficient for inventor bridges is negative and highly significant (b = -0.000685, p < .001), and the authors report that increasing the number of bridging inventors by one standard deviation would reduce forward citations by 11%. The authors report some support for Hypothesis 2a: the coefficient for technology bridges in Model 3 is negative and marginally significant (b = -1.171, p < .10). The coefficient for hybrid product bridges is negative in Model 4 but not significant (b = -0.00894), leaving Hypotheses 3a and 3b unresolved. On Hypothesis 4, the interaction between inventor bridges and analog inventive performance in Model 5 was insignificant, though positive (b = 0.000177), while the interactions between analog inventive performance and technology bridges (Model 6, b = 3.702, p < .01) and between analog inventive performance and hybrid product bridges (Model 7, b = 0.0358, p < .05) were positive and significant and provide support for Hypothesis 4. Figures 3 and 4 show that when analog inventive performance is high the effect of additional technology and hybrid product bridges becomes positive and is associated with higher digital inventive performance; the authors read this as Hypotheses 2a and 3a holding when firms have low analog inventive performance and Hypotheses 2b and 3b being supported when analog inventive performance is high. Model 8 includes all variables and obtained consistent results. Among the controls, digital exploration was positive and significant (p < .05), the number of digital patents was positive and highly significant, firm size was negative and significant, percent analog patents was negative but insignificant, and the number of digital cameras introduced and the number of new digital inventors were positive in most models but not significant. In robustness analysis using the mean scaling estimator, the main effects of all three bridges were negative and became highly significant (p < .001) and all three interaction effects were positive and highly significant, which the authors read as lending stronger support for Hypothesis 4. When the analysis was repeated with the number of digital cameras introduced as the dependent variable, the coefficient of inventor bridges remained negative and significant (p < .05), the coefficient of technology bridges lost significance, the coefficient of hybrid product bridges remained insignificant, and the interaction between hybrid product bridges and analog inventive performance was significant but became negative. A t-test comparing the average number of patents per year for bridging and non-bridging inventors could not reject the null that they were the same, and post-hoc tests of mediation failed, indicating that inventor bridges have a direct relationship to inventive performance.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends research on technological transitions by shifting attention from incumbent-versus-entrant comparisons to heterogeneity among incumbents' inventive performance in the new technology. It qualifies inertia-based accounts by showing that old-technology knowledge can be harmful on average but useful when paired with strong old-technology R&D capability. It also links absorptive capacity, recombination, and real options reasoning to explain why technology and hybrid product bridges can function as temporary stepping-stones during transitions.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that firms should not assume all old-technology knowledge is either obsolete or valuable during a transition. Redeploying old-technology inventors into new-technology work may hurt inventive performance, while technology bridges and hybrid products are more likely to help firms with strong existing R&D capability. Managers designing transition units should therefore consider both staffing choices and whether the organization has the capability to identify and leverage relevant old knowledge.

**Limitations**
The authors note that patent data do not directly measure inventors' beliefs or behaviors. The study is also limited to one technological domain, digital photography, in which the new technology ultimately overcame the old. The results should be interpreted as correlational rather than necessarily causal.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research in settings with multiple competing new technologies or where the new technology does not fully displace the old. They also call for work on when bridges are useful, when firms should stop bridging and disengage from old technology, how firms redeploy old expertise into new markets, and how organization design for new-technology development affects inventive performance.
