# Social Valuation Across Multiple Audiences: The Interplay of Ability and Identity Judgments

**Abstract**
How is an evaluating audience influenced by previous evaluations made by another audience? This question is critical to individuals and organizations reaching out to multiple audiences for key resources. While extant work has suggested evaluators are influenced by previous evaluations made by their peers, we develop theory about how evaluators' assessment of a candidate is shaped by previous evaluations made by an external (nonpeer) audience. We argue that the latter represent exogenous indices that affect evaluators in two opposing ways: they positively influence peer valuation by pointing to candidates' unobservable abilities, yet, since they are conferred by an external audience, they are also indicative of candidates' deviation from an expected peer identity. The combination of the two opposite effects suggests an inverted U-shaped relationship between exogenous indices and peer valuation. Further, this effect is moderated by the identity proximity between audiences, and the availability of previous peer evaluations (endogenous indices). We test and find support for our arguments using unique data on the peer valuation of 9,502 academic scientists applying for research grants at a research university. Our work contributes to the understanding of valuation and socially endogenous inferences, and has implications for the management of organizations in multi-audience environments.

**Research Question**
How is the peer evaluation of a candidate influenced by earlier evaluations made by external, nonpeer audiences? The paper studies this question in academic science, where scientists seek grants from peer evaluators while also receiving industry contracts from an external audience.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between the industry evaluation and peer evaluation of academic scientists.
H2a. The inverted U-shaped relationship between industry evaluation and peer evaluation of academic scientists is moderated by identity proximity, such that it is attenuated in academic disciplines with high identity proximity with industry, and accentuated in disciplines with low identity proximity with industry.
H2b. The inverted U-shaped relationship between the industry evaluation and peer evaluation of academic scientists is moderated by identity proximity, such that its turning point occurs at lower levels of industry evaluation in disciplines with low identity proximity with industry, and at higher levels of industry evaluation in disciplines with high identity proximity with industry.
H3a. The inverted U-shaped relationship between industry evaluation and peer valuation of academic scientists is moderated by the quality of their publishing track record, such that it is attenuated when quality is high, and accentuated when quality is low.
H3b. The inverted U-shaped relationship between industry evaluation and peer valuation of academic scientists is moderated by the irregularity of their publishing track record, such that it is attenuated when irregularity is low, and accentuated when irregularity is high.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Industry evaluation, operationalized as the cumulative number of industry contracts acquired by a scientist as principal investigator up to the focal year.
- DV(s): Peer evaluation, operationalized primarily as the count of grants awarded to a scientist as principal investigator in a given year.
- Mediators: Not directly tested as mediators; the theory distinguishes an ability-inference component and an identity-conformity component of exogenous indices.
- Moderators: Identity proximity with industry, quality of publishing record, and irregularity of publishing record.

The mechanism is a countervailing valuation process. External audience evaluations provide peer evaluators with positive information about a candidate's unobservable ability, so moderate industry evaluation can improve peer valuation. At higher levels, the same industry evaluation can signal deviation from the expected peer identity of an academic scientist, producing an inverted U-shaped relationship. This curvilinear effect is weaker where the academic discipline is closer to industry and where strong or regular publication records provide endogenous evidence of academic quality and identity.

**Data & Measures**
The data come from the administrative records of Minerva (a pseudonym), a large U.K. research university with approximately 15,000 students and 3,700 academic staff, from which the authors collected year-by-year information on all 9,502 academics employed by the university between 2001 and 2012. The records cover each scientist's grant applications, successful or not (28,579), successfully awarded grants (7,427), and all industry contracts (1,817) awarded by private sector firms, public health organizations and hospitals, and central government and authorities, together with organizational demographics (rank, departmental affiliation, length of tenure), author-approved publication records harvested from ISI Web of Knowledge and Pubmed, year-specific journal impact factors downloaded for about 11,000 journals for each year between 1997 and 2012, and webpage data on individuals' industry links and patents. The resulting unbalanced panel contains 34,647 scientist-year observations. The dependent variable, peer evaluation, is the count of grants awarded to each scientist as a principal investigator in a given year, with values ranging from 0 to 23. The independent variable, industry evaluation, is the cumulative number of contracts acquired by the scientist as a principal investigator up to the year under scrutiny. Identity proximity with industry is a dummy equal to 1 when the scientist belongs to the faculties of engineering and medicine and 0 otherwise, validated against a discipline-level university-industry collaboration measure (Tijssen, 2012) that ranges between 0 and 0.17 and yields faculty means of 0.071 for engineering, 0.061 for medicine, 0.046 for natural science, and 0.039 for business. Quality of publishing record is the cumulative impact factor of the journals in which a scientist's publications appeared up to the focal year, entered as a natural logarithm. Irregularity of publishing record divides the total number of years after a scientist's first publication in which they published no article in a journal with an ISI impact factor by their academic age, and ranges between 0 and 1. Controls are academic age, tenure, grant proposals filed (a time-variant categorical variable), previous peer evaluation, team quality (the first principal component of the cumulative number of grants, cumulative monetary value of grants, and cumulative publication impact factor of the investigators linked to a grant), patents, and industry experience, plus departmental dummies; all independent variables are lagged one year. The empirical method proceeds in three steps. The full panel analysis estimates a Poisson model with continuous endogenous covariates using an iterative generalized method of moments estimator, with a Probit selection equation supplying an inverse Mills ratio and three standard instruments (cumulative money awarded by U.K. government science funding bodies, the yearly percentage of U.K. gross domestic expenditure on R&D in the business sector, and a dummy for the 2004 introduction of a software system at Minerva that streamlined grant and contract administration); the models reported in Table 2 use 5,131 observations and 1,571 individuals, and the first-stage Probit in Table A1 uses 25,255 observations and 6,865 individuals. A confirmatory coarsened exact matching analysis matches exactly on faculty and year and coarsens previous peer evaluation, patents, industry experience, tenure, and team quality, matching 410 of the 552 treated individuals (74%) and yielding 3,091 person-year observations for a sample of 820 individuals. The design is a panel and matched-sample one rather than an experimental or quasi-experimental one; the authors state that reviewer scores for industry contracts are generally unavailable and that the matching method is not applicable as a substitute for randomized trials given the nature of their data. Robustness analyses include bootstrapped standard errors, a negative binomial estimator, alternative selection models drawn from a 2013 survey of Minerva scientists (1,352 individuals in the dataset), alternative specifications of industry and peer evaluation, censoring and winsorizing, and a linear system GMM replication that uses the logarithmic transformation of the monetary amount of grants awarded as the dependent variable. Finally, 10 interviews with scientists involved in reviewing grant proposals, lasting from 15 to 45 minutes and conducted with a semi-structured interview guide covering the postulated mechanisms, are used to elucidate and corroborate the quantitative findings.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. In Model 4 of the full-sample analysis, industry evaluation has a positive and significant effect on peer evaluation (b = 0.034, p < 0.05) and its squared term a negative and significant effect (b = -0.001, p < 0.05); following Lind and Mehlum (2010), the slope is 0.039 (p < 0.01) at the lower bound (0) and -0.077 (p < 0.05) at the upper bound (37), producing a significant overall test for the presence of an inverted U shape (t-value = 2.30; p < 0.05), and the estimated turning point of 12.48 (95% Fieller interval [8.31; 23.62]) falls within the range of the variable. The results are consistent with Hypothesis 2a in Model 5, where the interaction between industry evaluation and identity proximity with industry is negative and marginally significant (b = -0.128, p < 0.1) and the interaction between the squared term and identity proximity is positive and significant (b = 0.027, p < 0.05); the plotted effects differ significantly between low and high identity proximity for industry evaluation lower than three contracts and higher than five. Consistent with Hypothesis 2b, the inflection point moves to the left for disciplines less proximate to industry: in industry-proximate disciplines the estimated effect of acquired contracts on acquired grants is increasingly positive up to about 13 industry contracts, with a maximum equal to one grant, whereas in distant disciplines it takes only about three contracts for the effect to become negative, with a maximum of 0.76 grants; at the average amount of industry contracts (1.17) the estimated average number of acquired grants varies from 0.81 grants in industry-proximate disciplines to 0.70 grants in industry-distant disciplines. Hypothesis 3a is supported in Model 6, where the interaction between industry evaluation and quality of publishing record is negative and significant (b = -0.043, p < 0.001) and the interaction with the squared term is positive and significant (b = 0.002, p < 0.01); for scientists with a higher quality record the effect of contracts is increasingly positive up to about 11 industry contracts with a maximum of one grant, while lower-quality scientists reach a higher maximum (1.1 grants at a level of 10 contracts) and fall faster toward 0 at the right end of the contract distribution. Hypothesis 3b is supported in Model 7, where the interaction between industry evaluation and irregularity of publishing record is positive and marginally significant (b = 0.443, p < 0.1) and the interaction with the squared term is negative and significant (b = -0.047, p < 0.01), pointing to the strengthening of the inverted U shape as irregularity grows; for scientists with a regular publishing record the positive effect runs up to about 17 industry contracts with a maximum of 1.1 grants, whereas for those publishing less regularly the maximum is 0.85 grants at a level of five contracts. The fully specified Model 8 confirms these patterns. Tests of the secondary effects show that the secondary effects of identity proximity with industry, quality of publishing record, and irregularity of publishing record are in the expected direction and significant (p < .01) in at least 99% of cases, with the exception of the linear effects of identity proximity (p < 0.1) and irregularity (not significant), and the authors conclude that these results add support to Hypotheses 2a, 2b, 3a, and 3b. The coarsened exact matching analysis reproduces the pattern, finding a statistically significant inverted U-shaped effect of industry evaluation on peer evaluation in the baseline Model 12 and confirmation of the moderating effects of identity proximity, quality and irregularity of publishing records in Models 13 to 16. When industry evaluation is instead specified as a proportion of the total number of awards, the level of significance of the explanatory variables is lower or absent, which the authors read as discounting a time-and-effort argument and further supporting the identity nonconformance mechanism. The 10 grant-reviewer interviews converge with the quantitative results: informants described industry contracts as signaling generic ability to deliver, reported that negative judgments arose only when industry engagement became "excessive" rather than "normal" or "reasonable," and indicated that they relied less on industry engagement as an ability cue, and regarded high industry evaluations less critically, for candidates with strong academic track records.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to social valuation research by separating endogenous indices from exogenous indices and showing why prior positive evaluations do not always reinforce later valuation. It argues that evaluations by external audiences carry both ability information and identity information, making social approval from another audience potentially valuable up to a point and damaging beyond it. It also connects audience-based valuation to institutional logics by showing how peer audiences judge candidates partly through identity expectations embedded in their own valuation framework.

**Practical Implication**
For universities, the findings imply that faculty engagement with external audiences can help scientists obtain peer-valued resources, but excessive industry recognition may create an identity penalty, especially for junior researchers or those without strong publication records. University managers may reduce this penalty by strengthening alternative endogenous indices that reassure peer audiences about academic quality and identity. More broadly, organizations entering new audience domains should manage the trade-off between external audience appreciation and standing with their core audience.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study is situated in public science, raising questions about generalizability to settings where peer and external audiences use more orthogonal valuation criteria. They also state that their empirical approach relies on panel and matched-sample analyses rather than private reviewer scores or randomized assignment, because such scores and experimental designs are unavailable in their setting. External audience evaluations may also reflect unobserved reputation and status attributes beyond the measured industry contracts.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how intangible attributes such as reputation and status shape the relationship between external audience valuation and peer audience valuation. Additional work could test the framework in organizations and markets where candidates are simultaneously evaluated by consumers, business customers, shareholders, clients, regulators, or other audiences.
