# Different Strokes for Different Teams: The Contingent Effects of Positive and Negative Feedback on the Creativity of Informationally Homogeneous and Diverse Teams

**Abstract**
Feedback is a ubiquitous management tool. Employing it to enhance team creativity raises an important question of whether positive or negative feedback is more effective. Unfortunately, prior research on feedback valence and creativity is limited to the individual level, neglecting team creativity’s interdependent and knowledge-intensive nature. We address this issue and advance the team-information-processing perspective on team creativity by integrating two heretofore separate research streams to develop a team-specific model about how negative and positive feedback enhance creativity via two alternative information processing routes, contingent on teams’ informational diversity. Negative feedback fuels teams’ systematic effort and attention to external, novel information. In informationally diverse teams, in which members hold different information and perspectives, these efforts promote team creativity through information elaboration. Conversely, positive feedback propels members to flexibly use their information and contribute the resultant divergent insights to the team. In informationally homogeneous teams, wherein these insights relate to others’ information and perspectives, these divergent insights trigger teams’ generative processing and in turn creativity. Results from a team experiment support the predicted feedback valence by informational diversity interaction on team creativity through elaboration and generative processing.

**Research Question**
How do positive and negative feedback affect team creativity, and when does each form of feedback work better for informationally homogeneous versus informationally diverse teams? The paper specifically asks whether feedback valence interacts with informational diversity to promote team creativity through two team information-processing routes: information elaboration and generative processing.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Feedback valence and informational diversity interact in their effect on information elaboration: Negative (compared to positive) feedback has a positive effect on elaboration in diverse teams, but not in homogeneous teams.
- Hypothesis 1b. Information elaboration mediates in the interactive effect of feedback valence and informational diversity on team creativity.
- Hypothesis 2a. Feedback valence and informational diversity interact in their effect on team generative processing: Positive (compared to negative) feedback has a positive effect on team generative processing in homogeneous teams, but not in diverse teams.
- Hypothesis 2b. Team generative processing mediates in the interactive effect between feedback valence and informational diversity on team creativity.
- Hypothesis 3. Informational diversity interacts with feedback valence to affect team creativity: Negative feedback has a positive effect on creativity in diverse (but not homogeneous) teams and positive feedback has a positive effect on creativity in homogeneous (but not diverse) teams.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Feedback valence, manipulated as positive versus negative feedback on teams' initial ideas.
- DV(s): Team creativity, measured as the product of coded novelty and usefulness of the team's final plan.
- Mediators: Information elaboration and generative processing, both coded from post-feedback team discussion videos.
- Moderators: Informational diversity, manipulated as homogeneous versus diverse role-related information and perspectives.

The mechanism is a fit between feedback valence and the team's informational foundation. Negative feedback prompts systematic effort, attention to novel external information, and review of unused informational resources; this benefits informationally diverse teams because members hold different information and perspectives that can be elaborated and integrated. Positive feedback encourages flexible use of existing information and divergent insights; this benefits informationally homogeneous teams because shared knowledge makes members' ideas more likely to trigger one another's generative processing. The experiment supports the interaction on creativity and shows conditional indirect effects through elaboration and generative processing.

**Data & Measures**
The data come from one laboratory experiment with a 2 (informational diversity: diverse vs. homogeneous) x 2 (feedback valence: positive vs. negative) between-group design; the authors state that they tested their hypotheses experimentally to be able to draw inferences about causality. A total of 234 students (49.6% female; mean age = 20.88, SD = 2.11) from a Dutch university were assigned to 78 three-person teams that were randomly assigned to experimental conditions. The majority were business administration (80.3%) or economics (6.0%) students, compensated with 10 euro or course credit. The original sample contained 79 teams, but one was excluded because the video recording showed that they failed to follow the instructions. Due to technical problems, the video recording for one team was missing, reducing the sample size for all analyses involving video-coded measures to 77 teams; the note to Table 2 likewise reports n = 77 for the models involving the video-coded measures.

The task, adapted from Hoever et al. (2012), had participants take the roles of a team managing a theater and devise a creative action plan for the theater's future, submitted as one integrated plan rather than a list of unconnected ideas, with monetary rewards given to the most creative teams. Informational diversity was manipulated through functional role instructions: in diverse teams members were assigned the roles of artistic director, event manager, and finance manager and held partly unshared information (a calendar of plays for the artistic director, an overview of sales, prices, and revenues for the financial manager, and a plan of the theater's facilities for the event manager), whereas the homogeneous role contained the information and perspectives from all three diverse roles, holding the amount of information and perspectives constant at the team level. Feedback valence was manipulated with a bogus feedback sheet handed to teams midway through the task, reporting percentile ranks against the ideas of teams in a purported prior study: teams in the negative condition were told their ideas were more novel than 20%, more useful than 30%, and more creative than 25% of the prior teams' ideas, and teams receiving positive feedback were told their ideas were more novel than 70%, more useful than 80%, and more creative than 75%.

Team creativity (dependent variable) was coded by two independent coders who rated each idea in a team's plan for novelty (ICC(1) = .69, ICC(2) = .82, mean rWG = .89) and usefulness (ICC(1) = .61, ICC(2) = .76, mean rWG = .88) on a 7-point scale from 1 (not novel/useful at all) to 7 (very novel/useful), with usefulness assessed as the degree to which an idea addressed the three goals stated in the instructions; the team score averaged the product of novelty and usefulness of each idea included in the plan. Information elaboration (mediator) was coded from the post-feedback discussion videos, with two independent raters assigning each team a score on a 7-point scale reflecting whether members shared, thoroughly discussed, and integrated the full range of their task-relevant information and perspectives (ICC(1) = .32, ICC(2) = .47, mean rWG(J) = .86). Generative processing (mediator) was assessed as the frequency of statements verbalizing divergent thinking triggered by the input of other members, covering three statement types (drawing parallels between the task setting and other contexts, thinking aloud and sharing half-baked ideas, and taking a previous utterance out of context to move the discussion off on a tangent), summed into an aggregated score (ICC(1) = .38, ICC(2) = .54, mean rWG(J) = .82). Manipulation checks used an 8-item scale on which participants rated how novel, useful, and creative their team's ideas were relative to other teams' (items from prior feedback-valence studies adapted to the team context; alpha = .95, ICC(1) = .77, ICC(2) = .91, mean rWG = .96) and, for diversity, coders' classification of members' individual statements of what they considered important into the three managerial perspectives, from which Blau's coefficient of heterogeneity was computed and averaged across the three perspectives. Two alternative mediators were measured in a post-experimental survey: team creative efficacy, using a 4-item Tierney and Farmer (2002) scale with the referent shifted to the team (alpha = .87), and current positive affect, using ten items from the positive and negative affect schedule (Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988; alpha = .87), averaged to the team level.

The joint effects of the two manipulations on elaboration, generative processing, and creativity were tested with analyses of variance and planned contrasts. Hypotheses about conditional indirect effects were tested with Hayes's (2013) procedure (model 8), which allowed moderation of the first stage of the indirect effects but constrained the effects from each mediator on creativity to be equal for homogeneous and diverse teams. Path estimates were obtained by ordinary least squares regression, mediators were mean-centered, diversity and feedback valence were dummy coded with the homogeneous and the negative feedback conditions as the zero-coded reference categories, and indirect effects were tested with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals constructed from 1,000 bootstrap samples.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1a was supported. An ANOVA on information elaboration showed no significant difference between the negative (M = 3.44, SD = 1.29) and positive feedback conditions (M = 3.30, SD = 1.00), F < 1, and a significant main effect of diversity, F(1, 73) = 6.27, p = .01, partial eta-squared = .08, with higher elaboration in diverse (M = 3.62, SD = 1.32) than homogeneous teams (M = 3.13, SD = 0.90). That main effect was qualified by a significant feedback-diversity interaction, F(1, 73) = 38.95, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .35. Diverse teams showed more elaboration following negative feedback (M = 4.34, SD = 1.12) than positive feedback (M = 2.87, SD = 1.11), F(1, 73) = 23.44, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .24, whereas homogeneous teams showed higher elaboration following positive feedback (M = 3.66, SD = 0.76) than negative feedback (M = 2.49, SD = 0.61), F(1, 73) = 15.78, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .18. Diversity raised elaboration in the negative feedback condition, F(1, 73) = 36.98, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .34, but lowered it in the positive feedback condition, F(1, 73) = 7.23, p = .01, partial eta-squared = .09.

Hypothesis 2a was supported. Neither main effect on generative processing was significant (both F < 1), but the feedback-diversity interaction was, F(1, 73) = 21.80, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .23. Homogeneous teams showed higher generative processing in the positive (M = 3.49, SD = 2.38) than in the negative feedback condition (M = 1.50, SD = 0.97), F(1, 73) = 11.46, p < .01, partial eta-squared = .14. For diverse teams the pattern ran the other way: positive feedback led to less generative processing (M = 1.65, SD = 1.77) than negative feedback (M = 3.60, SD = 1.84), F(1, 73) = 10.38, p < .01, partial eta-squared = .12. The authors state that they did not a priori expect this negative effect of positive feedback on generative processing in diverse teams, and read the homogeneous-team pattern as supporting Hypothesis 2a.

Hypothesis 3 was supported. Diverse (M = 16.09, SD = 6.77) and homogeneous teams (M = 13.98, SD = 7.37) did not differ significantly in creativity, F(1, 74) = 2.77, p = .10, and the creativity of teams receiving negative (M = 14.04, SD = 6.59) and positive feedback (M = 15.88, SD = 7.57) was comparable, F(1, 74) = 1.31, p = .26, but the feedback-diversity interaction was significant, F(1, 74) = 23.03, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .24. Diverse teams devised more creative plans after negative feedback (M = 18.60, SD = 6.26) than after positive feedback (M = 13.44, SD = 6.41), F(1, 74) = 6.37, p = .01, partial eta-squared = .08, while homogeneous teams receiving positive feedback developed significantly more creative plans (M = 17.87, SD = 7.99) than those receiving negative feedback (M = 9.48, SD = 2.50), F(1, 74) = 18.57, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .20.

Hypotheses 1b and 2b were supported. The feedback-diversity interaction had a significant indirect effect on creativity through elaboration, b = -4.50, SE = 2.06, CI [-9.06, -0.87], and through generative processing, b = -4.82, SE = 2.55, CI [-11.54, -1.12]. The conditional indirect effects showed the hypothesized negative indirect effect of feedback valence (a relative benefit of negative feedback) on creativity for diverse teams through elaboration, b = -2.51, SE = 1.29, CI [-5.59, -0.48], and a positive indirect effect (a relative benefit of positive feedback) for homogeneous teams through elaboration, b = 1.99, SE = 0.89, CI [0.47, 3.97]. Through generative processing, the indirect effect was positive for homogeneous teams, b = 2.43, SE = 1.37, CI [0.43, 5.85], and negative for diverse teams, b = -2.39, SE = 1.41, CI [-6.69, -0.48]. In the model regressing creativity on feedback valence, diversity, their interaction, and both mediators (adjusted R-squared = .41, p < .001), elaboration (b = 1.71, SE = 0.74, t = 2.30, p = .02) and generative processing (b = 1.22, SE = 0.37, t = 3.29, p < .01) were the only significant predictors, rendering the feedback-diversity interaction non-significant (b = -4.16, SE = 3.18, t = -1.31, p = .19).

Exploratory analyses adding pre- versus post-feedback timing yielded significant three-way interactions of feedback, diversity, and time on elaboration, F(1, 73) = 23.97, p < .001, and on generative processing, F(1, 73) = 13.36, p < .001. After negative feedback, diverse teams engaged in higher elaboration than before it (Mpre = 3.64 vs. Mpost = 4.34), F(1, 73) = 9.61, p < .01, and their generative processing increased (Mpre = 1.51 vs. Mpost = 3.60), F(1, 73) = 14.99, p < .001; elaboration decreased for diverse teams after positive feedback (Mpre = 3.57 vs. Mpost = 2.87), F(1, 73) = 8.98, p < .01, and for homogeneous teams after negative feedback (Mpre = 3.41 vs. Mpost = 2.49), F(1, 73) = 15.49, p < .01, while homogeneous teams' generative processing increased following positive feedback (Mpre = 2.48 vs. Mpost = 3.49), F(1, 73) = 4.05, p < .05. Neither positive affect (homogeneous b = 0.51, SE = 0.76, CI [-0.70, 2.34]; diverse b = 0.28, SE = 0.49, CI [-0.27, 1.75]) nor team creative efficacy (homogeneous b = -0.43, SE = 1.06, CI [-2.40, 1.81]; diverse b = -0.29, SE = 0.74, CI [-1.98, 1.08]) mediated the effects of feedback valence, and the indirect effects through elaboration and through generative processing were significantly stronger than those associated with team creative efficacy and with positive affect.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to team creativity theory by integrating two previously separate information-processing routes: information elaboration from the team performance and decision-making tradition, and generative processing from the brainstorming tradition. It shows that both routes can enhance team creativity, but they depend on different combinations of feedback valence and informational diversity.

It also shifts feedback-valence theory from an individual-level main-effect account to a team-specific contingency account. Rather than assuming positive feedback is generally better for creativity, the study identifies informational diversity as a team-level moderator with no direct individual-level counterpart.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should match feedback valence to the informational composition of the team when the goal is creativity. Negative feedback can help informationally diverse teams by pushing members to search, discuss, and integrate their distinct informational resources, while positive feedback can help informationally homogeneous teams by encouraging flexible and mutually stimulating use of shared knowledge.

When managers must give feedback that does not fit the team's informational diversity, the paper suggests compensating through process support. Diverse teams receiving positive feedback may need prompts to share and integrate unshared information, while homogeneous teams receiving negative feedback may need novel information or support for generative processing.

**Limitations**
The study is a laboratory experiment with student teams, so the authors caution that follow-up work is needed to test generalizability to field settings. The feedback was randomly assigned and bogus rather than based on teams' actual creativity, although the authors argue this supports internal validity and may make the effects conservative if credibility was doubted.

The feedback occurred during an ongoing task, so its effects may differ from feedback on completed tasks or repeated feedback across future work. The teams were low in authority differentiation and temporal stability, and the informational-diversity manipulation may not capture demographic diversity or diversity that triggers social categorization.

**Future Research**
Future studies could test the model in field settings, with actual performance-based feedback, repeated feedback, and teams that vary in authority differentiation, skill differentiation, and temporal stability. Research could also examine whether external informational resources, such as leaders, knowledge-management systems, or boundary spanning, can substitute for the internal informational resources of diverse teams.

The paper also points to future work on how elaboration and generative processing interact over time, especially whether elaboration can trigger generative processing in diverse teams. Additional research should examine how team affective and motivational states interact dynamically with team information processing, and whether the feedback-diversity fit extends to other team tasks beyond creativity.
