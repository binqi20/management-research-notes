# Entrepreneurial Imaginativeness in New Venture Ideation

**Abstract**
Although theories of entrepreneurial action regularly acknowledge the importance of imagination, the ability is rarely defined or measured, and thus effectively treated as uniform in degree and type. Using a creative problem-solving lens, we identify and measure three different cognitive skills—creative, social, and practical imaginativeness—that vary across individuals. Each skill combines the ability of imagination with the knowledge needed to mentally simulate various task-related scenarios used in generating and selecting ideas for new value creation. We then conduct a quasi-experiment to examine each skill’s relative effect on new venture ideation. We find that the three imaginativeness skills vary across individuals and that they predict new venture idea quantity and quality differently over and above the effects of motivation, knowledge, and experience. We conclude with implications for theory development in entrepreneurship and creative problem-solving.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether entrepreneurial imaginativeness can be conceptualized and measured as distinct creative, social, and practical cognitive skills, and whether those skills explain new venture idea quantity and quality. It also asks whether idea quantity mediates the relationship between imaginativeness and idea quality.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Creative imaginativeness positively relates to new venture idea quantity.
- Hypothesis 2. Social imaginativeness positively relates to new venture idea quantity.
- Hypothesis 3. Practical imaginativeness positively relates to new venture idea quantity.
- Hypothesis 4a. Idea quantity will fully mediate the relationship between creative imaginativeness and idea quality.
- Hypothesis 4b. Idea quantity will fully mediate the relationship between social imaginativeness and idea quality.
- Hypothesis 5. Idea quantity will partially mediate the relationship between practical imaginativeness and idea quality.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Creative imaginativeness, social imaginativeness, and practical imaginativeness.
- DV(s): New venture idea quantity and new venture idea quality.
- Mediators: Idea quantity, tested as a mediator between each form of imaginativeness and idea quality.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The mechanism is that imaginativeness combines the cognitive ability of imagination with task-relevant entrepreneurial knowledge. Creative imaginativeness helps individuals form novel means-ends connections, social imaginativeness helps them understand others' needs and perspectives, and practical imaginativeness helps them identify problems and evaluate feasible solutions. The study finds that all three forms predict idea quantity, that idea quantity fully mediates the creative imaginativeness-idea quality link, and that idea quantity partially mediates the practical imaginativeness-idea quality link.

**Data & Measures**
The study draws on two separate Qualtrics online-panel samples collected by the authors. Scale development used a first sample of 210 respondents, to whom 40 candidate items were administered; those participants ranged in age from 20 to 68 (average = 42.42 years, SD = 10.78), 50% were male, 69.52% were Caucasian, all were college educated, all were employed full time (minimum = 30 hours per week, average = 43.24 hours, SD = 7.49) in a broad range of organizations, and they possessed over 20 years of work experience (average = 20.45 years, SD = 11.20) in 20 different industry classifications. Hypothesis testing used a separate, stratified sample of 506 individuals from the general working population, randomly distributed across the United States and varying in entrepreneurial experience: the panel service sent requests to 700 individuals in exchange for approximately $5 in compensation, and 506 completed responses were received, a response rate of 72.23%. These 506 participants ranged in age from 19 to 88 years old (average = 45.56 years, SD = 14.52), 53.20% were male, 78.46% were Caucasian, 62.65% had a college degree, and they possessed over 20 years of work experience (average = 22.45 years, SD = 13.62) in 20 different industry classifications; 154 participants (30.43%) had attempted one startup in their career, 75 (14.82%) had attempted two, and 43 (8.50%) had attempted three or more. The imaginativeness measure was developed following Hinkin (1998) and MacKenzie, Podsakoff, and Podsakoff (2011): a review of existing literature plus in-depth, semi-structured interviews with 16 entrepreneurs produced 77 original scale items; 21 subject matter experts performed a content validity assessment; the resulting 40 items were administered to the 210-respondent sample; and confirmatory factor analysis yielded an 18-item, three-factor measure rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), with acceptable goodness of fit (CFI = 0.91; SRMR = 0.05; RMSEA = 0.09), average variance extracted above Fornell and Larcker's (1981) suggested cutoff of 0.50 for each form of imaginativeness, average variances extracted greater than the square of the correlations between comparison constructs in all but one case, and reliability of 0.93, 0.92, and 0.89 for creative, social, and practical imaginativeness, respectively. That 18-item measure was then administered to the new sample of 506 participants. Procedurally, in Part I each participant was asked to generate as many new venture ideas as possible based on a description and diagram of facial recognition technology (Appendix A), and in Part II to select their best idea and write a short description of that idea; the order of the predictor and criterion variables was counterbalanced. Two expert raters independently scored the quantity (number) of ideas generated in Part I and the quality of the selected idea in Part II, which served as the dependent variables. Idea quality was viewed as the combination of originality, operationalized with Dean and colleagues' (2006) four-point originality scale ranging from 1 (common, mundane, or boring business idea) to 4 (rare, unusual, ingenious, imaginative, or surprising business idea), and feasibility, operationalized with a three-point scale ranging from 1 (low feasibility) to 3 (high feasibility); overall interrater reliability of the idea quality measure was acceptable (ICC = 0.92). Idea quantity also served as the hypothesized mediator. The controls were Knowledge: Education (a seven-point scale of highest educational degree achieved, anchored by less than high school through doctoral or professional degree), Knowledge: Technology Familiarity (a seven-point Likert-type scale anchored by 1 (very unfamiliar) to 7 (very familiar)), Experience: Age (the participant's age in years), Experience: Startup (a continuous variable of the number of business startups attempted in one's lifetime), Effort 1 (the amount of time the participant spent completing the survey, measured in seconds), Effort 2 (the amount of words written when describing ideas), and Gender (coded 0 = male, 1 = female). Hypotheses were tested with hierarchical linear regression; mediation was assessed with Baron and Kenny's (1986) procedure and a Sobel test; and a supplemental relative weight analysis used 95% confidence intervals and significance tests based on a bootstrapping approach with 10,000 replications. The authors describe the design as a quasi-experimental approach similar to Corbett (2007), Grégoire and Shepherd (2012), and Mueller and Shepherd (2016), in which participants generated new venture ideas based on a common technology and then completed survey measures of the variables, and in their limitations describe the study as employing a cross-sectional design.

**Key Findings**
In Models 1 to 3 of Table 3, creative imaginativeness (b = 0.06, p < .001), social imaginativeness (b = 0.06, p < .001), and practical imaginativeness (b = 0.05, p < .001) were each positively and significantly related to idea quantity over and above knowledge, experience, and motivation, and the authors find support for Hypotheses 1 to 3 (the Results text reports p < .001 for practical imaginativeness in Model 3, while Table 3 marks that coefficient at p < .01). Hypothesis 4a is supported: creative imaginativeness is positively and significantly related to idea quality in Model 5 (b = 0.01, p < .01) but is no longer related to idea quality in Model 8 (b = 0.01, ns) once the mediator idea quantity (b = 0.07, p < .001) is included, so idea quantity fully mediates the creative imaginativeness to idea quality relationship. Hypothesis 4b is not supported: the authors found no such relationship between social imaginativeness and idea quality (Models 6 and 9). Hypothesis 5 is supported: practical imaginativeness is positively and significantly related to idea quality in Model 7 (b = 0.03, p < .001) and is still positively and significantly related in Model 10 (b = 0.02, p < .001) when the mediator idea quantity (b = 0.07, p < .001) is included, so including idea quantity reduces but does not eliminate the significant correlation, and a Sobel test revealed that this reduction was statistically significant (z = 2.62, p < .01), indicating partial mediation. The authors characterize the b coefficient effect sizes on idea quantity and quality as small (for example, a one unit increase in creative imaginativeness results in 0.06 ideas generated) and report that the combined explained variance attributed to creative, social, and practical imaginativeness is approximately 5% on idea quantity and 2% on idea quality. In the supplemental relative weight analysis, creative imaginativeness (RW = 0.03, p < .05), social imaginativeness (RW = 0.02, p < .05), and practical imaginativeness (RW = 0.01, p < .05) each explained a statistically significant amount of variance in new venture idea quantity, as none of the 95% confidence intervals for those tests of significance contained zero, and practical imaginativeness (RW = 0.01, p < .05) was significantly related to new venture idea quality, while in Table 4 the relative-weight confidence intervals for creative and social imaginativeness on idea quality both contain zero.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to entrepreneurship theory by moving imagination from an assumed latent ability to a measurable set of entrepreneurial imaginativeness skills. It extends creative problem-solving theory by showing that creative, social, and practical imaginativeness explain new venture ideation beyond motivation, knowledge, and experience. It also suggests that opportunity recognition, effectuation, bricolage, narrative/storytelling, and analogical reasoning may depend on unevenly distributed forms of imaginativeness rather than on a uniform imaginative capacity.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that venture teams should not assume that all founders possess the same form of imagination. New venture ideation may benefit from diagnosing or developing creative, social, and practical imaginativeness separately, or from composing teams whose members compensate for one another's imaginative strengths and weaknesses. It also implies that tools such as effectuation, bricolage, design thinking, and lean startup may work differently depending on the imaginativeness of the individuals using them.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study uses a mechanistic, post-positivistic approach that measures imaginativeness as a cognitive skill rather than examining imagination more directly through alternative philosophical worldviews. They also state that the study uses a cross-sectional design, which may be limited because entrepreneurship unfolds over time. The common facial-recognition scenario improves comparability but leaves open whether the findings generalize across phases of entrepreneurship, teams, existing organizations, and other venture contexts.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how entrepreneurial imaginativeness affects other models of entrepreneurial action, including effectuation, bricolage, design thinking, lean startup, improvisation, and analogical or metaphorical reasoning. They suggest work across levels of analysis, especially how individual imaginativeness combines into team-level entrepreneurial imaginativeness. They also recommend longitudinal and interpretive research on the antecedents, development, unconscious, embodied, and sensory aspects of imagination.
