# From Protest to Product: Strategic Frame Brokerage in a Commercial Social Movement Organization

**Abstract**
Social movement organizations that engage directly in commercial activities face the particular challenge of mobilizing movement activists and commercial actors simultaneously. In a seven-year case study of a Sicilian anti-racket social movement organization that uses commercial activities to combat Mafia racketeering, we show how strategic framing enables such dual mobilization. Our findings show that original antiracket social movement frames were modified through a process of strategic frame brokerage that incorporated, through interaction, the distributed interpretations of tourists, tourism service providers, and anti-racket activists. As a result of this process, original social movement frames were retained, transformed, or managed through selective referral. We discuss the implications of our findings for research on processes of frame development, social enterprise, and commercialization as a “mobilizing technology” for social movements.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how a social movement organization entering an existing industry can frame its commercial activities so that they mobilize industry actors while retaining support from the social movement whose goals it shares. Empirically, it examines how Addiopizzo Travel reconciled anti-racket movement frames with responsible tourism industry frames while asking how to meet market demands without betraying the anti-racket movement.

**Mechanism Process**
- Initial frame transposition: AT transposed anti-racket movement frames of community, security, dignity, and denunciation into commercial tourism offerings.
- Validation with multiple audiences: AT tested whether these frames resonated with tourists and tourism service providers and whether they remained consistent with anti-racket movement ideology.
- Search through interaction: When some frames failed to resonate, AT interacted with tourists, tourism service providers, and activists to surface alternative interpretations of its activities.
- Strategic lamination and transformation: AT transformed security into sustainability and dignity into beauty by layering newly keyed industry and activist interpretations onto original movement frames.
- Retention and selective referral: AT retained community because it resonated with both audiences, while denunciation was selectively referred to other parts of the anti-racket movement rather than used to frame AT's commercial activities directly.
- Operationalization: Validated frames guided new tours, partnerships, booking services, and referrals that mobilized commercial actors without severing ideological ties to the movement.

The process is strategic frame brokerage: leaders of a commercial social movement organization broker between movement activists and commercial actors by repeatedly validating, revising, retaining, transforming, or selectively referring movement frames. The mechanism is not a statistical causal model; it is an inductive process model showing how interaction supplies distributed interpretations that leaders incorporate into frames capable of dual mobilization. The core tension is that frames strong enough to preserve movement ideology may be confusing or threatening to market actors, while market-resonant frames may be judged by activists as mission drift.

**Data & Measures**
The study is a seven-year field study of Addiopizzo Travel (AT), a social movement organization founded in 2009 by a group of anti-racket activists to enter the tourism industry. The authors learned of plans to create AT in July 2009, a few months before its official founding, began collecting data at that time, and continued through to September 2016. The corpus comprises 73 semi-structured interviews with AT's entrepreneurs, responsible tourism actors (tourists and tourism service providers such as travel agents, hoteliers, and restaurateurs), and anti-racket movement activists; 41 days of naturalistic observation of actors, events, and activities of AT and of the anti-racket movement; data on the internal and external communications of AT (224 documents) and of the anti-racket movement (443 documents); previous scholarly accounts of the anti-racket movement (15 documents); and reports produced by AT tourists (42 documents). Table 2 breaks the interviews down as 40 interviews with AT entrepreneurs, 11 interviews with tourism players, 7 interviews with tourists, and 15 interviews with activists; the AT documents as 56 internal documents, 51 communications to media, 31 advertising documents, and 86 media-coverage documents; and the anti-racket movement documents as 122 movement communications and 321 media-coverage documents. The text separately reports the informants as all six AT entrepreneurs (the three founders and three who joined later), five anti-racket activists not formally affiliated with AT, five tourism service providers with longstanding partnerships with AT, and seven tourists selected from AT's database. Interviews each lasted between 30 minutes and two hours and were audio-recorded and transcribed when possible; the protocol began from an initial guide reflecting the research question and was adapted to different informants and to constructs that emerged over the course of the research. Observation was carried out by the authors: one participated periodically in anti-racket movement events and meetings in Sicily beginning in the early 2000s and, before AT was founded, attended formal and informal meetings with the entrepreneurs discussing whether to establish it, while after the 2009 founding two of the authors observed AT board meetings, office hours, and tours. Archival sources include records provided by AT, media articles, and snapshots of the AT website captured over time. There are no quantitative measures and no estimation; constructs were built by coding, following grounded theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1990) in an inductive and interactive approach with three recursive steps (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013). Step 1 used open coding to identify key empirical themes describing how AT communicated its activities and interacted with external actors. Step 2 was more abductive and applied frame analysis: the values AT used to communicate about its activities — security, dignity, sustainability, beauty, environmentalism, denunciation, and community — could be coded as frames, and, following Gamson and Lasch (1983) as further developed by Creed and colleagues (2002), the authors constructed a signature matrix sorting idea elements into eight categories: metaphors, exemplars, catchphrases, depictions, visual images, roots, consequences, and appeals to principles (Table 4). Frame analysis also identified the three framing tasks the frames accomplished — diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational framing (Snow & Benford, 1988) — and the criteria of resonance: with anti-racket activists, a frame's resonance and consequent validation depended on its perceived consistency with the social movement ideology, that is, with its central claims and values; with tourists and tourism service providers, resonance depended on three mechanisms, (1) narrative fidelity, (2) consistency with social movement organizations' activities, and (3) empirical credibility. Keying and lamination (Goffman, 1974; Gray et al., 2015) were coded as the interactional processes through which new meanings emerged and were layered onto original frames. Step 3 aggregated the conceptual categories into an empirically grounded model; the resulting data structure (Figure 1) yields the aggregate dimensions of operationalization, validation, and search. The design is interpretive and inductive, and the paper advances a process model rather than a causal-identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
AT initially transposed four anti-racket movement frames — community, security, dignity, and denunciation — and operationalized them in its 2009–2010 tourism offerings and communications, designing tours that let tourists participate in the anti-racket community, occupy the storefronts of exposed businesses, and visit places symbolizing the dignity of locals who had denounced extortions. Validation of the transposed frames split by audience: activists expressed acceptance of the frames, which they perceived as consistent with the movement's ideology, but of the original anti-racket frames only the community frame resonated with tourists and tourism service providers. The paper reports that the security, dignity, and denunciation frames resonated only partially with industry actors and, later, that they generally failed to resonate with tourists and tourism service providers. Community was effective for diagnostic framing because of its fidelity to the narrative of the responsible tourism sector, for prognostic framing because tourism service providers saw it as consistent with AT's activities, and for motivational framing because it was empirically credible to tourists, who could visibly observe how their actions advanced the anti-racket community. The other three frames failed on the same three mechanisms: dignity lacked fidelity to the responsible tourism narrative because tourists and tour operators did not see dignity as relevant to responsible tourism; security was viewed as inconsistent with AT's activities, since tourists questioned whether short-term, pleasurable tourism could ensure the physical security of business owners; and all three lacked empirical credibility with respect to tourism activities, evoking images of adversarial interactions and sentiments of danger and fear, which decreased the motivation of both tour operators and tourists to collaborate with AT. AT's first frame search, conducted through interaction with responsible tourism industry actors alone, failed: tourists and service providers interpreted visits to anti-racket businesses in terms of environmental protection, AT laminated that newly keyed meaning onto the original security frame and by late 2011 transformed security into environmentalism, and although tourists and tourism service providers reacted positively, activists were openly dissatisfied and suggested that the environmentalism frame deviated from the social movement's ideology and purpose — the discussion describes use of the environmental frame as having been perceived by activists as mission drift. AT therefore changed its approach in late 2011, proactively engaging both activists and responsible tourism industry actors in frame search. This time keying generated a systematic and consensual reinterpretation among all three groups, and the new meanings were laminated onto the original frames, transforming security into sustainability and dignity into beauty. Both transformed frames were validated on both sides: beauty provided fidelity to the narrative of the responsible tourism industry and was effective for prognostic and motivational purposes due to its empirical credibility, while sustainability was empirically credible for tourists, who were open to interpreting AT tours as sustainable; activists accepted both as adding a new meaning to anti-racket activities while remaining consistent with movement ideology, seeing beauty as the application of dignity to Sicilian cultural and environmental heritage and interpreting sustainability as scaling the impact of the original security frame by laminating financial support as an additional type of security. Denunciation did not follow this path: activists viewed it as so central to anti-racket ideology that they interpreted any potential modification as a betrayal of the movement's purpose, so AT halted the keying of denunciation-framed activities and managed the frame through selective referral, which took two forms — using denunciation only for diagnostic framing but not for prognostic and motivational framing, and putting tourists in contact with anti-racket activists to observe how other movement activities enacted denunciation. Selective referral raised awareness of the anti-racket movement's broader activities among tourism service providers and tourists in 2013 and helped resolve activists' earlier concerns that AT was behaving inconsistently with movement ideology. In the final phase AT operationalized its validated frames: from 2012 onward it used community for new collaborations with anti-racket associations that exploited non-commercial mobilization opportunities for the movement at large, and beginning in 2013 it leveraged sustainability and beauty to design and advertise new tourism products such as a Sicily-by-bike cycling tour and the Corleone tour, while operationalizing denunciation through selective referral by encouraging tourists to meet local activists who had denounced extortionists and by intentionally designing tours that put tourists and tourism service providers in contact with the anti-racket movement. By the end of 2015, six years after AT's founding, a stable set of frames resonated with responsible tourists and tourism service providers and therefore mobilized their support for its commercial activities; the resulting frames were also perceived by the anti-racket movement as consistent with movement ideology, and AT was commercially viable and a point of reference in the responsible tourism industry. The emergent model of strategic frame brokerage (Figure 2) specifies five phases — transposition and operationalization of a social movement frame, validation of a transposed frame, frame search and transformation, new validation, and final frame operationalization — and three possible outcomes for an original movement frame: retention, as observed with community; transformation, as observed in the transformation of dignity into beauty and security into sustainability; and selective referral to other parts of the movement, as observed with denunciation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on frame development by integrating strategic framing and interactional framing. It shows that social movement leaders do not only impose frames top-down or wait for frames to emerge bottom-up; they can strategically elicit, broker, and laminate interpretations from multiple supporter groups. It also contributes to social enterprise and hybrid organization research by showing how mission drift concerns can be managed through framing, and to social movement research by theorizing commercialization as a mobilizing technology that can attract supporters who are not already ideological adherents.

**Practical Implication**
For commercial social movement organizations and social enterprises, the paper implies that market entry requires active frame work with both commercial audiences and movement constituencies. Managers should validate whether frames are narratively credible to customers and partners while also checking whether activists see them as ideologically consistent. When a movement frame is too central to transform, selective referral may preserve the movement connection without forcing the commercial activity to carry a frame that undermines market resonance.

**Limitations**
The authors identify as a boundary condition the existence of established interpretive frames in both the social movement and the commercial industry. In settings with nascent market categories or less established movements, a social movement organization may shape the category or the movement rather than merely broker between existing frames. They also note that some movements may not be amenable to commercial transactions, that frame brokerage may not always find common ground, and that the process is costly in time and attention.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on strategic frame brokerage in nascent market categories, newer social movements, and contexts where common ground between activists and industry actors is harder to find. It also suggests studying the limits of multivalent frames, delaminating processes through which frame meanings decay or become more specific, and the skills needed to transition from collective action to commerce. Future work could also examine how commercial social movement organizations coordinate with other movement organizations and whether commercial participation complements or substitutes for traditional activism.
