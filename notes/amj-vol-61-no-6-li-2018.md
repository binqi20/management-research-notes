# A Network View of Advice-giving and Individual Creativity in Teams: A Brokerage-driven, Socially Perpetuated Phenomenon

**Abstract**
One potential benefit of team contexts is that members can share knowledge and ideas to help one another solve problems (e.g., advice-giving), thereby enhancing members’ creative potential. However, individuals’ advice-giving actions do not guarantee increases in recipients’ creativity and, in some cases, may even be a creative liability for the giver’s own subsequent creativity. We argue that considering advice givers’ brokerage in the team’s advice-giving network (i.e., advice brokerage) may clarify this phenomenon. Specifically, we posit that advice given from a brokerage position contains more nonredundant content that boosts recipients’ creativity. Further, we expect advice brokerage to positively influence a giver’s own creativity, though this effect is at least partially explained via recipients’ proximal creativity gains (e.g., recipients’ creativity change mediates the relationship). Recipients’ perceptions of psychological safety accentuate these relationships. Longitudinal field data support the main hypotheses, but also suggest that advice brokerage has more immediate proximal effects on givers’ creativity; an additional qualitative study elucidates several mechanisms by which brokered advice boosts creativity, including two associated with generating diverse ideas (idea integration, perspective-taking), two associated with domain-specific knowledge accumulation (knowledge accumulation, reflection), collective creativity, and creative feedback. The study yields several important implications.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when advice-giving in teams improves creativity for advice recipients and advice givers, rather than simply consuming the giver's time or reinforcing existing ideas. It focuses on whether a giver's brokerage position in the team's advice-giving network makes advice more creatively useful, whether recipients' creativity gains socially perpetuate gains for givers, and whether recipients' psychological safety strengthens these effects.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Givers' advice brokerage will be positively related to advice recipients' creativity enhancement.
- H2: Advice recipients' creativity enhancement will be positively related to advice givers' creativity enhancement.
- H3: Advice recipients' creativity enhancement will mediate the relationship between a giver's advice brokerage and the giver's creativity enhancement.
- H4: Recipients' psychological safety perceptions will moderate the relationship between givers' advice brokerage and recipients' creativity enhancement, such that the relationship is more positive when psychological safety perceptions are higher (versus lower).
- H5: Recipients' psychological safety perceptions will moderate the positive indirect effect of the giver's advice brokerage on her or his own creativity enhancement via recipients' creativity enhancement, such that the indirect effect is more positive when psychological safety perceptions are higher (versus lower).

These five hypotheses belong to Study 1. Study 2 states no hypotheses; the authors report that its goal was to elucidate processes rather than to replicate Study 1, and that they intentionally phrased the interview questions so as not to lead to any a priori expectations.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Advice givers' brokerage in the team advice-giving network, measured with betweenness centrality.
- DV(s): Advice recipients' creativity enhancement and advice givers' creativity enhancement over time.
- Mediators: Advice recipients' creativity enhancement mediating the relationship between a giver's advice brokerage and the giver's later creativity enhancement.
- Moderators: Advice recipients' perceived psychological safety.

The mechanism is that brokers give advice from positions that connect otherwise unconnected teammates, so their advice is more likely to carry nonredundant knowledge, perspectives, and integrative ideas. Recipients who feel psychologically safe are more willing to use this brokered advice in creative problem solving, increasing their own creativity. Those recipient gains then feed back into the team context and the giver's subsequent creativity. The qualitative study extends the model by showing additional processes: idea integration, perspective taking, knowledge accumulation, reflection, collective creativity, and creative feedback.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 is a three-wave field survey of research and development teams from 17 manufacturing firms in China. The Time 1 survey included 278 employees from 43 teams, with 247 useable responses from 40 teams (overall response rate 88.8%; average within-team response rate 90.1%). Six months later (Time 2) the 40 responding team leaders returned 233 creativity ratings (response rate 94.3%), and six months after that (Time 3) 207 ratings were received from 38 team leaders (overall response rate 88.8%). Table 1 reports n = 207 for the giver-level and control variables and n = 200 for the advice-recipient variables; the Table 2 regression models report n = 200. Survey items were originally developed in English and translated to Chinese using back-translation (Brislin, 1986), with responses on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) unless otherwise noted.
- IV, givers' advice brokerage: betweenness centrality calculated at Time 1 from a full-roster question ("Do you give work-related advice to this team member?") answered on a five-point scale from 1 (never) to 5 (frequently), with responses dichotomized (1 recoded as "0", 2-5 recoded as "1") before betweenness was computed.
- DV, creativity: team leaders rated individual members' creative performance in all three waves using three items from Baer (2012); Cronbach's alpha values for Times 1-3 were 0.76, 0.81, and 0.87 respectively, and ICC1 values were .47, .60, and .50 for Times 1-3 respectively. Advice recipients' creativity was computed as the advice-tie-weighted average of recipients' leader-rated creativity.
- Change scores: latent change score modeling (McArdle, 2009), with recipients' creativity change derived from Time 1 and Time 2 ratings and advice givers' creativity change derived from Time 1 and Time 3 ratings.
- Moderator, advice recipients' perceived psychological safety: seven items from Edmondson (1999) rated at Time 1 (alpha = 0.76), then averaged across the individuals who received advice from a focal giver as an additive composition model (rwg = .86, ICC(1) = .10, ICC(2) = .30).
- Controls: gender, age, education, and organizational tenure, plus network density, knowledge acquisition (four items from Tortoriello et al. 2012, alpha = 0.79), team coordination (five items from Hoegl et al. 2004, alpha = 0.78), and intrateam trust (five items from De Jong & Elfring 2010, alpha = .92).
- Estimation: latent change scores entered into Mplus 7.0 regression models, structured for the nonindependence associated with team membership and with recipients being nested under specific givers via a sandwich estimator, plus a moderated path analysis (Edwards & Lambert, 2007) for the conditional indirect effect. The design is an observational three-wave survey; the paper names no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy, and its stated basis for temporal ordering is that recipients' creativity change came from waves 1 and 2 while givers' came from waves 1 and 3.

Study 2 interviewed 92 employees working in 21 teams from eight manufacturing firms in China (average age 29.49 years, average organizational tenure 5.75 years, 17.39% of participants female). Team leaders nominated individuals matching the authors' conceptualization of advice-giving brokers and common recipients of advice, resulting in 51 advice recipients and 41 advice givers, who were not matched to one another. Two study authors conducted face-to-face interviews in on-site conference rooms; the audio-recorded transcripts featured 170,026 decipherable words (recipients = 92,229, givers = 77,797), and interviews with recipients and givers lasted about 30 minutes and 60 minutes respectively. Two authors then inductively created conceptual categories through thematic analysis, working back and forth between transcripts to reach theoretical saturation and retaining themes that surfaced at least three times; two independent raters categorized all interview quotes into the thematic classification (Cohen's kappa = 0.83), with discrepancies resolved through discussion.

**Key Findings**
Study 1, the quantitative component, supported all five hypotheses. A latent mean structure analysis first showed positive creativity change for advice recipients from Time 1 to Time 2 (change in M = .19, SD = .07, p < .01) and for givers from Time 1 to Time 3 (change in M = .23, SD = .08, p < .01).
- H1 supported: a giver's advice brokerage was positively and significantly associated with recipients' creativity change (b = .02, p < .01, Model 1).
- H2 supported: recipients' creativity improvement significantly and positively predicted the giver's creativity improvement (b = .15, p < .05, Model 3).
- H3 supported: the indirect effect of a giver's brokerage on his or her own creativity improvement via recipients' improvement was significant and positive (b = .003, p < .05).
- H4 supported: the interaction between a giver's brokerage and recipients' psychological safety on recipients' creativity improvement was significant (b = .02, p < .001, Model 2), and the brokerage-creativity relationship was more strongly positive in psychologically safer conditions.
- H5 supported: the indirect effect was significant at higher recipient psychological safety (PMX x PYM(high) = .004, p < .05) but insignificant at lower levels (PMX x PYM(low) = .002, n.s.), and the difference between low and high was significant (.002, p < .05).

Supplementary and robustness analyses: results were significant and in the expected direction when control variables were excluded; when advice centrality was entered alongside brokerage, brokerage still positively predicted recipients' creativity improvement (b = .02, p < .01) while centrality was negative and nonsignificant (b = -.02, p = .75); no significant interactive effect between centrality and brokerage emerged; and team-level psychological safety did not moderate the relationship (b = -0.061, n.s.). Beyond the hypothesized distal path, givers' brokerage also positively predicted their own proximal (Time 1 to Time 2) creativity improvement (b = .02, p < .05), and an integrated model found that brokerage concurrently generates significant proximal (T1-T2) and distal (T1-T3) effects on the giver's own creativity improvement, with a 95% Monte Carlo confidence interval for the indirect effect via recipients' creativity enhancement of [.0002, .007] after the proximal effect was included. The authors also report a counterintuitive negative correlation between recipients' psychological safety and their creativity in this sample.

Study 2 did not retest the hypotheses; it was designed to elucidate the processes emerging from brokers' advice giving. Of the nominated participants, 93% of givers and 98% of receivers agreed they had been correctly identified. Two themes emerged for how received advice benefited recipients' creativity: exposure to new ideas and perspectives (21 mentions, 41% of recipients) and enhancing domain-specific knowledge (14 mentions, 28% of recipients); on boundary conditions, 40 mentions (78% of recipients) concerned the importance of an environment where they felt comfortable freely expressing ideas without criticism. Brokers' motives for giving advice were team interdependence (22 mentions, 54% of givers), prosocial motivation (18 mentions, 44% of givers), concern for the organization (17 mentions, 42% of givers), and self-improvement (10 mentions, 24% of givers). Six main themes explained how giving advice affected brokers' own creativity: idea integration (17 mentions, 42% of givers), perspective taking (26 mentions, 63% of givers), knowledge accumulation (9 mentions, 22% of givers), reflection on existing knowledge (16 mentions, 39% of givers), collective creativity (13 mentions, 32% of givers), and creative feedback (4 mentions, 10% of givers). Givers also emphasized that their advice enhanced recipients' experience and tacit knowledge (54%) and provided recipients with new perspectives (42%), which the authors describe as notable convergence with recipients' accounts.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to social views of creativity by showing that advice-giving is not uniformly helpful; its creative value depends on the giver's brokerage position in the team advice network. It extends brokerage theory into within-team networks by showing that brokers can act as catalysts for others' creativity rather than hoarding information for private advantage. It also connects network structure with psychological safety and dynamic creativity change, showing that creativity in teams can be socially perpetuated across givers and recipients over time.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that managers should encourage employees to provide novel and helpful advice across relatively separate camps or subgroups within teams. It also implies that managers can strengthen creativity by creating psychologically safe environments, publicly supporting creative behavior, discouraging ridicule of failed attempts, and making members' areas of expertise more visible so that cross-subgroup advice exchange becomes easier.

**Limitations**
The authors state that Study 1 could not capture the specific underlying processes linking brokered advice to creativity, which motivated the qualitative follow-up. They also note that the indirect effect through recipients' creativity was relatively small and that other mediators, such as creative feedback and knowledge reflection, may explain why giving advice enhances givers' creativity. Additional limitations are that Study 2 focused only on brokers rather than comparing brokers and nonbrokers, several qualitative processes overlapped, psychological safety had a counterintuitive negative correlation with recipient creativity, and both studies were conducted in China despite some theory being developed in Western contexts.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on other mediators of the advice brokerage-creativity relationship, especially creative feedback and knowledge reflection. They also encourage broker versus nonbroker comparisons, replication and extension of the qualitative themes, and work on when psychological safety becomes an asset or liability for creativity. Because the empirical setting was China, they further call for validation in other cultures.
