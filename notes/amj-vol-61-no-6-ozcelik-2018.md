# No Employee an Island: Workplace Loneliness and Job Performance

**Abstract**
This research investigates the link between workplace loneliness and job performance. Integrating the regulatory loop model of loneliness and the affect theory of social exchange, we develop a model of workplace loneliness. We focus on the central role of affiliation in explaining the loneliness–performance relationship, predicting that despite lonelier employees’ desire to connect with others, being lonelier is associated with lower job performance because of a lack of affiliation at work. Through a time-lagged field study of 672 employees and their 114 supervisors in two organizations, we find support that greater workplace loneliness is related to lower job performance; the mediators of this relationship are lonelier employees’ lower approachability and lesser affective commitment to their organizations. We also examine the moderating roles of the emotional cultures of companionate love and anger, as well as of the loneliness of other coworkers in the work group. Features of this affective affiliative context moderate some of the relationships between loneliness and the mediating variables; we also find support for the full moderated mediation model. This study highlights the importance of recognizing the pernicious power of workplace loneliness over both lonelier employees and their organizations. We offer implications for future research and practice.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how workplace loneliness relates to employee job performance and why that relationship occurs through affiliation at work. It also asks whether the affective affiliative context of the work group, including emotional cultures of companionate love and anger and coworker loneliness, moderates loneliness' effects on affiliative outcomes.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: Employees who experience higher levels of workplace loneliness will be less affiliatively approachable toward their coworkers (employee approachability).
H2: Employees who experience higher levels of workplace loneliness will be less affectively committed to their organization.
H3: Employees who are lonelier at work will have poorer job performance, as partially mediated by their lower approachability towards their coworkers.
H4: Employees who experience higher levels of workplace loneliness will have poorer job performance, as partially mediated by their lower levels of affective commitment to the organization.
H5: The emotional culture of companionate love in an employee's work group will moderate the negative relationship between employee workplace loneliness and (a) employee approachability and (b) employee affective commitment to the organization; specifically, a stronger emotional culture of companionate love will lessen the negative relationship between workplace loneliness and these affiliative outcomes, whereas a weaker culture of companionate love will increase it.
H6: The emotional culture of anger in an employee's work group will moderate the negative relationship between employee workplace loneliness and (a) employee approachability and (b) employee affective commitment to the organization; specifically, a stronger emotional culture of anger will increase the negative relationship between workplace loneliness and these affiliative outcomes, whereas a weaker culture of anger will reduce it.
H7: The workplace loneliness level of the other coworkers in an employee's work group will moderate the negative relationship between employee workplace loneliness and (a) employee approachability and (b) employee affective commitment to the organization; specifically, higher levels of coworker loneliness will increase the negative relationship between workplace loneliness and these affiliative outcomes, and lower levels of coworker loneliness will lessen it.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employee workplace loneliness.
- DV(s): Supervisor-rated employee job performance.
- Mediators: Coworker-rated employee approachability and employee self-reported affective commitment to the organization.
- Moderators: Work group emotional culture of companionate love, work group emotional culture of anger, and aggregate loneliness of other coworkers in the employee's work group.

The central mechanism is that workplace loneliness weakens affiliation. Drawing on the regulatory loop model of loneliness, the paper argues that lonelier employees become more socially vigilant, defensive, and withdrawn, which makes them appear less approachable to coworkers. Drawing on affect theory of social exchange, it argues that negative social exchanges are attributed beyond immediate coworkers to the organization, reducing affective commitment. Lower approachability and lower affective commitment then relate to poorer supervisor-rated job performance, with the broader affective affiliative context shaping some of the loneliness-affiliation links.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from a multirater, multilevel, time-lagged field study of employees, their coworkers, and their supervisors in two organizations: a for-profit private company (Private Company) and a city government of a West Coast U.S. metropolis of approximately 120,000 people (Public Municipality). At Time 1, participants completed measures of self-reported workplace loneliness, all control variables, and emotional culture; six weeks later at Time 2, supervisors rated employees' performance, employees reported their affective commitment to the organization, and employees rated each of the other members of their work group on approachability, using a work group list the authors provided from organizational records. The study covers 672 employees across 143 work groups and their 114 supervisors — the Public Municipality (477 employees across 99 work groups and their 81 supervisors) and the Private Company (195 employees across 44 work groups and their 33 supervisors). Time 1 response rates were 71% in the Public Municipality and 67% in the Private Company; of the Time 1 respondents, 391 in the Public Municipality and 167 in the Private Company completed the Time 2 survey, a Time 2 response rate of 82% and 86%, respectively.

- IV: Employee workplace loneliness = employee self-report on a version of the 20-item UCLA Loneliness Scale (Russell et al., 1980) adapted to the work setting (e.g., "I feel left out" became "I feel left out in this organization"), rated 1-5; mean 2.23 (SD = 0.64), Cronbach's alpha 0.94.
- DV: Job performance = supervisor ratings, averaging three individual task performance items with Welbourne, Johnson, and Erez's (1998) four-item team member role performance scale; mean 4.08 (SD = 0.70), alpha 0.93.
- Mediator: Employee approachability = coworker ratings on a four-item scale adapted from Wiemann's (1977) affiliation measure, averaged across the ratings each employee received from their other work group members; mean 3.77 (SD = 0.43), alpha .83.
- Mediator: Affective commitment to the organization = employee self-report on Allen and Meyer's (1990) six-item scale; mean 3.50 (SD = 0.75), alpha .81. A confirmatory factor analysis supported affective commitment and workplace loneliness as two distinct constructs.
- Moderators (aggregated to the work group): emotional culture of companionate love, four items — affection, caring, compassion, and tenderness — mean 2.91 (SD = .56), alpha 0.84; emotional culture of anger, six items, mean 2.82 (SD = .43), alpha 0.88, both rated by employees on scales from Barsade and O'Neill (2014); and coworker loneliness = the aggregation of each employee's coworkers' self-reports of workplace loneliness across the work group, excluding the focal employee, mean 2.23 (SD = .38). rwg, ICC(1), ICC(2), and average deviation indices supported aggregation for each.
- Controls: age, gender, education, organizational tenure, organization type, agreeableness, extraversion, trait negative affectivity, trait positive affectivity, and family-life, romantic-life, and social-life loneliness.

Estimation used mixed-effects models (hierarchical linear modeling) with the work group entered as a level 2 random effect, and multilevel structural equation modeling with a latent variable measurement model; mediation was tested as 1-1-1 multilevel mediation, and indirect effects with nonparametric multilevel bootstrapping using 1,000 bootstrap samples and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals. Interaction terms and noncategorical variables were grand-mean centered, and trait negative affectivity, family-life loneliness, romantic-life loneliness, social-life loneliness, job performance, and workplace loneliness were log-transformed to normalize their distributions. The design is time-lagged and associational; the authors state that they cannot confirm causality. Analytic sample sizes differ by outcome as reported: n = 533 for employee approachability and n = 566 for affective commitment (Tables 2 and 4), n = 477 for supervisor-rated job performance (Table 3), and n = 672 for the correlation table (Table 1).

**Key Findings**
- H1 and H2 supported. Workplace loneliness was significantly negatively related to employee approachability (γ = -.62, p < .01) and significantly negatively predicted employee affective commitment to the organization (γ = -3.76, p < .01).
- H3 and H4 supported as partial mediation. Workplace loneliness was significantly negatively related to supervisor-rated employee performance (γ = -70.38, p < .01). With both mediators entered, employee approachability (γ = 13.95, p < .01) and affective commitment (γ = 7.19, p < .05) were significant and the workplace loneliness coefficient weakened to γ = -39.30 (p < .05). Bootstrapped indirect effects excluded zero in both cases: -5.81 via employee approachability (95% CI [-12.34, -0.48]) and -24.73 via affective commitment (95% CI [-43.49, -8.43]).
- Moderation support was heterogeneous across the two affiliative outcomes. For employee approachability, no interaction with workplace loneliness was significant, so H5a, H6a, and H7a were not supported. For affective commitment to the organization, all three interactions were significant: H5b supported (culture of companionate love × loneliness, γ = 0.80, p < .01), the negative loneliness-commitment relationship being less negative in work groups with stronger cultures of companionate love (γ = -2.34, p < .01) than weaker ones (γ = -4.62, p < .01); H6b supported (culture of anger × loneliness, γ = -1.62, p < .01), the relationship being more negative under stronger anger cultures (γ = -7.18, p < .01) than weaker ones (γ = -2.05, p < .01); and H7b supported (coworker loneliness × loneliness, γ = -1.70, p < .01), the relationship being more negative where coworkers had higher workplace loneliness (γ = -5.51, p < .01) than lower (γ = -2.26, p < .01).
- The full moderated mediation model was supported. The conditional indirect effect of workplace loneliness on performance was more negative in "low affiliation" work groups (-38.5; 95% CI [-67.58, -9.41]; p < .05) than in "high affiliation" work groups (-18.26; 95% CI [-34.29, -2.24]; p < .05), and the difference between the two conditional indirect effects (-20.23) was statistically significant (p < .05).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends loneliness research into the workplace by showing that loneliness is not only an individual psychological state but also a relational workplace phenomenon associated with job performance. It contributes to organizational affect research by examining a discrete affective experience rather than generalized positive or negative affect. It also integrates the regulatory loop model of loneliness with affect theory of social exchange to explain both the psychological withdrawal of lonely employees and the interpersonal/contextual dynamics through which coworkers and organizations become implicated.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that managers should treat workplace loneliness as an organizational problem rather than merely as a private employee issue. Organizations can reduce the performance costs of loneliness by providing timely support, improving opportunities for social connection, and addressing maladaptive social cognitions rather than only bringing lonely employees together. The authors also caution that interventions require sustained effort because loneliness itself can inhibit employees' motivation and skill in reaching out.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the time-lagged design and theory suggest the modeled direction, but the study cannot confirm causality. They note that poorer performance could isolate employees and increase loneliness, and that reciprocal cycles may operate. They also identify limitations from using managerial performance ratings rather than objective performance measures and from studying workplace loneliness only within the United States.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how organizations and employees can alleviate workplace loneliness, how often and how quickly workplace loneliness changes, and which antecedents prevent loneliness from arising. They also suggest examining cognitive attributions, other moderators of approachability, objective performance outcomes such as sales volume or output, and cross-cultural variation in workplace loneliness.
