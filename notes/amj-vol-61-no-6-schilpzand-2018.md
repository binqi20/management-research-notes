# Not Too Tired to be Proactive: Daily Empowering Leadership Spurs Next-morning Employee Proactivity as Moderated by Nightly Sleep Quality

**Abstract**
Building on the Model of Proactive Motivation, we hypothesize that a leader’s daily empowering leadership behaviors function as a motivator for employees to display a greater amount of proactive goal-striving behavior, by means of enhanced daily proactive goal setting. Expanding the Model of Proactive Motivation, we incorporate the largely neglected self-regulatory resource perspective and hypothesize that employee sleep quality operates as a qualifying influence on employees’ ability to set and strive for proactive goals that allow employees to take advantage of empowerment opportunities. Using a time-lagged experience sampling methodology, our hypotheses were tested in a sample of 98 professionals who completed twice-daily surveys over a two-week period. Study results confirm our hypotheses that experiencing daily empowering leadership enhances employees’ next morning proactive behaviors of risk-taking and voice behavior due to more proactive goal setting at the start of the workday. Finally, employee next-day goal-setting and goal-striving behavior is especially enhanced for those employees who experienced higher levels of sleep quality. Theoretical and practical implications derived from our research are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do daily empowering leadership behaviors shape employees' next-morning proactive goal setting and next-day proactive goal-striving behaviors? The paper asks whether nightly sleep quality qualifies employees' ability to convert daily empowerment opportunities into proactive goals, risk-taking, and voice behavior.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. A statistically significant proportion of the overall variation in empowering leadership is due to within-person sources.
Hypothesis 2. Daily empowering leadership is positively related to daily risk-taking behavior (Hypothesis 2a) and daily voice behavior (Hypothesis 2b).
Hypothesis 3. Daily proactive goals mediate the effects of daily empowering leadership behavior on daily risk-taking behavior (Hypothesis 3a) and daily voice behavior (Hypothesis 3b).
Hypothesis 4. Employee nightly sleep quality will moderate the indirect positive effect of daily empowering leadership on daily risk-taking behavior (Hypothesis 4a) and daily voice behavior (Hypothesis 4b) via daily proactive goals, such that the indirect effects will be weakened for employees who report lower (vs. higher) sleep quality in the previous night.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily empowering leadership behavior.
- DV(s): Daily risk-taking behavior and daily voice behavior.
- Mediators: Next-morning proactive goal setting.
- Moderators: Nightly sleep quality, modeled as a first-stage and second-stage moderator of the empowering leadership to proactive goals path and the proactive goals to proactive behavior paths.

The mechanism is a daily carryover process. Empowering leadership creates accountability, ambiguity, and autonomy, which motivate employees to set proactive goals the next morning. Those proactive goals then guide goal-striving behaviors during the workday, expressed as informed risk-taking to improve one's own work and voice behavior to improve work situations. The self-regulatory resource argument is that high-quality sleep restores the resources employees need to set and enact those proactive goals, whereas poor sleep weakens both the goal-setting response to empowerment and the translation of goals into risk-taking and voice.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from a time-lagged experience sampling study of university alumni who had graduated from the College of Business at a large Pacific Northwest university between the years 2001 and 2010, representing a wide range of industries (e.g., banking, healthcare, education, technology) and occupations (e.g., executive assistant, sales associate, accountant, marketing coordinator, web developer). The authors obtained the contact information of 2,086 college of business alumni; 164 consented to participate and provided their work and contact information; 133 (81%) of the participants provided at least one data point; and, consistent with prior within-individual research, only participants who contributed at least three days of data were retained, leaving a final sample of 98 (53% male) employees and 609 data points, with a mean participant age of 34 and an average organizational tenure of 6.28 years. Participants were administered online surveys twice a day over a two-week period in exchange for a $30 Amazon gift card. On Monday at the end of their workday, participants were surveyed about the progress made on their work goals and their leader's empowering behaviors (Day 1); a 6am morning survey (Day 2; average completion time approximately 9:37am) asked about sleep quality the night before and motivation to act proactively during that workday; and a 3pm afternoon survey (Day 2; average completion time approximately 3:56pm) asked about risk-taking and voice behaviors enacted throughout the current workday. The authors state that this allowed for temporal precedence, with the independent variable measured at Time 1, the mediator and first-stage moderator measured at Time 2, and the dependent variables measured at Time 3. Participants responded to all scales using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree, and all items are listed in Table 1. The independent variable, daily empowering leadership (Day 1), used three items adopted from an abbreviated version of the scale developed by Ahearne, Mathieu, and Rapp (2005), measuring the extent to which participants were empowered by their leader with decision-making authority and task autonomy (α = .72). The moderator, nightly sleep quality (Night 1), used the scale developed by Scott and Judge (2006), asking each morning whether participants had trouble falling asleep, had trouble staying asleep (including waking up too early), or woke up several times during the night (α = .82). The mediator, proactive goals, was measured in the morning with a four-item measure adapted from Bateman and Crant (1993) that targeted daily intentions rather than dispositional tendency to be proactive (α = .85). The dependent variables were measured the next day: risk-taking behavior used two items adopted from Dewett's (2006) willingness to take risk scale (α = .91), and voice behavior used Farh, Hacket, and Liang's (2007) two-item voice scale, for which Table 2 reports α = .91. Goal progress on the prior workday (Day 1) was included as a control variable using a two-item scale developed by Brunstein (1993) that asked about goal progress broadly (α = .78). Content validity of the abbreviated measures was checked with additional data from 72 employed adults: the abbreviated empowering leadership scale correlated highly with the full measure (r = .70, p < .01), as did the abbreviated proactive goals (r = .88, p < .01) and risk-taking scales (r = .82, p < .01). A multilevel confirmatory factor analysis supported a six-factor measurement model (χ²[89] = 194.32, p < .01; CFI = .98; RMSEA = .04; SRMSR = .04), which fit better than a five-factor model in which risk-taking and voice loaded on one factor (χ²[94] = 693.04; Δχ²(5) = 498.72; p < .01) and a four-factor model in which empowering leadership, sleep quality, and goal progress loaded on one factor (χ²[98] = 1,391.59; Δχ²(9) = 1,197.27; p < .01). The hypothesized moderated mediation model was tested with multilevel structural equation modeling using Mplus 8, specifying at Level 1 the fixed effect of daily empowering leadership on morning proactive goal setting, the fixed effects of morning proactive goal setting on risk-taking and voice, and fixed effects for the daily empowering leadership × nightly sleep quality and morning proactive goal setting × nightly sleep quality interaction terms, with latent variables modeled using standardized scale scores. The design is a nonexperimental within-individual field study; the authors rely on the temporal spacing of the daily surveys for temporal precedence rather than on an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: the authors report that the significant percentage of within-individual variance in empowering leadership scores provides support for Hypothesis 1, and Table 2 reports that 48% of the total variance in empowering leadership was within-individual (σ² = .34; τ00 = .38), with intraclass correlation coefficients across the observed variables ranging from .38 to .52. Hypothesis 2a was not supported: daily empowering leadership did not have a significant, direct effect on daily risk-taking behavior (γ = .09, p = .08). Hypothesis 2b was supported: daily empowering leadership did have a positive and significant effect on voice behavior (γ = .11, p < .01). Daily empowering leadership was also positively related to proactive goals (γ = .28, p < .01), which in turn predicted risk-taking behavior (γ = .30, p < .01) and voice behavior (γ = .33, p < .01). Hypotheses 3a and 3b were supported: the indirect effects of daily empowering leadership through employee proactive goals were significant for risk-taking behavior (indirect effect = .08, p < .01) and voice behavior (indirect effect = .09, p < .01). Hypothesis 4 was supported: nightly sleep quality significantly influenced the relationship between leaders' daily empowering behaviors and proactive goals the next morning (γ = .10, p < .05) and modified the effects of proactive goals on risk-taking (γ = .10, p < .05) and on employee voice (γ = .13, p < .01). Simple slope analyses showed that the relationship between empowering leadership and daily proactive goals was stronger when sleep quality was high (+1 SD: .38, p < .01) than when it was low (−1 SD: .19, p < .01), and that setting proactive goals was more strongly related to proactive goal-striving behavior when sleep quality was high (risk-taking behavior: .40, p < .01; voice behavior: .47, p < .01) than low (risk-taking behavior: .21, p < .01; voice behavior: .20). Consistent with Hypothesis 4a, the positive daily effect of empowering leadership on risk-taking behavior indirectly through proactive goals was significantly stronger (difference = .11, p < .01) when sleep quality was high (indirect effect = .15, p < .01) compared to when sleep quality was low (indirect effect = .04, p < .05); consistent with Hypothesis 4b, empowering leadership had a substantially stronger effect on employee voice behavior (difference = .14, p < .01) when sleep quality was high (indirect effect = .18, p < .01) relative to when sleep quality was low (indirect effect = .04, p < .05). Table 3 reports the corresponding standardized coefficients as .29 (p < .01) for empowering leadership on proactive goals, .06 for empowering leadership on risk-taking, .08 (p ≤ .10) for empowering leadership on voice, .30 (p < .01) for proactive goals on risk-taking, and .34 (p < .01) for proactive goals on voice, with R² of .22 (p < .01) for proactive goals, .13 (p < .01) for risk-taking, and .19 (p < .01) for voice. In the same table the control variable goal progress was positively related to proactive goals (.31, p < .01) and voice (.11, p < .05) but not to risk-taking (.06), and the main effect of sleep quality was nonsignificant in all three equations (−.02, −.00, and .05).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to workplace proactivity theory by showing that proactivity is not only a stable individual difference but also a within-person, goal-driven process that fluctuates across days. It extends Parker et al.'s Model of Proactive Motivation by integrating a self-regulatory resource perspective and identifying sleep quality as a daily resource condition that strengthens or weakens proactive goal setting and goal striving.

The paper also contributes to empowering leadership research by treating empowering leadership as a daily behavior rather than only as a stable leadership style. This dynamic view explains why the same employee may respond differently to empowerment opportunities across days depending on nightly recovery.

**Practical Implication**
Managers can promote daily risk-taking and voice by sharing power and creating daily empowerment opportunities, but those opportunities are more likely to translate into proactivity when employees are well rested. The authors suggest assigning well-rested employees to novel and challenging work requiring initiative, while assigning employees with poorer sleep to more routine tasks that are less vulnerable to sleep-related cognitive deficits.

The paper also implies that organizations should treat sleep quality as relevant to proactive performance rather than as a purely private issue. The authors suggest that allowing naps may help employees capitalize on empowerment opportunities.

**Limitations**
All study variables were self-reported by participants, so common method concerns remain despite temporal separation of morning and afternoon surveys, interaction tests, and structural equation modeling. The study also measured sleep subjectively rather than with objective sleep measures such as actigraphy.

The authors note that the study did not examine possible negative consequences of employee proactivity, such as proactive goals being viewed as self-serving or ineffective by others. They also did not include a lengthy list of daily or stable control variables, including chronic sleep deprivation.

**Future Research**
Future research could employ nonself-report methods for reporting the study variables, such as obtaining supervisor ratings for the daily dependent variables of employee risk-taking and voice behavior. It could also study the possible costs of proactivity to provide a more balanced account of employee proactive behavior at work.

The paper encourages future studies to incorporate theoretically relevant controls such as chronic sleep deprivation. It also points to nutrition as another resource-restorative influence that could be integrated into models of proactive and overall employee performance.
