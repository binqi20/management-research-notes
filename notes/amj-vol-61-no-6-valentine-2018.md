# When Equity Seems Unfair: The Role of Justice Enforceability in Temporary Team Coordination

**Abstract**
Temporary teams can accomplish tightly coupled complex work even without the shared experience that enables coordination in longstanding teams. To advance understanding of this process, I conducted an inductive study of temporary teams in four hospital emergency departments (EDs), and found that the teams in two EDs coordinated effectively, but the teams in the other two EDs did not. To theorize an explanation, I draw on the organizational justice literature and introduce the idea of justice enforceability, defined as the perception that authorities can act fairly, given the potential for other people to cheat. The team members' perceptions of justice enforceability were focused on whether the distribution of work within and between teams was fair or could be cheated. When team members perceived that justice was enforceable, they were willing to engage in the extrarole behaviors that wove together their individual responsibilities. But when they perceived that cheating was possible, they avoided the extra-role behaviors that would have made them suckers for working hard while others cheated. Justice enforceability thus resolves a common tension in temporary teams, namely that the potential for uneven effort can undermine team coordination. In these ED teams, members only trusted that authorities could act fairly when the distribution of work was exactly equal; then team members felt they were all "in it together," and each put in the work to coordinate as a team.

**Research Question**
The paper asks under what conditions members of temporary groups engage in the extra-role behaviors that are core to effective group coordination. It examines why temporary teams with similar pod structures in emergency departments varied in whether they developed tightly coupled coordination.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key context: Temporary ED teams had shared role structures and tightly interdependent patient-care work, but team membership was fluid and short-lived.
- Assignment processes: Alpine and Belleview used exactly equal round-robin patient assignment, while Carter and Dyer used Charge Nurse judgments of pod capacity.
- Justice enforceability: Equal assignment made cheating difficult and supported perceptions that fair work distribution was enforceable; equitable assignment was more flexible but gameable.
- Team response: High justice enforceability fostered solidarity, updating, monitoring, helping, and synchrony; low justice enforceability fostered self-protection, individual focus, limited helping, and weak team coordination.

The process centers on whether temporary team members believe authorities can enforce fair work distribution when others may cheat. When fairness is enforceable, members see themselves as "in it together" and invest in extra-role behaviors that knit together their interdependent responsibilities. When fairness is unenforceable, members fear becoming suckers for others' slack effort and retreat into individual responsibilities, which weakens team coordination.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive comparative field study of four hospital emergency departments (Alpine, Belleview, Carter, and Dyer), matched on their use of pod structures and on characteristics known to influence doctor–nurse relationships: all four were academic medical centers, urban safety-net hospitals serving high volumes of indigent patients, trauma centers, users of electronic medical records, staffed by unionized nurses, and characterized by extremely fluid staffing. The pods in all four EDs were bounded by counters or walls and had the same role structure (one Attending physician, two to three Resident physicians, and three nurses); Belleview, Carter, and Dyer had three pods and Alpine had four. The study design is described as exploratory, involving collection and analysis of open-ended qualitative data. Fieldwork was conducted at the four EDs over a two-year period. Observations lasted between one and three weeks at the different EDs, where the three weeks were staggered across several months, and were conducted at every pod in each ED at different times of day; observations were used for triangulation, and the main source of data was interviews with the ED staff. Interviews were conducted with nearly 170 staff members across the four EDs; Table 1 reports 30 interviews at Alpine, 43 at Belleview, 45 at Carter, and 54 at Dyer. They typically lasted between 30 and 60 minutes, involved open-ended questions following a semi-structured protocol, were conducted in private rooms in the ED before, during, or after participants' shifts, and were recorded and transcribed; participation was voluntary and was solicited until interviews were not yielding new insight. Table 1 also lists archival materials at each site (floor plans, workflows, staffing plans) and one year of electronic medical record data per site, covering about 100,000 patient cases at Alpine, 70,000 at Belleview, 80,000 at Carter, and 60,000 at Dyer. Staffing data were collected using each ED's electronic medical records, downloaded as a de-identified unique identity (ID) number for the nurses and doctors associated with each case and each pod, and analyzed to assess (1) the total number of doctors and nurses who worked in the ED over a one- to three-year period, (2) the total number of doctors and nurses who worked in each ED during a 24-hour period, and (3) the total number of doctors associated with any one pod during a 24-hour period; these metrics reflect the fluidity of the ED staffing and pod teams. One-year totals were calculated by summing the unique IDs associated with patient cases from 7:00 am on July 1 to 7:00 am the following July 1, and 24-hour averages by summing unique IDs from 7:00 am on one day to 7:00 am the following day and averaging across a year; empty pods were not included in the averages, and the paper notes that the pod-level staff numbers do not sum to the ED-level 24-hour numbers because staff changed pods and helped with patients even when not working an entire shift in a pod. Table 2 reports the average total number of staff occupying a pod over a 24-hour period as 25 at Alpine, 30 at Belleview (estimated based on Attending, pod not recorded), 46 at Carter, and 32 at Dyer. Analysis followed an inductive theory development process: memos describing a within-hospital analysis were written for each field site; field notes and interview transcripts were read several times, resulting in records of high-level themes; using those memos as a broad framework, the author conducted line-by-line analysis of the interview transcripts, with open coding starting from a thought unit and the question "what is this an example of?" This first pass produced codes related to the design of the pods, including the composition of roles, the way that patients were assigned to the pods, and behaviors such as updating, asking questions, avoiding work, and working around those avoiding work; the codes were intended to be exhaustive. A final round of coding consisted of analyzing every piece of data related to the assignment process, focused on understanding the theoretical salience of the articulated relationships and patterns. The design is inductive and comparative rather than causally identified.

**Key Findings**
The assignment of patients to pods differed across the matched sites: Alpine and Belleview used an exactly equal "round-robin" process, explained by participants through the analogy of dealing "a deck of cards," which distributed patients to the pods in a repeating order; Carter and Dyer assigned new patients equitably, based on the Charge Nurse's perception of each pod's capacity to handle another patient. Both processes followed an idealized justice rule (equality or equity), but fairness perceptions diverged, and the teams in two EDs coordinated effectively while the teams in the other two EDs did not. At Alpine and Belleview, nurses and doctors perceived the justice of the round-robin assignment to be enforceable because cheating was not possible, though they struggled with the stress of the inevitable and unavoidable work; some staff still questioned particular imbalances (an Alpine nurse on the uneven spread of dialysis patients), but in general the nurses and doctors at both EDs judged the distribution of patients to be fair, and looking at other pods' queues took the form of competition — "friendly competition," "pod races" — rather than monitoring for cheating. At Carter and Dyer, people did not trust that workloads were fair, and cheating was observed and suspected in both EDs, although the dynamics differed somewhat between the two sites. At Carter, people from every role group observed teammates cheating the Charge Nurses' distributions, mostly by keeping patients in the beds longer or by discharging patients without entering updates into the electronic medical record, and one Attending described colleagues deliberately slowing their performance so as not to be assigned more patients. At Dyer, people from every role group also described cheating, but it played out around conflict between the Attendings and nurses: several Attendings said the Charge Nurses assigned patients in ways that "protected" the nurses and believed the Charge Nurses were "in on it," and a Resident described entering false information to upgrade a patient's recorded status in order to avoid getting more patients; Dyer staff therefore perceived low justice enforceability alongside many other injustices that reinforced and exacerbated those perceptions. Staff at both EDs described watching other pods' workloads to be sure they were not being "dumped on," though the Dyer nurses did not describe looking at other pods' queues and instead emphasized the importance of the Charge Nurses' judgment. Willingness to engage in extra-role behaviors depended on these perceptions. With high justice enforceability, Alpine and Belleview team members experienced solidarity and a unity of commitment toward their interdependent responsibilities and engaged in the extra-role behaviors that coordinated their efforts — updating and monitoring, helping (stepping in to do each other's work, such as a Resident starting an IV or an Attending seeing patients or placing an IV when the team was busy), and synchrony described as an "orchestrated symphony" or as "running the team" — along with emotional entrainment in the "mood" of the pod, and informants at these two EDs interpreted the temporary groups in the pods as teams; the paper qualifies that these dynamics were not a given and that some shifts struggled with teamwork. With low justice enforceability, Carter and Dyer team members experienced their interdependence as demoralizing and unfair and became self-protective: they focused on individual responsibilities, showed limited updating and monitoring (where such behaviors were experienced as bothersome or as harassment rather than helpful), gave limited help to teammates while redirecting discretionary effort to the Charge Nurse, and showed little evidence of synchrony, so individual responsibilities were carried out and teamwork happened in one-off episodes but group-level coordination did not emerge. Valentine reads this as a coordination process loss from the documented "sucker effect": hard-working teammates put in more individual initiative — perhaps a teammate version of the "martyr effect" — but avoided the extra-role behaviors that would have compensated for teammates who were slacking. Almost no respondents at Carter categorized the pods as a team, and no one at Dyer did. Because the formally defined role structure was the same in all four EDs and the in-role responsibilities of the Attendings, Residents, and nurses did not differ between sites, the variation lay in how the same role structure was enacted — with a focus on individual role responsibilities or on collective performance. The resulting process model casts justice enforceability as shaping expectations about how committed everyone is likely to be, with situational solidarity (unity of purpose) as an energizing and motivating state in which group members willingly and actively engage in the extra-role behaviors that advance their shared purpose, invigorated by virtuous cycles of mutual extra-role helping and felt-solidarity.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on temporary group coordination by showing that the same formal role structure can be enacted either as coordinated collective work or as individually bounded role performance. It introduces justice enforceability as an organizational justice construct: perceptions that authorities can act fairly given the possibility that others may cheat decisions or procedures. This extends justice theory beyond whether authorities follow justice rules to whether fair treatment can be protected in high-turnover, gameable settings.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations using temporary teams should attend to whether work assignment systems make fair contribution enforceable, not only whether leaders intend to allocate work equitably. The ED comparison suggests that team members may accept stressful equal assignment when it provides visible assurance that others cannot cheat workload distribution. Leaders should therefore examine whether accountability and assignment safeguards give temporary team members reason to trust that extra-role effort will not simply let others free ride.

**Limitations**
The study is based on four hospital emergency departments matched on several characteristics and centered on pod-based doctor-nurse teams, so the findings may depend on contexts where work is countable and roles are relatively clear. The paper notes that the ED setting is distinctive because patients can be counted and physician and nurse roles are clearly defined. It does not claim that exactly equal assignment is universally preferable; it shows a tradeoff between stressful enforceability and flexible but gameable equity.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on how temporary teams interface with larger systems, including boundary-spanning relationships beyond the immediate team. It also suggests studying change and learning in temporary teams, where constant membership change may alter expectations about "the way it's done here." Future work should examine justice enforceability when tasks are less divisible, roles are more ambiguous, or the dynamics of justice and coordination differ from emergency departments.
