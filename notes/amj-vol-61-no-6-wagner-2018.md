# Knowing Me, Knowing You: Inventor Mobility and the Formation of Technology-oriented Alliances

**Abstract**
We link the hiring of research and development scientists from industry competitors to the subsequent formation of collaborative agreements, namely technology-oriented alliances. By transferring technological knowledge as well as cognitive elements to the hiring firm, mobile inventors foster the alignment of decision frames applied by potential alliance partners in the process of alliance formation, thereby making collaboration more likely. Using data on inventor mobility and alliance formation among 42 global pharmaceutical firms over 16 years, we show that inventor mobility is positively associated with the likelihood of alliance formation in periods following inventor movements. This relationship becomes more pronounced if mobile employees bring additional knowledge about their prior firm’s technological capabilities and for alliances aimed at technology development rather than for agreements related to technology transfer. It is weakened, however, if a firm is already familiar with the competitor’s technological capabilities. By revealing these relationships, our study contributes to research on alliance formation, employee mobility, and organizational frames.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether hiring R&D scientists from a competitor increases the likelihood that the hiring firm later forms a technology-oriented alliance with that competitor. It further asks when this mobility-alliance relationship is strengthened or weakened by inventor firm-specific knowledge, the hiring firm's familiarity with the competitor's technology, and the distinction between technology-development and technology-transfer alliances.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. The likelihood of alliance formation between a firm and a competitor is positively associated with the number of inventors moving from that competitor to the firm in preceding periods.
H2. The positive relationship between inventor mobility and the likelihood of subsequent alliance formation between a firm and a competitor will be more pronounced for higher amounts of firm-specific knowledge of the mobile inventors.
H3. The positive relationship between inventor mobility and the likelihood of subsequent alliance formation between a firm and a competitor will be less pronounced for higher levels of the firm's familiarity with that competitor's technological capabilities.
H4. The positive relationship between inventor mobility and the likelihood of subsequent alliance formation between a firm and a competitor will be more pronounced for technology-development alliances than for technology-transfer alliances.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Number of inventors moving from competitor j to focal firm i during the preceding five-year window.
- DV(s): Formation of technology-oriented alliances between the firm dyad, separated into joint R&D alliances, pooled technology-development alliances, and technology-transfer/licensing agreements.
- Mediators: The paper theorizes reduced informational uncertainty, transfer of technological and cognitive knowledge, hybrid decision frames, and interfirm frame alignment, but it does not estimate these as formal mediators.
- Moderators: Mobile inventors' firm-specific knowledge, the hiring firm's technological familiarity with the competitor, and alliance type.
- Controls / identification checks: Prior alliances, technological convergence, technological distance, geographical distance, firm patents, patent citations, R&D intensity, employees, year dummies, three-way clustered standard errors, lagged mobility windows, and instrumental-variable probit regressions.

The mechanism is cross-level: mobile inventors carry technological knowledge and cognitive elements from a prior employer into the hiring firm, helping the hiring firm understand the competitor and align decision frames during alliance formation. This makes collaboration more likely for technology-development alliances, where uncertainty, joint coordination, and interpretive alignment are more important than in technology-transfer agreements. The effect is stronger when inventors know more about the prior firm and weaker when the hiring firm is already technologically familiar with the potential partner.

**Data & Measures**
Archival dyad-year panel on large global pharmaceutical firms. The sample frame is the 20 firms of the Scripp's 1985 League Table plus all 1985 members of the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturing Association (PhRMA), with a few firms added that later merged with any of these, yielding a global sample of 42 firms active in pharmaceuticals in 1985 that are tracked until 2005. All possible dyads are formed for the 16-year period between 1990 and 2005; because several variables are measured over a five-year window prior to the focal year (t-5 to t-1), the firms are observed from 1985 while the analysis uses the period from 1990 to 2005. Dyads are excluded for a five-year period surrounding any within-sample acquisition event, and the number of firms falls over time due to M&A activity, so the dataset contains 11,502 dyad-year observations. Alliance details come from the SDC Platinum database, patent data from the EPO PatStat database (matched by assignee name to a firm and all its subsidiaries), and balance-sheet information from Mergent WebReports.

- DV: Annual 0/1 dummies for alliance formation in a dyad, coded 1 if the firms in a dyad formed an alliance in that year and 0 otherwise, reported separately for three alliance types — joint R&D only alliances, all technology-development alliances (joint R&D pooled with technology-sharing agreements), and technology-transfer (licensing) alliances. The paper notes that SDC Platinum's classification of R&D and technology-sharing alliances is not mutually exclusive and that several alliances are classified as both.
- IV: Mobility, a dyad-year count variable capturing the number of inventors moving from competitor j to the focal firm i during the period t-5 to t-1, identified by observing inventor names on both firms' patents and combining the methodology of Hoisl (2007) and Corredoira and Rosenkopf (2010). Each mobility event was manually verified against externally available information such as LinkedIn profiles, publication records, and personal webpages; false positives (for example, academics and contract-research-organization employees listed on several firms' patents) were excluded. The natural logarithm of the count enters the multivariate analyses.
- Moderator: Mobile inventors' firm-specific knowledge, measured as the number of unique co-inventors on the patents an inventor filed before the move (equivalent to the inventor's degree centrality in the within-firm co-invention network). For each dyad, the average across all inventors moving from that competitor in the past five years is taken and log-transformed, then interacted with mobility to test Hypothesis 2.
- Moderator: Firm i's familiarity with competitor j's technological capabilities, computed as the number of unique citations that patents filed by i and j in t-5 to t-1 have in common, normalized by the total number of unique cites contained in i's patent filings in t-5 to t-1. This is an indirect measure that does not rely on citations made by the firm to the competitor's patents. It is interacted with mobility to test Hypothesis 3.
- Moderator (alliance type): Hypothesis 4 is tested by estimating and comparing separate models for technology-development and technology-transfer alliances rather than through an interaction term.
- Controls: prior alliances (a count that increases by one for each year in which any technology-oriented alliance is observed in the dyad); technological distance (cosine similarity of the two firms' patent shares across IPC main groups for filings in t-5 to t-1, ranging from 0 for lowest distance to 1 for maximum distance); technological convergence (the difference between the two firms' technological distance computed on patent applications filed in t-10 to t-6 and the distance based on applications filed in t-5 to t-1); geographical distance (the log-transformed distance between the headquarters of firm i and competitor j); and, for both the focal firm and the competitor, patent applications in a given year, citations received by the patent portfolio, R&D intensity (R&D expenditures over sales), and number of employees. Year dummies are included in all models.

Estimation uses probit models of the 0/1 dyad-year outcome, with robust standard errors clustered simultaneously on three dimensions — the dyad i-j, the focal firm i, and the competitor j — following Cameron, Gelbach, and Miller (2011). Marginal effects at the mean are computed with the delta method, and because interaction terms are difficult to interpret in nonlinear models the interaction effects are plotted (Figures 1 and 2) rather than reported as marginal effects in the tables. The authors state that exogenous variations such as those exploited in (natural) experiments are difficult to find in this setting, so they are unable to conclusively rule out endogeneity and resort to second-best methods: a comprehensive set of controls, a lag structure that measures mobility in the five years preceding the alliance announcement but not in the year of alliance formation itself, and a two-stage instrumental-variables regression. The excluded instruments are the total number of inventors in the sample moving between companies in t-5 to t-1 minus the mobile inventors of the focal dyad, and the square of the mobility measure; the interaction terms are instrumented by multiplying these instruments with the familiarity and firm-specific-knowledge measures. The technology-development models are estimated on 11,142 observations and the licensing models on 10,128; the paper reports that the number of observations differs across alliance outcomes because in years where no such type of alliance is formed the year dummy perfectly predicts the outcome and all those observations are excluded from the regression, which reduces the count relative to the 11,502 observed dyad-years.

The qualitative component consists of 15 exploratory interviews with R&D managers and scientists in the biopharmaceutical field, which the paper describes as covering pharmaceuticals, medical devices, and biotech; Table B1 lists respondents from mid-sized and large American and European pharmaceutical firms, medical device firms, a contract research organization, a pharmaceutical start-up, and a biotech firm. The interviews were conducted prior to and during the data collection for the study, with a few follow-up interviews conducted during the review process that were semi-structured and lasted from 45 minutes to almost two hours. Topics covered the firm's R&D process, the origin of scientists' knowledge, the functioning of alliances, mobility of employees, and the role of connections, varying by respondent role. Interviews were transcribed, but the paper states that no formal analysis of these transcripts was performed because the study is primarily based on econometric analysis of dyad-level characteristics.

**Key Findings**
- Descriptively (Table 1), across all 11,502 dyad-years technology-development alliances form in 2.1% of dyad-years, pure R&D alliances in 1.5%, and technology-transfer alliances in 1.1%. Splitting dyad-years at the median of mobility, the above-median group (3,228 dyad-years, mean of 2.550 inventors hired from the alter firm) shows higher technology-development alliance rates than the below-median group (8,274 dyad-years, mean of 0): 0.024 versus 0.012 for R&D-only alliances and 0.028 versus 0.018 for all development alliances, both differences significantly different from 0 at the 5% level. Technology-transfer alliance rates are 0.011 in both groups. The authors read this as some descriptive support for Hypothesis 4.
- H1 supported for joint R&D alliances. Looking at joint R&D alliances exclusively, inventor mobility has a positive and significant relationship with alliance formation (coefficient 0.1426 in the baseline specification and 0.2416 once the interaction terms are added; Table 2, columns 1-2), which the paper reports as support for its key hypothesis.
- For all technology-development agreements pooled (joint R&D combined with knowledge-sharing alliances), the mobility coefficients are smaller (0.0444 and 0.1318; Table 2, columns 3-4). The authors describe the only notable difference from the joint R&D models as a loss in precision for the effect of mobility, which they say is captured in a more pronounced interaction between mobility and inventor firm-specific knowledge, and they read the pooled models as again supporting Hypotheses 1-3.
- H2 supported for development alliances. The plotted marginal effects at the mean of mobility rise across the range of inventor firm-specific knowledge, confirming a positive interaction: the knowledge stock of mobile inventors increases the positive association between inventor mobility and alliance formation. The Mobility x Firm-specific knowledge coefficients are positive in both development specifications (0.0086 for joint R&D only and 0.0175 for all development alliances; Table 2, columns 2 and 4).
- H3 supported for development alliances. The Mobility x Familiarity interaction is negative in both development specifications (-0.2653 for joint R&D only and -0.2935 for all development alliances; Table 2, columns 2 and 4), and the plotted marginal effects show a clearly negative slope: increasing familiarity is associated with a weaker relation between mobility and alliance formation.
- Familiarity has a positive main effect on alliance formation that the paper describes as strongly significant for R&D alliances and in line with existing literature; the coefficient is positive and starred in all six probit columns of Table 2, ranging from 0.2021 to 0.4074, including the licensing columns.
- Inventor mobility is not related to the formation of technology-transfer alliances (coefficients 0.0589 and 0.1931; Table 2, columns 5-6), and the authors state that none of their key variables has a significant association with the likelihood of transfer agreements. They add that technology-transfer alliances seem to be driven by previous alliances between the two firms rather than inventor mobility (prior alliances 0.2492 and 0.2459 in those columns).
- H4 only weakly supported. The pattern across alliance types is in line with the prediction that the mobility-alliance relationship is less pronounced for technology-transfer than for technology-development alliances, but a statistical test conducted in a seemingly unrelated regression framework did not reject the null hypothesis that the coefficients are significantly different when comparing technology-development and technology-transfer alliances, so the authors conclude they have only weak evidence in support of Hypothesis 4.
- Robustness. The results from the instrumental-variable regressions differ only marginally from the probit results, and instrumenting inventor mobility does not change the moderation results. The F statistics of instrument strength are clearly above the critical thresholds reported in Stock and Yogo (2005), and Wald tests of exogeneity do not reject the null hypothesis that the error terms of the first- and second-stage regressions are uncorrelated. The authors also report that results are robust to a three-year mobility window (t-3 to t-1).
- Convergence with the qualitative component. The authors state that they cannot observe frame alignment directly, but that the qualitative insights obtained through the interviews confirm its relevance and that the quantitative results are in line with those interview insights. Interviewees described R&D personnel identifying and short-listing potential partners, and mobile inventors acting as mediators who facilitate and speed up alliance formation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to alliance formation research by showing how nonexecutive mobile inventors can shape interfirm collaboration, not only CEOs, board ties, prior alliances, or resource complementarities. It extends employee mobility and learning-by-hiring research by arguing that mobility transfers cognitive elements and decision frames, not only technical knowledge, and that mobility and alliances can be complementary rather than substitutes. It also brings an organizational-frames lens into technology-oriented alliance formation by specifying how frame alignment helps reduce interpretive uncertainty between potential partners.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that managers should involve R&D scientists in identifying and evaluating collaboration partners rather than treating them only as implementation resources after alliances are formed. It also reframes employee mobility as more than a threat of knowledge leakage: mobile inventors may create contact points, reduce uncertainty, and enable future R&D collaboration with competitors. For firms managing mobility restrictions, the findings suggest that limiting inventor movement may also reduce indirect knowledge spillovers through alliances.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study is rooted in a knowledge-intensive pharmaceutical context and relies heavily on patent data, so the conclusions may not generalize to industries where knowledge is more explicit or embedded in organizational routines, objects, or procedures rather than individuals. They also acknowledge that patents are imperfect for identifying employee mobility, despite their manual verification of mobility events. Finally, without a natural experiment, the analysis cannot fully rule out remaining unobserved heterogeneity, and the authors cannot observe which firm initiates alliance negotiations.

**Future Research**
The authors call for future research that identifies exogenous changes in mobility or alliance formation to examine the relationship more causally. They suggest studying whether alliances related to prior inventor mobility are more effective or less likely to fail because organizational frames are bridged by mobile employees. They also propose case studies or ethnographies of frame alignment processes, and research on directional events such as acquisitions or reverse mobility from the focal firm to competitors.
