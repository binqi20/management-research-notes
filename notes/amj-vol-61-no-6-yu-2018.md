# Consequences of Downward Envy: A Model of Self-esteem Threat, Abusive Supervision, and Supervisory Leader Self-improvement

**Abstract**
We invoke theory and research on the social functional view of envy to propose a novel theoretical framework of supervisory leader envy of direct reports. Findings from two multi-source, multi-wave studies of supervisor–subordinate dyads provide support for the theses that (1) downward envy of subordinates threatens supervisors’ self-esteem and triggers adaptive strategies in the form of abusive supervision and supervisory self-improvement; and (2) supervisors are more likely to respond to downward envy induced self-esteem threat with abuse when they perceive envied subordinates to be cold and competent and they are more likely to respond with self-improvement when they perceive envied subordinates to be warm and competent.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how supervisory leaders express and manage envy of direct reports, and whether downward envy leads to either abusive supervision or supervisory leader self-improvement through self-esteem threat. It also asks when supervisors choose each response, focusing on supervisors' perceptions of subordinates' warmth and competence.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1a. Self-esteem threat mediates the positive relationship between downward envy and abusive supervision.
Hypothesis 1b. Self-esteem threat mediates the positive relationship between downward envy and supervisory leader self-improvement behaviors.
Hypothesis 2a. Subordinates' warmth and competence moderate the indirect relationship between downward envy and abusive supervision through self-esteem threat; the indirect relationship will be stronger when subordinates are perceived to be lower in warmth and higher in competence.
Hypothesis 2b. Subordinates' warmth and competence moderate the indirect relationship between downward envy and supervisory self-improvement through self-esteem threat; the indirect relationship will be stronger when subordinates are perceived to be higher in warmth and higher in competence.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Supervisory leaders' downward envy of a direct report.
- DV(s): Abusive supervision and supervisory leader self-improvement.
- Mediators: Self-esteem threat, modeled by Time 2 state self-esteem while controlling for baseline self-esteem.
- Moderators: Supervisors' perceptions of the envied subordinate's warmth and competence.

The mechanism begins with downward envy as a painful feeling of inferiority toward a subordinate who possesses something the supervisor desires but lacks. From the social functional view of envy, this emotion threatens the supervisor's self-esteem and motivates gap-reduction behavior. When the envied subordinate is perceived as cold and competent, self-esteem threat is more likely to translate into abusive supervision as a leveling-down response. When the envied subordinate is perceived as warm and competent, self-esteem threat is more likely to translate into supervisory self-improvement as a leveling-up response.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 drew on a human resources management-consulting company in an Eastern province of China: invitations went to all 281 supervisors plus one randomly selected subordinate each, and after excluding cases with missing data the final sample was 216 independent supervisor-subordinate dyads (response rate 76.9%). Data were collected in four waves over six months — Time 1 demographics and supervisors' baseline self-esteem; Time 2 (one month later) supervisors' downward envy and self-esteem; Time 3 (two months after Time 2) supervisors' ratings of subordinate warmth and competence plus subordinates' ratings of abusive supervision; Time 4 (three months later) self-improvement from an annual organization-wide questionnaire. Study 2 drew on a natural gas service company in an Eastern province of China: invitations went to all 285 supervisors plus one randomly selected subordinate each, yielding a useable sample of 225 independent dyads (response rate 79%), collected in three waves — Time 1 negative affectivity and supervisors' baseline self-esteem; Time 2 (one month later) downward envy toward the referent subordinate and self-esteem; Time 3 (two months later) subordinates' reports of abusive supervision and supervisors' reports of the subordinate's warmth and competence and of their own self-improvement behaviors, with demographics taken from organizational records.

All scales were translated and back-translated from English to Chinese following Brislin's (1980) procedures. Downward envy: Cohen-Charash's (2009) nine-item scale, completed by supervisors with the randomly selected subordinate as referent, five-point agreement format (alpha .81 in Study 1, .84 in Study 2). Self-esteem threat: Heatherton and Polivy's (1991) 20-item State Self-Esteem Scale, operationalized as Time 2 state self-esteem (alpha .87 in Study 1, .82 in Study 2) with Time 1 baseline self-esteem (alpha .85 in Study 1, .83 in Study 2) controlled in every model involving Time 2 self-esteem. Subordinates' warmth (six items) and competence (six items): Fiske et al. (2002) measures rated by supervisors on a five-point scale (warmth alpha .83 in Study 1 and .89 in Study 2; competence alpha .84 in Study 1 and .87 in Study 2). Abusive supervision: Tepper's (2000) 15-item measure completed by subordinates (alpha .91 in Study 1, .94 in Study 2). Self-improvement was operationalized differently in the two studies. Study 1 used four indicators — two annual organization-wide questionnaire items about actively seeking advice/opinions from employees (coded no = 0, yes = 1) and two archival organizational-record indicators (registered for a voluntary advanced-level training program; passed an exam to earn a high-level job certificate) — standardized and averaged within the self-report and archival sets respectively; the self-report and archival indicators correlated .46, and a principal components analysis of the four indicators produced a single factor (eigenvalue 2.31) explaining 63.47% of the item variance (alpha .78). Study 2 instead adapted Major and Kozlowski's (1997) eight-item information/help-seeking measure to supervisory self-improvement, self-reported on a five-point never-to-always scale (alpha .84). Control variables explored in both studies were supervisor-subordinate relationship tenure, supervisor and subordinate sex and age, and trait negative affectivity (10 items from Watson, Clark, and Tellegen's 1988 PANAS); they had no substantive relationships with the model variables and were excluded from the reported analyses. Analysis proceeded from confirmatory factor analysis to hierarchical regression, with bias-corrected confidence intervals from 10,000 bootstrapped samples, RMediation distribution-of-the-product confidence intervals for the indirect effects, and Edwards and Lambert's (2007) procedure for the moderated indirect effects. The design is multi-source and time-lagged rather than experimental, and the paper claims no causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
All four hypotheses were supported, and the pattern held in both studies; the paper describes the two studies as yielding converging evidence.

Study 1 (n = 216): downward envy was negatively related to Time 2 self-esteem (b = -.34, p < .01), and Time 2 self-esteem was negatively related to abusive supervision (b = -.25, p < .01) and to self-improvement (b = -.19, p < .05); the model with baseline self-esteem and downward envy explained 28% of the variance in Time 2 self-esteem, and adding Time 2 self-esteem explained 32% of the variance in both abusive supervision and self-improvement. The indirect effect of downward envy through Time 2 self-esteem was .085 for abusive supervision (95% CI [.047, .130]) and .065 for self-improvement (95% CI [.011, .125]), supporting Hypotheses 1a and 1b. For Hypothesis 2a, the indirect effect on abusive supervision was positive with a confidence interval excluding zero only for low warmth and high competence (.19, 95% CI [.11, .28]); zero fell inside the interval for every other warmth-competence combination, and this indirect effect was stronger than those for low warmth and low competence (difference = .11, p < .05), high warmth and low competence (difference = .24, p < .01), and high warmth and high competence (difference = .22, p < .01). For Hypothesis 2b, the indirect effect on self-improvement was positive with a confidence interval excluding zero only for high warmth and high competence (.16, 95% CI [.08, .26]), stronger than high warmth and low competence (difference = .09, p < .05), low warmth and high competence (difference = .20, p < .01), and low warmth and low competence (difference = .20, p < .01). The moderated models explained 37% of the variance in abusive supervision and 35% in self-improvement.

Study 2 (n = 225) reproduced the pattern: downward envy was negatively related to Time 2 self-esteem (b = -.27, p < .01), and Time 2 self-esteem was negatively related to abusive supervision (b = -.23, p < .01) and to self-improvement (b = -.20, p < .05); indirect effects of downward envy through self-esteem were .06 for abusive supervision (95% CI [.024, .108]) and .05 for self-improvement (95% CI [.015, .106]), again supporting Hypotheses 1a and 1b. Hypothesis 2a was supported: the indirect effect on abusive supervision was positive only for low warmth and high competence (.16, 95% CI [.05, .30]), stronger than low warmth and low competence (difference = .11, p < .05), high warmth and low competence (difference = .20, p < .01), and high warmth and high competence (difference = .18, p < .01). Hypothesis 2b was supported: the indirect effect on self-improvement was positive only for high warmth and high competence (.13, 95% CI [.04, .24]), stronger than high warmth and low competence (difference = .10, p < .05), low warmth and high competence (difference = .19, p < .01), and low warmth and low competence (difference = .16, p < .01). The moderated models explained 36% of the variance in abusive supervision and 34% in self-improvement; the earlier step explained 26% of the variance in Time 2 self-esteem, 33% in abusive supervision, and 30% in self-improvement.

Supplementary analyses in both studies found that subordinates' warmth, competence, and their interaction did not predict downward envy (Study 1: b = -.06, b = .08, b = -.10, all p > .05; Study 2: b = -.07, b = .06, b = -.09, all p > .05) and did not moderate the relationship between downward envy and self-esteem (generalized r-square .55 for both the hypothesized and the alternative model in Study 1 and .50 for both in Study 2; Q = 1, W = 0, d = 6, not significant). Control variables were unrelated to the substantive variables in both studies and were dropped from the reported results.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends leadership theory by showing that supervisory leaders can experience downward envy toward direct reports, despite the formal privileges of leadership roles. It contributes to envy theory by specifying self-esteem threat as the mediator linking downward envy to two different adaptive responses, and by using warmth and competence from social cognition theory to explain why one response becomes abusive while the other becomes developmental. It also reframes abusive supervision as potentially strategic and goal-directed, not only as an impulsive hostile reaction to frustration.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that organizations should treat downward envy as a possible risk factor for abusive supervision, while recognizing that abuse is not inevitable. Training can help supervisors recognize envy and abusive triggers, manage those reactions, and channel envy into formal or informal self-improvement. The authors also suggest that employees should not hide competence, but that pairing competence with warmth may reduce the likelihood that supervisory envy turns into abuse.

**Limitations**
The authors note that they did not test whether abusive supervision or self-improvement actually reduced the perceived gap and self-esteem threat that motivated those behaviors. They also did not examine the precursors of downward envy, how direct reports respond to being envied, or fully rule out common method bias for the relationship between downward envy and self-esteem threat because both were measured from supervisors at the same time.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine whether leveling-down through abusive supervision or leveling-up through self-improvement repairs envy-induced self-esteem threat, and whether abusive responses may worsen rather than repair supervisors' emotional states. The authors also call for work on antecedents of downward envy, including supervisors' relative standing on warmth and competence compared with direct reports. Additional research should study how envied subordinates respond to downward envy and which reactions are more or less effective.
