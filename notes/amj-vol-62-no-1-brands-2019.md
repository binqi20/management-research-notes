# Gender, Brokerage, and Performance: A Construal Approach

**Abstract**
We present a new theory that seeks to explain differences in the performance of men and women friendship network brokers—individuals who bridge disconnected friends. In contrast to previous audience-centered explanations, our phenomenological theory emphasizes how brokers construe (i.e., perceive and interpret) their networks. We contend that when women perceive themselves as brokers in friendship networks they experience threat, rooted in negative stereotypes about women brokers, which undermines their performance. Using data from a cohort of MBA students, Study 1 finds that women (but not men) exhibit lower performance when they perceive themselves as brokers in small-group friendship networks. Using data from a larger group of MBA students, Study 2 replicates this finding and rules out the possibility that underlying differences in the propensity to connect those whom one bridges may explain the observed gender-based difference in broker performance. Using an experimental design, Study 3 finds that elevated anxiety about task performance and negative social evaluations mediate the relationship between brokerage and performance for women but not for men. Women and men differ in how they psychologically construe brokerage in friendship networks, and this difference helps to account for gender differences in the performance of network brokers.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why women and men obtain different performance returns from friendship network brokerage. It specifically examines whether the difference comes from brokers' own construal of brokerage, rather than only from audience reactions to brokers.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: To the extent that women (but not men) perceive themselves as brokers in their surrounding network of friends, they will perform worse.
- H2: Anxiety about task performance and negative social evaluations will mediate the relationship between perceived friendship broker networks and performance for women (but not for men).
- Studies 1 and 2 tested Hypothesis 1; Study 3 tested Hypothesis 2. The paper also states, before testing the hypotheses, that it sought to establish directly the presence of a shared stereotype that women perform worse than men in broker networks, with the supporting methods and results reported in Appendix A.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived friendship network brokerage in Studies 1 and 2; assigned broker versus closed friendship network condition in Study 3.
- DV(s): Individual performance, measured through course exam performance, final paper performance, and expert-rated quality of advice on a workplace problem.
- Mediators: Anxiety about task performance and negative social evaluations in Study 3.
- Moderators: Gender; the predicted negative brokerage-performance process applies to women but not men.

The mechanism is that brokerage in friendship networks carries masculine associations and a negative stereotype about women's broker performance. When women perceive themselves as friendship network brokers, this activates stereotype threat, increasing anxiety about performance and negative social evaluation. That anxiety reduces their ability to capitalize on informational advantages from brokerage, lowering performance relative to comparable men or women in closed networks.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (field): participants were 160 MBA students at a business school (79 men, 31 women) located in England, formally assigned to five-person study groups; the response rate was 69% and the sample consisted of 110 individuals in 20 study groups, average age 29.78 years (SD = 3.66). The survey was administered about two weeks after participants were allocated to their study groups. Friendship networks were captured with Krackhardt's (1987) cognitive social structures approach, each respondent supplying a 5 x 5 matrix of the friendship relations perceived in their group; actual ties were extracted by a row-dominated local aggregation in which a directed tie from i to j counted if i reported considering j a friend. Perceived brokerage was Burt's (1992: 50-54) network constraint computed on the ego-perceived matrix and reverse-scored (1 - constraint) so that higher scores indicate higher brokerage; actual brokerage was the same reverse-scored constraint on the actual matrix and was entered as a control, alongside team gender composition (number of women in the team: 1 or 2). Individual performance was each student's score out of 100 on the course final exam, which was identified by student number and therefore graded blind to gender and network relations; gender was collected from the MBA profiles published by the school. Analysis used hierarchical linear modeling with individual (level 1) and team (level 2) effects, and Table 2 reports N = 110 observations at Level 1 and N = 22 observations at Level 2.
- Study 2 (field): participants were 164 Executive MBA (EMBA), MBA, master's, and exchange students (113 men, 58 women) at a business school in the United Kingdom, enrolled in three classes of the same elective running consecutively with 56, 37, and 71 members, respectively; average age was 33.26 years (SD = 4.92) and the response rate was 94%. Network data used a sampling procedure adapted from Flynn, Reagans, Amanatullah, and Ames (2006): each respondent nominated up to 10 friends from the class and then indicated whom each nominee would consider a friend, producing 164 perceived friendship network matrices; actual ties again used the row-dominated local aggregation method from Study 1. Perceived brokerage was Burt's (1992: 64) measure of network constraint, reverse-scored as in Study 1, and individual performance was each student's score out of 100 on the final paper for the course, as there was no exam for this course. Tertius iungens orientation was measured with the six-item scale from Obstfeld (2005) (alpha = .84). Controls were respondents' actual networks, measured with Burt's (1992) network constraint in the network of confirmed friendships and reverse-scored, plus the degree program each respondent was enrolled in. Analysis used a linear mixed model; the random effects of class and degree program were removed because the variance attributable to them was 0 once fixed effects were added.
- Study 3 (experiment): participants were 232 U.S. and Canadian residents, all currently employed, recruited from Amazon Mechanical Turk, on average 33.96 years old (SD = 9.13) with 14.22 years worked (SD = 9.29), who self-reported their gender (118 men, 114 women). Network structure (broker vs. closed) was manipulated in a between-participants design with random assignment, which the authors describe as allowing them to establish causality: participants took the role of a human resources director of a steel company who had made three close friends, and saw a network diagram with gender-neutral node names in which either all possible ties among the friends existed (closed) or none did (broker), holding the number of ties held by ego constant across conditions. Anxiety was measured with the performance and social dimensions of the state self-esteem scale (Heatherton & Polivy, 1991) on a 7-point Likert scale (overall alpha = .94). Performance was the quality of the advice participants wrote to a manager, rated by two independent human resources experts who were blind to the hypotheses and to each participant's gender and network condition, as a composite of the proposed solution's effectiveness (1 = very ineffective to 7 = very effective) and appropriateness (1 = very inappropriate to 7 = very appropriate); Cronbach's alpha was .81 and the ICC was .68. The first-stage moderated mediation was tested with Hayes's (2013) PROCESS macro (Model 7) using a bootstrap sample of 5,000.
- Stereotype pretest (Appendix A): 97 men and 110 women (108 from the United States and Canada from Amazon Mechanical Turk, and 99 from the United Kingdom from Prolific Academic) were shown a broker network diagram and a closed network diagram with written descriptions, then indicated on a 5-point scale (1 = women will perform much more highly than men to 5 = men will perform much more highly than women) both their own personal beliefs and other people's beliefs about whether men or women would perform better in broker networks.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported Hypothesis 1: the gender x perceived constraint interaction was significant, B = -22.45, p = .007 (Table 2, Model 2). Women who perceived themselves as brokers performed significantly worse than women in closed networks, t = -3.26, p = .002, while men's performance was unaffected by their perception of themselves as brokers, t = -0.76, p = .45. In the robustness check using actual rather than perceived brokerage, the gender x constraint interaction was not significant, B = -2.48, p = .75 (Table 2, Model 3). The authors also report a statistically nonsignificant trend toward men performing better in broker networks.
- Study 2 replicated the Study 1 result: the gender x perceived constraint interaction was significant, B = -9.64, p = .03 (Table 4, Model 2). Women who perceived themselves as brokers performed worse, t = -1.95, p = .05, whereas men's performance was unaffected by their perceptions, t = 0.9, p = .37, and support for Hypothesis 1 held when tertius iungens orientation was controlled, a variable that was not significantly related to gender. Using actual brokerage scores, the gender x constraint interaction was approaching significance, B = -8.71, p = .15 (Table 4, Model 3); with both interaction terms in the model (Table 4, Model 4), the gender x actual constraint term was not a significant predictor of performance, p = .29, while the gender x perceived constraint term was marginally significant, p = .06.
- Study 3 supported Hypothesis 2: the gender x network interaction on anxiety was significant, B = 0.61, p = .03. Women assigned to the broker network condition reported greater anxiety about their task performance and negative social evaluations (M = 2.94, SE = .15) than women assigned to the closed network condition (M = 2.33, SE = .13), B = -0.61, p = .002, whereas men's anxiety was unaffected by whether they were assigned to the broker condition (M = 2.46, SE = .14) or the closed condition (M = 2.45, SE = .14), B = 0.005, p = .98. Higher anxiety in turn predicted worse performance, B = -0.23, p = .005. Based on a bootstrap sample of 5,000, zero fell outside the 95% confidence interval for women [-0.32, -0.04], indicating that anxiety mediated the network-performance relationship for women, and inside the interval for men [-0.10, 0.08], indicating no mediation for men; the index of moderated mediation was B = -0.14 (SE = .08), 95% confidence interval [-0.36, -0.02].
- The Appendix A pretest established the stereotype the theory presumes: respondents did not personally endorse the view that men would perform better than women in broker networks (M = 3.06, SD = .98, t(206) = .85, p = .39), but did tend to believe that this view was widely shared (M = 3.21, SD = .99, t(206) = 3.1, p = .002), with no difference between U.S. and U.K. participants, F(1, 206) = .02, p = .89, and no difference between men and women, F(1, 206) = 1.16, p = .28. The authors note that the mean for the shared stereotype was close to the scale midpoint, so the stereotype is not strong.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a construal approach to brokerage that shifts attention from network structure alone to how individuals perceive and interpret the network positions they occupy. It contributes to research on gender and social networks by showing that women brokers may be disadvantaged not only because others penalize them, but also because gendered stereotypes alter their own experience of brokerage. It also extends network cognition research by showing that social identity and stereotype threat shape how network structures affect performance.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should not assume that encouraging brokerage will generate equal returns for all employees. The authors suggest raising awareness of stereotype threat, explicitly challenging the legitimacy of gendered broker stereotypes, and helping women reappraise threat in brokerage roles as part of career training and professional education.

**Limitations**
The authors identify the business-school and business-context setting as a possible boundary condition because gender stereotypes about competence and leadership are especially salient in business. The studies focus on friendship relations, so the argument may not apply in the same way to instrumental or mandated workflow networks. The paper also notes that ego-centric construal may interact with others' reactions, but it does not directly test that combined process.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether stereotype threat is weaker when brokerage occurs in instrumental rather than friendship networks. Scholars could also test how contextual factors such as gender composition, cultural artifacts, nationality, ethnicity, rank, or role affect the salience of gender in brokerage construal. The authors further call for research on whether prior brokerage success, women in formal authority positions, withdrawal, turnover, and network change alter or follow from network-based stereotype threat.
