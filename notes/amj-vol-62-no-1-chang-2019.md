# Diversity Thresholds: How Social Norms, Visibility, and Scrutiny Relate to Group Composition

**Abstract**
Across a field study and four experiments, we examine how social norms and scrutiny affect decisions about adding members of underrepresented populations (e.g., women, racial minorities) to groups. When groups are scrutinized, we theorize that decision makers strive to match the diversity observed in peer groups due to impression management concerns, thereby conforming to the descriptive social norm. We examine this first in the context of U.S. corporate boards, where firms face pressure to increase gender diversity. Analyses of S&P 1500 boards reveal that significantly more boards include exactly two women (the descriptive social norm) than would be expected by chance. This overrepresentation of two-women boards—a phenomenon we call “twokenism”—is more pronounced among more visible companies, consistent with our theorizing around impression management and scrutiny. Experimental data corroborate these findings and provide support for our theoretical mechanism: decision makers are discontinuously less likely to add a woman to a board once it includes two women (the social norm), and decision makers’ likelihood of adding a woman or minority to a group is influenced by the descriptive social norms and scrutiny faced. Together, these findings provide a new perspective on the persistent underrepresentation of women and minorities in organizations.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how social norms and scrutiny affect decisions about adding members of underrepresented populations to groups. It specifically examines whether decision makers use descriptive social norms as diversity thresholds and whether visibility and scrutiny strengthen this tendency.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a: Groups' diversity levels will cluster at (or just above) the descriptive social norm set by peers for diversity.
- Hypothesis 1b: Groups (and the individuals who shape their composition) will add new members from underrepresented populations at a lower rate once they have surpassed the pertinent descriptive social norm for diversity.
- Hypothesis 2: Scrutiny moderates the effects of descriptive social norms on group diversity decisions; specifically, descriptive social norms will only influence group diversity decisions and outcomes when scrutiny is present on a given diversity dimension.
- Hypothesis 3: Visibility moderates the effects of descriptive social norms on group diversity decisions on scrutinized diversity dimensions; specifically, more visible groups will be more likely to follow the descriptive social norm for diversity on scrutinized diversity dimensions than less visible groups.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Descriptive social norms for diversity, scrutiny of diversity, and group or firm visibility.
- DV(s): Group diversity composition and the likelihood of selecting women or racial minority candidates for open group positions.
- Mediators: Impression management concerns and goal-threshold behavior around matching peer diversity levels.
- Moderators: Scrutiny and visibility.

The proposed mechanism is that decision makers facing scrutiny seek safety in numbers by matching the diversity levels observed in peer groups. Once the descriptive social norm is reached, they relax efforts to add additional underrepresented members, producing clustering at salient thresholds such as two women on a board. Visibility increases the expected scrutiny of group composition, making conformity to descriptive social norms more likely.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1A (archival, S&P 1500 board composition): ISS Director Data compiled by Institutional Shareholder Services, covering the 1,514 companies of the S&P Composite 1500 in 2013 and trimmed to companies in the middle 95% of the board-size distribution (fewer than six or more than 14 directors excluded), leaving 1,441 companies with 13,440 distinct board seats and 11,185 distinct directors. The descriptive social norm is the average number of women per board (1.36 in the S&P 1500 and 1.89 in the S&P 500 in 2013). Expected board-composition distributions come from a Monte Carlo simulation that randomly reassigned the observed directors to the observed board seats 10,000 times. Visibility is proxied by LexisNexis media mentions in 2012 (mean = 307; SD = 441), used to sort companies into deciles of 144 companies each, with 1,000 simulations run within each decile. Supplementary data cover industry (NASDAQ), IPO year (Bloomberg and company websites), market capitalization (Center for Research in Security Prices and Google Finance), and institutional ownership (Bloomberg). Historical board compositions come from the RiskMetrics Directors Legacy dataset (2002 to 2006) and ISS (RiskMetrics) Director Data (2007 to 2012); an OLS regression with robust standard errors clustered at the firm level predicts yearly tokenism and twokenism from whether the descriptive social norm exceeded one woman that year. Placebo simulations repeat the procedure on a non-gender director characteristic.
- Study 1B (archival, board additions): 9,989 board member additions in the S&P 1500 from 2004 to 2013, using ISS Director Data for board compositions from 2007 to 2013 and the RiskMetrics Directors Legacy dataset for 2004 to 2006. DV: whether each newly added board member was female. Predictors: the number of women currently on the board, plus an indicator for whether the board already included at least two women (the discontinuity predictor). Estimated by OLS with robust standard errors clustered by firm, run with and without fixed effects for board size, industry, and stock market index and a continuous control for market capitalization. The visibility test interacts the discontinuity indicator with the centered logarithm of media mentions in year t - 1.
- Study 1C (randomized online experiment): 479 U.S. Amazon Mechanical Turk participants (55% male; 77% Caucasian) paid $0.25, randomly assigned to view a 10-member board containing zero, one, two, or three women, then asked to choose one of three qualified candidates for an open seat (a CEO, a sitting director at another company, and an industry consultant), with the female name (Jill Davis) and male names (Matthew Anderson and Todd Miller) and the pairing of names to qualifications randomized. DV: the fraction of participants choosing the female candidate.
- Study 2A (randomized experiment, MBA students): 556 MBA students representing the entire incoming class at a U.S. business school (57% male; 25% with previous managerial experience; average age 27.7 years), asked to complete a seven-person industry-conference panel whose six predetermined members were two women and four men. Random assignment to one of four conditions crossing social norm with scrutiny: the surpassed-norm condition saw other panel submissions averaging 0.8 women, the unmet-norm condition saw an average of 2.8 women; the unscrutinized condition was told the review process was "blind" so names and photos would not be submitted, while the scrutinized condition saw no such statement. Choice set: a female candidate with 10 years of industry experience and a speaker rating of 4.6 versus a male candidate with 12 years and a rating of 4.8. DV: preference for the female candidate on a 1-to-7 scale.
- Study 2B (incentivized randomized online experiment): 200 U.S. Mechanical Turk participants (51.5% male) paid $0.15, given the same panel task, with the norm simplified to competitive intelligence that other panel submissions would average 1.25 women (surpassed) or 2.75 women (unmet), and the same blind-review scrutiny manipulation. Selection was costly against an initial $0.25 bonus: the female candidate (10 years of experience, speaker rating 4.6) cost $0.15 and the male candidates (11 or 12 years, ratings 4.5 or 4.8) cost $0.10 and $0.11; a reviewer would accept 75% of panels for a bonus payment. DV: the fraction of participants choosing the female candidate.
- Study 3 (incentivized randomized online experiment): 603 U.S. Mechanical Turk participants (52.9% male; 80.4% Caucasian) paid $0.30, hiring a sixth member for a team shown as one black man and four white men, with all conditions told the HR department could review team compositions and punish teams deemed to have inadequate racial diversity. Social norm was manipulated as other teams of their size including an average of 0.25 black people (surpassed) or 1.75 black people (unmet); visibility was manipulated by describing the team as "not very important" with a low probability of HR review (low visibility) or "very important" with a high probability of review (high visibility). Choosing the black candidate carried a $0.03 bonus and the white candidate a $0.10 bonus. DV: the choice of the black candidate.

Identification differs by study. Studies 1A and 1B are archival analyses that the authors describe as ultimately only correlational, with the simulation providing the counterfactual distribution rather than a causal design. Studies 1C, 2A, 2B, and 3 are randomized experiments in which board composition, descriptive social norms, scrutiny, and visibility were randomly assigned.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1A (H1a, H3): Against the simulated expectation, the S&P 1500 had 8% fewer boards with no women (p = .019) and 12% more boards with exactly two women (p = .008), with other frequencies in line with expectations; the S&P 500 had 45% more boards with exactly two female directors (p < .001) and 45% fewer with no female directors (p < .001). Repeating the simulations across 2002 to 2013, the descriptive social norm exceeding one woman was a significant negative predictor of tokenism (b = -0.11; p < .001) and a significant positive predictor of twokenism (b = 0.12; p = .002). Hypothesis 1a is supported. For H3, the logarithm of a decile's media mentions positively and significantly predicted the absolute gap between observed and expected boards with exactly two female directors (b = 6.12, p = .014); the sharper S&P 500 pattern is described only as suggestive evidence for H3. Placebo simulations on a non-gender characteristic produced no significant deviations.
- Study 1B (H1b, H3): The indicator for a board already including at least two women negatively predicted adding a woman in the S&P 1500 (b = -0.039; p = .017 without controls; b = -0.034; p = .037 with controls) and more strongly in the S&P 500 (b = -0.092; p < .001 without controls; b = -0.090; p < .001 with controls), supporting Hypothesis 1b. Supporting H3, the interaction between the centered logarithm of media mentions in year t - 1 and having two or more women was negative and significant (b = -0.021; p = .042 without controls; b = -0.021; p = .041 with controls).
- Study 1C (H1b): Directionally consistent but weaker than the field results. The chi-square test relating the number of women on the board to choosing the female candidate was only marginally significant (chi-square(3, n = 479) = 7.51, p = .057), and the OLS indicator for two or more women was negative and marginally significant (b = -0.19, p = .062). The two-women versus one-woman contrast was significant: 36.0% (SD = 0.48) chose the female candidate when the board already had two women versus 50.4% (SD = 0.50) when it had one (z = 2.26, p = .024).
- Study 2A (H1b, H2): Under scrutiny, preference for the female candidate was significantly stronger in the unmet-norm than in the surpassed-norm condition (t(277) = 2.24; p = .026), and there was no such difference when decisions were unscrutinized (t(275) = 0.22; p = .83). The scrutiny-by-unmet-norm interaction term was positive but did not reach standard levels of statistical significance (p = .14), which is why the authors ran an incentivized follow-up.
- Study 2B (H1b, H2): With incentivized choices, participants under scrutiny were significantly more likely to select the female candidate in the unmet-norm than the surpassed-norm condition (z = 2.94; p = .0033), with no such difference absent scrutiny (z = 0.24; p = .81), and here the interaction term was positive and statistically significant (b = 0.27; p = .028), further supporting Hypothesis 2.
- Study 3 (H1b, H3): Extending the pattern to race, participants were significantly more likely to select the black candidate in the unmet-norm than the surpassed-norm condition (z = 4.28; p < .001); there was a significant main effect of visibility, with high-visibility teams more likely to select the black candidate (z = 9.25; p < .001); and the visibility-by-unmet-norm interaction was positive and significant (b = 0.15; p = .043), supporting Hypothesis 3.
- Field evidence on H2: supplemental simulations of board members' race or ethnicity showed no clustering at the descriptive social norm, which the authors read as consistent with scrutiny being required, noting that 97% of 2013 newspaper articles mentioning board diversity discussed gender diversity while 18% mentioned racial or ethnic diversity.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes a threshold-based account of persistent underrepresentation by showing how descriptive social norms can limit, rather than simply encourage, diversity gains. It links social norm theory, impression management, and goal-setting logic to explain why scrutinized groups may cluster at minimally acceptable levels of representation.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that scrutiny can increase diversity, but only up to the descriptive norm that decision makers perceive as sufficient. Policymakers and advocates may need to shift salient norms toward more ambitious standards, such as population parity or explicitly endorsed ideals, rather than allowing peer averages to define adequacy.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Studies 1A and 1B are correlational and cannot fully rule out reverse causality or omitted confounds such as firm performance. They also acknowledge that the experiments examine individual decisions in simplified settings, while real group-member selection processes are more complex and often involve multiple decision makers.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on how descriptive social norms around diversity shift, including whether interventions can make more ambitious norms salient. It also recommends examining other organizational contexts, group sizes, and psychological mechanisms through which descriptive social norms and scrutiny create diversity-threshold effects.
