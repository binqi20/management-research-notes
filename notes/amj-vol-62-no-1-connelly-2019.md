# Something in Common: Competitive Dissimilarity and Performance of Rivals with Common Shareholders

**Abstract**
Economists have long held that bringing firms under the umbrella of a common ownership structure creates monopolistic conditions that reduce competition. We challenge this view by investigating competition between firms in a nuanced manner. We examine the competitive and performance implications of “common institutional ownership,” which occurs when an institutional investor owns a sizeable number of shares in two publicly traded firms. We argue that rival firms with common ownership structures will engage in dissimilar competitive action repertoires to avoid direct competition with each other. Competing aggressively, but with dissimilar action repertoires, allows rivals to maintain high levels of performance. Competing with dissimilar action repertoires also helps ensure that performance disparity between the two firms remains low. In other words, competitive aggressiveness with dissimilar action repertoires yields an optimal competitive solution for rival firms and their common owners.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how common institutional ownership shapes competition between rival firms and whether rival firms with common owners achieve better joint performance and lower performance disparity when they compete with dissimilar competitive action repertoires.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Common institutional ownership between rival firms in an industry is positively associated with dissimilarity of the firms' competitive action repertoires.
Hypothesis 2. The relationship between competitive aggressiveness and the joint performance of rival firms is moderated by dissimilarity of the rivals' competitive action repertoires. The relationship is negative when dissimilarity is low and positive when dissimilarity is high.
Hypothesis 3. The relationship between competitive aggressiveness and the performance disparity of rival firms is moderated by dissimilarity of the rivals' competitive action repertoires. The relationship is positive when dissimilarity is low and negative when dissimilarity is high.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Common institutional ownership; competitive aggressiveness as the volume of competitive actions in a dyad.
- DV(s): Competitive action repertoire dissimilarity, joint ROA, and disparity ROA.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Dissimilarity of rival firms' competitive action repertoires moderates the relationship between competitive aggressiveness and both joint performance and performance disparity.

The mechanism is that common institutional owners function as shared principals across rival firms. Instead of simply reducing competition, these owners can pressure managers to avoid direct rivalry by competing in different action spheres. When rivals are both competitively aggressive and dissimilar in their action repertoires, they can increase joint performance while keeping performance disparity low.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: firms that appeared at least one time in the S&P 500 during the years 2000 to 2013, which yielded 1,984 firms over the 14 years examined after excluding institutional investors with less than 1% of shareholdings; common institutional ownership was computed from the Thomson Reuters Institutional Holdings database, resulting in 2,727 unique investors; competitive actions came from RavenPack News Analytics. The unit of analysis is the firm-dyad, and the data set was structured as N x (N - 1)/2 x T, where N and T indicated the number of firms and years respectively; with N of 1,984 and T of 14 there were 27,539,904 possible total firm dyads, of which 68,227 were rival (intra-industry) dyads, an average of 1,049 per year, including all intra-industry dyads regardless of their level of common ownership. Rivals are firms that operate within the same primary four-digit Standard Industrial Classification code, following the U.S. Census Bureau classification, and the main analysis uses an industry block design.
- IV: Common institutional ownership = the ratio of common ownership to total ownership between firms i and j, built from the percentage holdings of the common and disjoint institutional owners of the two firms; the measure ranges from 0 (no common institutional ownership) to 1 (complete overlap of institutional ownership between rival firms).
- IV: Competitive aggressiveness (volume) = the sum of competitive actions for both firms in an intra-industry dyad.
- DV / moderator: Competitive dissimilarity = the Euclidean distance between firm i's and firm j's competitive action portfolio vectors, where each vector counts the firm's actions in each of seven behavior classes (new product actions, pricing actions, marketing actions, acquisitions, equity joint ventures, strategic alliances, and market expansion); the measure equals zero if the two portfolios are identical. The 1,984 firms engaged in a total of 163,843 competitive actions during the sampling window, an average of 8.6 actions per firm per year. To check the RavenPack coding, two fifth-year doctoral students in strategic management independently classified a 1% random sample of all competitive actions from the first (2000) and last (2013) years; inter-rater reliability with RavenPack's assigned codes was .93 for the first coder and .89 for the second.
- DVs: Joint performance = JROAijt = ROAit + ROAjt, the summed return on assets of the two rivals; performance disparity = DROAijt = |ROAit - ROAjt|, the absolute difference in their ROAs. The overall correlation between these two variables was small (-0.12).
- Controls, all calculated at the dyadic level by summing the values for the two firms in the dyad: firm size (natural log of the number of employees), board independence, CEO duality (1 if the CEO was chair or co-chair of the board in a given year and 0 otherwise), CEO tenure (years the CEO had held that title in the current firm), CEO contingent compensation (ratio of contingent to total compensation), prior performance (ROA at t - 1), resource dissimilarity (Euclidean distance between the dyad's standardized financial, physical (PP&E), and social (board interlocks) resource profiles), and market position (absolute difference between each firm's sales in the focal industry as a percentage of total industry sales).

Estimation: longitudinal multiple regression quadratic assignment procedure (L-MR-QAP), with adjacency matrices constructed in R and QAP regressions run in STATA. QAP proceeds in two stages: parameters are first estimated as in an ordinary least squares regression treating each dyad as an observation, then the rows and columns of the dependent variable matrix are randomly permuted and the regression re-estimated over many iterations, so that p values are not a function of sample size. To mitigate endogeneity stemming from bi-directionality between common ownership and competitive action, the authors adopt an instrumental two-stage least squares (2SLS) approach with the L-MR-QAP method, using lagged common ownership plus portfolio scope (the sum of the average total number of firms in the common owners' portfolio for each of the two firms in a dyad) in linear and quadratic form as instruments; the instrument F test gave F = 1,251.89, p < .001. An Arellano-Bond test on a dynamic model with three-way latent components was insignificant (z = .30, p = .764), so the authors retain first- and higher-order lags as instruments. All reported models use one-year lagged values of the explanatory variables.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported: in the 2SLS L-MR-QAP model (Model 2 of Table 2) the coefficient estimate of common ownership is positive and statistically significant (b = 16.121, p < .01), so higher common institutional ownership between rivals goes with greater dissimilarity of their competitive action repertoires.
Hypothesis 2 is supported: the Dissimilarity x Volume interaction on joint ROA is positive (b = 6.335, p < .01; Model 3 of Table 3). The moderation plot (Figure 1) shows that the relationship between competitive aggressiveness and joint performance of rival firms is negative when the firms have similar competitive action profiles and is flipped when action repertoire dissimilarity is high, where competitively aggressive rivals both realize gains and joint performance increases; where the number of competitive actions is low, action repertoire dissimilarity is irrelevant.
Hypothesis 3 is supported: the Dissimilarity x Volume interaction on disparity ROA is negative (b = -16.393, p < .05; Model 6 of Table 3). Figure 2 shows that the relationship between competitive aggressiveness and performance disparity between rival firms is positive when the firms have similar action profiles, whereas competing with dissimilar competitive action repertoires allows both rivals to be competitively aggressive and benefit from increased competitive behavior; where competitive activity is low, repertoire dissimilarity is again irrelevant.
Common ownership also has a positive direct effect on joint performance in the performance models.
Post-hoc analysis (Models 3 and 4 of Table 2, with volume of competitive actions as the dependent variable) shows that common institutional ownership is associated with higher levels of competitive aggressiveness between rival firms (b = 37.441, p < .001), which the authors call a highly counterintuitive finding from the perspective of traditional economics. A further post-hoc test of whether the influence of common ownership has an inverted U-shaped relationship with competitive repertoire dissimilarity and with competitive aggressiveness did not find significant results in either case.
Supplementary analyses: after calculating each institutional investor's ownership gap between the two rival firms and removing the upper 5% and the upper 15% of gaps, results from the additional L-MR-QAP models are substantively the same as those reported in the main study; a simultaneous equation model with three-way latent components (for Hypothesis 1) and generalized method of moments estimation (for Hypotheses 2 and 3) together indicate the results are unlikely to be influenced by the bi-directionality problem.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to competitive dynamics by identifying common institutional ownership as an antecedent of competitive action repertoire dissimilarity in rival dyads. It also extends agency theory from a within-firm principal-agent framing to an interfirm governance mechanism in which common principals influence multiple managerial agents across rival firms.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should attend not only to who owns their firm but also to where those owners are invested among rivals. The findings also caution regulators against assuming that common ownership necessarily suppresses competition, because the study finds that common ownership can be associated with more competitive actions when those actions are differentiated across rivals.

**Limitations**
The study measures whether rivals use different action types but does not observe whether different actions are used to pursue the same underlying strategic objective. It also does not distinguish among types of shareholders, shareholder time horizons, or the magnitude and nature of specific competitive moves.

**Future Research**
Future research could unpack finer-grained action types, the pace at which firms move into dissimilar action repertoires, and boundary conditions that prevent firms from reaching the optimal competitive solution. Additional work could examine industry-level common ownership, more complex rivalry sets beyond dyads, and survey evidence on how common owners motivate managers to take or refrain from competitive actions.
