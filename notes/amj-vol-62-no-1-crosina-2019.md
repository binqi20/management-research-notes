# Toward a Model of Organizational Mourning: The Case of Former Lehman Brothers Bankers

**Abstract**
Focusing on the post-bankruptcy reactions of former Lehman Brothers’ bankers, we build a model of organizational mourning that depicts the thoughts, feelings, and actions of individual members dealing with the loss of their organization. We argue that organizational mourning is a process comprised of five interrelated phases, namely: (1) “experiencing the death event,” (2) “remembering the organization,” (3) “assessing loss,” (4) “salvaging: evaluate and restore,” and (5) “creating continuity and detaching.” Our empirical case suggests that at its core, organizational mourning involves both holding on and letting go of a defunct organization. Understanding how former members mourn is crucial to appreciate how they may ultimately find continuity after an organizational death, including how they enact their subsequent career paths. We conclude by discussing implications for theory of our research—notably, for literatures on post-death organizing, and personal mourning—as well as implications for practice.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how organizational mourning unfolds for former members after organizational death and what effects mourning has on those members. It also examines why different expressions of mourning emerge among individuals mourning the same organization and how those expressions relate to subsequent career paths.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / phases: Experiencing the death event, remembering the organization, assessing loss, salvaging through evaluation and restoration, and creating continuity and detaching.
- Pathways: Recreators mourn by salvaging relationships and recreating Lehman-like teams in similar finance work; Repurposers mourn by salvaging entrepreneurial culture and skills for new entrepreneurial ventures.
- Triggering conditions: The unexpected bankruptcy and organizational death of Lehman Brothers created shock, sadness, uncertainty, and perceived loss among former members.
- Emergent outcomes: Former bankers created continuing bonds with Lehman in different ways, shaping whether they stayed in similar jobs with former colleagues or moved into entrepreneurial careers.
- Boundary conditions: The authors present an inductive model from former Lehman Brothers bankers and do not claim statistical generalizability to all organizational deaths.

The process begins with the shock and sadness of Lehman's sudden death, then moves through remembering what Lehman was, assessing what was lost, and salvaging what could still be kept alive. Recreators' familial memories made the loss feel like losing a dear person, so they restored continuity through former colleagues and similar banking work. Repurposers' learning-oriented memories made the loss feel like losing a valued job, so they restored continuity through entrepreneurial skills and ventures. Organizational mourning therefore involves both holding on and letting go, with different forms of continuity shaping later detachment and career trajectories.

**Data & Measures**
Data are semi-structured interviews with bankers who were at Lehman Brothers when it filed for Chapter 11, recruited through a combination of LinkedIn searches and personal contacts in the industry, with access facilitated by the first author, herself a former Lehman banker. Following four exploratory interviews, the authors completed 72 additional semi-structured interviews with 45 former Lehman bankers between May 2013 and September 2014, interviewing 27 informants twice and 18 once: a first round between May and June 2013 (n = 12); a second between August and November 2013 (n = 28); a third between January and March 2014 (n = 16); and a fourth between June and September 2014 (n = 16). Each interview lasted between 50 and 90 minutes and was transcribed verbatim and then checked for accuracy; the number of interviews was determined by theoretical saturation, and the second-round interviews with 27 informants also served as member checks. Sampling began purposefully across three groups (those who remained in banking, those who left banking for other careers in finance, and those who left banking and finance to pursue interests in other fields) and shifted to theoretical sampling across two groups (those who stayed in the finance industry doing largely what they did at Lehman, and those who left "traditional" organizational careers in finance to pursue entrepreneurial opportunities). Appendix A reports the 45 informants as 30 Recreators and 15 Repurposers, and as 31 males and 14 females, spanning titles from intern (average tenure under one year) to managing director (average tenure 11 years). Interview data were supplemented with archival documents (historical texts about the organization, news articles, and Lehman Brothers' annual reports) that provided background context and were not subject to formal analysis. Coding followed grounded theory and the three stages outlined by Pratt and colleagues (2006): open coding that stayed close to informants' own words; axial coding into more abstract categories such as "shock, sadness, and lack of career safety," "globally: collegial and entrepreneurial environment," "locally: familial environment," and "locally: learning environment"; and theoretical coding that combined those axial codes into the five aggregate phases. The authors adopted an insider/outsider approach in which the second author served as devil's advocate, with frequent debriefing meetings between the two authors.

**Key Findings**
Organizational mourning emerged as a process of five interrelated phases: experiencing the death event, remembering the organization, assessing loss, salvaging (evaluate and restore), and creating continuity and detaching. All informants went through each phase in roughly this order, although for some the process was messier, with remembering hard to parse from assessing loss and with evaluating and restoring salvage sometimes interwoven. At its core the process involved both holding on to and letting go of the defunct organization. The same phases were expressed along two pathways, and all but one informant could be categorized as either a Recreator or a Repurposer; that individual stayed on the Recreator path only briefly before turning to the Repurposer path. Both groups experienced the death event as shock, disbelief, and deep sadness, and both came to perceive investment banks as no longer providing safe or stable environments; both also remembered Lehman globally as a collegial and entrepreneurial firm. Their local memories diverged: Recreators recalled a familial environment and assessed their loss as that of a dear person or family member, whereas Repurposers recalled a learning environment and cast the bankruptcy as the loss of a valued job. Recreators salvaged their relationships and restored them by seeking similar jobs with the same people, while Repurposers salvaged Lehman's entrepreneurial spirit and the skills they had developed and restored them by launching entrepreneurial ventures; informants in both groups recognized the familial and the entrepreneurial aspects of Lehman's culture as central to what made the organization unique, but chose to keep different facets alive. Both groups created continuity through their subsequent career choices, but Recreators described those choices as safe while Repurposers described theirs as asserting control over their own destiny, and career paths were stable over time, with all informants but one staying with the career they started out with post-Lehman. Detachment differed by pathway: Repurposers appear to have largely moved beyond mourning, having accepted Lehman's death and seeing lessons in its demise, whereas Recreators appear not to have, with the loss remaining more potent and expressed through familiar artifacts, locations, and old relationships. Attempts to narrow down what predicted a banker's pathway were inconclusive: both Recreators and Repurposers thought Lehman was a scapegoat for broader problems in the industry, no one in the sample blamed Lehman's leadership for its demise, and both groups admitted to not knowing exactly what they were getting into when they entered banking. Only two paths were found, and both appear relatively functional.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a theoretical model of organizational mourning as a post-death process linking organizational attachment, loss, salvaging, continuity, detachment, and career action. It extends research on organizational death and post-death organizing by explaining what happens between organizational closure and later responses, rather than focusing only on death announcements or legacy organizations. It also contributes to personal mourning research by showing how the quality of remembering a lost entity can shape what continuing bonds people seek to preserve.

**Practical Implication**
Dying organizations and those supporting displaced employees should attend to what former members believe they have lost and what they may want to carry forward. Employees who mainly miss colleagues may benefit from alumni clubs, team-based hiring, or other ways to preserve relationships, whereas those who miss the work or skills may benefit from support in recombining those skills into new career paths. The paper also warns that spreading former members into new organizations can transfer both valuable skills and potentially problematic practices.

**Limitations**
The study is an inductive single-case analysis of former Lehman Brothers bankers, so the authors claim analytic rather than statistical generalizability. The authors captured mourning at one point in time several years after Lehman's organizational death; while they defend retrospective inquiry as legitimate for mourning, they note mourning may look different closer to or further from the death event and call for other-timepoint or longitudinal designs. The sample also includes informants who landed in new jobs or ventures, so it may not capture more dysfunctional mourning trajectories among those who could not move on.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the model across multiple cases of organizational death and examine whether similar mourning processes occur after mergers, acquisitions, or broad organizational change. Scholars could study whether different attachments to organizations predict different mourning paths and whether attributions for organizational death affect the quality of mourning. Longitudinal work could also examine mourning closer to the death event, later after the event, and among individuals whose mourning trajectories are less functional.
