# Relational Attachment at Work: A Complementary Fit Perspective on the Role of Relationships in Organizational Life

**Abstract**
Organizational scholars concur that supportive work relationships can attach employees to their workplace and improve the quality of organizational life, but they lack insights into how or why this occurs. Moreover, models of social support focus on what relationships provide without considering the fit between what people need and receive from their work relationships. Addressing these limitations, we developed and tested a theoretical model of relational attachment at work using a complementary fit perspective. After validating measures, we tested, extended, and replicated our model in three field studies. Results revealed that employees became more attached to their organization and experienced more beneficial outcomes when they had stronger psychological attachment to others at work. This relational attachment was not driven by general social support, but, rather, by “relational needs fit,” which reflects employees’ experience of having their needs met by their work relationships. However, receiving more personal support than what was wanted or needed intruded into employees’ privacy boundaries and created a “too close for comfort” reaction whereby they pulled back from their work relationships. This “too much of a good thing” finding indicates that, while positive relationships at work are effective, social support should be tailored to individuals’ needs.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why and how employees become attached to others at work, and how this relational attachment affects organizational attachment, desirable work attitudes, positive psychological states, turnover, absenteeism, and privacy-boundary reactions. It specifically examines whether relational needs fit, rather than general social support, explains when work relationships produce attachment and when too much personal support becomes intrusive.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Relational attachment will increase as actual levels of (a) instrumental and (b) personal support (i.e., supplies) increase toward preferred levels (i.e., needs).
- Hypothesis 2. Relational attachment will decrease as actual levels of (a) instrumental and (b) personal support (i.e., supplies) increase beyond preferred levels (i.e., needs).
- Hypothesis 3. Relational attachment will be positively related to organizational commitment.
- Hypothesis 4. Relational attachment will be negatively related to (a) turnover and (b) absenteeism.
- Hypothesis 5. Relational attachment will be positively related to (a) work engagement, (b) interpersonal citizenship behaviors (OCB-I), and (c) thriving at work.
- Hypothesis 6. Relational attachment will be positively related to (a) flourishing, (b) life satisfaction, and (c) general happiness.
- Hypothesis 7. Perceived intrusion will mediate the relationship between personal support needs fit and relational attachment.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Relational needs fit for instrumental support and personal support, modeled as the fit between employees' desired support and perceived received support.
- DV(s): Relational attachment, organizational commitment, turnover, absenteeism, work engagement, interpersonal citizenship behaviors, thriving at work, flourishing, life satisfaction, general happiness, and perceived intrusion.
- Mediators: Relational attachment mediates effects of relational needs fit on work and psychological outcomes; perceived intrusion mediates the effect of excess personal support on relational attachment.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The proposed mechanism is that employees become attached to others at work when their work relationships meet their instrumental and personal support needs. This relational attachment then generalizes into stronger organizational commitment and other positive work and well-being outcomes. The model also specifies a threshold process: when personal support exceeds what employees want or need, it can violate privacy boundaries, increase perceived intrusion, and reduce relational attachment.

**Data & Measures**
Validation study (Appendix A): two samples of part-time employees (25 hours or fewer per week on average) who were also enrolled as students at four universities in the United States. Sample I (n = 182) developed and refined the scales; Sample II (n = 147) evaluated them. The final relational attachment scale had six items (α = .92; 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). The commensurate needs/supplies scales for instrumental and personal support had four items each (instrumental support needs α = .90; instrumental support supplies α = .82; personal support needs α = .92; personal support supplies α = .89; 1 = very rarely to 7 = very often), with needs items using the stem "To what degree would you prefer that others at work..." and supplies items the stem "To what degree do others at work...".
Study 1: a national sample of employees in a range of occupations obtained through the StudyResponse project, collected in three waves. 708 individuals completed the Time 1 survey (77% initial response rate), 647 completed Time 2 four weeks later (91% retention), and 463 completed Time 3 one year later (72% retention); careless-response screening removed 151 respondents, leaving 312 cases. Time 1 measured the needs/supplies scales plus two controls, perceived organizational support (Eisenberger, Huntington, Hutchison, & Sowa, 1986, eight items) and job tenure. Time 2 measured relational attachment and interpersonal citizenship behaviors (Williams & Anderson, 1991, seven items). Time 3 measured turnover (reports of voluntarily leaving one's organization during the previous year, dichotomous), absenteeism (days missed during the previous year due to sickness or illness, a count), work engagement (Rich et al., 2010, 18 items), thriving at work (Porath, Spreitzer, Gibson, & Garnett, 2012, 11 items), and organizational commitment (Meyer, Allen, & Smith, 1993, six-item affective commitment scale). A general social support measure (Caplan, Cobb, French, Harrison, & Pinneau, 1975; 1 = not at all to 4 = very much) was used as a comparison predictor.
Study 2: non-faculty employees at 45 randomly selected small colleges across the United States. 1,361 invitations were emailed (104 undeliverable); 270 employees completed Time 1 (21% response rate) and 224 completed Time 2 four weeks later (83% retention); 34 careless respondents and 17 part-time respondents were removed, leaving 173 full-time employees. Time 1 carried the needs/supplies scales, relational attachment, and seven controls: relational identification (Sluss, Ployhart, Cobb, & Ashforth, 2012, four items), LMX (Liden & Maslyn, 1998, 12 items), TMX (Seers, Petty, & Cashman, 1995, 10 items), psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999, seven items), trust (McAllister, 1995, six-item cognition-based trust scale), perceived organizational support, and supplementary person-organization fit (Cable & DeRue, 2002, three items). Job tenure was measured at Time 2, as were organizational commitment, flourishing (Diener et al., 2010, eight items), life satisfaction (Diener, Emmons, Larsen, & Griffin, 1985, five items), and general happiness (Lyubomirsky & Lepper, 1999, four items).
Study 3: three data sources, alumni of two universities in the Western and Midwestern United States, individuals employed at a Midwestern-based health care company working in seven offices across the nation, and employees at 24 randomly selected small colleges with no overlap with Study 2, yielding 922 potential respondents (48 invitations undeliverable). 321 employees completed Time 1 (37% response rate) and 255 completed Time 2 four weeks later (79% retention); 16 careless respondents and 29 part-time respondents were removed, leaving 210 full-time employees. Time 1 repeated the needs/supplies scales and the Study 2 control set and added a four-item perceived intrusion scale developed for this study (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree; an exploratory factor analysis yielded one clear factor explaining 86% of the variance, with all loadings greater than .85). Relational attachment, organizational commitment, and job tenure were measured at Time 2.
Analysis: confirmatory factor analysis reaffirmed the factor structure of the new measures in each sample. Hypotheses 1 and 2 were tested with polynomial regression and response surface analysis along the line of misfit, where needs equal the opposite of supplies, judging the surface slope at zero and the surface curvature. Logistic regression was used for turnover because it was dichotomous and Poisson regression for absenteeism because it was a count variable. Indirect effects used bias-corrected bootstrapping with 1,000 bootstrapped samples, with relational needs fit entered as block variables summarizing the five needs/supplies terms for instrumental and personal support. The design is associational: all constructs are self-reported survey measures collected in multiple waves, and the paper reports no causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported in all three studies. Along the misfit line, relational attachment increased as actual levels of instrumental and personal support rose toward desired levels; H1(a) and H1(b) were supported in Study 1, confirmed in Study 2, and replicated in Study 3.
Hypothesis 2 was supported only for personal support. H2(a) was not supported in any of the three studies: for instrumental support, relational attachment continued to increase as supplies rose beyond desired levels. H2(b) was supported in Studies 1, 2, and 3, where the personal-support surface showed downward curvature along the misfit line, so relational attachment decreased once supplies exceeded needs. This is the "too much of a good thing" threshold effect.
Hypothesis 3 was supported in all three studies: relational attachment was positively related to organizational commitment (Study 1 β = .31, p < .01; Study 2 β = .24, p < .01; Study 3 β = .39, p < .01). In Studies 2 and 3 the effect held while controlling for relational identification, LMX, TMX, psychological safety, trust, perceived organizational support, and person-organization fit.
Hypotheses 4(a) and 4(b) were supported in Study 1, the only study testing them: relational attachment was negatively related to turnover (logistic regression, semistandardized coefficient = -.97, p < .05) and to absenteeism (Poisson regression, semistandardized coefficient = -.14, p < .05).
Hypotheses 5(a), 5(b), and 5(c) were supported in Study 1, the only study testing them: relational attachment was positively related to work engagement (β = .16, p < .05), interpersonal citizenship behaviors (β = .20, p < .01), and thriving at work (β = .14, p < .05).
Hypothesis 6 received mixed support in Study 2, the only study testing it. H6(b) was supported: relational attachment was positively related to life satisfaction (β = .22, p < .05). H6(a) flourishing (β = .19, n.s.) and H6(c) general happiness (β = .14, n.s.) were not supported; relational attachment had bivariate relationships with all three, but only life satisfaction remained significant in the complete model.
Hypothesis 7 was supported in Study 3, the only study testing it: perceived intrusion mediated the relationship between personal support needs fit and relational attachment (indirect effect a*b = -.12, 95% two-sided CI [-.35, -.02]), and along the misfit line for intrusion the surface slope at zero was positive with upward curvature, indicating more reported intrusion as personal support exceeded desired levels.
Mediation and incremental-validity tests: in Study 1, relational needs fit for both instrumental and personal support had indirect effects through relational attachment on every outcome variable. In Study 2, relational attachment mediated the effects on organizational commitment and life satisfaction, but not on flourishing or general happiness. Relational needs fit for instrumental (b = .14, p = .05) and personal support (b = .60, p < .01) together explained variance in relational attachment beyond a general social support measure (ΔR² = .46, p < .01), and that general measure was not significant in the regression model.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends Kahn's needs-based model of meaningful connections by integrating a person-environment complementary fit perspective, shifting attention from whether support is available to whether received support fits individual relational needs. It contributes to positive relationships at work by theorizing and testing relational attachment as a cumulative attachment to one's constellation of work relationships. It also brings the social support paradox into organizational research by showing that personal support can become "too close for comfort" when it exceeds employees' desired level.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should treat work relationships as a source of organizational attachment and well-being, but should not assume that more support is always better. Support needs to be tailored to individual preferences, especially when support concerns personal matters. The findings also imply that communication and relationship-building training should help employees and managers recognize needs, boundaries, and nonverbal cues of privacy violation.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the studies used self-report data, creating potential same-source bias, although they used multiple time points, behavioral outcomes in Study 1, and common-method checks to reduce this concern. They also note limits to generalizability because samples were more educated and more non-Hispanic White than the U.S. working-adult population, all respondents were employed in the United States, and women were overrepresented in Studies 2 and 3.

**Future Research**
Future research could extend the complementary fit model to career needs, developmental relationships, relationships outside the workplace, and outcomes such as career efficacy and satisfaction. The authors also call for work on privacy thresholds, including how relationship roles, power, cultural norms, social media, generation, diversity, and changing needs affect when closeness becomes intrusive. Longitudinal studies could examine how privacy thresholds and work relationships evolve over time.
