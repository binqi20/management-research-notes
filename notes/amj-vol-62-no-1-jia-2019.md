# Public Governance, Corporate Governance, and Firm Innovation: An Examination of State-Owned Enterprises

**Abstract**
Innovation activities create substantial firm value, but they are difficult to manage owing to agency risk, which is commonly thought to result in shirking, and hence underinvestment in innovation. However, agency risk can also create inefficient allocation of resources among innovation activities, on which the literature has provided limited understanding. We examine an important outcome created by agency risk—that agents pursue quantity of innovation at the expense of novelty—and investigate how it is influenced by corporate and public governance. We theorize that improved corporate governance tools, including better alignment of agents’ private incentives and stronger monitoring, and high-quality public governance reduce such agency risk in state-owned enterprises (SOEs). Furthermore, higher-quality public governance enhances the functioning of corporate governance tools in further reducing such agency risk in innovation. We test our theory by examining SOEs in China that responded to the state’s pro-innovation policies relying disproportionately on quantifiable outcomes (e.g., patent counts) for assessing innovation performance. Our difference-in-differences estimates provide overall support for our hypotheses. These findings provide new insights on how agency risk affects innovation by distinguishing the consequences for quantity and novelty of innovation and for how conventional corporate governance tools shaping innovation depend on public governance.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how agency risk affects the allocation of effort across innovation activities when principals rely heavily on quantifiable innovation metrics. It examines whether corporate governance and public governance reduce SOE agents' tendency to pursue patent quantity at the expense of patent novelty.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, firms whose agents' private interests are more aligned with firm value will generate a smaller quantity of innovation compared to firms where this alignment is weak.
- Hypothesis 1b. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, firms whose agents' private interests are more aligned with firm value will generate a larger proportion of novel innovation relative to all the innovation produced by the focal firm compared to firms where this alignment is weaker.
- Hypothesis 2a. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, firms with stronger monitoring of their agents will generate a smaller quantity of innovation compared to firms with weaker monitoring.
- Hypothesis 2b. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, firms with stronger monitoring of their agents will generate a larger proportion of novel innovation relative to all the innovation produced by the focal firm compared to firms with weaker monitoring.
- Hypothesis 3a. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, SOEs governed by a higher-quality government will generate a smaller quantity of innovation compared to SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.
- Hypothesis 3b. When principals adopt metrics that rely on quantifying innovation outcomes, SOEs governed by a higher-quality government will generate a larger proportion of novel innovation relative to all the innovation produced by the focal firm compared to SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.
- Hypothesis 4a. The effect predicted by Hypothesis 1a is stronger for SOEs governed by a higher-quality government than for SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.
- Hypothesis 4b. The effect predicted by Hypothesis 1b is stronger for SOEs governed by a higher-quality government than for SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.
- Hypothesis 4c. The effect predicted by Hypothesis 2a is stronger for SOEs governed by a higher-quality government than for SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.
- Hypothesis 4d. The effect predicted by Hypothesis 2b is stronger for SOEs governed by a higher-quality government than for SOEs governed by a lower-quality government.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Post-policy exposure to China's 2006 innovation procurement policy; proportion of board share; proportion of state share; provincial government quality; interactions among policy exposure, corporate governance, and public governance.
- DV(s): Number of patents; proportion of novel patents among all patents produced by the firm-year.
- Mediators: Agency risk is the theorized mechanism, but it is not directly measured as a mediator.
- Moderators: Corporate governance tools and public governance quality moderate the post-policy change in innovation quantity and novelty.

The mechanism is grounded in agency theory and the multitasking model: when principals evaluate innovation through patent counts, agents can satisfy measurable targets by producing more general patents while underinvesting in more novel patents whose value is harder to measure. Better incentive alignment and stronger monitoring reduce this moral hazard by making agents more attentive to firm value rather than only to quantifiable policy targets. Higher-quality public governance makes state principals more accountable and better able to collect information, which strengthens the effect of corporate governance on innovation novelty.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: firm-level data on all publicly listed firms in China from WIND, a database that compiles public information disclosed by listed firms; invention patents filed by all listed firms in China from 2000 to 2012 and eventually granted by the State Intellectual Property Office (SIPO), with the application year treated as the year a patent was produced; and province-year data from the China Statistical Yearbooks and the National Economic Research Institute (NERI) index of marketization of Chinese provinces (Fan, Wang, & Zhu, 2011). The main analysis is restricted to SOEs. Only invention patents are used, because they face the highest scrutiny and the strictest screening for quality and novelty, unlike the utility model and design patents that cover more incremental inventions.
- Dependent variables: number of patents, the total number of patents generated by a firm in a given year; and proportion of novel patents, the number of novel patents divided by the total number of patents produced by the firm in that year, which takes a value between 0 and 1. Following the method of Fleming, Mingo, and Chen (2007), a patent counts as novel if it involves the first occurrence of a new combination of patent classes compared with all patents ever granted by SIPO up to the observation year.
- Treatment and policy variable: an SOE is treated if its SIC 3 industry classification belongs to the industrial categories that produced one or more products designated in the 2006 government procurement catalogs. Post-policy equals 1 for a treated SOE's observation years from 2007 onward, the year after the policy announcement, and 0 otherwise; for a nontreated SOE post-policy is always 0.
- Corporate governance measures: incentive alignment is the proportion of board share, the percentage of firm shares owned by board members (a robustness check using the percentage of board members who held any firm share is reported as consistent); monitoring is the proportion of state share, the proportion of firm shares owned by the state, with a higher value taken to proxy for a higher degree of monitoring by the state principal.
- Public governance measure: government quality is the first principal component, extracted each year, of four NERI subindices: nontax fees and charges on firms as a percentage of focal firms' sales; nontax fees and charges on rural residents as a percentage of their annual income; the importance of dealing with government officials in business operations; and the excessive number of government employees. Across all years the average eigenvalue of the first component is 1.72, explaining 43% of the variance, with average factor loadings of 0.68, 0.31, 0.67, and 0.09 respectively.
- Controls: firm age, firm size (total assets), ROA, market-to-book ratio, unabsorbed slack (current assets to current liabilities), potential slack (debt to equity), cumulative number of patents, government experience, independent director, CEO and chairman duality, and CEO salary at the firm level; province GDP per capita, province R&D expenditure, and province market development at the province-year level. With the exception of post-policy, all explanatory and control variables are lagged by one year, and firm fixed effects are included in all models.
- Identification: a difference-in-differences design that uses the 2006 pro-indigenous-innovation government procurement policy as an exogenous top-down quasi-experiment, estimated through the interaction between policy treatment and the governance variables. The control group of nontreated but comparable SOEs is constructed by propensity score matching with a caliper of 0.15 (calipers of 0.20 and 0.25 are reported to yield similar results) on firm age, firm size, proportion of state share, cumulative number of patents, and year indicators. The paper reports that the pre-policy difference between treatment and control groups was not statistically significant either for the number of patents or for the number of novel patents.
- Estimation: number of patents is estimated with conditional quasi-maximum-likelihood fixed-effects Poisson models using Huber-White robust standard errors, with negative binomial models reported as a consistent robustness check; proportion of novel patents is estimated with double-censored Tobit models with firm fixed effects, clustered by firm, also with robust standard errors.
- Analysis samples: full-sample models that include the treated and matched control groups report 3,676 observations for the number of patents and 6,622 observations for the proportion of novel patents; the more stringent treatment-group-only models report 2,539 and 3,324 observations. The subsamples of firms in provinces with low versus high government quality report 1,525 and 1,822 observations for the number of patents and 3,263 and 3,359 observations for the proportion of novel patents.

**Key Findings**
- Overall verdict as the paper states it: all the hypotheses on the novelty of patents (Hypotheses 1b, 2b, 3b, and 4b) received empirical support; on the overall quantity of patents, Hypotheses 1a and 4c were supported but Hypotheses 2a, 3a, 4a, and 4d were not.
- Baseline policy effects: in models without interaction terms, both the number of patents (Models 2-1 and 2-3) and the proportion of novel patents (Models 2-5 and 2-7) increased after the policy was implemented.
- Hypothesis 1a supported: the interaction of post-policy and proportion of board share on the number of patents is negative and significant in the full sample (Model 2-2, p < .05) and in the treatment-group subsample (Model 2-4, p < .01). Firms at the mean proportion of board share increased patent production by 54.44% after the policy, whereas firms one standard deviation above the mean increased it by a smaller 37.05%, a gap of close to 18 percentage points.
- Hypothesis 1b supported: the same interaction is positive and significant (p < .01) for the proportion of novel patents in both Model 2-6 (full sample) and Model 2-8 (treatment group only). Firms at the mean increased their proportion of novel patents by 11.52% relative to the pre-policy level versus 30.40% for firms one standard deviation above the mean, almost 19 percentage points higher. The authors describe this as strong support for Hypothesis 1b.
- Hypothesis 2a not supported: the interaction of post-policy and proportion of state share is not statistically significant in predicting the number of patents (Models 3-2 and 3-4).
- Hypothesis 2b supported: that same interaction is a positive and significant (p < .01) predictor of the proportion of novel patents (Models 3-6 and 3-8). SOEs at the mean proportion of state share increased their proportion of novel patents by 9.89% and SOEs one standard deviation above the mean by 15.69%, a difference of close to 6 percentage points.
- Hypothesis 3a not supported: the interaction of post-policy and government quality on the number of patents is positive for the full sample (Model 4-2, p < .10) and for the treated sample (Model 4-4, p < .05), which the authors state does not support Hypothesis 3a.
- Hypothesis 3b supported: in the preferred full-sample model (Model 4-6) the interaction of post-policy and government quality on the proportion of novel patents is positive and statistically significant (p < .01). SOEs in provinces at mean government quality increased their proportion of novel patents by about 10.33% and those in provinces one standard deviation above the mean by about 11.14%; the authors note that this differential of close to one percentage point is relatively small but should be read against an average proportion of novel patents of only 2.8%. In the treatment-group subsample the interaction term is not statistically significant from zero.
- Hypothesis 4a not supported: the three-way interaction of government quality, proportion of board share, and post-policy does not affect the number of patents at conventional significance levels (Model 5-3).
- Hypothesis 4b supported: the interaction of post-policy and proportion of board share on the proportion of novel patents is negative and statistically significant (p < .01) for SOEs in provinces with low government quality (Model 5-4) and positive and significant (p < .01) for SOEs in provinces with high government quality (Model 5-5), and the three-way interaction is positive and statistically significant (p < .01) in Model 5-6. The authors conclude that the positive effect of board share on post-policy production of novel innovation is mainly driven by firms located in provinces with high-quality governments.
- Hypothesis 4c supported: the three-way interaction of post-policy, proportion of state share, and government quality is a negative and statistically significant (p < .01) predictor of the number of patents (Model 6-3), while the two-way interaction of post-policy and proportion of state share is not statistically significant in either government-quality subsample (Models 6-1 and 6-2).
- Hypothesis 4d not supported: for the proportion of novel patents the two-way interaction of post-policy and proportion of state share remains positive and statistically significant in both the low (Model 6-4) and high (Model 6-5) government-quality subsamples, the difference in this interaction effect between the two subsamples is not statistically significant at conventional levels, and the three-way interaction term in Model 6-6 is not a statistically significant predictor.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends agency research on innovation by shifting attention from underinvestment in innovation to inefficient deployment of innovation resources. Its core contribution is to distinguish innovation quantity from innovation novelty and show that agency risk can increase observable innovation while reducing the proportion of more novel innovation.

It also links corporate governance to public governance in state capitalism. The study argues that the accountability and quality of state principals shape whether conventional governance tools, such as incentive alignment and monitoring, reduce agency risk in SOEs.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that patent-count targets can increase visible innovation output while still weakening the novelty mix of firm innovation if governance is weak. For SOEs and policymakers, promoting innovation requires governance systems that reward novelty and value creation, not only measurable patent volume.

The paper also suggests that corporate governance reforms work better when public governance is stronger. State owners seeking innovation should therefore attend both to firm-level governance tools and to the quality of the public governance environment in which SOE principals operate.

**Limitations**
The authors use granted invention patents only, which is a conservative choice and may underestimate a broader post-policy increase in lower-quality patenting. They also acknowledge that they cannot entirely rule out the alternative explanation that the Chinese state intentionally prioritized patent quantity, although the interaction results are more consistent with the agency-based explanation.

The authors state that their findings revealed one limitation: their theory explains why, in a good institutional environment with higher-quality governments, more diligent agents are more likely to behave in ways consistent with promoting firm value, but they do not have a theory about what happens in a bad institutional environment, in which corporate governance appears to function in very different ways. The study focuses on SOEs because public governance directly affects how effectively state officials perform the state's role as the principals of SOEs; the authors hold that the general theory of the effect of corporate governance on firm innovation should, in principle, be applicable to privately owned enterprises as well.

**Future Research**
The authors identify privately owned enterprises as a fruitful setting for future work on the same moral hazard because incentive alignment and monitoring also address agency risk outside SOEs. Future research could examine how the governance of principals in private firms, including different ownership types, shapes innovation outcomes.

They also suggest connecting innovation research to work on financial control and multidivisional firms. Future studies could test whether financial control affects not only the overall amount of R&D or innovation but also the composition of innovation between incremental and novel outputs.
