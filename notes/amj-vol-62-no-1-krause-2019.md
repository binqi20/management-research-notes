# The Coercive Isomorphism Ripple Effect: An Investigation of Nonprofit Interlocks on Corporate Boards

**Abstract**
Institutional theorists have long recognized that board interlocks serve as conduits of information, which can facilitate mimetic isomorphism under conditions of uncertainty. In contrast, the potential for coercive isomorphism—which is a response to direct coercion rather than uncertainty—to spread through board interlocks remains unknown. Drawing on the forced compliance literature from social psychology, we propose that coercive isomorphism exhibits a ripple effect, whereby conforming to coercive pressures leads directors to internalize their acquiescence and apply it to the other boards on which they sit. We test this relationship in the context of nonprofit interlocks on the boards of 209 publicly traded U.S. firms between 2010 and 2014, predicting that coercive pressure to minimize overhead in the nonprofit sphere—a phenomenon known as the nonprofit starvation cycle—spreads to corporate boards through nonprofit interlocks, leading the corporate boards to reduce firm investment in intangible assets. We find that this relationship manifests across our sample, but is strongest via interlocks held by powerful directors and in industries characterized by high investment in intangible assets. Finally, we also find that the coercive isomorphism ripple effect ultimately decreases firm value.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether coercive isomorphism can spread indirectly through board interlocks rather than only through direct pressure inside the organizational field where the pressure originates. It examines whether nonprofit board interlocks transmit overhead aversion to corporate boards and thereby reduce corporate investment in intangible assets.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Coercive isomorphism exhibits a ripple effect through board interlocks, wherein a focal organization's board interlocks with organizations experiencing coercive isomorphism are positively associated with the focal organization's adoption of the interlocked organizations' coerced attributes. In the study's context, this means nonprofit interlocks on a firm's board should be negatively associated with the firm's investment in intangible assets.
Hypothesis 2. The coercive isomorphism ripple effect through board interlocks is stronger when the interlocks are held by directors who are powerful on the focal board. In context, the negative relationship between nonprofit interlocks and intangible asset investment should be stronger when the interlocked directors have high power than when they have low power.
Hypothesis 3. The coercive isomorphism ripple effect through board interlocks is stronger to the extent that mimetic pressures in the focal organization's field contradict the coercive pressures in the interlocked organizations' field. In context, the negative effect of nonprofit interlocks on intangible asset investment should be stronger when industry intangible asset investment is high. The authors note in a footnote that they do not theorize that the ripple effect overrides mimetic isomorphism, only that its marginal impact on practice adoption is greater when it pushes against mimetic pressures.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Nonprofit interlocks on corporate boards, separated in some models into high-power and low-power nonprofit interlocks.
- DV(s): Firm intangible asset investment, with Tobin's Q examined post hoc as a firm-value outcome.
- Mediators: The proposed psychological process is directors' internalization of coerced overhead aversion through the forced compliance effect; the post-hoc firm-value analysis tests intangible asset investment as a partial mediator.
- Moderators: Director power on the focal corporate board and industry intangible asset investment as a measure of contrasting mimetic pressure.

The mechanism is that directors who acquiesce to nonprofit-sector coercive pressures to minimize overhead internalize overhead aversion and carry that attitude to corporate boards on which they also serve. When those directors influence corporate board decision making, especially if they are powerful, the firm reduces investment in intangible assets such as R&D and advertising. The ripple effect is stronger when industry mimetic pressures favor high intangible asset investment because the imported overhead aversion runs against the receiving field's prevailing norm.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: because director biographical data had to be collected manually, the authors limited the sample to the largest publicly traded U.S. firms, collecting data for the 225 largest firms in terms of revenue between 2010 and 2014, inclusive. Board members' external board seats (public, private, and nonprofit) and education levels were hand-collected from the Bloomberg Terminal; interlocking organizations were classified as public, private, or nonprofit through an iterative process of checking the organization's website and then cross-referencing Google, LinkedIn, PR Newswire, and the National Center for Charitable Statistics. Other corporate governance data came from ISS (formerly RiskMetrics) and MSCI (formerly GMI Ratings); accounting and market data came from Compustat. After excluding observations with missing values, the sample consisted of 984 observations from 209 firms.
- DV: Intangible Asset Investment = a summative index of R&D intensity (total R&D expenditure divided by revenue) and advertising intensity (total advertising expenditure divided by revenue), following the two strategic components of Barth, Kasznik, and McNichols' (2001) intangible asset ratio. Missing R&D and advertising spending values were replaced with 0, and the index was standardized prior to analysis.
- IV: Nonprofit Interlocks = the total number of unique nonprofit board interlocks held by all directors on each corporate board in each year, divided by the total number of directors on the board in that year, measured in the same year as intangible asset investment. For Hypothesis 2 the authors specified separate variables, High-Power Nonprofit Interlocks and Low-Power Nonprofit Interlocks, respectively the average number of unique nonprofit interlocks held by each high-power and each low-power director at each firm in each year; directors were identified as high-power if they held the title of CEO, board chair, or lead director, or owned at least 1% of the firm's stock.
- Moderator: Industry Intangible Asset Investment = the mean prior-year intangible asset investment across all firms in the Compustat database in the same two-digit SIC category as the focal firm with revenues above $20 million; standardized prior to analysis.
- Controls: board size, board independence, CEO duality, board ownership, board tenure, board age, female percentage, for-profit interlocks, institutional ownership, firm size, debt, ROA, and liquidity. Accounting controls were lagged one year.
- Post-hoc outcome: firm value measured as Tobin's Q using Chung and Pruitt's (1994) formula, measured one year subsequent to intangible asset investment to maintain causal priority.
- Estimation: linear regression with firm fixed effects and year fixed effects. The authors state that fixed effects help alleviate endogeneity concerns from unobserved firm-specific heterogeneity, while noting the analysis cannot draw conclusions about between-firm effects. A Hausman test indicated random effects would not be appropriate (x2 = 47.91, p < 0.001). An endogeneity check used two-stage least squares with a dichotomous indicator of whether the CEO had any nonprofit interlocks and the industry average of the independent variable as instruments; the Durbin-Wu-Hausman test was not significant (Durbin x2 = 0.13, p = 0.72; Wu-Hausman F = 0.10, p = 0.75), so the fixed-effects regression models were retained.
- Qualitative post-hoc component (separate from the panel data): semi-structured recorded interviews with nine high-profile U.S. corporate directors who currently serve or have served on publicly traded, private, and nonprofit boards, whose board work spanned education, energy, finance, healthcare, hospitality, publishing, and retail.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: nonprofit interlocks were negatively and significantly associated with intangible asset investment (b = -0.13, p < 0.001; Model 2, Table 2).
Hypothesis 2 was supported: the effect of high-power nonprofit interlocks was negative and significant (b = -0.27, p < 0.001), as was the effect of low-power nonprofit interlocks (b = -0.08, p < 0.05), and a difference-in-coefficients F test showed the effect was significantly more negative for high-power interlocks (F = 9.29, p < 0.01). A board whose average nonprofit interlocks among high-power directors increases from 0 to 1 sees intangible asset investment as a percentage of revenue drop by more than 130 basis points; the same increase among low-power directors is associated with a decrease of fewer than 50 basis points.
Hypothesis 3 was supported: the interaction of nonprofit interlocks with industry intangible asset investment was negative and significant (b = -0.09, p < 0.01; Model 4, Table 2), and Figure 2 shows the relationship does not change direction but becomes weaker as industry intangible asset investment decreases. In Model 5 the high-power interaction was significant and the low-power interaction marginally significant, but the two interaction terms were not statistically different, which the authors had not hypothesized would differ.
Robustness: results held under alternative independent-variable measures, an alternative one-year lag specification (Table 3), firm random effects, industry fixed effects, and exclusion of missing-value observations. Restricting the sample to firms reporting both types of intangible asset investment (264 observations from 58 firms) left the hypothesized effects significant except for Hypothesis 3, whose p value increased to 0.12 while the coefficient remained in the hypothesized direction and became slightly greater in magnitude. Limiting the sample to firms reporting R&D spending (507 observations from 107 firms) did not change the results, while limiting it to firms reporting advertising spending (367 observations from 83 firms) again cost Hypothesis 3 its statistical significance.
Post-hoc firm value (outside the formal theory): nonprofit interlocks were negatively and significantly related to Tobin's Q (b = -0.23, p < 0.01) and intangible asset investment was positively related to Tobin's Q (b = 0.26, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis showed a significant indirect effect of nonprofit interlocks on firm value through intangible asset investment (b = -0.03, p < 0.05) alongside a significant direct effect (b = -0.23, p < 0.01), with intangible asset investment mediating 12% of the overall effect; Sobel, Goodman I, and Goodman II statistics were all significant at p < 0.05. A bootstrap mediation analysis with 5,000 replications produced a bias-corrected 95% confidence interval that just barely included 0 [-0.025, 0.001] while the 90% interval did not [-0.023, -0.002], leading the authors to conclude there is some evidence of partial mediation. Distinguishing director power, high-power nonprofit interlocks were negatively related to Tobin's Q (b = -0.47, p < 0.001), as were low-power nonprofit interlocks (b = -0.16, p < 0.05), with the high-power effect significantly greater (F = 6.05, p < 0.05).
Alternative dependent variables: the relationships between nonprofit interlocks and both SG&A intensity and capital intensity were in the hypothesized direction but not statistically significant; the effects of high-power nonprofit interlocks were negative and significant for SG&A intensity (b = -0.02, p < 0.05) and capital intensity (b = -0.06, p < 0.05), and significantly stronger than any low-power effect for both SG&A intensity (F = 4.88, p < 0.05) and capital intensity (F = 5.07, p < 0.05).
Qualitative post-hoc evidence: in separate recorded interviews, all nine directors reported conforming to external pressures on at least one board, and many described the kind of subsequent attitude change the forced compliance effect would suggest; the authors note that directors would often not admit to having experienced the effect and vacillated between endorsing external pressures for nonprofit efficiency and criticizing the resulting cuts to investment.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends institutional theory by arguing that coercive isomorphism can ripple across organizational fields through interlocking directors, not only through direct dependence or pressure. It contributes to board interlock research by treating directors as psychologically affected actors rather than impersonal information conduits. It also identifies director power and conflicting mimetic pressure as contingencies that shape when the coercive isomorphism ripple effect is most consequential.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that CEOs and boards should evaluate nonprofit interlocks carefully because such ties may bring legitimacy benefits while also importing overhead aversion. For large public firms, the risk is that directors' nonprofit-board experience may unintentionally discourage investment in intangible assets that support future growth and firm value.

**Limitations**
The authors state that their measured variables are distal from the proposed psychological mechanisms, so the evidence depends on assumptions about directors' exposure to coercive isomorphism and internalization of overhead aversion. They also note that the study does not empirically distinguish attitude-congruent from attitude-discrepant forced compliance. The sample is limited to the largest U.S. firms, and the firm-value analysis using Tobin's Q is post hoc rather than a formal test of theory.

**Future Research**
The authors call for finer-grained measurement of the psychological assumptions linking nonprofit board exposure to corporate board behavior. They encourage research on attitude-congruent versus attitude-discrepant forced compliance, other types of cross-field linkages beyond board interlocks, distinctions among nonprofit organizations, other contexts such as healthcare administration or social enterprises, and a wider array of performance outcomes.
