# When Do Ethnic Communities Affect Foreign Location Choice? Dual Entry Strategies of Korean Banks in China

**Abstract**
Research has shown that firms typically expand into foreign locations with sizeable coethnic communities. However, in many cases the ethnic community influences the investment choice of the same firm in one location but not another. We offer an institutional lens to explain this heterogeneity. Ethnic groups function like informal institutions that facilitate transactions between foreign firms and customers, suppliers, and information providers through interpersonal exchange. Relying on ethnic communities to mediate transactions in foreign markets is valuable but limited by the relatively small scale of these communities. In contrast, relying on formal institutions allows firms to expand more broadly into foreign markets because the impersonal exchange inherent in formal governance is more scalable. This is manifested in “dual entry strategies” wherein ethnic communities have a significantly stronger influence on location choice in places with unreliable (weak and unstable) formal institutions than in places with reliable formal institutions. We found support for these ideas using a unique dataset of South Korean banks’ investments in Chinese provinces during 1992–2013. To mitigate endogeneity concerns, we exploited a historical migration that created a quasirandom distribution of ethnic Koreans across provinces. Our work contributes to research on international expansion, ethnic communities, and institutional theory.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when ethnic communities affect foreign location choice by coethnic firms. It examines why the same foreign firm may rely on a coethnic community in one location but not in another, focusing on the reliability of formal institutions as the boundary condition.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Baseline: Ceteris paribus, the larger the size of an ethnic community in a location, the greater the likelihood that a bank from a country of the same ethnic origin will invest in that location.
Hypothesis 1. The positive effect of the ethnic community's size on the location choice of a bank from a country of the same ethnic origin is weaker (less positive) in locations with strong property rights laws than in locations with weak property rights laws.
Hypothesis 2a. The positive effect of the ethnic community's size on the location choice of a bank from a country of the same ethnic origin is weaker (less positive) in locations with low macroeconomic uncertainty than in locations with high macroeconomic uncertainty.
Hypothesis 2b. The positive effect of the ethnic community's size on the location choice of a bank from a country of the same ethnic origin is weaker (less positive) in locations with low lending uncertainty than in locations with high lending uncertainty.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Size of the Chaoxian ethnic Korean community in a Chinese province.
- DV(s): Whether a South Korean bank invests in a Chinese province in a given year.
- Mediators: The institutional role of ethnic communities is theorized as the mechanism, but not directly estimated as a mediator.
- Moderators: Strength of formal institutions measured by property rights laws; stability of formal institutions measured by macroeconomic uncertainty and lending uncertainty.

The mechanism is that coethnic communities act as informal institutions that reduce exchange hazards through shared language, norms, solidarity, and social enforcement. These community-based mechanisms help foreign banks transact with borrowers, workers, and information providers when formal institutions are weak or unstable. Because interpersonal exchange within an ethnic community is not scalable beyond the community's social sphere, the community's effect on location choice weakens when formal institutions are reliable enough to support broader impersonal exchange.

**Data & Measures**
The dataset is described as a comprehensive account of South Korean banks' direct investments in Chinese provinces from 1992 until 2013; during this period 11 Korean banks made 82 unique investments in 11 provinces, and one investment in Jilin was excluded because data on lending uncertainty and industry distribution are missing for that province. Information on the timing and location of investments was collected from the banks' webpages, the Financial Supervisory Service in Korea, the Export-Import Bank of Korea, the Korea Trade Investment Promotion Agency, and the banks' published annual reports; the sample includes 55 initial investments and 27 repeated investments. The dependent variable is an indicator coded 1 if a Korean bank made an investment in province j in year t, and 0 otherwise. For each realized investment the choice set contains 16 location options: the 11 locations that received Korean bank investments during 1992-2013 plus five provinces (Fujian, Hainan, Shaanxi, Yunnan, and Zhejiang) that received investments from non-Korean foreign banks and are therefore feasible but unrealized choices. Each choice set is defined by a firm-investment year; there were 45 instances in which firms made one investment in a year and 16 in which firms made two or more in the same year (37 investments), giving a sample size of 976 (61 firm-investment years x 16 provinces). All other variables are lagged by a year relative to the dependent variable. Ethnic community is the count of Chaoxian - those who moved to China pre-1949 and their descendants - by province and year, entered as standardized counts in the estimation to reduce multicollinearity; the counts come from the Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs (2003, 2005, 2007, 2009, 2010, and 2012) and the Chinese Census (1990, 2000, and 2010), with missing data replaced by counts from the closest year. Strength of formal institutions is captured by legal, the average of four property-rights components of the marketization index developed by Fan, Wang, and Zhu (2011) - protection of intellectual property rights, producers' rights, consumers' rights, and the presence of intermediaries for law enforcement; because the index is available for 1997-2009, the 1997 values were used for 1992-1996 and the 2009 values for 2010-2013. Macroeconomic uncertainty is the root mean squared error from recursively regressing logged gross regional product on its lagged value and a linear time trend from 1986-2012, a region-specific and time-varying measure. Lending uncertainty is the standard deviation of the interest rate paid by all firms in the province the year before the investment is observed, using interest-rate data for firms with sales of more than 5 million yuan collected by the Chinese National Bureau of Statistics annually during 1998-2007 and made available by Aghion and colleagues (2015); as with legal, values were extrapolated for the pre-1998 and post-2007 years. Controls include post-1992 Korean immigrants, firm experience, GRP per capita, high tech firms, foreign banks, Korean banks, Korean investment, industry distribution (a Herfindahl-Hirschman measure of province-level industry output), foreign employment, and a yearly count of state-owned enterprises. Estimation uses a conditional logit model, which conditions out chooser attributes and year-specific effects; because interaction terms in nonlinear models are not directly interpretable and post-estimation corrections for logit do not apply to conditional logit, the moderation hypotheses are tested by splitting the sample at the median of each moderating variable, estimating the main effect of Chaoxian within each subsample, and comparing the magnitude of the effect across subsamples. The two subsample equations are jointly estimated using seemingly unrelated estimation (SUEST in Stata) so that Wald tests can compare effect sizes, and coefficients are scaled by firm experience (or another meaningful covariate) to present comparable magnitudes across subsamples. Identification rests on the historical isolation of the Chaoxian from South Korea between 1949 and 1992, which the authors treat as making their geographic distribution relatively exogenous to the socioeconomic factors driving South Korean banks' post-1992 location choices, while controlling for post-1992 Korean immigrants and nonbanking firms. Robustness analyses re-estimate the models on initial investments only and replace the count measure with a ratio measure (number of Chaoxian per thousand).

**Key Findings**
The baseline expectation is supported: in the full model with controls (Model 2 of Table 3, n = 976) the coefficient of Chaoxian is positive and significant (0.414, p < .05; all hypothesis tests are reported as one-tailed), and the authors calculate that a 10% increase in the Chaoxian population in a region increases the probability of entering that region by 3.88% relative to other regions. Hypothesis 1 is supported by the split at the median of legal: the coefficient of Chaoxian is negative and insignificant in the high-property-rights subsample (Model 3, p = .245) but positive and significant in the low-property-rights subsample (Model 4, p = .0226), and a Wald test after joint estimation shows that the difference across subsamples is statistically significant (p = .0170, one-tailed), with the coefficients scaled by firm experience differing only marginally (p = .0604, one-tailed). Hypothesis 2a is supported: the effect of Chaoxian is positive and significant in provinces with high macroeconomic uncertainty (Model 5, p = .00518) but negative and insignificant in places with low uncertainty (Model 6, p = .257), and the Wald test indicates that the difference is statistically significant for both the unscaled (p = .00307) and the scaled coefficients (p = .0584). Hypothesis 2b is supported: the coefficient of Chaoxian is positive and significant under high lending uncertainty (Model 7, p = .00807) but negative and insignificant under low lending uncertainty (Model 8, p = .215), with the difference only marginally statistically significant for both the unscaled (p = .0511) and the scaled coefficients (p = .0920). The authors conclude that overall the subsample comparisons are consistent with Hypotheses 1, 2a, and 2b. Two further subsample patterns are reported explicitly as post-hoc tests: the presence of other Korean banks discourages entry into provinces with unreliable formal institutions but not into provinces with reliable ones, a difference that is significant for legal (chi-square(1) = 3.402, p = .0326, one-tailed) and for macroeconomic uncertainty (chi-square(1) = 4.877, p = .0136, one-tailed); and prior experience is an especially strong predictor of repeated investment in provinces with unreliable formal institutions, a difference that is marginally significant for legal (chi-square(1) = 1.699, p = .0962, one-tailed) and significant for macroeconomic uncertainty (chi-square(1) = 5.264, p = .0109, one-tailed). The results are reported as robust to using initial investments only (Table 4), to ratio rather than count measures of the Chaoxian population (Tables 5 and 6), to clustering errors by parent bank, to restricting legal and lending uncertainty to the years with actual data (both hypotheses remained supported with the reduced samples), to alternative sources for the Chaoxian counts, to adding options to the choice set over time, to dropping Beijing and Shanghai (more than 39% of all investments by Korean banks) and to dropping the northeastern provinces where most ethnic Koreans are concentrated, and to confining the analysis to data since China's 2002 WTO accession. A placebo test regressing the number of non-Korean foreign banks by province on the Chaoxian measure plus all controls found no significant impact of Chaoxian on non-Korean banks' location choices.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on ethnic communities and international expansion by explaining heterogeneity in firms' use of coethnic communities across locations. It reframes ethnic communities as informal institutions and identifies formal institutional reliability as a boundary condition for their influence on foreign location choice.

It also contributes to institutional theory by shifting attention from whether formal and informal institutions substitute or complement each other to the scalability of governance mechanisms. The argument is that formal institutions are "first best" for growth-oriented foreign entry because they can support more numerous and broader transactions than community-based informal governance.

**Practical Implication**
Foreign firms should not treat the presence of coethnic communities as uniformly valuable across all locations. The value of such communities is higher where property rights are weak or uncertainty makes formal governance less reliable.

For banks and other growth-oriented entrants, the findings imply that coethnic communities can help manage demand, labor, and information hazards, but they may also limit market scale if firms rely on them as the primary exchange infrastructure. Location strategy should therefore weigh both ethnic community size and the reliability of formal institutions.

**Limitations**
The authors could not examine performance consequences because nearly all Korean bank subsidiaries established in China since 1992 survived through 2016, making survival uninformative. Subsidiary-level financial and operational performance data were unavailable.

The study also does not separately observe the demand, supply, and knowledge benefits of ethnic community reliance. Instead, it relies on prior research for those mechanisms and tests institutional reliability as the boundary condition for the community-location choice relationship.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether ethnic communities matter differently for foreign investments motivated by goals other than growth, such as research and development. Scholars could also compare similar dual entry strategies in developed markets where institutional quality varies across regions within a country.

The authors call for more work from the ethnic community's perspective, including whether the entry of coethnic firms affects jobs, wages, and entrepreneurship for ethnic individuals. Future studies could also investigate whether formal versus informal institutional arrangements shape the complexity of acquisitions, alliances, and other cross-border exchanges.
