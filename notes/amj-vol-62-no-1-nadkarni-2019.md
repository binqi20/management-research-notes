# Only Timeline Will Tell: Temporal Framing of Competitive Announcements and Rivals’ Responses

**Abstract**
Research has focused predominantly on the influence of the language firms use in their announcements on the reactions of constituents such as shareholders and the media. We extend this research by examining a new form of linguistic construction––temporal framing—in the context of competitive interactions. Building on intertemporal choice theory, we theorized and tested how a focal firm’s temporal framing of its competitive action announcements affects rivals’ response speed. We examined three dimensions of temporal framing: temporal vagueness (lack of clarity and completeness regarding timelines), temporal distance (length of the action timeline: proximal versus distal), and frequency (repetition of vagueness and distance cues). Based on analysis of 2,130 competitive action press releases of 28 duopoly firms from 14 industries, we found that the temporal framing dimensions, both individually and interactively, were related to the response speed of rivals. Specifically, temporally vague and distally framed timelines in action announcements delayed rivals’ response speed, and increased frequency of vagueness and distance cues strengthened these effects. This study explicates the linguistic underpinnings of competitive interactions and highlights the importance of temporal framing in the competitive context.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether variation in focal firms' temporal framing of competitive action announcements influences rivals' response speed. It examines whether temporal vagueness, temporal distance, and the frequency of these cues shape how quickly rivals retaliate.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Temporal vagueness in a focal firm's framing of its competitive action announcements will be negatively related to the speed of the rivals' response.
- Hypothesis 2. Temporal distance in a focal firm's framing of its competitive action announcements will be negatively related to the speed of the rivals' response.
- Hypothesis 3. The negative effect of temporal vagueness in the focal firm's framing of its competitive announcements on the rivals' response speed will be stronger when the temporal distance is distal than when it is proximal.
- Hypothesis 4. The negative effect of temporal vagueness in a focal firm's framing of its competitive announcements on rivals' response speed will be stronger when the frequency of vague cues is higher than when it is lower.
- Hypothesis 5. The negative effect of temporal distance on the focal firm's framing of its competitive announcements on the rivals' response speed will be stronger when the frequency of the given temporal distance is higher than when it is lower.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Temporal vagueness, temporal distance, temporal vagueness frequency, and temporal distance frequency in focal firms' competitive action press releases.
- DV(s): Rivals' response speed, modeled as the hazard of a rival response after a focal firm's announcement.
- Mediators: Rivals' perceived uncertainty, perceived urgency, and temporal discounting are theorized mechanisms, but they are not directly estimated as mediators.
- Moderators: Temporal distance moderates the effect of temporal vagueness; temporal vagueness frequency and temporal distance frequency amplify the respective framing effects.

The mechanism is that rivals rely on focal firms' press-release timelines because they lack access to the focal firms' actual action timelines. Vague timelines increase uncertainty about when and how the action will unfold, encouraging rivals to discount the action and delay response. Distal timelines reduce perceived imminence, and repeated vagueness or distance cues increase salience, further slowing rivals' response speed.

**Data & Measures**
- Setting and sample: duopoly industries, defined as industries in which the top two firms hold a market share of at least 80% for the entire duration of the time frame; industries in which incumbents' competitive behaviors are not easily observable (e.g., the financial and real estate industries) were excluded. The final sample comprised 28 firms from 14 industries (four-digit SIC classification), with a study time frame of 1999 to 2011.
- Action data: competitive actions were identified by triangulating the firms' own press releases with external media reports. All articles and press releases between 1999 and 2011 containing the name of one of the 28 sampled firms were collected from Factiva and LexisNexis. Two coders applied structured content analysis to article headlines, retaining only headlines reporting an observable competitive move initiated by a firm to enhance its market position (k = 0.98) and removing duplicate announcements of the same action; this procedure yielded 3,762 unique competitive action announcements. Original press releases issued by the sampled firms were found in PR Newswire and Business Wire for 2,130 competitive actions; 30% of the media articles did not have corresponding original press releases, whereas 41% of the original press releases did not have corresponding media reports. A Heckman (1979) two-stage model was run for the missing original press releases and its inverse Mills ratio was entered as a control.
- Dependent variable: rivals' response speed, computed on the basis of the earliest action taken by a rival in response to a focal firm's announcement, following Yu and Cannella's (2007) process-based approach. Actions of the rival between a focal firm's two successive announcements were deemed responses to the first announcement, and the absence of such actions was deemed a nonresponse; two coders used competitive keywords (e.g., "combat," "to compete") to confirm that a rival's action was a response against the focal firm (Cohen's k = .81). Two variables were generated: response, a dichotomous variable indicating whether a response occurred, and time delay, a continuous variable indicating the timespan. If a response was observed, response was recorded as 1 and time delay as the number of days between the focal firm's announcement and the rival's first response; if no response was observed during the observation window, the case was right-censored with response recorded as 0 and time delay as the number of days between two consecutive announcements.
- Temporal framing measurement, phase one: an inductive check of the intentionality premise through interviews with a panel of experts — top executives from the sampled companies directly involved in strategy development (n = 3) and corporate communications (n = 2), journalists from a business newspaper (n = 3), and industry analysts familiar with the sampled industries (n = 2) — each interview lasting about 30 minutes. The experts corroborated the premise of intentionality behind focal firms' framing of their action timelines.
- Temporal framing measurement, phase two: the framing variables were computed by a customized automated content analysis program applied to the focal firms' original press releases, using a dictionary of dates, numeric time frames, and nonnumeric temporal descriptors (Table A1/A2, Appendix A). Existing programs such as LIWC and Diction were not used because they do not allow simultaneous coding and categorization of numerical and nonnumerical keywords or complex coding of numeric values.
- Temporal vagueness: an ordered categorical measure for each press release — 3 (most vague) = only qualitative cues and no numeric cues, 2 (moderately vague) = a mix of numeric and qualitative cues, 1 (least vague) = only numeric cues and no qualitative cues. Frequency of temporal vagueness = the total number of qualitative cues.
- Temporal distance: the number of days between the date of the press release and the latest date (e.g., August 22, 2013) or time frame (e.g., in the next three years) cited in the press release. For press releases with no numeric cues, three coders blind to the hypotheses categorized the nonnumeric descriptors into proximal, distal, and medium-term distances (ICC = 0.71), and proximal, distal, and medium-term descriptors were assigned respectively the minimum, the maximum, and the median temporal distance found in all the press releases of that focal firm. Frequency of temporal distance = the number of times the longest time frame was repeated in a press release.
- Coding reliability: the coding was manually checked for 10% of the press releases (n = 213), yielding Krippendorff's alpha of 0.87 for extracting qualitative and numeric temporal cues, and 0.91 for temporal vagueness, 0.79 for temporal distance, and 0.87 for temporal frequency.
- Controls: action type, coded from media articles into one of nine types by two coders blind to the hypotheses (k = 0.94, with 15% of headlines validated by an expert panel, ICC = 0.97) and entered as action-type dummies; action announcement timing; the volume and duration of the focal firm's uninterrupted action sequence; whether the framed action was implemented prior to the rival's response; focal and rival firm slack (the average of the current ratio, the debt-equity ratio, and the general and administrative expenses to sales ratio), size (log of yearly employee size), and past performance (one-year change in return on assets); relative market share; industry dynamism, munificence, capital intensity, R&D intensity, and advertising intensity; year dummies for 1999 to 2011 with 2011 as the comparison group; the length of the press release; and the inverse Mills ratio. Independent variables were standardized in all regression models, and all VIF values were below 2.
- Estimation: stratified Cox proportional hazard event history models of the hazard of a rival response, with the hazard evaluated on a daily basis since the focal firm's announcement and the observation closed when the rival responded or the focal firm announced another action; an increase in response speed is represented by an increase in the hazard function. The full-sample Cox regression models report 47,688 observations. Moderation was tested both with multiplicative interaction terms and with split-sample marginal-effect models based on a median split, compared using Wald chi-square tests, with interaction plots drawn at one standard deviation above and below the mean.
- Identification: the analysis is archival and associational. Potential endogeneity was addressed with a recommended two-stage procedure in which four holdout focal firm variables (number of CEO stock options, percentage of institutional ownership, debt to equity ratio, and stock price declines) were used in first-stage models of temporal vagueness and temporal distance, and the fitted values and residuals were entered in the second stage (Appendix B).

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported: temporal vagueness relates negatively to rivals' response speed (b = -0.13, p < .05). If temporal vagueness in the focal firm's press release increases by one unit, the hazard of response by the rival decreases by 12%.
- Hypothesis 2 supported: temporal distance relates negatively to rivals' response speed (b = -0.32, p < .001). If temporal distance increases by one unit, the hazard of response by the rival decreases by 27%.
- Hypothesis 3 supported: the interaction of temporal vagueness and temporal distance is negative and significant (b = -0.50, p < .001). In the split-sample tests the effect of temporal vagueness on rivals' response speed is strong and negative for the distal subgroup (b = -0.36, p < .001) but not significant for the proximal subgroup (b = 0.07, n.s.), and the Wald chi-square statistic confirms the difference (11.8, p < .001). A one-unit increase in this interaction is associated with a 39% decrease in the rival's hazard of response.
- Hypothesis 4 supported: the interaction of temporal vagueness and temporal vagueness frequency is negative and significant (b = -0.74, p < .001). The effect of temporal vagueness is negative and significant for the high-frequency subgroup (b = -0.81, p < .001) but not significant for the low-frequency subgroup (b = -0.02, n.s.), with a chi-square statistic of 16.0, p < .001. A one-unit increase in this interaction is associated with a 52% decrease in the rival's hazard of response.
- Hypothesis 5 supported: the interaction of temporal distance and temporal distance frequency is significant (b = -0.28, p < .05). Temporal distance exerts a strong negative effect on rivals' response speed when the frequency is high (b = -0.45, p < .001), but this effect is not significant when the frequency is low (b = 0.06, n.s.), with a chi-square statistic of 7.0, p < .01. A one-unit increase in this interaction is associated with a 24% decrease in the rival's hazard of response.
- Frequency main effects: in the model that adds the study variables, temporal vagueness frequency (b = -0.10) and temporal distance frequency (b = 0.07) are reported without significance markers; the paper hypothesizes frequency only as a moderator, not as a main effect.
- Construct distinctness in the descriptive statistics: the correlation between temporal vagueness and temporal distance is low (r = -0.07); the length of the press release relates much more strongly to the frequency of temporal vagueness (r = 0.57) than to the frequency of temporal distance (r = 0.23); and action announcement timing correlates weakly with temporal distance (r = 0.04).
- Robustness: the results are reported as consistent with the main results under an alternative vagueness measure based on the completeness of numeric cues, an alternative frequency-of-vagueness measure (vague cues divided by total temporal cues), several fixed observation windows (30, 60, 90, 120, and 180 days), alternative start dates for identifying the first action-response dyads (2000, 2003, and 2006), coded action characteristics in place of action-type dummies, dropping actions with no corresponding media report, and firm fixed effects. In the two-stage endogeneity models the residual of temporal distance (b = -0.30, p < .01) and the residual of temporal vagueness (b = -0.13, p < .05) remain negative predictors of the hazard of response.
- Summary of the observed retaliation pattern (Table 4): clear and proximal timelines drew the most speedy retaliation from rivals; clear and distal timelines and vague and proximal timelines drew moderately speedy retaliation; vague and distal timelines drew the most delayed retaliation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends competitive dynamics research by adding linguistic temporal framing to action-based explanations of rivalry. It shows that rivals respond not only to observable action characteristics, announcement timing, and competitive context, but also to how firms linguistically present action timelines.

The paper also contributes to organizational communication research by showing a competitive downside of language that may be favorable to constituents. Clear and proximal framing can please shareholders or other constituents but also invite faster rival retaliation, creating a trade-off in how firms communicate competitive actions.

**Practical Implication**
Managers communicating competitive actions face a trade-off between mobilizing constituents and deterring rivals. Clear, proximal timelines may generate favorable reactions from shareholders, suppliers, and complementors, but they can also accelerate competitor retaliation.

For firms in intense rivalry, vague or distal temporal framing can delay rival responses, but it may weaken constituent confidence. Communication strategy should therefore consider both public-relations audiences and competitors that monitor the same announcements.

**Limitations**
The sample is restricted to duopoly industries, where rivalry is unusually intense and the top two firms have explicit competitive interdependence. The authors caution that the framing effects may not generalize to less concentrated industries with more varied rivals and competitive positions.

The study focuses only on temporal framing of competitive action announcements and on competitors' responses. It does not test other forms of framing, such as positive versus negative framing or attribution framing, and it does not directly examine reactions from other audiences such as shareholders.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine temporal framing in less concentrated industries and test whether the effects differ when firms face many rivals with heterogeneous stakes. Studies could also investigate other framing forms, including valence framing and attribution framing, to clarify how language more broadly shapes interfirm rivalry.

The authors also point to research on other audiences, especially shareholders. Future studies could examine how firms balance constituent reactions against competitive deterrence when deciding whether to frame action timelines clearly, vaguely, proximally, or distally.
