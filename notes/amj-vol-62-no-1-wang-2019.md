# Human Capital Acquisition and Organizational Innovation: A Temporal Perspective

**Abstract**
Newcomers contribute to organizational innovation by bringing in new knowledge and ideas, on the one hand, and by collaborating and exchanging with incumbents, on the other. We propose that an organization’s ability to use these contributions is influenced by hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion, which affect both the flow of new ideas into the organization and the level of collaboration between newcomers and incumbents. Using four years of data from a large, multi-industry sample, we find that hiring rate and hiring rate dispersion increase organizational innovation. We also find that increases in hiring rates from year to year are positively related to innovation for organizations with more collaborative work practices, while the relationship between hiring rate dispersion and innovation is less positive when organizations have more collaborative work practices. This study highlights how temporal patterns of hiring influence human capital acquisition and development.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how temporal patterns of managerial and professional hiring influence organizational innovation. It specifically examines whether hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion affect product and process innovation, and whether collaborative work practices condition those relationships.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Managerial and professional hiring rate is positively related to organizational innovation.
- H2: The relationship between managerial and professional hiring rate and organizational innovation is moderated by CWPs; this relationship is stronger (more positive) when organizations have more CWPs.
- H3: After accounting for managerial and professional hiring rate, hiring rate change in these positions is positively related to organizational innovation.
- H4: The relationship between managerial and professional hiring rate change and organizational innovation is moderated by CWPs; this relationship is stronger (more positive) when organizations have more CWPs.
- H5: After accounting for managerial and professional hiring rate and hiring rate change, hiring rate dispersion for these positions is positively related to organizational innovation.
- H6: The relationship between managerial and professional hiring rate dispersion and organizational innovation is moderated by CWPs; this relationship is weaker (less positive) when organizations have more CWPs.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Managerial and professional hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion.
- DV(s): Organizational innovation, measured as an ordinal index of improved and new products, services, and processes.
- Mediators: Knowledge acquisition, knowledge combination, and knowledge exchange are theorized mechanisms, but they are not directly measured as mediators.
- Moderators: Collaborative work practices moderate the effects of hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion.

The mechanism is that managerial and professional newcomers bring novel knowledge, skills, abilities, and perspectives that can support product and process innovation. Higher hiring rates and more time-dispersed hiring increase the flow of new ideas while also shaping how easily newcomers and incumbents collaborate. Collaborative work practices support communication, participation, information sharing, and trust, which can help organizations integrate newcomers when hiring increases, but can also substitute partly for the integration benefits of evenly dispersed hiring.

**Data & Measures**
The data are the 2003-2006 workplace surveys of the Workplace and Employee Survey (WES), collected and administered by Statistics Canada, which drew the WES samples from the Business Register, a list of workplaces operating in specific locations. Response rates to the 2003-2006 workplace surveys were 83.1%, 81.7%, 77.7%, and 74.9%, respectively. Nonprofit enterprises and organizations with fewer than 10 employees were excluded, leaving a sample of 1,006 organizations that hired during at least one year of the newcomer observation window. The 2003-2005 workplace surveys measure the independent variables and the 2006 workplace survey measures the dependent variable, which the authors state further mitigates potential issues of common-method bias. DV: organizational innovation, an ordinal index that ranges between 0 and 4 and is formed from four 2006 survey items (improved processes; improved goods or services; new processes; new goods or services), where zero indicates no innovation, one improved processes or products or services, two improved processes and products or services, three new processes or products or services, and four new processes and products or services; across the 1,006 sampled organizations, 434 (43%), 68 (7%), 39 (4%), 222 (22%), and 243 (24%) had innovation scores of 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. IV hiring rate for managers and professionals: the total number of managerial and professional new hires during 2003, 2004, and 2005 divided by the total number of managers and professionals in the organization at the beginning of each year, respectively, winsorized by specifying 1% of observations in each tail and averaged across the three years. IV hiring rate change: the differences in hiring rates between 2003 and 2004 and between 2004 and 2005, averaged across the two changes. IV hiring rate dispersion: a refined entropy index (Raghunathan, 1995) computed for the 2003-2005 window, where Pi is the ratio of the hiring rate for managers and professionals in each year over the total hiring rates for these positions during 2003-2005 and a Pi of zero is replaced by 0.0001; the index ranges from zero to one, and higher values mean the organization hired more evenly across the three years. Moderator CWPs: an additive index of six "yes-no" items asked in the 2003 and 2005 workplace surveys, covering employee suggestion programs, information-sharing programs, problem-solving teams, self-directed teams, labor-management committees, and flexible work design (Cronbach's alpha = 0.73), divided by six to give the ratio of CWPs in place for 2003 and 2005 and then averaged across the two years. Controls, drawn from the 2003-2005 workplace surveys and averaged across the three years unless noted: hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion for the other job categories; turnover rate (voluntary resignations and quits plus involuntary dismissals over total employees at the beginning of each year); firm size (log of total employees); firm age (log of years in operation); return on sales; sales growth rate; union density; training intensity; pay level (log of payroll per employee); importance of employee involvement (two five-point items, Cronbach's alpha = 0.71); prior innovation; and 13 dummy variables for the 14 industries in the sample. Because the innovation measure is ordinal, the hypotheses are tested with ordinal logistic regressions on organizational innovation; the design is associational, with the hiring measures observed before the innovation outcome.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: in Model 1 the coefficient of hiring rate for managers and professionals was positive and significant (b = 5.13, p < 0.001), and a one standard deviation increase in hiring rate for these positions (11%) results in a 7% decrease in the probability of the lowest innovation outcome (innovation = 0) and a 10% increase in the probability of the highest innovation outcome (innovation = 4). Hypothesis 2 did not receive statistical support: in Model 2 the interaction between hiring rate for managers and professionals and CWPs was positive (b = 4.39, n.s.), which the authors describe as consistent with the direction of Hypothesis 2 but not statistically significant. Hypothesis 3 was not supported: in Model 3 the coefficient of hiring rate change for managers and professionals was positive and nonsignificant (b = 0.87, n.s.). Hypothesis 4 was supported: in Model 4 the interaction between hiring rate change for managers and professionals and CWPs was positive and significant (b = 6.90, p < 0.05); for organizations with more CWPs a one standard deviation increase in hiring rate change (11%) is associated with a 7% decrease in the probability of the lowest innovation outcome and a 7% increase in the probability of the highest, whereas for organizations with fewer CWPs the same increase is associated with a 2% increase in the probability of the lowest innovation outcome and a 2% decrease in the probability of the highest, so hiring rate change was positively related to innovation for organizations with more CWPs but not for organizations with fewer CWPs. Hypothesis 5 was supported: in Model 5 the coefficient of hiring rate dispersion for managers and professionals was positive and significant (b = 0.62, p < 0.05), and a one standard deviation increase in dispersion for these positions (39%) results in a 5% decrease in the probability of the lowest innovation outcome and a 4% increase in the probability of the highest. Hypothesis 6 was supported: in Model 6 the interaction between hiring rate dispersion for managers and professionals and CWPs was negative and significant (b = -1.85, p < 0.05); for organizations with fewer CWPs a one standard deviation increase in dispersion is associated with a 7% decrease in the probability of the lowest innovation outcome and a 5% increase in the probability of the highest, while for organizations with more CWPs the corresponding changes are a 1% decrease in the lowest outcome and a 1.5% increase in the highest, so the effects of hiring rate dispersion on innovation were greater for organizations with fewer CWPs. Model 7 enters all three interaction terms together (CWPs x hiring rate = 16.20, p < 0.001; CWPs x hiring rate change = 9.60, p < 0.01; CWPs x hiring rate dispersion = -4.78, p < 0.001), and the authors report that these coefficients were larger than those in the previous models while their standard errors did not increase proportionally, that the variance inflation factors of the Model 7 interaction terms were all lower than 3, and that the Model 7 results are consistent with and further support their hypotheses. In the limitations discussion the authors also note that Figures 1 and 2 suggest hiring rate change and hiring rate dispersion did not affect the probabilities of improvements in products or processes (innovation = 1 or innovation = 2).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances strategic human capital and learning-by-hiring research by shifting attention from who organizations hire to when they hire. It separates hiring rate, hiring rate change, and hiring rate dispersion to show that temporal hiring patterns have distinct implications for organizational innovation.

It also extends human capital research by treating hiring as more than replacement for employee turnover. The study argues that hiring has strategic value because continuous or increasing inflows of managerial and professional newcomers shape both new knowledge acquisition and the development of collaborative human capital.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that organizations seeking innovation should manage hiring as a temporal process, not only as a headcount or talent-quality decision. Regular hiring of managers and professionals can maintain a continuous inflow of new knowledge and perspectives.

The study also suggests that collaborative work practices are especially useful when hiring rates increase or fluctuate, because they help integrate newcomers with incumbents. Organizations with fewer such practices may need more continuous and even hiring to reduce integration problems.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the WES data do not include specific measures of knowledge combination and exchange, so the proposed mechanisms are inferred rather than directly tested. The data also focus on managerial and professional newcomers and lack finer-grained measures of newcomer quality, exact within-year hiring timing, and whether new hires are returnees.

The innovation outcome is an additive self-reported index of improved and new products and processes, without information on ultimate economic benefits. The authors also note that industry contexts may differ because some organizations hire specifically for innovation tasks while service and retail businesses may hire mainly to meet operational demand.

**Future Research**
Future research could directly measure how different kinds of knowledge, skills, abilities, and other characteristics diffuse after hiring and how long that diffusion takes. Researchers could also examine finer-grained hiring timing, returnees, newcomer quality, and other strategic or cultural conditions that shape whether new hires contribute to innovation.

The authors also call for research using alternative innovation measures, such as sales and profits from new products and services, to test the economic consequences of hiring patterns. Additional studies could examine whether temporal hiring patterns influence broader outcomes beyond innovation, including productivity and profitability, across industries with different hiring motives.
