# Dominant Coalitions Directing Acquisitions: Different Decision Makers, Different Decisions

**Abstract**
Coalitions are important in organizational decision making, but the question of how coalitions are built and make decisions in response to firm performance is still not sufficiently explored. In this study, we develop and test theory on how potential coalitions are built through shared experience and recruitment of allies. When organizations respond to performance relative to aspiration levels, either as problemistic search following low performance or opportunity exploration following high performance, members form coalitions to influence decisions. We develop theory of coalition formation that builds on upper echelons theory and the theory of dominant coalitions to predict how past experience of decision makers leads to preferred actions by each member and subsequent coalition formation. We use this theory to make new measures of potential coalitions and apply it to acquisitions made by firms in China. We find evidence that the experience of members of the key decision-making group—the board of directors—affects the potential coalition building, and hence the type of acquisition target, as predicted.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how potential dominant coalitions are built within boards and how those coalitions shape acquisition decisions when firm performance differs from aspiration levels. It focuses on whether board members' state versus market experience changes the type of acquisition target, and whether similar coalition logic appears in state-bank loan choices.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Because the theory is premised on the dominant coalition making decisions that are triggered by organizational performance relative to aspiration levels, the paper states that the hypotheses are interaction effects of the dominant coalition and the performance level, which distinguishes them from upper echelons hypotheses (which do not consider the performance level jointly with the formation of the dominant coalition) and from performance feedback hypotheses (which do not consider the composition of the decision-making group).
Hypothesis 1. When performance compared with aspiration level triggers organizational change, a board with a stronger potential state coalition is less likely to choose market-oriented actions.
Hypothesis 2. When performance compared with aspiration level triggers organizational change, a board with a stronger potential state coalition is more likely to choose state-related actions.
The authors also raise a "realistic null hypothesis" that boards with a strong potential state coalition will lead to the firm not searching at all, because profitability is an insufficiently important goal. They keep it in mind but note that it makes a clearer prediction on the rate of searching than on the choice of action, and proceed to Hypothesis 2 as the stated prediction. The main test of the theory is the extent to which firms choose market-oriented acquisition targets; loans from the state bank, an action preferred by the state coalition, provide an additional empirical test.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Board potential state coalition, measured with stateness factor, stateness ASW, and stateness proportion; firm performance relative to historical and social aspiration levels.
- DV(s): Market orientation of acquisition target type, nature of market-oriented acquisition, and whether a loan comes from a state-owned or policy bank.
- Mediators: Coalition building is theorized as the process linking decision-maker experience to strategic choices, but it is not directly estimated as a mediator.
- Moderators: Performance above or below historical and social aspiration levels conditions when coalition preferences shape decisions.

The mechanism is that board members use prior state or market experience to evaluate which strategic actions feel familiar, feasible, and proximate. Performance below or above aspiration levels triggers a decision occasion, after which experience-based subgroups try to assemble dominant coalitions by relying on member number, commitment, status, and recruitable allies. In the acquisition tests, stronger state coalitions reduce responsiveness toward market-oriented acquisitions, whereas weaker state coalitions leave more scope for market-oriented acquisition choices. The separate state-bank loan analysis is treated more cautiously: its results run contrary to Hypothesis 2 and suggest a boundary condition because loan-source decisions mix firm and bank decision making.

**Data & Measures**
Two archival sources are used. The first is the China Stock Market and Accounting Research (CSMAR) database, which covers the ownership, board, and financial data of all listed firms in China since 1992 and supplied complete resume data on each director. The second is the WIND database, which provides detailed information on firm M&As and loans, including the M&A date of announcement, type of acquisition, and nature of the acquisition, and, for loans, the lender and the stated purpose. WIND was merged with one-year lagged CSMAR ownership data, board data, and fiscal year performance data, and the dataset covered every M&A and loan application between 2000 and 2012 by all Chinese listed firms. Three dependent variables are used. Type of acquisition is 0 for an internal acquisition, 1 for a state-bridged acquisition, and 2 for a market-oriented acquisition; Table 1 tabulates 31,442 acquisitions of equity or assets, of which 11,769 (37.43%) are internal, 3,894 (12.38%) state-bridged, and 15,779 (50.18%) market-oriented. Nature of acquisition, defined only within market-oriented acquisitions, is 0 for acquisition of assets without any equity stake, 1 for acquisition of minority equity, and 2 for acquisition of a controlling equity stake (3,217 asset-only, 7,298 minority share, and 5,264 majority share events in Table 1). State-bank loan takes the value of 1 when the loan is taken from a state-owned bank or a policy bank and 0 for loans taken from a commercial bank, either Chinese or foreign; only loans of a size exceeding 1% of firm assets are analyzed, so the variable measures the selection of loan source rather than whether a loan is taken.
The focal independent variable, the potential state coalition, is measured three ways from director resumes. Market experience is the proportion of board members owning shares in the company or having education or work experience in an Anglo-Saxon nation, and state experience is the proportion of board members with work experience in a state agency or in a state-owned bank. The stateness factor is taken from a principal factor analysis of all boards using the proportions of board members with two sources of state experience, one source of state experience, both state and market experience, and market experience, with each board member weighted linearly by age (the zero point set to the youngest board member's age) to take status into account; the retained first factor loads positively on the state and both-experience proportions and negatively on market experience (Table 2). The stateness ASW is a faultline measure: market and state experience are treated as binary characteristics, the board is reduced to two groups using the Average Silhouette Weight procedure, the largest group is selected as the dominant coalition, and the measure is the proportion of board members in that group multiplied by their average state experience. The stateness proportion is a heuristic measure that accounts only for member proportions, ignoring commitment and status: it equals 1 when more than half the board has only state experience and 0.5 when state-only members exceed market-only members and the sum of state-only and neutral (both or none) members exceeds a threshold p, with the mirror-image negative values when market-only membership dominates and 0 for the remainder; the reported tables set p conservatively to 0.75, and p as low as 0.6 gave similar findings. Performance is calculated as return on assets (ROA) minus either a historical aspiration level (a weighted average of past performance with most of the weight, 0.9, assigned to the most recent ROA) or a social aspiration level (the average ROA of all other firms in the same three-digit industry); each performance-aspiration gap is divided into performance above and below the aspiration level and interacted with the board experience variables. Controls are firm age, firm size (logarithm of total assets), market-to-book ratio, debt-to-equity ratio, prior acquisition experience entered as three cumulative counts (assets only, minority stake, controlling stake), percentage of free cash flow, diversification level (count of industries a firm engages in), proportion of state ownership, and the inverse Mills ratio.
Estimation uses a sequential model rather than a competing-risks event history model, because the sequential model best captures dependencies among the alternatives. An event history model of the acquisition rate — the hazard rate model reported in Table 2, Model 7 of Greve and Zhang (2017: 685) — forms an inverse Mills ratio to control for selectivity (Heckman, 1979), which is entered as a control variable in an ordered logit model of the target choice, so the findings indicate choices rather than rates of change. Results are shown from an ordinary ordered logit assuming proportional odds; a general ordered logit produced equivalent findings for the type of acquisition and stronger support for the hypotheses for the nature of acquisition. State-bank loans are modeled with logit. All models carry province and industry fixed effects and are reported both with and without year fixed effects, because average stateness decreased over time and the authors judge the correct estimate to be intermediate between the two; robust standard errors are clustered on the firm. The design is observational archival panel analysis with a selectivity correction, and no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy is claimed. Table 3 notes that a total of 2,337 firms and 28,847 observations comprise the data; the type-of-acquisition models report 28,847 observations, the nature-of-acquisition models 14,154 observations, and the state-bank loan models 16,823 observations.

**Key Findings**
For the type of acquisition (Table 4, 28,847 observations), the main effects of performance indicate that market-oriented acquisitions are more likely to be chosen when performance is high relative to the historical aspiration level, but less likely as it is high relative to the social aspiration level, so performance improvements produce the confidence to choose more market-oriented acquisitions whereas higher performance than other firms induces caution. In Models 2 through 7, the interactions with the board stateness measures show support for Hypothesis 1 for performance above the historical aspiration level both without and with year fixed effects, as every coefficient estimate of the interactions shows that a stronger state coalition weakens the effect of performance on the choice of market-oriented acquisitions. Hypothesis 1 has full support above the social aspiration level without year fixed effects, but partial loss of significance with year fixed effects. Below the aspiration levels there are significant coefficient estimates without fixed effects, but the significance is lost when the year fixed effects are entered. Graphing the best-fitting Model 5 (Figure 1), for low-stateness boards the predicted probability of a market-oriented acquisition increases from 50 to 75% when performance is above the historical aspiration level, and drops from 50 to 34% when performance is above the social aspiration level; the shift is smaller for average stateness boards but is still statistically significant, and the stateness ASW measure gives similar graphs.
For the nature of acquisition (Table 5, 14,154 observations), which examines only the subset of acquisitions that are already highly market-oriented, the analysis supports Hypothesis 1 above the historical aspiration level without year fixed effects, and these coefficient estimates remain significant in the fixed-effect model using the faultline measure (which has best fit to the data) and the proportion measure. Hypothesis 1 has full support above the social aspiration level with and without year fixed effects. There is a shift toward market-oriented majority control acquisitions as a main effect, but the state coalition works against this effect, and in the best-fitting Model 6 (Figure 2) the effect sizes are weaker overall while the interaction with stateness is strong enough that high performance and stateness predict that firms will avoid taking higher market orientation such as majority acquisitions.
For state-bank loans (Table 6, 16,823 observations), the result runs contrary to Hypothesis 2: both without and with fixed effects, stateness works against the main effect of performance relative to the historical aspiration level. Boards with low stateness avoid state banks when performance is improving, but high stateness cancels this effect, and firms with high stateness do not respond to the performance by changing the source of their loans, which the authors read as a boundary condition on the theory. They also caution that performance relative to social aspiration levels cannot be interpreted as indicating firm intentions in this test, because banks use profitability compared to other firms to assess loan risk, making these coefficients a mixture of firm and bank decision making. In the discussion the authors report that analysis using the factor analysis, faultline, or proportion measures produced consistent results.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to dominant coalition theory by turning coalition building into measurable potential coalitions based on board members' experience, commitment, status, and recruitable allies. It also extends upper echelons theory by replacing simple average-composition logic with a coalition-based account of how decision-making groups reach strategic choices.

It contributes to performance feedback theory by making upper-echelons effects contingent on performance relative to aspiration levels. The resulting account links the trigger for organizational change with the content of the decision selected by the board.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that boards' experience composition can shape consequential acquisition choices, not merely the propensity to change. For Chinese listed firms, state-experienced boards may be more likely to favor internal or state-bridged actions, while market-experienced coalitions support more market-oriented acquisitions when performance feedback creates a problem or opportunity.

The paper also implies that governance reform does not erase legacy decision logics simply by formalizing board structures. Directors' prior state and market experience remain relevant to how boards interpret performance feedback and choose strategic responses.

**Limitations**
The study predicts potential rather than realized coalitions, so the authors cannot observe the actual bargaining, persuasion, agenda control, or alliance recruitment inside each board decision. They also note that features of each specific choice could make the realized coalition differ from the potential coalition inferred from board composition.

The state-bank loan test is a boundary condition because loan-source choices mix firm and bank decision making: banks assess loan risk using profitability relative to other firms, and high-stateness firms do not respond to performance by changing loan source in the same way the acquisition theory predicts.

**Future Research**
Future research could apply the coalition-building approach to other kinds of decision-maker experience, decision-making groups, organizational behaviors, and high-stakes decisions. The authors specifically suggest studying financial, technological, and market affiliations in top management teams and boards.

Further work could also examine the actual processes of coalition influence, including agenda control, information release, order of speech, and other procedural interventions. Such studies would extend the paper's potential-coalition measures toward more direct evidence on how coalitions are built and retained across decisions.
