# Actions Speak Louder than Words: How Figurative Language and Gesturing in Entrepreneurial Pitches Influences Investment Judgments

**Abstract**
A key challenge for entrepreneurs is to convince investors of their business ideas via a pitch. While scholars have started to explore how entrepreneurs convey their passion and preparedness in a pitch, they have overlooked the possible variation that exists in entrepreneurs’ verbal and nonverbal expressions. We build on research in cognitive science and entrepreneurship to examine the nature and influence of specific forms of speech and gesturing used by entrepreneurs when pitching. In an initial qualitative field study we identify distinct pitching strategies entrepreneurs use, involving different combinations of verbal tactics (i.e., using literal and figurative language to frame a venture) and gesture (i.e., using different types of hand gestures to emphasize parts of their pitch and convey product and venture ideas). In a subsequent experimental study, we examine the impact of these strategies on investors’ propensity to invest. We find that, although variation in the type of language used by an entrepreneur has limited effects, using gestures to depict and symbolize business ideas has strong positive effects. Our findings indicate that the skilled use of gestures by entrepreneurs helps potential investors imagine aspects of a new venture for themselves, thereby enhancing perception of its investment potential.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how entrepreneurs combine verbal tactics and expressive nonverbal behaviors when pitching to investors, and how those channels separately and jointly influence potential investors' judgments. It specifically examines whether figurative language, gesturing, and mental imagery shape investors' propensity to invest.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Study 1 is inductive and states no a priori hypotheses. The four hypotheses below are developed in Study 2 by extending the pitching typology that emerged from Study 1.
- Hypothesis 1. When entrepreneurs use a high (vs. low) level of figurative language when pitching their business, people will be more inclined to invest.
- Hypothesis 2. When entrepreneurs use a high (vs. low) level of gesturing to emphasize and depict their ideas when pitching their business, people will be more inclined to invest.
- Hypothesis 3. There will be a significant interaction effect between figurative language and gesture on the propensity to invest; specifically, individuals will be more inclined to invest in a venture when entrepreneurs use a high (vs. low) level of figurative language combined with a high (vs. low) level of gesturing to emphasize and depict their ideas when pitching their business.
- Hypothesis 4. The degree to which gestures evoke mental imagery among potential investors mediates the effect of an entrepreneur's gestures on the propensity to invest.

The moderated-mediation expectation that the indirect effect of gesture through mental imagery depends on the level of figurative language is not stated as a hypothesis; the authors introduce it later as a post hoc analysis.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / pitching strategies: Literal, rhetorical, demonstrative, and integrative pitching approaches, distinguished by high or low use of figurative language and high or low use of gesturing.
- Study 1 process: Inductive coding of naturalistic technology-entrepreneur pitches identifies variation in literal versus figurative language and beat, iconic, deictic, and metaphoric gestures.
- Study 2 experimental factors: High versus low figurative language and high versus low gesturing in video pitches.
- Outcome: Propensity to invest, measured through investment intentions and investment likelihood.
- Mediators: Mental imagery is the focal mediator; passion and preparedness are comparative mechanisms.
- Boundary condition: The indirect effect of gesture through mental imagery is stronger when gestures are coupled with figurative language.

The central process is that ideational gestures do more than signal passion: they help viewers mentally simulate the product, venture, and market. Figurative language alone has limited direct impact, but gestures that depict and symbolize the business idea increase propensity to invest by evoking mental imagery. The integrative value of figurative language appears mainly in strengthening the gesture-to-imagery pathway rather than directly persuading investors.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data: an entire cohort of 17 independent technology entrepreneurs pitching to specialist technology investors at a regional investment forum in the North of England. All pitches were recorded live with cameras placed discreetly at the side and back of the room; two of the authors attended the event, made field notes, and then analyzed each of the 17 videotaped pitches.
- Study 1 coding: open, inductive coding first (Corbin & Strauss, 2008), then deductive coding using established protocols — the Pragglejaz Group (2007) applied-linguistics protocol to identify figurative words and expressions, and a cognitive-linguistics protocol (Cienki, 2005) focused on gesture strokes. Hand movements were split into gestures and non-gestures, then categorized as "beat" gestures or "ideational" gestures, with ideational gestures further subdivided into metaphoric, iconic, and deictic. Axial coding followed to relate the emerging patterns to theoretical precedents.
- Study 1 measures: Table 1 reports "proportion figurative" as the number of figurative words per 1,000 words of the pitch, and gesture frequency as the number of gestures used per minute; both were banded inductively into high versus low. Reliability was calculated on a sample of five pitches, with an alpha coefficient of 0.85 for the figurative-language coding and 0.90 for the gesture coding.
- Study 2 design: a randomized 2 (figurative language: high, low) x 2 (gesture: high, low) between-subjects experiment with participants randomly assigned to one of the four treatment groups. The authors chose an experiment because it controls the language and gesture used and because an experimental design permits robust causal inferences. Four videos were created in which a professional actor performed four versions of the same pitch, scripted from a real case (with identifying details changed) for a digital temperature-controlled compression device for treating sports and physical injuries; the script, product information, and other nonverbal parameters were held constant across conditions.
- Study 2 samples: Sample 1 comprised 124 investors with significant experience investing in new ventures, recruited at national investor events and by email through a regional investment hub in Manchester, UK (65 in person and 59 by email, with no significant difference in their pitch evaluations). Sample 2 comprised 180 business students from a large U.K. university, each paid £5, used as a test of empirical generalization. The manipulation check used a separate group of 128 working adults recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk and paid one dollar.
- Manipulation checks: perceived figurative language was the mean of three items (alpha = .72) and perceived gesturing the mean of three further items (alpha = .87), both on 11-point scales (0 = not at all to 10 = a lot).
- Dependent variable, propensity to invest: four investment-intention items on a five-point Likert scale (sample 1 alpha = .94; sample 2 alpha = .82) plus a single investment-likelihood item on an 11-point scale anchored at 0 (0% chance) and 10 (100% certain). A principal components analysis on sample 1 and a confirmatory factor analysis on sample 2 supported a single underlying factor, so the two measures were standardized and combined into one summative indicator termed "propensity to invest" (five-item scale: sample 1 alpha = .90; sample 2 alpha = .73).
- Mediator, mental imagery: six items from the communication-evoked imagery measure developed by Bone and Ellen (1992) and refined by Babin and Burns (1998), covering imagery quantity (three items) and imagery elaboration (three items) on seven-point Likert scales, summed into a single measure (sample 1 alpha = .86; sample 2 alpha = .88).
- Comparative mediators, passion and preparedness: the 11-item scale developed by Chen et al. (2009), with five preparedness items and six affective-passion items on five-point scales (passion: sample 1 alpha = .92, sample 2 alpha = .92; preparedness: sample 1 alpha = .87, sample 2 alpha = .88).
- Controls: age and gender in both samples, plus years of experience investing in new ventures and a dummy-coded investor type in sample 1. None of the controls was significantly correlated with the dependent variable and the pattern of results was the same with and without them, so the paper reports results without controls.
- Estimation: a two-way analysis of variance tested Hypotheses 1 to 3, concerning respectively the main effect of figurative language, the main effect of gesturing, and their interaction effect on the propensity to invest. Mediation was tested with Hayes's (2013) PROCESS procedure, bootstrapping 5,000 replacement samples to construct bias-correcting confidence intervals; preparedness was dropped as a mediator after gesture proved not significantly related to it, leaving mental imagery and passion as parallel mediators. A post hoc conditional process analysis and the index of moderated mediation (Hayes, 2015) tested whether the indirect effects varied with the level of figurative language.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: coding the 17 pitches produced four distinct pitching strategies structured around the same topics but differing in language and gesture use — the literal approach (low gesture, low figurative language; six cases), the rhetorical approach (low gesture, high figurative language; two cases), the demonstrative approach (high gesture, low figurative language; five cases), and the integrative approach (high gesture, high figurative language; four cases).
- Hypothesis 1 is not supported in either sample: there was no main effect of figurative language on the propensity to invest (sample 1: F(1, 120) = 0.08, p = .76; sample 2: F(1, 176) = 3.08, p = .08).
- Hypothesis 2 is supported in both samples: the main effect of gesture was significant (sample 1: F(1, 120) = 6.28, p = .01, eta-squared = .05; sample 2: F(1, 176) = 7.15, p = .008, eta-squared = .04), with a high level of gesturing producing greater propensity to invest (sample 1: M = 0.22, SE = .12; sample 2: M = 0.17, SE = .09) than a low level (sample 1: M = -0.19, SE = .11; sample 2: M = -0.17, SE = .09).
- Hypothesis 3 is not supported in either sample: the interaction between figurative language and gesture was not significant (sample 1: F(1, 120) = 0.02, p = .89; sample 2: F(1, 176) = 0.53, p = .47).
- Hypothesis 4 is supported in both samples: the indirect effect of gesture through mental imagery was significant (sample 1: b = .20, SE = .09, 95% CI [0.04, 0.39]; sample 2: b = .10, SE = .06, 95% CI [0.01, 0.24]).
- The comparison mediators diverge by sample. The indirect effect of gesture via passion was not significant for sample 1 (b = .14, SE = .12, 95% CI [-0.09, 0.39]) but was significant for sample 2 (b = .13, SE = .06, 95% CI [0.04, 0.27]), where it did not differ significantly from the imagery pathway (contrast coefficient = .04, SE = .08, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.18]). The authors read this as mental imagery rather than passion mediating the effect of gesture for professional investors, while mental imagery and passion collectively mediate it for non-professional evaluators. Gesture was not significantly related to preparedness in either sample (sample 1: b = .14, SE = .16, t = .85, p = .40; sample 2: b = .13, SE = .11, t = 1.25, p = .21).
- In the full regression predicting propensity to invest (Table 4), mental imagery was significant in both samples (sample 1: b = .33, p < .001; sample 2: b = .29, p < .001), passion was significant only in sample 2 (b = .19, p < .05; sample 1: b = .11, ns), and the direct effect of gesture was not significant in either sample (sample 1: b = .06; sample 2: b = .10); R-squared was 0.33 for sample 1 and 0.26 for sample 2.
- Post hoc moderated mediation: when a pitch involved a low level of figurative language, the indirect effect of gesture through mental imagery was not significant (sample 1: b = .11, SE = .08, 95% CI [0.00, 0.31]; sample 2: b = .04, SE = .04, 95% CI [-0.01, 0.17]), whereas when figurative language was high it was significant (sample 1: b = .28, SE = .11, 95% CI [0.08, 0.52]; sample 2: b = .14, SE = .08, 95% CI [0.01, 0.32]). The index of moderated mediation was significant for mental imagery in both samples (sample 1: .17, SE = .09, 95% CI [0.04, 0.45]; sample 2: .10, SE = .07, 95% CI [0.01, 0.30]) but not for passion (sample 1: -.29, SE = .23, 95% CI [-0.78, 0.12]; sample 2: .08, SE = .10, 95% CI [-0.12, 0.28]).
- Effect size for investors: the likelihood of investing was 12.06% higher for pitches with a high level of gesturing compared to those with a low level of gesturing (95% CI mean difference [3.63%, 18.29%]).
- Overall the authors conclude that figurative language had a limited direct effect across the two samples, while gesturing raised the propensity to invest by evoking mental imagery of the venture and its product, with that indirect effect particularly strong when gestures were coupled with figurative language.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends entrepreneurship research on pitching by shifting attention from generic passion displays to the communicative functions of specific verbal and nonverbal behaviors. It shows that gestures can carry meaning, not only affect, and that beat and ideational gestures should not be collapsed into a simple frequency-based indicator of passion.

It also qualifies rhetoric-and-framing arguments in entrepreneurship. Figurative language does not directly persuade investors in this setting, but it matters when aligned with gestures that enable mental imagery, thereby offering a more integrated account of entrepreneurial communication.

**Practical Implication**
For entrepreneurs, the findings imply that effective pitching depends not only on what is said but on how product and venture ideas are embodied through gestures. Entrepreneurs should use gestures to depict concrete product features and abstract venture trajectories, rather than relying only on metaphors, anecdotes, or enthusiastic delivery.

For investors, the study suggests that nonverbal cues can shape investment judgments through cognitive imagery as well as emotional impressions. The authors argue that distinguishing meaningful gestural depiction from generalized energetic body language can support more evidence-based recommendations for entrepreneurs and investors in practice.

**Limitations**
The experimental materials used a male actor, so the authors note that future work should test whether the effects hold for female entrepreneurs. The experiment jointly manipulated beat and ideational gestures, which means the separate effects of these gesture types remain unresolved.

The experimental venture involved a basic technological product that experienced and novice investors could understand, so the findings may differ for more technically or conceptually complex ventures. The mediators and dependent variable were also measured contemporaneously, which may inflate observed relationships.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine other nonverbal modalities in entrepreneurial communication, including intonation, pitch, prosody, posture, facial expressions, gaze, and the development of entrepreneur-investor relationships over time. It should also compare interpersonal pitching with mediated forms of entrepreneurial communication such as business plans and IPO prospectuses.

The authors also call for research separating beat gestures from ideational gestures and testing whether the same mechanisms operate across entrepreneur gender and venture complexity. Additional studies could measure mediators and investment judgments at different times to strengthen causal evidence about the communication process.
