# Experience and Behavior: How Negative Feedback in New Versus Experienced Domains Affects Firm Action and Subsequent Performance

**Abstract**
How organizations respond to failure (negative feedback) has important implications for subsequent organizational performance. This study focuses on theoretical and empirical distinctions between the different potential organizational reactions to failure (in this case around new product development) and how those feedback-driven choices aggregate to affect firm performance. To understand when and why negative feedback is organizationally beneficial, we focus on how possession of domain-specific experience affects the way in which firms interpret and respond to negative feedback, offering both organizationand mechanism-level theories. In particular, we distinguish between conditions that should lead to different behavioral responses to negative feedback: retreat, local search, or distant search. We find support for our theory using data on all U.S. mutual fund companies from 1962 to 2002, and testing both aggregated and mechanism-level theories. Our results show that negative feedback on new funds launched in new domains is particularly damaging to subsequent organizational performance, as firms retreat from the unknown in favor of already exploited domains. In contrast, negative feedback in experienced domains can produce positive outcomes by encouraging action to search for both local solutions to correct the problem and distant solutions to expand the firm's opportunities.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when and why negative feedback improves versus harms subsequent organizational performance. It specifically examines how domain-specific experience shapes firms' interpretation of poor performance on new products and their subsequent choices among retreat, local search, and distant search.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: New products in new (versus experienced) domains that perform below aspirations lead to fewer new products introduced in the same domains.
- H2: New products in new (versus experienced) domains that perform below aspirations lead to fewer new products introduced in other new domains.
- H3: New products in new (versus experienced) domains that perform below aspirations lead to more new products introduced in experienced domains.
- H4: Creating new products in experienced domains leads to lower future performance for existing products in experienced domains.
- H5: New products in experienced (versus new) domains that perform below aspirations lead to higher future performance for those new products.
- H6: New products in experienced domains that perform below aspirations lead to higher future performance for existing products in the same domains.
- H7: New products in experienced (versus new) domains that perform below aspirations lead to more new products introduced in new domains.
- H8: Increases in the number of poorly performing new products in new domains lead to lower subsequent firm performance, while increases in the number of poorly performing new products in experienced domains lead to higher subsequent firm performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Negative versus positive feedback on newly launched funds; whether the fund domain is new or experienced for the firm.
- DV(s): Firm-level net new cash inflow; subsequent new fund creation in same, new, or experienced categories; third-year new fund cash inflow; existing fund cash inflow.
- Mediators: Behavioral responses to feedback, especially retreat from new domains, local search to repair experienced-domain failures, and distant search into new domains.
- Moderators: Domain-specific experience is the central contingency shaping how negative feedback is interpreted and acted upon.

The mechanism is grounded in the behavioral theory of the firm. Negative feedback in a new domain is interpreted as evidence that the unfamiliar domain itself is risky, leading firms to retreat toward familiar domains and thereby incur opportunity costs and cannibalization. Negative feedback in an experienced domain is interpreted as a fixable product-level problem, leading firms to allocate resources locally and, when needed, search more distantly for new opportunities.

**Data & Measures**
- Data: The U.S. mutual fund industry during 1962-2002. The main source is the Survivor Bias Free U.S. Mutual Fund Database maintained by CRSP at the University of Chicago, covering virtually all open-end equity, bond, and money market funds, supplemented by hand-collected data on firm ownership of individual funds and by public information used to verify mergers, spinoffs, and other corporate-structure changes. Domains are defined using Strategic Insight's classification, which sorts funds into 83 categories (collapsed from 181 by folding state-specific bond categories together). Funds and firms grew from 215 and 100 in 1962 to 15,524 and 478 in 2002, respectively; 948 firms existed between 1962 and 2002, and the firm-level analysis uses the 810 firms and 8,817 firm-year observations with usable information.
- DV, firm level: Firm Flow, the net new cash inflows into all funds offered by a provider, measured in millions of dollars as the year-to-year change in assets under management adjusted for the appreciation or depreciation of each fund's investments.
- DVs, fund level: counts of new funds created in the year after a focal fund is categorized, split into the same category as the focal fund (Model 5), completely new categories for the firm (Model 6), and other categories in which the firm has prior experience (Model 7), on 14,064 new-fund observations; the focal new fund's net new cash flow in its third year (Model 8, 12,488 observations); and net new cash inflow for existing funds at least four years old (Models 9-10, 58,168 fund-year observations).
- IVs: Each new fund is assigned to exactly one of four experience types (Negative & New, Negative & Experienced, Positive & New, Positive & Experienced), which are counted at the firm-year level and accumulated into decaying experience stocks. A domain is coded new if the firm has no prior experience in that Strategic Insight category and experienced if the firm has previously operated at least one fund there. Performance is assessed against social rather than historical aspirations: a new fund is classified as positively performing if its cash inflows in its second year (first full calendar year) exceed the median cash inflow of all other funds in the same category that year, and negatively performing otherwise. The firm's cumulative experience is decayed by 15% per year, so experience accumulated last year counts as 1.0, 0.85, and 0.72 in consecutive years. Extreme failures (bottom 25th percentile) are excluded because of the industry's fund-incubation practice, affecting roughly 5% of the new funds; results are robust to including them.
- Controls: Firm Age, Firm Size (logged and lagged two years), and Firm Categories at the firm level; asset-weighted fund-level measures (Fund Return, Fund SD, Fund Expense, Fund Age) and category-level measures (Category Density, Category Flow, Noncategory Flow), each lagged one year.
- Identification: The design is associational panel estimation rather than a named causal-identification strategy. Firm-level models use OLS with both firm and year fixed effects (a Hausman test rejects random effects) and standard errors clustered at the firm level, with all tests two-tailed. Because none of the candidate specifications is ideal for the data structure, the authors report three models - fixed-effects OLS without a lagged dependent variable (their conservative focus), fixed-effects OLS with the lagged dependent variable, and an Arellano-Bond estimator that fails the Sargan overidentification test and so may be biased. Fund-creation counts use negative binomial models with year fixed effects and firm dummies (fixed-effect negative binomial models are not true fixed-effects models). Third-year fund performance uses OLS because the dependent variable is unbounded, and the spillover models use OLS with fund-level fixed effects. The authors argue that finding opposite effects in new versus experienced domains rules out mean reversion and firm quality as alternative explanations.

**Key Findings**
- H8 supported (firm level, Table 2, 8,817 firm-year observations; 7,195 for the Arellano-Bond model). In Models 2-4 the coefficient on Negative & New is strongly negative and significant (-368.997, p < .001 in Model 2), while the coefficient on Negative & Experienced is positive and significant (58.201, p < .01 in Model 2) and significantly greater than the Negative & New coefficient (p < .001). One failed fund in a new domain results in a reduction in net new investment of $369 million, while a failed fund in an experienced domain results in an investment increase of $58. Positive & New is positive but significant only in the Arellano-Bond model (119.844, p < .001), and Positive & Experienced is insignificant in Model 2 but negative and significant in Models 3 and 4.
- H1, H2, and H3 supported (fund level, Table 3, 14,064 observations, negative binomial with Negative & Experienced as the omitted category). Negative feedback in new domains is associated with fewer new funds in the same domain (-0.508, p < .001, Model 5), fewer new funds in other new domains (-0.113, p < .001, Model 6), and more new funds in other experienced domains (0.135, p < .001, Model 7).
- H4 supported with a boundary condition (Table 4, 58,168 fund-year observations). Successful new funds in experienced categories have negative and significant consequences for the future performance of other funds in the same category (-2.065, p < .05 in Model 9; -4.741, p < .001 in Model 10), which the authors read as clear evidence of cannibalization consistent with H4. However, there is no evidence of an effect for unsuccessful new funds in the same category (2.600 in Model 9 and 1.469 in Model 10, both nonsignificant), which the authors call an important boundary condition on H4: it is not negative feedback in new domains itself that hurts existing funds, but the extent to which that feedback spurs more exploitative new funds.
- H5 supported (Table 3, Model 8, 12,488 observations). Poorly performing funds in new domains perform significantly worse in their third year than poorly performing funds in experienced domains (-7.423, p < .05 versus the omitted category), a difference of $7.4 million in net new investment against an average annual investment of around $22.3 million per fund. The coefficients on positive feedback in both new and experienced domains are positive but not significantly different from the omitted category.
- H6 received at best limited support (Table 4, Model 9). The coefficient for poorly performing funds in experienced domains on the returns of existing funds is positive but insignificant. It differs significantly from the positive-feedback coefficient in experienced domains (p < .001), but the authors judge that difference to be largely driven by the negative effects of cannibalization rather than by spillover benefits.
- H7 generally consistent with the results (Table 3, Model 6). The omitted category of negative feedback in experienced domains leads to significantly more entries into new domains than negative feedback in new domains (p < .001). Additional unreported analysis of fourth-year entry as a function of the failed fund's third-year recovery is significant only at p < .10 but is consistent with the authors' timing story that distant search follows failed local search.
- Supporting analyses available from the authors show that entries into new domains generate positive returns in the first, second, and third years after entry (the opportunity-cost argument), whereas entry into experienced domains generates positive returns in the first year, neutral returns in the second, and negative returns subsequently (the cannibalization argument).
- Robustness (Table 5). Using fund closure as an alternate negative-feedback measure, the authors report that closing funds in experienced domains is beneficial for future firm performance while closures in new domains are negatively but not significantly related to it, with the two coefficients differing at p < .001 in a Wald test (Model 11 reports Fund Closed New = -175.656, p < .001, and Fund Closed Experienced = 40.341, p < .10). Splitting the sample into large and small firms (Models 12-13) yields effects that are generally consistent for both. Removing the firm's own funds from the aspiration comparison set (Model 14) and benchmarking only against similarly sized firms (Model 15) produce results consistent with Model 2. A generic historical-aspirations specification (Model 16) produces results very different from the primary results, which the authors take as further evidence that aspirations in this setting are set socially rather than historically.
- Overall, the authors describe the process results as largely consistent with their theory and as providing broad support for the overall theory of behavioral response to negative feedback based on domain.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends behavioral theory of the firm research by showing that negative feedback does not have uniform performance implications. Domain-specific experience determines whether poor performance triggers damaging retreat from exploration or productive problem-driven search.

It also moves beyond a simple change-versus-persistence account of performance feedback. By distinguishing retreat, local search, and distant search, the study provides a mechanism-level explanation of how firms choose among different behavioral responses to failure and how those responses aggregate into firm performance.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that managers should not treat failure in unfamiliar domains as straightforward evidence that exploration is unattractive. Premature retreat from new domains can leave growth opportunities unpursued and redirect resources into familiar domains where new products cannibalize existing ones.

For failures in experienced domains, the study suggests that firms can benefit from allocating resources to correct the problem because relevant experience makes the feedback more interpretable and actionable. The practical lesson is to diagnose whether poor performance reflects a fixable product-level issue or uncertainty in a genuinely new domain before reallocating resources.

**Limitations**
The authors note that they directly test only a subset of the full theoretical model, leaving some mechanisms supported indirectly rather than through direct empirical tests. The mutual fund industry is also a relatively stable context in which retreat to familiar domains is viable, so the performance implications may differ in high-technology industries where continued exploration is necessary for survival.

The study treats experience as binary, distinguishing whether the firm has any prior experience in a domain. The authors acknowledge that depth of experience may further shape how managers interpret feedback, but incorporating continuous experience would add conceptual and empirical complexity.

**Future Research**
Future research could test the behavioral mechanisms in other industries, especially high-technology contexts where firms cannot easily retreat from new domains. Such work would clarify whether negative feedback in new domains is always damaging or only damaging when retreat is strategically feasible.

Future studies could also examine how the depth of prior experience changes feedback interpretation. More direct evidence on when local search is judged to have failed would help explain the timing of shifts from local problem solving to distant search.
