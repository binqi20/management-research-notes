# Boards as a Source of Inertia: Examining the Internal Challenges and Dynamics of Boards of Directors in Times of Environmental Discontinuities

**Abstract**
The literature on corporate governance indicates that, by engaging in strategy-related activities, boards of directors can help firms adapt to environmental discontinuities. So far, however, studies shed limited light on board-internal challenges and dynamics in such difficult times, thus providing few insights into the conditions under which boards may contribute to organizational inertia. We use a comparative case study of 10 major Swiss electric utility companies during the energy transition to show in detail how environmental and strategic change impair boards' ability to judge strategic issues, and how boards use self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration to renew this ability. Moreover, we offer original insights into the board-internal antecedents of board renewal, and show that environmental discontinuities pose a dilemma for boards, since self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration are critical for preventing organizational inertia yet may run counter to board members' self-interest. By showing that board members, like managers, experience conflicts of interest that can harm firm performance, our study contributes to agency theory and an emerging micro-perspective on boards. Moreover, by highlighting boards as a source of organizational inertia, our study challenges existing findings in the field of strategic management and makes several more specific contributions to important debates in the corporate governance literature.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how boards of directors contribute to organizational inertia during environmental discontinuities. More specifically, it examines how environmental and strategic change affect boards' ability to govern strategic change, how boards renew that ability, and what board-internal factors explain variation in board adjustment.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / challenges: Environmental discontinuity, strategic change, board ability to judge strategic issues, strategy evaluation, strategy reconfiguration, self-evaluation, self-reconfiguration, board members' self-interest, and board chair actions and experience.
- Process / phases: Environmental discontinuities and strategic change reduce boards' ability to judge strategic issues; boards must identify this gap through self-evaluation and renew the ability through self-reconfiguration.
- Board activities: Strategy evaluation monitors the environment and reviews strategy; strategy reconfiguration approves, stimulates, or limits strategic change initiatives.
- Renewal activities: Self-reconfiguration occurs through adaptation of composition, adaptation of size, learning and education, and adjustment of decision-making.
- Boundary conditions: The empirical setting is 10 large Swiss electric utilities exposed to the energy transition between 2009 and 2015.

The process begins when environmental discontinuities make existing board knowledge, routines, and ties less useful for judging strategic issues. If a board recognizes this loss of ability through self-evaluation, it can renew itself by changing composition, reducing size, improving learning, or changing decision-making routines. If board members' self-interest blocks self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration, the board may limit or rubber-stamp strategic change in ways that prolong organizational inertia. Experienced board chairs can partly overcome this barrier by initiating board renewal in a top-down manner.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and case selection: an inductive, comparative case-study analysis built on theoretical sampling. From the population of Swiss electricity suppliers affected by the post-2009 changes, the authors selected 10 large, well-established firms, all of which had a board of directors; in 2013 these firms had at least $150 million in revenues, placing them among the top 25 of roughly 800 Swiss electricity companies. Firms were deliberately sampled to differ in exposure to the environmental discontinuity and are identified by the pseudonyms "LowCorp" (L1 through L5) for low exposure and "HighCorp" (H1 through H5) for high exposure. In 2009 five sample firms possessed electricity-generation assets and focused primarily on generation and wholesaling, while the other five mostly purchased electricity on the wholesale market and focused on retail plus other services; the paper reports that the generation-focused firms were hit particularly hard by the discontinuity and the retail-focused firms were less exposed.
- Archival corpus: a comprehensive set of secondary data for all 10 firms, including annual reports, press articles over seven years, letters to shareholders, company brochures, employee presentations, official company releases, earnings call transcripts, general assembly notes, and videos, plus a database of background information on all board members (professional background, additional board mandates). The total number of unique board members across the 10 sample firms in 2015 was 101.
- Interview corpus: 52 semi-structured interviews with 41 directors and top-level executives, conducted in four rounds and covering 45 different executive and nonexecutive mandates. For each firm the authors interviewed two to four nonexecutive directors, including the chair or vice-chair for eight of the 10 firms, as well as the CEOs of all 10 firms and additional senior managers. The 41 interviews of the first two rounds lasted 65-130 minutes; the 11 interviews of the third and fourth rounds were shorter (30-45 minutes). Only two invited individuals declined. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and stored in a central interview database, and validation interviews with experienced directors inside and outside the Swiss energy sector plus workshops with an industry expert panel supported triangulation.
- Coding and construct development (four analysis phases): phase one synthesized the secondary data into a per-firm event timeline and applied an initial coding scheme drawn from the corporate governance literature; phase two used a second interview round and an updated coding scheme focused on board-management interaction, with open coding used to inductively develop constructs of board activity that were clustered into strategy evaluation and strategy reconfiguration, and constructs of board-internal change that were clustered into self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration; phase three used additional interviews to examine antecedents of the two renewal activities; phase four synthesized archival and interview data into tables juxtaposing exposure, strategic changes, board activities, changes in boards, and financial performance, plus a per-firm timeline of boards' strategy-related activities between 2009 and 2015, with axial coding used to distill the mechanisms behind observed differences.
- Reliability and validity: all interviews were recoded under the final coding scheme, closed coding was used in later project stages, and intercoder reliability (Cohen's kappa) for the coding of strategy-related board activities was 92.3%. Construct, internal, and external validity and reliability were addressed following Yin (2009) through triangulation across data sources, review of rival explanations, strict theoretical sampling, cross-case analysis, transcripts, case-study protocols, and a case-study database.
- Outcome measures used in the cross-case comparison: firm financial performance is measured as net income and return on assets over 2009 to 2015; boards' focus of strategy reconfiguration is coded per firm-year in Table 3 as approving (A), limiting (L), or stimulating (S) change initiatives, with some firm-years carrying more than one code.
- Identification: the study is interpretive and theory-building rather than causally identified. The authors claim external validity through analytical generalization via abstraction from empirical observations, not statistical inference, and the cross-case performance comparison is observational.

**Key Findings**
- Board members from all 10 firms reported routine strategy evaluation (monitoring environmental change; reviewing firm strategy and strategic proposals), and all boards also engaged in strategy reconfiguration through three activities: approving, stimulating, and limiting strategic change initiatives.
- Table 3 shows that HighCorp boards were more strongly engaged in stimulating change initiatives than LowCorp boards, but striking differences in activity focus appeared even among firms with similar exposure. The authors read this as evidence that firms' exposure to the discontinuity is not necessarily what drives boards' strategy reconfiguration; instead these activities depend on boards' subjective evaluation of the firm's environment relative to their subjective evaluation of managerial change initiatives.
- Boards' effectiveness in both roles depends on their ability to judge strategic issues, and both the environmental discontinuity and the strategic change it prompted reduced that ability. Even firms with low exposure and no need for dramatic strategic shifts reported that rising uncertainty and complexity made strategic judgment harder, and interviewees reported that prior expertise and social networks lost value in the new energy world.
- Because no institutionalized governance body oversees board renewal, boards rely mainly on themselves. Owners could seldom take on this control function: distributed ownership, an annual general meeting that functions as a formal discharge, and appointment processes that are not competency-based left board supervision weak even where a majority shareholder existed.
- Renewal runs through self-evaluation (assessing the board's own strengths and weaknesses) and self-reconfiguration, which comprises four activities: adaptation of composition, adaptation of size, learning and education, and adjustment of decision-making. Self-reconfiguration did not necessarily require prior self-evaluation, since it could be triggered by other factors such as ownership change or scheduled retirements, but for self-reconfiguration to effectively improve the board's ability to judge strategic issues, self-evaluation had to happen first.
- Engagement differed sharply across firms and over time, even among firms with the same exposure: the boards of HighCorp 2 and 4 and LowCorp 2 began reconfiguring early and engaged in almost all of these activities; the boards of HighCorp 1 and 5 and LowCorp 5 were initially passive but began major reconfigurations in the last two years of the study period; the boards of HighCorp 3 and LowCorp 1, 3, and 4 remained passive throughout, showing limited self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration.
- Consequences depended on exposure. At the less exposed firms a lack of board self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration did not trigger major organizational inertia, because these firms did not need radical change initiatives. Among the highly exposed firms, HighCorp 2 and 4 engaged strongly and were able to govern the strategic change proactively, whereas at HighCorp 1, 3, and 5 the lack of these activities led to serious organizational inertia, with boards actively opposing management-proposed changes for a long time. For those three firms the authors observe the sharpest decline in financial performance, measured as net income and return on assets, between 2009 and 2015 among all firms studied.
- Two board-internal antecedents explain the variation. First, board members' self-interest: directors joined boards for prestige, for monetary compensation (USD 20,000-1m per annum depending on firm size), and for an intrinsic interest in shaping firm strategy, and all three motivations limited members' interest in self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration, since these activities require questioning oneself and risk revealing that members are ill-equipped and should lose their mandate. Neither activity was usually driven by ordinary board members.
- Second, the board chair's actions and experience: chairs were typically the main drivers of self-evaluation and of adjustments to composition, size, collective learning, and decision-making, often drawing on tacit knowledge from prior roles as CEOs or chairs elsewhere and delegating to nomination committees. Because board practices are opaque across firms and no systematic benchmarking occurred, changes of chair served as the main channel of knowledge spillovers about these practices. Where firms were strongly exposed to the discontinuity and lacked an experienced board chair, that is HighCorp 1, 3, and 5, the boards lost their ability to govern strategic change and contributed to organizational inertia.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to agency theory by showing that board members themselves may face conflicts of interest that harm firm performance, rather than only acting as monitors of managerial self-interest. It extends strategic management work on organizational inertia by identifying boards as a potential source of inertia when they fail to renew their ability to judge strategic issues. It also contributes to corporate governance research by developing a dynamic view of board capital, board evolution, and the role of the board chair in sustaining board power vis-a-vis management.

**Practical Implication**
Firms facing environmental discontinuities should not assume that existing board expertise and routines remain adequate as strategic conditions change. Boards need explicit self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration practices, including competency reviews, education, size adjustment, and decision-making reforms. The findings also imply that board chairs are critical governance actors because they can initiate renewal when ordinary board members have incentives to avoid exposing weaknesses or losing valued mandates.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on the Swiss electricity industry, a highly regulated setting with country-specific board responsibilities, so the authors call for caution in generalizing to other sectors or national governance systems. The study relies on retrospective interviews rather than direct real-time observation of boardroom processes, although the authors triangulate interviews with multiple archival and informant sources. The paper also provides initial evidence on antecedents of self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration rather than a complete account of why some boards engage in these practices more than others.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine board mechanisms during environmental change in other national and industrial contexts. The authors call for ethnographic studies of boardroom processes over extended periods and for micro-level work on how board members' motivations and interests shape board dynamics and organizational outcomes. They also raise several open questions: how firms or owners select and change the board chair, how board self-evaluation and self-reconfiguration relate to the firm's history, board members' backgrounds, or the firm's ownership structure, and what incentives and processes firms can use to prevent harmful consequences from board members' conflicts of interest.
