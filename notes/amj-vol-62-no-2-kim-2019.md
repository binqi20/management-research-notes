# No Time Like the Present: How a Present Time Perspective Can Foster Sustainable Development

**Abstract**
Sustainable development research has assumed that organizations must make intertemporal trade-offs between benefits now versus benefits later. However, under extreme resource constraints, organizations are unable to sacrifice resources now for benefits later without risking their survival. In these conditions, prior theory has suggested that organizations would be present focused, making sustainable development elusive. Through an ethnographic study, we investigated how tea producer organizations in eight communities in East Africa confronting severe resource constraints acted for sustainable development. We discovered that a “present” time perspective is richer than has been described previously. Prior time research has described the present as a “moment” in time, which allows managers to juxtapose the present against the future to make the intertemporal trade-offs for sustainable development. However, our tea producers did not see the future as a trade-off with the present. We discovered that they see duration in the present—what we call a “long present.” Because the present is long, they see connections among processes such as resource flows, which inspired incremental actions that continuously ease extreme resource shortages. We therefore offer an alternative to the trade-off thinking that currently dominates sustainable development discourse.

**Research Question**
How are organizations under severe resource constraints able to act for sustainable development? The paper asks this inductively in order to contribute to theories of time and sustainable development.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key tension: Fairtrade's future-focused interventions assumed that producers should sacrifice some present benefits to build a better future, while producers faced acute resource constraints that made such sacrifice risky.
- Time perspectives: Fairtrade treated the present as a short, discrete moment; some tea producer organizations enacted a long-present perspective in which the present had duration and contained connected processes.
- Process visibility: The long-present perspective made seasonal production, school fees, bonus payments, loans, rainfall, bees, tea, and other resource rhythms visible as connected flows rather than isolated stocks.
- Producer actions: Producers redirected Fairtrade resources into immediate and incremental initiatives such as beekeeping, concrete benches, savings schemes, and school-fee timing.
- Emergent outcome: These actions eased immediate resource shortages while cumulatively building resources over time, enabling sustainable development through temporal connections rather than intertemporal trade-offs.

The central process is not that resource-poor organizations become future-focused in the conventional sense. Rather, interaction with Fairtrade supplied limited slack, governance forums, and pressure to comply with standards, while producers' long-present perspective helped them see how small present actions could connect resource flows across seasons and cycles. Beekeeping, concrete benches, and related initiatives looked short-term from Fairtrade's short-present lens, but they created immediate resource relief that accumulated into longer-term development.

**Data & Measures**
Multi-sited ethnography of eight Fairtrade-certified tea producer organizations and their surrounding communities in Kenya (Dubu, Kifaru, Kuro, and Nyuki), Tanzania (Samaki and Twiga), and Uganda (Kipepeo and Punda Milia); all producer-organization names in the paper are pseudonyms. Samaki is certified under the Fairtrade "Hired Labor" standards because the company is owned by external shareholders and employs workers in tea estates; the other seven are factories owned by small-scale tea producers and are certified under the "Small Producer Organizations" standards. The eight were Fairtrade-certified in different years — Samaki (1998), Kipepeo and Punda Milia (1999), Twiga (2001), Dubu (2003), Kifaru and Nyuki (2006), and Kuro (2007) — so the field researcher could follow the development and diffusion of Fairtrade certification in the region by moving across sites, albeit retrospectively. The first author spent five months in the field: a first round in East Africa between July and September 2010, the dry and low tea production season, followed by a two-month stay between December 2011 and January 2012, the wet and high tea production season. She prepared through an eight-month training program on East African cultures, languages, and development issues and 14 months of part-time Swahili classes and private tutoring, and spoke Swahili during fieldwork.

Data were collected from four sources, summarized in Table 1: participant observation, interviews, freehand informant drawings, and secondary data. Field observations on 119 occasions — counted by the number of meetings, events, or site visits lasting from half a day to multiple days — were recorded in over 500 typed pages of field notes. The field researcher conducted 133 face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with tea farmers, estate and factory workers, managers, and board directors (elected among farmers) in the tea producer organizations, plus 23 interviews with staff of other organizations involved in the Fairtrade tea business, including Fairtrade International (formerly Fairtrade Labeling Organizations International, FLO), the Kenya Tea Development Agency (KTDA), and the producer organizations' trading partners, known as fair trade organizations (FTOs); Table 1 reports 156 interviews in total, of which 22 are with FTO/FLO/KTDA staff and one with a tea broker. Fifteen individuals were interviewed in both rounds of data collection. Interviews were conducted in English and Swahili, audio recorded, and transcribed verbatim in the language of origin; they ranged from 30 minutes to three hours, with the majority lasting about an hour. In the first round the questions were framed broadly around the impact of Fairtrade, and the subject of time was not explicit in the interview questions at that stage; in the second round the questions explicitly addressed time-related themes such as seasonal income fluctuation, household expenditure patterns, and bonus payment schedules. The drawing corpus is 128 freehand drawings by 56 informants: in the first round each informant was asked for a pair of drawings of life before and after Fairtrade certification, and in the second round for multiple drawings representing work and family life over an extended time period. Secondary data included Fairtrade standards and compliance criteria in English and Swahili, Fairtrade training materials, annual reports and financial statements of producer organizations including Fairtrade sales data and premium payments over time, green tea leaf collection receipts, marketing materials, tea auction catalogs, industry reports, European campaign materials, and the visitors books recording Fairtrade audits and buyers' visits.

The subject of time emerged as a recurring theme during data collection and analysis, and the analysis ran through four distinct stages. In the first stage, interview transcripts and field notes were coded by the first and third authors, and the informants' drawings and related explanations were thematically coded; the presence of multiple rhythms in tea producer organizations, often in relation to the seasonal patterns of rainfall, emerged as an important theme, and the authors then decided to depart from highly structured coding methods in order to better see the rhythms of tea producers. In the second stage, they analyzed the impact of Fairtrade interventions and producers' actions to coordinate needs and resource flows: they created a list of typical needs and resources of tea producers from the empirical data, mapped them over time by approximating monetary values, and produced diagrams illustrating the temporal flows of needs and resources (for example, the school fee payment cycle and seasonal income fluctuation) and how these were embedded in the rhythms of the social and biophysical environment (for example, school terms and rainfall patterns). In the third stage, they reanalyzed interview transcripts, field notes, drawings, and secondary data with a revised focus on time perspectives and the actions of Fairtrade and producers, investigating the words with which different actors framed the short and long term and the present and future. In the fourth stage, at the suggestion of reviewers, they reanalyzed 10 vignettes that had emerged as the most illuminating stories in earlier stages — the first six about Fairtrade's initiatives, such as building schools and clinics and prohibiting child labor, and four about producers' initiatives, such as beekeeping and concrete benches — in terms of initiatives, reactions, and the outcomes of interactions. The design is interpretive and inductive: there are no measured variables, no statistical estimation, and no causal-identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Tea producers exhibited a strong focus on the present, driven first by the pressing need to survive — farmers and workers frequently skipped meals and went through the day on chai — and reinforced by adversity that was difficult to predict or control, including the severe frost and drought that crippled tea leaf growth in the region for several months in 2009, the ethnic violence following the contested 2007 Kenyan presidential election, and unpredictable power cuts in factories where fresh leaves must be processed within 24 hours of plucking. Producers nonetheless did not want to remain trapped in poverty and collectively sought Fairtrade certification, mainly to sell into the Fairtrade market and to benefit from the Fairtrade premium.

Fairtrade's own perspective was future-focused and, in the authors' theorizing, assumed a short present: it contrasted a static, problematic present — commonly described as living below "the poverty line," an average income of 1.25 USD per day — with a brighter future state, and its interventions asked producers to sacrifice immediate needs. The study finds that some of these interventions unintentionally exacerbated resource scarcity rather than relieving it (Table 2). At Samaki, premium-funded iron sheets meant to replace grass-thatched roofs were sold by many workers for cash to buy food, and auditors problematized the "misuse" of funds. At Dubu, a premium-funded new primary school had only four of the 11 teachers it needed assigned and paid for by the government, so it charged higher fees than established schools and children moved in and out of school as parents struggled to pay. At Punda Milia, the premium funded the clinic building but not consumables; between the 2010 and 2012 visits the clinic could not afford medicine and eventually became an empty building. At Kuro, the prohibition of child labor reduced immediate household income, child labor silently persisted, and Fairtrade certification was temporarily suspended; at Samaki, the 48-hour monthly overtime limit conflicted with the seasonality of tea and overtime persisted, also leading to suspension. Producers found Fairtrade training sessions helpful — but, the paper reports, not in becoming focused on a distant future, rather in developing ideas for improvements in their immediate circumstances.

The central emergent finding is that a present focus need not mean a short-term orientation. When asked about their current situation or challenges, producers answered by describing seasons and other cycles that had not been raised by the researcher, and their description of the present usually encompassed at least one full calendar year, including two dry seasons (January to March and July to September) and two rainy seasons (April to June and October to December), with variation across regions and years. Their accounts connected tea income (paid per kilogram, and higher in the rainy season), food prices and food-crop growing seasons, school fees levied in January, May, and September, the annual factory "bonus" paid in October in Kenya, and the loan cycle of borrowing in the dry season and clearing debt in the bonus month. The authors read this as a long-present perspective, defined as perceiving the present over an extended duration constituted by processes that are inseparable from one another, as against a short-present perspective, defined as perceiving the present as a discrete moment separate from moments in the past and the future. Their theorized implication is directional: the short present reinforces intertemporal resource trade-offs, whereas the long present supports building resources through temporal connections, so that a focus on the short present may imply short-termism while a focus on the long present does not.

Two vignettes show the long present in practice, and both are site-specific. At Nyuki, producers invested part of the premium in beehives placed in a collectively managed eucalyptus forest, along with passion vines to attract bees; honey production declines in the rainy season and peaks in the dry season, and the reduced time needed for tea farming in the dry season freed two or three days a week for beekeeping, so most farmers at Nyuki managed between 10 and 20 beehives and earned honey income precisely when tea income was lowest. This eased dry-season hardship, reduced the need to borrow and therefore the interest burden in the following rainy season, and raised total resources without displacing existing tea income; profits from honey and passion fruit inside the forest were transferred back into the Fairtrade premium fund and reinvested in more beehives, creating an amplifying cycle — even though a Nyuki manager acknowledged it was "not a Fairtrade kind of project." At Kuro, producers spent part of the premium on concrete drying benches to replace wooden ones at tea leaf collection centers; concrete delayed fermentation until the factory and reduced leaf loss through cracks, improving leaf quality and hence auction prices and producer income. Benches had been built at six of Kuro's 42 collection centers and were ranked priority number one by farmers and workers, ahead of water tanks and electricity, even though trading partners in the UK asked whether the money could go to something else "like hospitals." Table 3 records two further producer initiatives: a school in the Nyuki catchment area unofficially allowed parents to pay the whole year's fees in the October bonus month, breaking the borrow-and-repay debt cycle; and at Kipepeo, producers who disliked interest-bearing microfinance loans redirected the initiative into a collective savings scheme that deducted a preagreed amount from monthly earnings only during the rainy season for withdrawal in the dry season.

The paper is careful about scope and about what enabled the pattern. It reports that producers had a strong present focus that was sometimes seemingly short-term-oriented (selling iron sheets for immediate cash) and that only some of the tea producers in the study enacted a long-present perspective. Ecological embeddedness in overlapping biophysical cycles is offered as part of the explanation, but the authors state explicitly that it cannot be the whole story: the long-present perspective was enacted in actions oriented toward sustainable development when producers were interacting with Fairtrade, because Fairtrade supplied just enough slack resources (the premium) for an initial investment — producers were unlikely to have considered or implemented beekeeping or concrete benches without those additional resources — and because the mandated Fairtrade Premium Committee and Fairtrade meetings and training helped producers devise and implement incremental improvements. The authors conclude that Fairtrade's interventions helped producers, but not in the way originally intended, and that Fairtrade's principle of respecting producers' own decisions was fundamental to making the shift happen.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances time-perspective theory by separating a present focus from short-termism and theorizing short-present and long-present perspectives. It contributes to sustainable development research by showing that intertemporal trade-offs are not the only pathway: organizations under severe resource constraints may build resources through temporal connections. It also brings process thinking into a concrete empirical account of how actors perceive the present as an extended duration rather than as a discrete point.

**Practical Implication**
Development organizations should not assume that local producers' present focus reflects ignorance of the future or simple short-termism. Interventions are more likely to help when they understand local social and biophysical rhythms, provide enough initial resources for incremental improvement, and work with local organizations' own temporal knowledge. Fairtrade's premium, committee structures, meetings, and training mattered, but they were most useful when producers could redirect them toward locally connected resource flows.

**Limitations**
The study is based on an extreme case: resource-poor tea producer organizations deeply embedded in rural East African biophysical systems. The authors argue that short- and long-present perspectives may travel beyond this context, but they state that broader generalization to developed and developing economies requires further empirical investigation. The analysis also foregrounds temporality, while power, control, standardization, certification, and auditing are acknowledged as relevant mechanisms outside the study's main scope.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine short- and long-present perspectives in different cultural and geographical spaces, especially developed-country market economies where everyday life may be less directly connected to biophysical systems. Researchers could also investigate how power and control interact with time perspectives in certification and development interventions. More broadly, the paper calls for research on how organizations transition toward sustainable development through temporal connections rather than trade-off thinking.
