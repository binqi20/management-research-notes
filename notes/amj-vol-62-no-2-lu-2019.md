# Pitching Novel Ideas to the Boss: The Interactive Effects of Employees’ Idea Enactment and Influence Tactics on Creativity Assessment and Implementation

**Abstract**
Employees’ creative ideas often do not receive positive assessments from managers and, therefore, lose the opportunity to be implemented. We draw on Dutton and Ashford’s (1993) issue-selling framework to test a dual-approach model predicting that employees’ use of both high levels of idea enactment behaviors (characterized by use of demos, prototypes, or other physical objects when presenting ideas) and high levels of upward influence tactics will have an interactive effect on the extent to which their creative ideas are positively assessed by their supervisors and, in turn, implemented. We found support for this interactive effect of idea enactment and influence tactics in two studies: a field study of 192 employees and 54 supervisors in a video game and animation company and an experimental study with 264 participants. In the experimental study, we also demonstrated that the benefits of using this dual approach are more likely to accrue when selling a more novel idea rather than a less novel, more mundane one. Both studies highlight the mediating role of supervisors’ creativity assessment in implementing employees’ creative ideas.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how employees can increase supervisors' positive assessment and implementation of their creative ideas. It specifically examines whether idea enactment and upward influence tactics interact to improve creativity assessment, and whether this dual approach works especially well for more novel ideas.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Employees' idea enactment and use of upward influence tactics interact to predict supervisors' assessment of employee creativity, such that enactment results in more favorable creativity assessments when paired with the use of influence tactics.
- H2: Idea enactment, influence tactics, and idea novelty jointly interact to predict supervisors' assessment of employee creativity, such that the joint effect of enactment and influence tactics is more likely to result in favorable creativity assessments when ideas are high as opposed to low in novelty.
- H3: Supervisors' assessment of employees' creativity is positively related to the implementation of focal employees' ideas.
- Study 1 tested Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 3; Study 2 tested all three hypotheses.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employees' idea enactment and upward influence tactics; in Study 2, experimentally manipulated idea enactment, influence tactics, and idea novelty.
- DV(s): Supervisors' creativity assessment and implementation in Study 1; participants' creativity assessment, willingness to implement, and intended investment amount in Study 2.
- Mediators: Supervisors' or participants' creativity assessment mediates the link from idea enactment and influence tactics to implementation.
- Moderators: Upward influence tactics moderate the effect of idea enactment; idea novelty further conditions the enactment-by-influence interaction in Study 2.

The mechanism is that idea enactment makes abstract novel ideas more tangible through demos, prototypes, drawings, or similar objects, while upward influence tactics persuade supervisors to attend to and support the idea. Together, these tactics help supervisors recognize novelty, usefulness, and feasibility, which improves creativity assessment. That assessment then increases the likelihood that the idea receives resources, approval, or implementation.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 was a two-wave, multisource field survey in a video game and animation company in southern China, producing a matched sample of 192 employees and 54 supervisors across 16 divisions, with the 192 employees nested within 54 supervisors who were in turn nested within 16 divisions. At Time 1, employees reported idea enactment on three items the authors developed from Harvey's (2014) notion of enacting ideas, Carlile's (2002) work on drawings, and Harrison and Rouse's (2015) work on prototypes (Cronbach's alpha = .85), and reported upward influence tactics on 15 items from Yukl et al.'s (2008) Influence Behavior Questionnaire covering rational persuasion, inspirational appeal, consultation, and collaboration (overall alpha = .92); supervisors rated employee creativity at Time 1 on four items from Tierney, Farmer, and Graen (1999) (alpha = .95). At Time 2, one month later, supervisors reported implementation on three items from Baer (2012) (alpha = .92). Controls were leader-member exchange, perseverance of effort, creative self-efficacy, organizational tenure, gender, and education, and the surveys were administered in Chinese using translation back-translation. The authors used three-level multilevel modeling in Stata 13.0 with grand mean-centered predictors, plus a regression-based method with Monte Carlo simulation for the conditional indirect effects, and state that the cross-sectional field design did not allow causal inferences. Study 2 was a 2 (idea enactment: enactment vs. no enactment) x 2 (influence: high vs. low) x 2 (idea novelty: high vs. low) between-subjects video-based laboratory experiment at a mid-Atlantic university in the United States; 294 undergraduates with full- or part-time work experience participated and 264 remained after 30 were excluded for careless responding (enactment n = 133 and no enactment n = 131; high influence n = 131 and low influence n = 133; high novelty n = 134 and low novelty n = 130). Creativity assessment was constructed by multiplying three novelty items (alpha = .81) by two usefulness items (alpha = .79), giving a multiplicative score with a mean of 20.50 and a standard deviation of 10.24; willingness to implement used the same three Baer (2012) items on a 7-point scale (alpha = .96); and the alternative dependent variable was the amount participants were willing to allocate to the idea from a $20,000 budget. Manipulation checks covered all three manipulations, and hypothesis tests used a three-way ANOVA together with bootstrapping (1,000 iterations) for the conditional indirect effects.

**Key Findings**
In Study 1, Hypothesis 1 was supported: the interaction between influence tactics and idea enactment on creativity assessment was significant (B = 0.17, p < .01), and a simple slope test showed that idea enactment was significantly related to supervisors' creativity assessment when employees used a higher level of influence tactics (mean + 1 SD; estimate = 0.19, p < .05) but not when they used a lower level (mean - 1 SD; estimate = -0.05, p > .05). Hypothesis 3 was also supported in Study 1: Time 1 creativity assessment was significantly related to Time 2 implementation (B = 0.22, p < .01) after controlling for idea enactment, influence tactics, and their interaction, and the indirect effect of enactment on implementation via creativity assessment was significant at higher levels of influence tactics (estimate = 0.04, 95% CI [0.01, 0.08]) but not at lower levels (estimate = -0.01, 95% CI [-0.05, 0.02]; difference = 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.11]). In Study 2, Hypothesis 1 was not supported: the enactment-by-influence interaction term on creativity assessment was only marginally significant, F(1, 256) = 2.80, p < .10, although the authors report that the interaction pattern was consistent with the prediction. Hypothesis 2 was supported: the three-way interaction of enactment, influence, and idea novelty was significant, F(1, 256) = 4.35, p < .05, and the underlying enactment-by-influence interaction was significant when idea novelty was high, F(1, 130) = 6.10, p < .05, but not when idea novelty was low, F(1, 126) = 0.10, p > .05; the highest creativity rating (M = 29.27) was obtained when idea enactment, influence tactics, and idea novelty were all high, significantly higher than the means in the other seven conditions. The three-way ANOVA also revealed a significant enactment-by-novelty interaction, F(1, 256) = 15.49, p < .01: when novelty was high, enacting the idea produced significantly higher creativity ratings (M = 25.28, SE = 1.23) than not enacting it (M = 21.43, SE = 1.23), t(132) = 2.46, p < .05, whereas when the idea was less novel, enactment produced significantly lower ratings (M = 12.52, SE = 1.24) than not enacting it (M = 17.31, SE = 1.27), t(128) = -3.02, p < .01. Hypothesis 3 was supported in Study 2 as well: creativity assessment was significantly positively related to willingness to implement (B = 0.11, p < .01) and to the intended investment amount (B = 264.35, p < .01) after controlling for enactment, influence, and idea novelty. Bootstrapped analyses showed that, under high idea novelty, the indirect effect of enactment via creativity assessment was significant when influence was high for both willingness to implement (estimate = 0.45, 95% CI [0.19, 0.71]) and investment amount (estimate = 1,020.07, 95% CI [416.95, 1671.79]) but not when influence was low, with significant indirect-effect differences for both outcomes; under low idea novelty, the indirect-effect differences between levels of influence were not significant for willingness to implement (estimate = -0.05, 95% CI [-0.37, 0.24]) or investment (estimate = -105.74, 95% CI [-824.66, 538.59]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends Dutton and Ashford's issue-selling framework into the creativity-assessment domain by showing that issue packaging and selling tactics interact when employees pitch creative ideas upward. It shifts creativity research from idea generation alone toward the social process through which supervisors recognize and implement employee creativity. It also shows that the dual approach is contingent on idea novelty: enactment and influence help novel ideas but may expose the mundanity of less novel ones.

**Practical Implication**
Employees pitching novel ideas should combine tangible enactment, such as prototypes or visual demonstrations, with strong upward influence tactics rather than relying on either approach alone. Supervisors and organizations should create environments that make idea enactment possible and should be receptive to employees' proactive selling efforts when evaluating creative ideas.

**Limitations**
The field study could not support strong causal inference and did not verify actual implementation of a particular project. The laboratory study measured creativity assessment and implementation intention from the same respondents at the same time, creating possible common source bias. The authors also note that they did not directly measure the cognitive and behavioral microprocesses through which enactment and influence affect supervisors' assessments.

**Future Research**
Future research could trace ideas from proposal through actual project-level implementation, using separate sources or objective implementation measures. Scholars could study how idea enactment, influence tactics, and supervisor assessment co-evolve across iterative innovation processes. Further work could examine other packaging devices, other selling-process choices, coalition building with peers, and whether too-frequent use of enactment and influence creates decreasing returns.
