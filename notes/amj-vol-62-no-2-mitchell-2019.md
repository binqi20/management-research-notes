# Can You Handle the Pressure? The Effect of Performance Pressure on Stress Appraisals, Self-regulation, and Behavior

**Abstract**
Performance pressure focuses employee efforts toward enhanced performance. It is unclear, however, whether performance pressure serves as a productive or unproductive strategy for producing beneficial work behavior. Our research provides clarity on the dynamic nature of performance pressure. We theorize that reactions to performance pressure are influenced by daily fluctuations in how the pressure is appraised, and these fluctuations explain why performance pressure can be a double-edged sword, producing bright and dark side effects for organizations. We predict that, on a daily basis, performance pressure may be appraised as a threat, which promotes self-regulation depletion that explains dysfunctional behavior (i.e., incivility); daily performance pressure may also be appraised as a challenge, which elicits engagement that explains enhanced task proficiency and citizenship. Trait resilience is predicted to moderate these effects, promoting performance pressure to be appraised as a challenge rather than a threat, which then mitigates the depleting effects that produce dysfunctional behavior and enhances the engaging effects that produce functional behavior. Results from an experience sampling study support our predictions. Implications for theory and research are provided.

**Research Question**
How do daily appraisals of performance pressure explain why the same stressor can produce both dysfunctional behavior and functional behavior, and why are some employees better able to handle this pressure than others?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of performance pressure on self-regulation depletion through threat appraisal.
- H2: On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of performance pressure on engagement through challenge appraisal.
- H3: On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of performance pressure on incivility through threat appraisal and self-regulation depletion.
- H4: On a daily basis, there is a positive indirect effect of performance pressure on (a) task proficiency and (b) citizenship through challenge appraisal and engagement.
- H5: On a daily basis, the positive indirect effect of performance pressure on (a) self-regulation depletion (through threat appraisal) will be stronger when trait resilience is lower rather than higher, and, on a daily basis, the positive indirect effect of performance pressure on (b) engagement (through challenge appraisal) will be stronger when trait resilience is higher rather than lower.
- H6: On a daily basis, the positive indirect effect of performance pressure on incivility (through threat appraisal and self-regulation depletion) will be stronger when trait resilience is lower rather than higher.
- H7: On a daily basis, the positive indirect effect of performance pressure on (a) task proficiency and (b) citizenship (through challenge appraisal and engagement) will be stronger when trait resilience is higher rather than lower.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily performance pressure.
- DV(s): Incivility, task proficiency, and citizenship.
- Mediators: Threat appraisal and self-regulation depletion for the dark-side path; challenge appraisal and engagement for the bright-side path.
- Moderators: Trait resilience moderates the daily effects of performance pressure on threat and challenge appraisals, shaping downstream self-regulatory states and behavior.

The paper theorizes performance pressure as a dynamic work stressor rather than a uniformly harmful or beneficial demand. On days when employees appraise performance pressure as a threat, they focus on potential harm and negative consequences, experience self-regulation depletion, and become more likely to act uncivilly. On days when employees appraise performance pressure as a challenge, they focus on opportunities for growth and rewards, experience engagement, and become more likely to show task proficiency and citizenship. Trait resilience shifts this process by making challenge appraisals more likely and threat appraisals less likely.

**Data & Measures**
The data come from an experience sampling methodology (ESM) study of working adults recruited through a series of online classified advertisements to complete a one-time registration survey and two daily surveys, one at noon and another after their work day, across 10 days. Participants were compensated $1.00 for each noon survey, $2.00 for each after-work survey, and a $5 bonus for completing both surveys on all 10 days. A total of 266 individuals indicated interest in participating; instructed response items were embedded in each survey and participants who answered them incorrectly were excluded, and based on eligibility criteria and careful response checks 144 individuals were registered for the study. The sample for analysis included 945 total observations nested within 108 participants, with an average of 8.75 observations completed per participant. On average participants were 39.8 years old (SD = 10.3) and had worked at their company for 5.88 years (SD = 5.42); 65.7% were female, 63.9% identified as Caucasian, 50.9% held nonsupervisory positions, and participants worked in a variety of industries of which education, healthcare, and manufacturing were most frequently cited. The noon survey contained the performance pressure measure and the after-work survey contained measures of the other study variables. Daily measures were adapted so that respondents rated the items on their experiences each day at work, and unless indicated participants responded on a five-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The within-person independent variable, daily performance pressure, was assessed with Mitchell et al.'s (2018) four-item measure ("Today, I feel tremendous pressure to produce results"). The appraisal mediators each used three items from Drach-Zahvy and Erez (2002): daily threat appraisal ("Today, I felt threatened") and daily challenge appraisal ("Today, I was challenged in a positive way"). The self-regulatory-state mediators were daily self-regulation depletion, four items from Welsh and Ordóñez (2014), and daily engagement, nine items representing emotional, cognitive, and physical engagement from Rich et al. (2010). The three behavioral outcomes were all self-reports of whether the respondent had engaged in the listed behavior that day: daily incivility, three items from Cortina, Magley, Williams, and Langhout's (2001) measure; daily task proficiency, Griffin et al.'s (2007) three-item measure; and daily citizenship, four items from Lee and Allen's (2002) measure. The between-person moderator, trait resilience, was assessed in the registration survey prior to the daily surveys with three items from Smith et al.'s (2008) measure. Controls were within-person state positive affect and state negative affect, shortened three-item measures from Watson, Clark, and Tellegen (1988) rated on a five-point scale (1 = very slightly or not at all, 5 = extremely). Core self-evaluations (Judge et al.'s 2003 12-item measure) and the performance pressure by CSE interaction were examined to test whether trait resilience is truly driving the interaction effects, but were not included in the final analyses because the results were significant irrespective of these controls. Cronbach's alphas reported in the Measures section range from .76 to .94. The model was estimated with multilevel path analysis using robust full maximum likelihood estimation in Mplus version 8.0, with performance pressure (the Level 1 predictor) group-mean centered and the between-person variables grand-mean centered; error variances and correlations were not constrained to be fixed. Indirect effects were computed with the distribution-of-the-product method and 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals, moderated mediation followed Preacher, Rucker, and Hayes's (2007) recommendations, and within-person incremental variance explained was computed as pseudo R-squared following Raudenbush and Bryk (2002). A multilevel confirmatory factor analysis indicated the measurement model provided a reasonable fit to the data (chi-square = 3852.12, df = 657, CFI = .90, RMSEA = .072, SRMR = .07) and a better fit than a variety of alternative models. The design is an observational within-person experience sampling study rather than an experiment or quasi-experiment; the authors do not claim a causal identification strategy, and instead point to the temporal separation of predictor and criterion variables across the surveys, the within-person controls, the measurement-model tests, and the presence of moderator effects as reasons that common method variance is unlikely to bias the results.

**Key Findings**
All seven hypotheses were supported in the single experience sampling study (945 observations, 108 participants). Hypothesis 1 was supported: the direct effect of performance pressure on threat appraisal was significant and positive (.14, p < .001), the direct effect of threat appraisal on self-regulation depletion was significant and positive (.37, p < .001), and the indirect effect of performance pressure on self-regulation depletion through threat appraisal was significant (.05, p < .001, 95% CI [.024, .084]). Hypothesis 2 was supported: the direct effect of performance pressure on challenge appraisal was significant and positive (.20, p < .001), the direct effect of challenge appraisal on engagement was significant and positive (.38, p < .001), and the indirect effect of performance pressure on engagement through challenge appraisal was significant (.07, p < .001, 95% CI [.045, .108]). Hypothesis 3 was supported: the direct effect of self-regulation depletion on incivility was significant and positive (.10, p < .01), and the indirect effect of performance pressure on incivility was significant (.01, p < .05, 95% CI [.001, .010]). Hypothesis 4 was supported: the direct effects of engagement were significant and positive on task proficiency (.36, p < .001) and citizenship (.28, p < .001), and the indirect effects of performance pressure were significant on task proficiency (.03, p < .01, 95% CI [.013, .043]) and citizenship (.02, p < .01, 95% CI [.009, .035]).

The cross-level moderation predictions were also supported. Hypothesis 5a was supported: the cross-level performance pressure by trait resilience interaction term was significant (-.12, p < .01), the direct effect of performance pressure on threat appraisal was stronger when trait resilience was lower (.22, p < .001) rather than higher (.06, n.s.), and the indirect effect on self-regulation depletion through threat appraisal was stronger when trait resilience was lower (.08, p < .001, 95% CI [.037, .134]) rather than higher (.02, n.s., 95% CI [-.004, .052]), with a significant difference in strength between the two effects (.06, p < .05, 95% CI [.016, .104]). Hypothesis 5b was supported: the cross-level interaction term was significant (.25, p < .001), the direct effect of performance pressure on challenge appraisal was stronger when trait resilience was higher (.37, p < .001) rather than lower (.03, n.s.), and the indirect effect on engagement was stronger when trait resilience was higher (.14, p < .001, 95% CI [.081, .206]) rather than lower (.01, n.s., 95% CI [-.031, .053]), with a significant difference in strength (.13, p < .05, 95% CI [.057, .202]). Hypothesis 6 was supported: the indirect effect of performance pressure on incivility through threat appraisal and self-regulation depletion was stronger when trait resilience was lower (.01, p < .05, 95% CI [.002, .016]) rather than higher (.00, n.s., 95% CI [-.001, .006]), with a significant difference in strength (.01, p < .05, 95% CI [.001, .011]). Hypotheses 7a and 7b were supported: the indirect effect of performance pressure on task proficiency was stronger when trait resilience was higher (.05, p < .01, 95% CI [.023, .082]) rather than lower (.00, n.s., 95% CI [-.011, .019]), with a significant difference in strength (.05, p < .05, 95% CI [.010, .082]); and the indirect effect on citizenship was stronger when trait resilience was higher (.04, p < .01, 95% CI [.017, .067]) rather than lower (.00, n.s., 95% CI [-.009, .016]), with a significant difference in strength (.04, p < .05, 95% CI [.009, .063]). Following Aguinis, Gottfredson, and Culpepper (2013), the authors report that trait resilience accounted for 16.67% and 28.89% of the total variance in threat and challenge appraisals, respectively, across employees.

A supplementary model comparison favored the hypothesized ordering of the mediators over the reverse: the hypothesized model (AIC = 13868.66, BIC = 14145.18) had smaller AIC and BIC values than the alternative reverse causal model in which self-regulatory states influenced appraisals (AIC = 13906.76, BIC = 14183.28), which the authors read as reducing concerns about the causal ordering of the mediators. Supplemental analyses requested by the editorial team reported that task proficiency and citizenship positively influenced challenge appraisals and that challenge appraisals crossed over to fuel next-day challenge appraisals, and that in a negative mood employees were more likely to experience performance pressure as threatening and then became depleted, though those effects did not carry over to behaviors, while in a positive mood employees were more likely to experience challenge appraisals that enhanced engagement, resulting in enhanced task proficiency.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to the performance-pressure literature by explaining its paradoxical effects through within-person fluctuations in threat and challenge appraisals. It extends organizational stress theory by showing that a single stressor can be appraised in flux rather than categorized as only a threat or only a challenge. It also contributes to self-regulation theory by showing that performance pressure can both deplete and energize self-regulatory resources, depending on daily appraisal and trait resilience.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should take care in how they communicate performance expectations, because the same pressure can be construed as threatening or challenging. The paper suggests emphasizing the benefits and opportunities attached to performance pressure rather than only the negative consequences of underperformance. It also notes possible value in assessing trait resilience for roles involving strong performance pressure, while cautioning that any such measures should be psychometrically sound and paired with other screening techniques.

**Limitations**
The study relies on self-reported daily survey data, creating possible common method variance concerns, although the authors separate predictor and criterion variables in time and test alternative explanations. Stress appraisals and self-regulatory states are measured at the same point in time, which limits causal certainty about the ordering of mediators. The study also does not examine specific work-environment or person-level triggers that make performance pressure more likely to be appraised as threat versus challenge.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine precipitating conditions, such as negative work events, positive work events, social support, or nonwork demands, that shift performance pressure toward threat or challenge appraisals. Scholars could also test whether challenge and threat appraisals occur simultaneously or ebb and flow within a day. Additional work could examine other moderators, such as emotional stability, perfectionism, social structures, or error-management culture, and apply the same within-person lens to other inconsistent organizational findings.
