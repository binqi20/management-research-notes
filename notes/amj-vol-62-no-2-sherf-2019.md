# Too Busy to Be Fair? The Effect of Workload and Rewards on Managers’ Justice Rule Adherence

**Abstract**
Why do managers act unfairly even when they recognize the significant organizational benefits of treating employees fairly? Prior research has explained this puzzling phenomenon predominantly through an “actor-centric” perspective, proposing that managers’ just behavior is an outcome of their own individual differences. This paper shifts the locus of theorizing away from managers to their organizational context by arguing that managers’ adherence to principles of justice is often influenced by two key contextual features—workload and rewards. Drawing on the multiple-task pursuit framework, we propose that under high workloads, just treatment of employees often competes with other managerial tasks. We further argue that organizations implicitly and explicitly signal to managers that acting justly is not as important as their other technical tasks. Hence, managers who face high workloads (i.e., high amounts of work and time pressures) tend to prioritize other technical responsibilities at the expense of acting justly, unless explicitly rewarded otherwise. We find support for our theorizing across three studies (an experience sampling study, a field study, and an experiment). We discuss the implications of our theory and findings for future research on managerial fairness and offer novel practical implications for organizations attempting to ensure employee fair treatment.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why managers sometimes fail to treat employees fairly even when they recognize the value of fairness. It examines whether managerial workload reduces justice rule adherence by pushing managers to prioritize technical tasks, and whether organizational rewards for justice weaken that process.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. There is a negative indirect effect of managers' workload on their justice rule adherence via prioritization of technical tasks.
- Hypothesis 2. The effect of managers' workload on prioritization is moderated by the rewards for justice, such that when such rewards are low (high), the relationship between workload and prioritization of technical tasks is stronger (weaker).
- Hypothesis 3. The indirect effect of workload on justice rule adherence via prioritization of technical tasks is moderated by rewards for justice, such that when such rewards are low (high), justice rule adherence is more (less) negatively affected.

Two assumptions underlying the hypotheses were treated as requiring prior empirical validation rather than as hypotheses: that managers on average see technical tasks as more important or central to their role than justice tasks, and that organizations on average reward the former more than the latter. Both were tested in the pilot study reported in Appendix A.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Managerial workload, operationalized as daily workload, perceived three-month workload, and experimentally manipulated time pressure.
- DV(s): Managers' justice rule adherence toward employees; Studies 2 and 3 also examine technical task performance.
- Mediators: Prioritization of technical tasks.
- Moderators: Justice rewards, measured as organizational rewards for fair treatment and experimentally manipulated through balanced versus low justice reward systems.

The mechanism is that high workload creates competition among managerial tasks for limited time, attention, and effort. Because organizations often signal that technical responsibilities are more important and more rewarded than justice tasks, overloaded managers prioritize technical deliverables over distributive, procedural, informational, and interpersonal justice rule adherence. Justice rewards alter this prioritization calculus by making fair treatment more salient and valued, reducing the negative indirect effect of workload on justice behavior.

**Data & Measures**
A pilot study reported in Appendix A developed and validated the measures used in the main studies: 11 parallel items capturing the organizational rewards attached to technical and justice behaviors and the perceived centrality of those behaviors to the managerial role (rewards rated 1 = "Does not reward" to 10 = "Rewards to a very large extent"; centrality rated 1 = "Very secondary" to 10 = "Very core, central, or primary"), plus a new six-item technical task prioritization scale. Two separate managerial samples were recruited: 202 managers via Prolific Academic for the exploratory factor analysis and 263 managers via Qualtrics Panels for the confirmatory factor analysis. The pilot supported the two assumptions underlying the theorizing: managers rated technical tasks as more central to their role (M = 8.25, SD = 1.36) than justice tasks (M = 6.82, SD = 2.29; t = 13.59, p < .01), and reported that their organizations reward technical performance (M = 5.85, SD = 2.44) more than justice performance (M = 5.03, SD = 2.79; t = 9.04, p < .01).

Study 1 is a two-week experience sampling study of 107 U.S.-based managers recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk, who provided 862 full day-level data points out of a possible 1,070 (81% completion rate). Daily workload was rated in a midday survey using five items taken from prior research (e.g., "So far today, I had too much work to do"; 1 = "Strongly disagree" to 5 = "Strongly agree"; alpha = .94). Prioritization of technical tasks (four of the six pilot items, adapted to the daily level; e.g., "Today, I prioritized getting things done over treating my employees fairly"; alpha = .92) and justice rule adherence (11 items representing the four justice rules, treated as a higher-order construct; alpha = .92) were rated in an end-of-day survey. Justice rewards was a between-person presurvey measure using the six-item pilot scale (alpha = .96). Daily positive and negative affect (four PANAS items each; alpha = .93 for both) and day of data collection were controls. Estimation was a multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8, with level-1 variables group-mean centered, the level-2 moderator grand-mean centered, random slopes at level 1, and parametric bootstrapping for the indirect effects.

Study 2 is a cross-sectional, multisource field study of 166 manager-employee triads in India, recruited by a market research firm from managers working in different firms across a variety of industries; 201 managers completed the manager survey, of which 166 could be matched with employee pairs. Managers reported their workload over the last three months (five items adapted from previous research; e.g., "Do you work under time pressure?"; 1 = "Never" to 7 = "Most of the time"; alpha = .75), justice rewards (six-item pilot scale, 1 = "Does not reward at all" to 10 = "Rewards to a very large extent"; alpha = .89), and prioritization of technical tasks (six-item pilot scale, 1 = "Never" to 6 = "Very frequently"; alpha = .81). Each manager nominated two employees: one, chosen at random, rated the manager's justice rule adherence using a shortened version of Colquitt's (2001) measure with three representative items per justice facet, combined into a higher-order construct (alpha = .86); the other rated the manager's technical task performance using the four-item job dimension of Welbourne, Johnson, and Amir's (1998) role-based performance scale (alpha = .76). Controls were the manager's motive to establish fairness, perceived dependence on employees, span of control, and company tenure. Estimation was a single-level, regression-based path analysis with a maximum likelihood estimator in Mplus 8, with indirect effects tested by a bootstrapping approach involving 10,000 data draws. Workload and rewards were measured rather than manipulated in Studies 1 and 2, and the authors state that they therefore cannot make strong inferences about the causal nature of those findings.

Study 3 is a one-hour lab simulation completed by 249 undergraduates enrolled in an introductory management class at an East Coast public university; 10 who did not follow instructions were excluded, leaving 239 participants (M age = 22.1; 50% female). Participants played a senior client liaison manager at a fictitious company ("ENS") in a response-inbox simulation and had to complete two tasks, presented in random order and both due before they left for a week-long training: a technical task (writing a business proposal to their boss for a new client acquisition role) and a justice task (writing a message explaining the promotion decision to the employee not selected for that role). Workload was manipulated by giving both conditions the same tasks but different lengths of time: 30 minutes in the low workload condition (n = 121) and 20 minutes in the high workload condition (n = 118). Justice rewards were manipulated through a description of the company's appraisal culture and the weights on a balanced scorecard: in the low justice rewards condition (n = 120), productivity and performance carried 70%, managing employees and treating them fairly 20%, and being a good organizational citizen 10%; in the balanced justice rewards condition (n = 119), productivity and performance and fair treatment were both weighted 45%. Manipulation checks used a three-item felt time pressure measure (alpha = .80) plus three technical-rewards items (alpha = .76) and two justice-rewards items (alpha = .78). Performance was assessed by two independent coders blind to the hypotheses and conditions, who rated the messages written for each task from 0-5 against four criteria specified for that task (ICC[2] = .92 for the technical task, .86 for the justice task); scores were averaged and then standardized. Prioritization of the technical task was a three-item self-report administered after the simulation (1 = "Strongly disagree" to 5 = "Strongly agree"; alpha = .78). Analyses were an ANOVA on prioritization, a repeated measures analysis with performance on the two tasks as the within-person dependent variables, and the PROCESS macro for SPSS for the conditional indirect effects. Study 3 is a randomized experiment, which the authors describe as establishing causality.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (experience sampling; 107 managers, 862 day-level observations): workload during the day was positively related to prioritization of technical tasks on that day (gamma = 0.13, p < .01; Table 3, Model 1), and the interaction between daily workload and justice rewards on daily prioritization was significant (gamma = -0.04, p = .01; Table 3, Model 2). Simple slopes: when justice rewards were low, daily workload related positively to technical task prioritization (-1 SD, gamma = .21, p < .01); when justice rewards were high, daily workload was unrelated to such prioritization (+1 SD, gamma = .05, p = .33). Daily prioritization of technical tasks was negatively related to daily justice rule adherence (gamma = -0.16, p < .01; Table 3, Model 4).
- Study 1 indirect effects: the unconditional indirect effect of daily workload on daily justice rule adherence via daily prioritization of technical tasks was significant (-.02, 95% CI [-.04, -.01]), as was the effect when justice rewards were low (-1 SD, -.03, 95% CI [-.05, -.01]); it was not significant when justice rewards were high (+1 SD, -.01, SE = .01, 95% CI [-.02, .01]), and the difference between the two effects was significant (.03, 95% CI [.003, .05]). The authors state that these results support all of their hypotheses, and report in a footnote that indirect effects estimated separately for each justice facet were similar.
- Study 2 (field study; 166 manager-employee triads): workload was positively related to prioritization of technical tasks (B = .19, p < .01; Table 5, Model 1) and interacted with justice rewards in predicting prioritization (B = -.09, p = .03; Table 5, Model 2). Simple slopes: workload was not related to prioritization of technical tasks when justice rewards were high (+1 SD, B = .11, p = .09) but was positively related when justice rewards were low (-1 SD, B = .35, p < .01). Prioritization of technical tasks was negatively related to justice rule adherence (B = -.18, p = .01; Model 4) but not to technical task performance (B = -.04, p = .64; Model 6).
- Study 2 indirect effects: the unconditional indirect effect of workload on justice rule adherence via prioritization was negative (-.04, 95% CI [-.10, -.01]), as was the indirect effect when justice rewards were low (-1 SD, -.06, 95% CI [-.14, -.02]), but not when they were high (+1 SD, -.02, 95% CI [-.07, .01]); the two indirect effects differed significantly from one another (.04, 95% CI [.001, .12]). Re-running the models excluding all control variables again indicated an unconditional negative indirect effect (-.06, 95% CI [-.11, -.01]), but the interaction between workload and justice rewards was no longer significant (B = -.04, p = .44). A footnote reports that the facet-level indirect effects were consistent in direction with the overall justice rule adherence measure but were significant only for distributive and interpersonal justice adherence (p < .05), only marginally significant for procedural justice rule adherence (p < .10), and not significant for informational justice rule adherence (p > .10).
- Study 3 (lab simulation; 239 participants): the workload-by-justice-rewards interaction was significant for prioritization of the technical task (F[1, 239] = 4.14, p = .04, partial eta-squared = .02) and, in the repeated measures analysis with performance on the two tasks as the within-person dependent variables, for performance (F[1, 235] = 3.98, p = .047, partial eta-squared = .02). Prioritization of the technical task was significantly higher in the high workload-low justice rewards condition (M = 3.65, SE = 0.14) than in all other conditions (low workload-low rewards M = 3.18; low workload-balanced M = 2.93; high workload-balanced M = 2.86), and performance on the justice task was significantly lower in that condition (M = -0.62, SE = 0.12) than in all other conditions (0.23, 0.35, and 0.03).
- Study 3, technical performance: in the high workload condition technical task performance was lower than in the low workload condition (M = -0.23 and -0.27 versus 0.24 and 0.25), but within the high workload condition it was not significantly affected by the rewards condition. The authors read this as participants in the high workload-balanced justice rewards condition showing higher adherence to justice rules without diminished technical task performance.
- Study 3 indirect effects: the indirect effect of workload via prioritization of the technical task was negative when justice rewards were low (-.12, 95% CI [-.26, -.03]) but not significant when they were balanced (.02, 95% CI [-.07, .11]); the index of moderated mediation indicated that these indirect effects differed significantly (index = .14, 95% CI [.01, .31]).
- Across the three studies the authors report that their hypotheses were supported: Study 1's results are described as supporting all of the hypotheses, Studies 1 and 2 together as supporting them, and the Study 3 analyses as supporting them, with Study 3 described as providing the causal evidence that justice rewards moderate the workload-prioritization relationship. The qualification the paper itself records is the Study 2 sensitivity analysis in which the workload-by-justice-rewards interaction lost significance once the control variables were dropped.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends organizational justice research by shifting from a primarily actor-centric explanation of managerial unfairness toward a context-centric account grounded in workload, rewards, and task prioritization. It conceptualizes justice rule adherence as an effortful managerial task that competes with other responsibilities rather than as an isolated expression of disposition or motive. It also contributes to multiple-task pursuit theory by showing that reward systems can shape prioritization between technical and relational tasks and that justice rewards need not reduce technical performance.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations seeking fairer managerial behavior should not rely only on selecting or training fair-minded managers. They should manage avoidable workload pressures and explicitly include justice rule adherence in formal and informal evaluation and reward systems. The paper also suggests scheduling practices, such as regular employee meetings or protected time for explanations and listening, to make justice-related work harder to crowd out under high workload.

**Limitations**
Study 1 separated workload from later outcomes during the day but measured prioritization and justice rule adherence at the same time, limiting causal inference. Study 2 used multisource data but remained cross-sectional and relied on manager nominations of employee raters. Study 3 established causality through a simulation, but its student sample and laboratory task limit generalizability to practicing managers.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine technical performance tradeoffs using stronger field measures, such as top-management ratings or objective performance indicators. Scholars could also test whether workload and justice rewards affect distributive, procedural, interpersonal, and informational justice differently. The authors further encourage research on justice climates, manager time-management skills, long-term orientation, and synergistic strategies that allow managers to meet technical and justice demands together.
