# Examination of CEO–CFO Social Interaction through Language Style Matching: Outcomes for the CFO and the Organization

**Abstract**
This study proposes that CEO–CFO “language style matching”—a form of unconscious verbal mimicry based on function words—can provide insights into social interaction processes between CEOs and CFOs. We argue and empirically verify that high CEO– CFO language style matching reflects CFOs’ strong attempts to ingratiate themselves with CEOs. Because ingratiation with superiors can lead to the superiors’ positive evaluations of subordinates, CFOs who exhibit higher language style matching with CEOs will receive higher compensation and are more likely to become board members of the associated firms. In addition, the proposed relationships will be stronger when CEOs are more powerful. Yet, in the presence of high CEO–CFO language style matching, CFOs are less likely to voice different viewpoints and challenge CEOs in strategic decision processes. As a result, firms tend to undertake more mergers and acquisitions, and such mergers and acquisitions will be paid with a low percentage of cash (vs. stock) and realize lower announcement returns. Using a sample of over 2,000 U.S. firms from the period 2002–2013, we find empirical support for these predictions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether CEO-CFO language style matching reveals otherwise hard-to-observe social interaction processes between top executives and whether those interactions affect both CFO personal outcomes and firm strategic outcomes. It specifically examines whether higher CEO-CFO LSM predicts CFO compensation, CFO board appointment, M&A intensity, M&A payment method, and investor reactions to M&A announcements.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The level of LSM between the CEO and the CFO of a firm is positively associated with the CFO's compensation level.
Hypothesis 2. The level of LSM between the CEO and the CFO is positively related to the likelihood that the CFO becomes a board member at his or her focal firm.
Hypothesis 3a. The positive relationship between the level of LSM between the CEO and the CFO and the CFO's compensation level is stronger when the CEO has greater power.
Hypothesis 3b. The positive relationship between the level of LSM between the CEO and the CFO and the likelihood that the CFO will become a board member at his or her focal firm is stronger when the CEO has greater power.
Hypothesis 4. The level of LSM between the CEO and the CFO of a firm is positively associated with (a) the firm's number of M&As and (b) value of M&As.
Hypothesis 5. CEO-CFO LSM has a negative relationship with investor reactions to M&A announcements, and the percentage of cash payment partially mediates this negative relationship.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO-CFO language style matching in Q&A sections of conference calls, measured from function-word use.
- DV(s): CFO total compensation, likelihood that the CFO becomes a board member, number and value of M&As, percentage of cash payment, M&A announcement returns, and ROA change.
- Mediators: Percentage of cash payment partially mediates the relationship between CEO-CFO LSM and M&A announcement returns.
- Moderators: CEO power strengthens the positive relationships between CEO-CFO LSM and CFO compensation and directorship.

The mechanism is that high CEO-CFO LSM reflects the CFO's attempt to affiliate with and ingratiate themselves with the CEO. That ingratiation can lead CEOs to evaluate CFOs more favorably, supporting higher compensation and greater likelihood of board appointment, especially when CEOs have more power over pay and nomination processes. The same social dynamic weakens CFO dissent and bottom-up monitoring in strategic decisions, allowing CEOs to undertake more M&As, use less cash in deals, and generate lower announcement returns.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: conference-call transcripts for U.S. firms from Thomson Reuters StreetEvents over 2002-2013; accounting data from Compustat; stock returns from CRSP; CEO and CFO demographics from Capital IQ's People Intelligence, Morningstar's Historical Governance, and BoardEx; M&A data from SDC. After matching all variables needed for the study the authors had 2,384 firms, 1,406 of which belonged to the S&P 1500 index.
- IV: CEO-CFO LSM, measured with the Ireland et al. (2011) measure applied to the words the CEO and the CFO spoke in the Q&A section of each conference call. The two speakers' files are analyzed separately in LIWC over nine basic-level function-word categories; the nine category-level similarity scores are summed into a composite, and each transcript is weighted by its share of total CEO-plus-CFO Q&A words to give a CFO-year value (Hypotheses 1-3) or a firm-year value (Hypotheses 4 and 5).
- DVs: CFO compensation = natural logarithm of the CFO's total compensation (salary, bonus, options granted, restricted stock, other pay), from Capital IQ and Morningstar; CFO director = 1 in the year the CFO becomes a board member of the focal firm and 0 otherwise, with all observations after that event excluded; number of M&As = total majority-ownership deals announced in a year (transaction value over $1 million) and value of M&As = their total transaction value, both logged plus one; investor reactions = cumulative abnormal returns over a [-1, +1] event window (also [-2, +2]); ROA change = ROA at t+1 minus ROA at t-1 as an alternative measure of M&A deal quality.
- Mediator: percentage of cash (as opposed to stock) used to pay for the focal deal.
- Moderator: CEO power, a formative index of five standardized variables - CEO tenure, CEO duality, board independence (reverse coded), CEO equity ownership, and percentage of directors appointed by the CEO.
- Controls: CEO-CFO language content matching (LCM), built by the same procedure from Loughran and McDonald's (2011) positive and negative word dictionaries, plus firm-, CEO-, CFO-, board-, dyad-, and deal-level controls.

Estimation: firm fixed-effects OLS with standard errors clustered by CFO for Hypotheses 1 and 3a; Cox proportional hazard models and a discrete-time logistic model for Hypotheses 2 and 3b; firm fixed-effects OLS and fixed-effects Poisson with errors clustered by firm for Hypothesis 4; pooled OLS with a Heckman first-stage probit and inverse Mills ratio, Fama-French 48 industry fixed effects, and year fixed effects for Hypothesis 5, with a Sobel test for the mediation. Dependent variables are measured at t and all other variables at t-1, except for the deal-level Hypothesis 5 tests, where deal-level variables are measured at t. Reported observations vary by model: 12,546 (CFO compensation OLS), 7,877 (Cox), 8,915 (logit), 16,182 (compensation OLS with CEO power), 15,984 and 11,753 (M&A intensity), and 4,828 deals (4,775 for ROA change). The design is associational; the authors use the Heckman selection model to address potential sample selection bias in whether a firm undertakes M&As, address the non-random assignment of CEO-CFO LSM with supplementary inverse-probability-of-treatment-weighted (IPTW) estimation, and use hybrid within/between-firm regressions to rule out the influence of firm-level heterogeneity, rather than claiming causal identification.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported: in the firm fixed-effects OLS the coefficient on CEO-CFO LSM is positive and significant (b = 0.032, p < .01), and a one-unit increase is associated with a 3.25% increase in CFO total compensation. In unreported models the coefficient is positive and significant for CFO short-term compensation (b = 0.026, p < .01) but negative and not significant for CFO long-term compensation.
Hypothesis 2 is supported in both estimators: the Cox model gives b = 0.215, p < .01 (a one-unit increase raises the "risk" of becoming a board member by 23.99%), and the discrete-time logit gives b = 0.269, p < .01 (a 30.87% increase in the likelihood of becoming a director at the focal firm).
Hypothesis 3a: the CEO-CFO LSM x CEO power interaction is positive and marginally significant (b = 0.005, p < .10, two-tailed), which the authors report as consistent with the prediction. Hypothesis 3b: the interaction is positive and statistically significant (b = 0.031, p < .05), consistent with the prediction.
Hypothesis 4 is supported for both proxies: number of M&As, firm fixed-effects OLS b = 0.023, p < .05 (a 2.3% increase) and Poisson b = 0.107, p < .05; value of M&As b = 0.111, p < .05 (an 11.7% increase in value).
Hypothesis 5 is supported: CEO-CFO LSM is negatively related to the percentage of cash payment (b = -0.0526, p < .01); the percentage of cash payment is positively related to CARs [-1, +1] (b = 0.0054, p < .01); with both predictors entered, cash percentage remains positive (b = 0.0053, p < .01) and LSM is negative (b = -0.0050, p < .05), with a Sobel statistic of -2.05, p < .05, supporting partial mediation. The same pattern holds for CARs [-2, +2] (cash percentage b = 0.0060 and LSM b = -0.0083, both p < .01; Sobel -2.46, p < .05). Using ROA change around the announcement as an alternative deal-quality measure, LSM is negative and significant (b = -0.0115, p < .01).
Across all the models in Table 3 the coefficients on CEO-CFO LCM are not statistically significant.
Supplementary analyses: in the IPTW regressions LSM remains positive for CFO compensation (b = 0.025, p < .10), CFO board membership (b = 0.404, p < .01), number of M&As (b = 0.024, p < .05), and value of M&As (b = 0.110, p < .05). In the tenure-order subgroup tests, with CFO compensation as the dependent variable the LSM coefficient is positive but not statistically significant both in the subsample in which CEO tenure starts after CFO tenure starts and in the subsample in which CEO tenure starts before CFO tenure; in the subsample in which CEO tenure starts after CFO tenure the logistic coefficient for CFO board appointment is positive and statistically significant, and with number and value of M&As as dependent variables the LSM coefficients are positive and statistically significant using the sample in which CFO tenure starts before CEO tenure. In unreported hybrid regressions both within- and between-firm variance in LSM is significantly related to CFO compensation, while only within-firm variance is significantly related to the number and value of M&As.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to upper echelons research by replacing demographic proxies with language style matching as a behavioral indicator of social interaction between top executives. It also shifts attention from the whole TMT or CEO alone to the CEO-CFO dyad as a consequential subteam for strategic decisions.

The paper extends corporate governance and executive compensation research by showing that subordinate executives' interactions with the CEO can affect both their own career outcomes and firm-level governance quality. It also contributes to M&A research by theorizing the CFO's role as a potential source of constructive dissent whose absence can reduce acquisition quality.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that boards and other stakeholders can use unusually high language style matching between senior executives as a signal that their relationship may lack constructive challenge. Boards should be concerned when the CFO appears too linguistically and behaviorally aligned with the CEO, because this may reduce independent judgment in major strategic decisions.

The paper also implies that CEOs and boards should encourage CFOs to voice dissent and introduce structured disagreement in decision processes. When CFOs do not play the devil's advocate role, other executives or governance mechanisms may need to compensate for the loss of bottom-up monitoring.

**Limitations**
The authors acknowledge that they infer CFO ingratiation from CEO-CFO LSM, although their evidence cannot fully separate ingratiation from social cohesion. The study focuses on unconscious verbal mimicry and does not examine other forms of behavioral mimicry, such as facial mimicry.

The evidence comes from conference-call language, so the authors call for work on other social-interaction indicators, such as surveys, facial mimicry, and language content matching. The analysis also focuses on M&A decisions, leaving other strategic decisions for future testing.

**Future Research**
Future research could collect CEO-CFO behavioral data through surveys or other direct measures to disentangle social cohesion from CFO ingratiation more fully. Scholars could also study whether facial mimicry, language content matching, or experimental evidence changes third-party evaluations of CEO-CFO dyads.

Future work could extend the LSM approach to other executive dyads, whole TMTs, non-CEO executive relationships, and CEO-board chair relationships. These extensions would test whether language style matching predicts the quality of other strategic decisions and board monitoring.
