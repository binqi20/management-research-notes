# Bored by Interest: How Intrinsic Motivation in One Task Can Reduce Performance on Other Tasks

**Abstract**
While existing research has demonstrated that intrinsic motivation can increase task performance, jobs are composed of multiple tasks, and it remains to be seen how intrinsic motivation in one task affects performance on other tasks. Drawing on theories of psychological contrast, we hypothesize that high intrinsic motivation in one task reduces performance on less intrinsically motivating tasks. In a field study at a Korean department store, employees with the highest maximum intrinsic motivation in one task had lower average and minimum performance across their other tasks as well as more performance variance across their tasks. In a laboratory experiment in the United States, working on a highly intrinsically motivating initial task led participants to perform worse in a subsequent task if it was uninteresting, but not if it was interesting. This effect was mediated by boredom, but not by a range of other psychological processes. Across both studies, moderate intrinsic motivation in one task was associated with better performance in less interesting tasks than high intrinsic motivation, revealing a curvilinear cross-task effect of intrinsic motivation. Our research advances knowledge about the dark side of intrinsic motivation, the design of work, and the drivers of task performance.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how intrinsic motivation in one task affects performance on other tasks within multi-task work. It focuses on whether very high intrinsic motivation in a focal task can reduce performance on less interesting tasks, and whether boredom explains that cross-task performance cost.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Intrinsic motivation in one task has an inverted U-shaped effect on performance in other tasks, such that low or high intrinsic motivation decreases cross-task performance, whereas moderate intrinsic motivation increases cross-task performance.
- Hypothesis 2: The curvilinear effect of intrinsic motivation in one task on performance in other tasks is moderated by intrinsic motivation in those tasks, such that high intrinsic motivation in one task decreases performance in other tasks that are less interesting but not as or more interesting.
- Hypothesis 3: The curvilinear effect of intrinsic motivation in one task on performance in less interesting tasks is mediated by boredom.

Study 1 tested only Hypothesis 1: the paper states that boredom was not measured there and that, because task sequencing was not controlled, the moderating hypothesis could not be rigorously tested. Study 2 was designed to test the mediating and moderating hypotheses as well.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Intrinsic motivation in one task, operationalized in Study 1 as maximum task-level intrinsic motivation and in Study 2 as experimentally manipulated low, moderate, or high intrinsic motivation in an initial YouTube-search task.
- DV(s): Cross-task performance, including average performance in less intrinsically motivating tasks, minimum performance, performance variance, and objective performance on a subsequent phone-book or Little Alchemy task.
- Mediators: Boredom during the subsequent task.
- Moderators: Intrinsic motivation in the other task; the negative effect appears for less interesting subsequent tasks but not for highly interesting subsequent tasks.

The mechanism is psychological contrast. A highly intrinsically motivating task raises the experiential standard against which other tasks are judged, making less interesting tasks feel more boring. Boredom then weakens focus and performance on those other tasks. Across the field and laboratory studies, moderate intrinsic motivation produced better cross-task performance than either low or high intrinsic motivation.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (field survey; associational design). Data came from 105 salespeople and their direct supervisors at a department store in Seoul, South Korea. Surveys were distributed to 117 salespeople and 105 were completed (90% response rate); 11 supervisors rated the 105 employees, with a 100% supervisor response rate. The job comprised six core tasks: selling, inventory management, product learning, arranging items for display, making returns and exchanges, and assisting other salespeople. All measures used a seven-point Likert-type scale. Task-level intrinsic motivation was a four-item measure adapted from existing scales, asking how interesting, enjoyable, fun, and engaging each task was (α = .92); the independent variable, maximum intrinsic motivation, was each employee's highest task intrinsic motivation score across the six core tasks. Task-level performance was a single supervisor-rated item per task, from 1 (very poor performance in this task) to 7 (very high performance in this task). The dependent variables were average performance in one's less intrinsically motivating tasks (the mean across tasks other than the one(s) with maximum intrinsic motivation), minimum performance (the employee's lowest task performance rating), and performance variance (the standard deviation of performance ratings across all tasks). Controls were trait intrinsic motivation (Amabile et al., 1994; α = .86), job-level extrinsic motivation (Ryan & Connell, 1989; α = .77), job-level prosocial motivation (Grant, 2008; α = .88), gender, age, and job experience. Estimation used hierarchical linear modeling with random-intercept models, with results reported as consistent when a supervisor fixed effect is included, plus the "two-lines" test (Simonsohn, 2018). The authors state that it is unclear whether intrinsic motivation in a task has a causal impact on performance in other tasks.
Study 2 (randomized laboratory experiment). 255 undergraduates at a large public U.S. university (67% female), recruited from the university's behavioral lab subject pool and paid $12 for an hour, completed the study on computer using Qualtrics and were randomly assigned to one of six conditions in a 3 (task 1 intrinsic motivation: low, medium, high) x 2 (task 2 intrinsic motivation: low, high) between-subjects factorial design; the per-condition ns in Table 5 range from 39 to 44. Task 1 held task structure constant and varied content: all participants searched for 15 YouTube videos in 10 minutes, on math tutorials (low), life hacks (medium), or the most interesting and enjoyable videos (high), then spent five more minutes watching one video, with choice varied by condition (assigned one video; select one of three found; any one found). These manipulations were pretested on Amazon Mechanical Turk with a different sample (n = 137) using Study 1's intrinsic motivation measure (α = .96). Task 2 was either Little Alchemy, an online game (interesting), or copying names and phone numbers from a phone book (uninteresting); the two tasks were pretested on Mechanical Turk with a different sample (n = 97) using Study 1's scale (α = .97), yielding M = 6.06, SD = 0.96 for Little Alchemy and M = 1.96, SD = 1.07 for the phone book task, t(95) = 4.11, p < .001. Performance was measured objectively: the number of correct entries on the phone book task, and the game's objective score of the number of elements created in Little Alchemy (self-reported plus a screenshot to verify), analyzed as standardized performance scores. Boredom during the second task used four items from van Tilburg and Igou (2012) anchored at 1 (disagree strongly) and 7 (agree strongly) (α = .84). Alternative mechanisms measured during the second task were anger, disgust, anxiety, sadness, desire, relaxation, and happiness (four items each from the Discrete Emotions Questionnaire), alongside perceived humor of the first task (Abel & Maxwell, 2002; α = .97) and time spent on the second task; the manipulation pretest additionally assessed cognitive depletion, attention residue, and perceptions of task difficulty, complexity, and cognitive load. Manipulation checks reused Study 1's four-item scale (α = .92 for the YouTube task, .96 for Little Alchemy, .88 for the phone book task). Analyses were 3 x 2 ANOVA with simple effects and pairwise comparisons, regression, and bootstrapped moderated mediation with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals from 1,000 random samples with replacement (Hayes & Preacher, 2010).

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (Hypothesis 1 only). The coefficient for maximum intrinsic motivation squared was negative and significant for average performance in other, less intrinsically motivating tasks (γ = -.13, p < .01) and for minimum performance (γ = -.29, p < .001), and positive and significant for performance variance (γ = .11, p < .01) — an inverted U-shape for the two performance outcomes and a U-shape for variance. In the "two-lines" test, the relationship between maximum intrinsic motivation and average performance in other tasks was significant and positive for low-to-moderate values (γ = .24, p < .05) and significant and negative for moderate-to-high values (γ = -.44, p < .001); for minimum performance it was positive for low-to-moderate values (γ = .31, p < .001) and negative for moderate-to-high values (γ = -.75, p < .001); for performance variance it fell short of significance for low-to-moderate values (γ = -.11, p = .10) but was significantly positive for moderate-to-high values (γ = .39, p < .001). The authors conclude that these results support Hypothesis 1. Supplementary analyses found task-level intrinsic motivation positively related to performance in that same task (γ = .14, p < .001; γ = .12, p < .001 with employee-level controls), with no significant curvilinear within-task relationship, and a task-level analysis in which maximum intrinsic motivation squared significantly predicted performance in the less intrinsically motivating tasks (γ = -.12, p < .01), while intrinsic motivation in the less motivating task itself was not statistically significant in predicting performance in that task and did not moderate the curvilinear effect.
Study 2 (Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3). Manipulation checks confirmed that the task 1 and task 2 manipulations created significant differences in intrinsic motivation. The interaction of task 1 and task 2 intrinsic motivation on standardized performance was significant, F(2, 248) = 4.18, p < .05; simple effects showed a significant curvilinear effect of task 1 intrinsic motivation on phone book performance, F(2, 248) = 3.70, p < .05, but not on Little Alchemy performance, F(2, 248) = 0.04. On the phone book task, the moderate intrinsic motivation condition performed significantly better than the high condition (Mdifference = 0.55, p = .01) and than the low condition (Mdifference = 0.44, p < .05), while the low and high conditions did not differ significantly from each other; no pairwise differences emerged for Little Alchemy. The effect was a function of the number of correct entries, not the number of attempted entries. The authors read this as support for Hypotheses 1 and 2. For Hypothesis 3, the interaction of task 1 and task 2 intrinsic motivation predicted boredom (b = -.42, SE = .21, t(249) = -2.00, p < .05); adding boredom reduced the interaction effect on performance below significance, and boredom significantly predicted performance (b = -.29, SE = .08, t(248) = -3.66, p < .001). The index of moderated mediation was .13 (95% CI [0.01, 0.32]); the indirect curvilinear effect through boredom was positive and significant at low task 2 intrinsic motivation, the phone book task (.12, 95% CI [0.03, 0.24]), and not significant at high task 2 intrinsic motivation, Little Alchemy (-.01, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.08]). Supplementary analyses ruled out alternatives: a multivariate analysis of variance showed no significant effects of task 1 intrinsic motivation on anger, disgust, anxiety, sadness, desire, relaxation, or happiness; humor differed significantly between conditions but did not predict phone book performance, and the performance and moderated mediation effects held when humor was controlled; and there were no significant differences between conditions in time spent on the phone book task or perceived intrinsic motivation toward it.
Across the two studies, moderate intrinsic motivation in one task was associated with better performance in less interesting tasks than high intrinsic motivation. Hypothesis 1 was supported in both studies; Hypotheses 2 and 3 were tested and supported only in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends motivation theory by shifting attention from within-task benefits of intrinsic motivation to cross-task tradeoffs in multi-task jobs. It shows that intrinsic motivation can be a double-edged sword: beneficial for the focal task but harmful for less interesting tasks through boredom. It also contributes to work design research by explaining why enriching one task may unintentionally depress performance on other tasks.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that employees who are intensely interested in one task may be at risk of underperforming on necessary but less exciting tasks. Work scheduling may matter: the authors suggest sequencing moderately interesting tasks before tedious tasks, rather than placing highly interesting tasks immediately before them. Employees can also use this awareness to manage attention, energy, and task order.

**Limitations**
The field study was correlational and could not establish whether intrinsic motivation caused lower performance in other tasks. It also lacked task-sequencing data, relied on supervisor performance ratings, and came from one Korean department-store context. The laboratory study improved causal inference, but the authors controlled task sequencing, which prevented them from examining whether employees neglect a less interesting task in favor of a more interesting one when both are available within the same window of time.

**Future Research**
Future work could use diary or field-study designs to examine task sequencing, multitasking, and time allocation in real jobs. Researchers could test whether the same cross-task pattern appears for work with greater complexity and creative requirements rather than accuracy and proficiency outcomes. Further studies could examine cross-task effects of extrinsic, achievement, or prosocial motivation and the role of job crafting in making dull tasks less boring.
