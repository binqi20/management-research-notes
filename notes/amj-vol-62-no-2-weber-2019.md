# The Cognitive and Behavioral Impact of Promotion and Prevention Contracts on Trust in Repeated Exchanges

**Abstract**
Although contracts certainly facilitate exchange, scholars have debated whether contracts and trust are complements or substitutes. Recent theoretical work has suggested that contract frames influence the relationship between contracts and trust. We test and extend this theorizing by examining the effects of prevention and promotion contract frames on trust and some potential cognitive and emotional mechanisms responsible for them. We also explore how unexpected negative events affect trust developed under different contract frames. Experiment 1 found that promotion contracts fostered stronger attributions of benevolence than prevention contracts, but emotional experiences of the exchanges did not differ. Additionally, trusting intentions were higher following positive exchange experience under promotion than prevention contracts. Experiment 2 found that people were more willing to engage in trusting behavior following positive exchange experience under promotion than prevention contracts. However, violations of exchange expectations were more damaging to trust developed under promotion than prevention contracts. Together, the studies indicate that contract frames and whether exchange experiences are positive or negative affect the relationship between contracts and trust, likely because contract frames influence attributions of benevolence.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether prevention and promotion contract frames lead contracts to function differently as substitutes for or complements to trust in repeated exchanges. It also asks which cognitive or emotional mechanisms explain those effects and how trust formed under different contract frames responds to unexpected negative exchange events.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: People will experience more cheerfulness (less dejection) in repeated positive exchanges under promotion than prevention contracts.
- H1b: People will experience more quiescence (less agitation) in repeated positive exchanges under prevention than promotion contracts.
- H1c: People will attribute more benevolence to their exchange partners over repeated positive exchange under promotion than prevention contracts.
- H2a: People will report less willingness to be vulnerable following repeated positive exchange experience under prevention than promotion contracts.
- H2b: When contracts are removed, people will exhibit less trusting behavior following repeated positive exchange experience under prevention than promotion contracts.
- H3: Unexpected negative exchange events will lead to a smaller decline in trust established under prevention than promotion contracts.
- Study 1 tested Hypotheses 1a, 1b, 1c, and 2a; Study 2 tested Hypotheses 2b and 3.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Contract frame, comparing promotion contracts, prevention contracts, and in Study 2 a no-contract baseline; exchange history, including repeated positive exchanges and an unexpected negative event.
- DV(s): Trusting intentions in Study 1 and trusting behavior, operationalized as joint-venture investment without a contract, in Study 2.
- Mediators: Attributions of exchange partner benevolence are theorized and supported as a cognitive mechanism; emotional experiences are theorized but not empirically supported.
- Moderators: The valence and timing of exchange experience, especially whether an unexpected negative event follows prior positive exchange experience.

The mechanism is that promotion contracts frame exchange safeguards as rewards and opportunities, making positive partner behavior more likely to be attributed to benevolence. Prevention contracts frame safeguards as penalties and obligations, making contractual constraints more salient and reducing benevolence attributions. As a result, promotion contracts foster stronger trust after positive exchanges, but that trust is more fragile when subsequent exchange behavior violates expectations; prevention contracts develop less trust but preserve it somewhat better after negative events.

**Data & Measures**
Both studies used modified versions of the "Trust Game" in which the participant managed Company A and decided how to invest eight resource units either in-house (multiplied by 1.1) or in a joint venture (JV) with Company B (multiplied by 5, with Company B controlling how the output was redistributed); Company B's responses were pre-programmed. Study 1 was a randomized two-condition experiment with 140 adult residents of the United States (43% female; age M = 35.51, SD = 10.48; years of full-time work experience M = 13.53, SD = 10.21) recruited from Amazon's Mechanical Turk and paid $1.00 base plus up to $4.00 bonus on a randomly selected round; participants were randomly assigned to an economically equivalent promotion or prevention contract frame that was held constant across Rounds 1 and 2, the study ended without warning after Round 2, few participants rejected the contract (n = 5 promotion; n = 4 prevention), and data from nine participants who invested no units in the JV in Round 1 or Round 2 were removed, with the reported Study 1 regressions carrying t(130). A separate pilot of 100 adult U.S. Mechanical Turk residents (32% female; age M = 33.77, SD = 9.52) paid $0.50 checked the manipulation using 12 regulatory-focus items adapted from Ferris et al. (2013), averaged into promotion focus (alpha = .95) and prevention focus (alpha = .90) indices. Study 1 measures: emotions used 12 items from Higgins et al. (1997) averaged into dejection-cheerfulness (alpha = .91) and agitation-quiescence (alpha = .89) indices, plus six alternative indices reported in Table 2 (positive emotions alpha = .87; cheerfulness alpha = .90; quiescence alpha = .87; negative emotions alpha = .96; dejection alpha = .96; agitation alpha = .92); attributions of partner benevolence used five items adapted from Mayer and Davis (1999) on 7-point strongly-disagree-to-strongly-agree scales (alpha = .95), with parallel integrity (five items, alpha = .71) and ability (six items, alpha = .91) scales; trusting intentions used four items adapted from Mayer and Davis (1999) capturing willingness to be vulnerable (alpha = .76). Study 2 was a randomized three-condition experiment with 116 full-time or part-time MBA students who completed the task by computer during class and were randomly assigned to a promotion contract, a prevention contract, or no contract; participants completed five decision rounds after being told the exercise would comprise six, all participants faced Round 3 without a contract, and in Round 3 Company B returned only what Company A had invested, producing a small net loss for the round (less than 1 unit) once opportunity costs were counted. Following Malhotra and Murnighan (2002), Study 2 operationalized trusting behavior as JV investment in the absence of a contract, so it could be assessed in Rounds 1 and 2 only in the no-contract condition and in Rounds 3, 4, and 5 in all conditions, with more JV investment indicating more trusting behavior. Study 2 estimation combined panel regression with fixed effects for individuals, using interacted dummy variables for decision round and contract condition (Table 3A), with cross-sectional OLS regressions on single rounds using the prevention condition as the reference category (Table 3B). Identification rests on random assignment to contract condition; the authors state that their design did not allow them to firmly establish the causal relation between attributions of benevolence and trust, only that the two covary.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 did not support Hypothesis 1a: scores on the dejection-cheerfulness index were the same in the prevention (M = 1.84) and promotion (M = 1.77) conditions, B = 0.07, t(130) = 0.31, p = .76. Hypothesis 1b was likewise not supported: the agitation-quiescence index did not differ between the prevention (M = 1.85) and promotion (M = 1.98) conditions, B = .13, t(130) = 0.58, p = .57, and none of the six alternative emotion indices in Table 2 produced a statistically significant result. Hypothesis 1c was supported: attributions of benevolence were greater in the promotion (M = 4.34) than the prevention (M = 3.57) condition, B = .78, t(130) = 3.41, p = .001. Exploratory tests of the other two trustworthiness dimensions found no contract-frame differences: integrity, promotion M = 5.00 versus prevention M = 4.78, t(130) = 1.32, p = .19; ability, promotion M = 5.53 versus prevention M = 5.42, t(130) = 0.62, p = .54. Hypothesis 2a was supported: trusting intentions were lower in the prevention (M = 3.70) than the promotion (M = 4.20) condition, B = -.50, t(130) = -2.38, p = .02. Study 2 supported Hypothesis 2b in all three of its tests. In the longitudinal test, participants in the promotion condition invested significantly less in the JV in Round 3 (M = 6.00) than Round 2 (M = 6.97), and participants in the prevention condition also invested significantly less in Round 3 (M = 4.79) than Round 2 (M = 7.74), but a test of differences confirmed the drop across Rounds 2 and 3 was larger in the prevention than the promotion condition, p = .006. In the cross-sectional test, Round 3 trusting behavior was significantly higher in the promotion (M = 6.00) than the prevention (M = 4.82) condition (Table 3B: promotion B = 1.18, p < .05, prevention as reference). Against a no-contract baseline, participants in both the promotion condition (Round 3 M = 6.00) and the prevention condition (Round 3 M = 4.82) exhibited more trusting behavior than no-contract participants in Round 1 (M = 3.21), but a test of differences indicated the prevention effect was significantly smaller, p = .03. Hypothesis 3 was also supported: participants with promotion-contract experience exhibited less trusting behavior in Round 4 (M = 3.18) than Round 3 (M = 6.00), whereas participants with prevention-contract experience exhibited the same trusting behavior in Round 3 (M = 4.79) and Round 4 (M = 4.62), and a test of differences confirmed the drop across rounds was larger in the promotion than the prevention condition, p < .001; the cross-sectional comparison of Round 4 JV investment was significantly lower in the promotion (M = 3.18) than the prevention (M = 4.62) condition (Table 3B: promotion B = -1.44, p < .05). Taken together, the emotional mechanism proposed by Weber and Mayer (2011) received no support in Study 1, while the benevolence-attribution mechanism did, and the authors caution that they observed only that attributions of benevolence and trusting intentions covary rather than establishing a causal relation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the contract-trust complement-substitute debate by showing that the effect of contracts on trust depends on contract framing rather than on the mere presence of a contract. It extends Weber and Mayer's contract-frame theory by providing experimental evidence that cognitive attributions of benevolence, rather than emotional experiences, help explain why promotion and prevention contracts affect trust differently. It also brings unexpected negative events into contracting research, showing that contract frames differ in their capacity to develop versus preserve trust over time.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should treat contract wording as a strategic governance tool, not only as a legal or economic instrument. Promotion frames may be useful when rapid trust development is important, but managers should recognize that trust developed under promotion contracts can be especially vulnerable to unexpected negative events. Prevention frames may generate less trust during positive exchanges, yet they may better preserve expectations when exchange outcomes deteriorate.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the studies examine the impact of unexpected exchange events on trust but do not give participants opportunities to offer explanations or apologies, which prior work suggests can shape attributions and trust repair. They also note that the studies examine boundary spanners' trust development but do not determine whether trust resides at the interpersonal or organizational level. They also examine trust developed from exchange outcomes, not trust built from deeper knowledge, identification, information sharing, or long-term partner history.

**Future Research**
Future experiments could distinguish interpersonal from organizational trust by varying whether the representative for one organization changes during an ongoing exchange. Researchers could adapt the paradigm to study trust developed through information sharing, firm identity, or decision-maker identity under different contract frames. The authors also call for work on how trust can be repaired after unexpected negative events, including the roles of explanations, apologies, and new contracts.
