# Friends, Gifts, and Cliques: Social Proximity and Recognition in Peer-Based Tournament Rituals

**Abstract**
Two main accounts of the effect of social proximity between candidates competing for recognition and members of the evaluating audience can be extrapolated from extant literature on peer-based tournament rituals and cultural fields. Following a Bourdieusian tradition, one account—which we label “self-reproduction”—points to the catalyzing effect of social proximity in shaping recognition along relational lines. Drawing from recent scholarship on social evaluation, a second account—which we call “intellectual distance”—suggests that social proximity deters recognition. We probe the influence of different articulations of social proximity (i.e., direct ties, cliquishness, and reciprocity) on recognition by studying awarding decisions within the context of the Norwegian advertising industry. Interviews with key informants as well as econometric results suggest that, while self-reproduction tends to prevail over intellectual distance, these effects coexist and their relative influence varies across levels of recognition. We gauge the relative saliency of the two accounts by using a mixed methods approach. Important implications for research on social evaluation and recognition in peer-based tournament rituals are drawn.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how the social structure cutting across the audience-producer interface affects the allocation of recognition among competing candidates in peer-based tournament rituals. It focuses on whether social proximity between jurors and project members catalyzes recognition through self-reproduction, deters recognition through intellectual distance, or produces different effects across weaker and stronger forms of recognition.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The paper states no formal numbered hypotheses and explicitly declines a falsification design: "we do not follow a standard hypothesis-based falsifiability logic whereby explicit predictions that embrace one or the other account are tested." It instead extrapolates two contrasting directional expectations from the literature and asks which one prevails.
- Self-reproduction: "if the self-reproduction effect prevails, we expect that, ceteris paribus, social proximity will catalyze recognition." Under this account social proximity should help candidates enter both the audience's attention space (e.g., an honorable mention) and its consecration space (winning the award).
- Intellectual distance: "If the intellectual distance effect prevails, we expect that, ceteris paribus, social proximity will deter recognition." Under this account social proximity may impede candidates' entry into the evaluating audience's attention and/or consecration space.
The authors frame the two accounts separately "for the sake of clarity, not because we think they are mutually exclusive," and state that their goal "is not to establish whether one or other account is at work, but to ascertain which one prevails and under what conditions."

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Social proximity between project members and jurors, operationalized as direct ties, cliquishness, and reciprocity.
- DV(s): Allocation of accolades in the Silver Tag contest, ordered as no placement, honorable mention, and award won.
- Mediators: Not modeled as formal mediators; the theoretical process contrasts self-reproduction through relationally patterned consecration with intellectual distance through reputational concern and disinterestedness.
- Moderators: Level of recognition functions as the key distinction, separating entry into the audience's attention space from entry into the consecration space.

The mechanism is ambivalent rather than one-directional. Self-reproduction implies that socially proximate candidates are more likely to receive recognition because peer evaluators share tastes, loyalties, and symbolic commitments with them. Intellectual distance implies that proximity can become a liability when jurors worry that favoring close peers will expose them to reputational criticism or accusations of biased judgment. The findings show that direct ties and cliquishness help projects enter the attention space, while cliquishness lowers the likelihood of moving to award victory and reciprocity remains positive across both levels.

**Data & Measures**
- Quantitative data: all projects entered into the Silver Tag digital advertising contest from May 2003 to April 2010, taken from the online Silver Tag archive published by INMA, originally collected by Aadland (2012, 2013) and completed by contacting agencies for missing participant names. The data comprise 1,734 distinct individuals, 350 distinct organizations, and 902 projects over a total of 75 contest months, and include losers as well as winners. Data on all jury members were collected from May 2003 to March 2010 from the Silver Tag website and industry press, covering seven juries ranging in size from four (first jury) to 11 (last jury) members. The reported models (Tables 3, 4, 5, and A2) are estimated on n = 654.
- Qualitative data: 19 distinct interviews lasting between 20 minutes and 2 hours with élite advertising professionals, professionals struggling to make their mark, contest jurors, industry association representatives, and INMA managers (agency descriptives in Table 1), plus archival material and close following of the Norwegian advertising news press by one of the authors. Interview topics covered collaborative practices, the meaning and relevance of awards, evaluation criteria, perceptions of distance or proximity in the social space, personal anecdotal evidence of jury decisions, and deliberation processes. Representative quotes are arrayed in Table 2 under the two perspectives, self-reproduction and intellectual distance.
- DV: allocation of accolades, coded 0 if a project did not receive any accolade, 1 if it received an honorable mention, and 2 if it reached first place (won the award); honorable mentions from fifth to second place were collapsed into the single honorable-mention category.
- IV — direct ties: bipartite project-affiliation network matrices built from the monthly contest in UCINET 6 for Windows, using a 24-month moving window updated monthly; because the unit of analysis is the project, the variable counts the number of jurors with direct ties to project members.
- IV — cliquishness: the n-clan measure (n = 2) computed for each juror–professional dyad over the same 24-month moving window in UCINET 6; for each project the variable is the median of the n-clan values of the professionals working on that project, capturing the extent to which jury and project members partake in the same clan.
- IV — reciprocity: a tally of the number of current jurors who won or received an honorable mention from project members who served as jurors over the previous two years and whose work was under evaluation in the focal contest month.
- Controls: project quality (predicted values of a single-factor latent measure estimated with the SEM command in Stata 14 from project sophistication, joint project, and project size); status (share of project individuals with Bonacich beta centrality above the median in the global Silver Tag network, on a 24-month moving affiliation window); prior positive co-experience (1 if a candidate and a juror collaborated and won together in the prior 24 months); median experience (median count of prior Silver Tag entries across project members); conflict of interest (1 if a project member had a colleague in the jury or a juror was a project member); competitive intensity (number of projects competing in the contest month).
- Estimation: partially constrained generalized ordered logit models (Williams, 2006) estimated with the gologit2 command in Stata 14, imposing parallel-lines constraints on all controls and letting the three social-proximity variables vary across outcome categories, clustering projects on contest month with Huber–White robust standard errors. The Brant (1990) test showed that reciprocity and cliquishness violated the proportional odds assumption; direct ties did not, but was allowed to vary for theoretical reasons.
- Identification: the design is associational. Inverse probability weighting is used in Appendix B to estimate average treatment effects of dichotomized direct ties, reciprocity, and cliquishness on the likelihood of receiving an accolade, but the authors state plainly, "Although we cannot claim causality, the results of these analyses lend additional support to the relationship between our variables of theoretical interest and the allocation of awards in peer-based tournament rituals."

**Key Findings**
- The dominant quantitative results come from the partially constrained generalized ordered logit models in Table 4. Panel 0 contrasts no placement against honorable mention or award won (entry into the attention space); panel 1 contrasts no placement or honorable mention against award won (entry into the consecration space).
- Direct ties: positive and significant for panel 0 throughout (0.513, p < .01 in the bivariate Model 1; 0.247, p < .01 in Model 5; 0.246, p < .05 in the full Model 8). For panel 1 the coefficient was positive and significant before controls (0.406, p < .01 in Model 1) but positive and non-significant once controls were added (0.143 in Model 5; 0.148 in Model 8). Higher direct ties therefore help entry into the jury's attention space but do not increase the likelihood of winning an award.
- Reciprocity: positive and significant in both panels (7.484, p < .01 and 0.359, p < .01 in Model 2; 7.084, p < .01 and 0.190, p < .05 in Model 6; 6.856, p < .01 and 0.203, p < .05 in Model 8).
- Cliquishness: the sign reverses across levels of recognition — positive and significant for panel 0 (0.128, p < .05 in Model 3; 0.280, p < .01 in Model 7; 0.252, p < .01 in Model 8) but negative and significant for panel 1 (-12.079, p < .01 in Model 3; -10.419, p < .01 in Model 7; -12.356, p < .01 in Model 8). The authors read this as cliquishness helping producers gain an honorable mention while preventing further progress toward consecration.
- Conditional marginal effects reproduce the same pattern: direct ties positive and significant for panel 0 (0.045, p < .05) and not significant for panel 1; reciprocity positive and significant in both panels (1.374, p < .01 and 0.006, p < .05); cliquishness positive and significant for panel 0 (0.422, p < .01) and negative and significant for panel 1 (-0.371, p < .01).
- Controls: project quality, status, median experience, and competitive intensity were significant and in the expected direction in Model 4, while conflict of interest and prior positive co-experience were not statistically significant.
- Supplementary analyses: tie strength was positive (0.645, p < .05) in the first panel and non-significant in the second; the interaction of prior positive experience with direct ties was negative and significant in both panels (-0.403, p < .05 and -0.465, p < .05), which the authors interpret as evidence supporting intellectual distance; a nearness measure based on median geodesic distance was positive (5.579, p < .01) in the first panel and non-significant in the second; no quadratic effect of direct ties was found, but the degree-of-separation measure showed an inverted U-shaped effect in the second panel (linear term 36.770, p < .05; quadratic term -62.068, p < .05).
- Robustness: patterns held when clustering on jury (Model 9), when the latent quality measure was replaced by its components, when a November–December contest dummy was added (not significant), when explanatory variables were orthogonalized, and under a multinomial logit. The IPW estimates in Table A2 gave a positive ATE for direct ties ≥ 4 (0.635, p < .05), a positive ATE for reciprocity ≥ 1 (0.866, p < .01), and a negative ATE for cliquishness > 1 (-0.382, p < .01).
- Qualitative convergence: the interviews substantiated both accounts rather than only one. Informants described relationship-driven preferential allocation (status beliefs, shared canons, "camaraderie," an industry that "becomes cliquish"), and at the same time described proximity as a reputational liability ("you are saying something about yourself when you declare who deserves gold. You expose yourself"; the risk of others believing "you have a vested interest").
- Overall: the authors conclude that self-reproduction tends to prevail over intellectual distance, but that the two effects coexist and their relative influence varies across levels of recognition — all three proximity measures predicted entry into the audience's attention space, while only reciprocity supplied the extra boost needed to move from honorable mention to award victory.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on social evaluation by showing that recognition in peer-based tournament rituals is patterned by audience-candidate relationships, not only by producer characteristics, audience characteristics, status, or quality proxies. It extends Bourdieusian accounts of symbolic fields by showing that self-reproduction coexists with intellectual distance, so relational proximity can both catalyze and inhibit recognition depending on the level of recognition. It also contributes to tournament theory by using network constructs to place award contests more explicitly in their social context.

**Practical Implication**
The paper has no dedicated practice section. Its closest applied observations are that recognition is patterned along relational lines and is conducive to economic opportunities, so this preferential allocation pattern places a significant burden on peripheral cultural producers who are loosely connected to the field's insiders. The authors also note that the rotating structure of the jury - audience members today will be competing candidates tomorrow - is conceivably what mitigates preferential allocation along relational lines, and that absent this rotating structure the dominance of self-reproduction over intellectual distance would probably be much more likely.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that peer-based tournament rituals have features that overlap with but differ from promotion processes, funding committees, auditions, and online user communities, so generalization should be careful. The study analyzes project-level award outcomes while the theoretical arguments are mainly at the individual level, creating a level-of-analysis limitation. The data do not directly observe jury deliberation processes, so the paper cannot fully show how conflicting juror opinions are reconciled into collective award decisions.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how relational proximity shapes recognition in other peer-based evaluative settings, including labor-market promotion, funding committees, R&D selection panels, orchestra auditions, and online communities. The authors call for more work on how peripheral actors and novel claims gain traction when social structures favor insiders. They also suggest experimental or more processual studies that can observe micro-level deliberation and better control the quality of the project under evaluation.
