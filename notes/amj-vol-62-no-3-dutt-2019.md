# Regulatory Uncertainty, Corporate Structure, and Strategic Agendas: Evidence from the U.S. Renewable Electricity Industry

**Abstract**
Prior research has demonstrated that although organizations generally avoid uncertainties, this behavior is not universally true. To better understand the contingencies around organizations' responses to regulatory uncertainty, we consider the influence of corporate structure on attentional processing. Our theory and findings suggest that because corporate structure differentiates their problem-solving and learning activities, headquarters and subsidiaries differ in attention allocation on their strategic agendas. Our results show that headquarters display lower levels of uncertainty aversion than do subsidiaries, but all organizations generally make greater shifts in attention toward alternative courses of action if they possess complementary experience and access shared experience. We test these ideas using a sample of U.S. electric utility companies attending to renewable technologies from 2000 to 2010—a market greatly affected by regulatory uncertainty. By linking models of decision making under uncertainty and learning with the attention-based view, we develop a more comprehensive understanding of the ways uncertainty influences organizational attention.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how organizations allocate attention to alternative courses of action under regulatory uncertainty, and why headquarters and subsidiaries within the same corporate hierarchy differ in that response. It further asks whether complementary experience and shared experience help units direct more attention toward renewable electricity technologies on their strategic agendas.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. In the presence of regulatory uncertainty, the reduction in attention to alternative courses of action (i.e., renewable technologies) is greater for subsidiaries than for headquarters.
H2a. Complementary experience (i.e., the presence of both regulatory and operational experience) increases headquarters and subsidiary attention to alternative courses of action (i.e., renewable technologies) in their strategic agendas.
H2b. Complementary experience will have a greater impact on headquarters attention versus subsidiary attention to alternative courses of action (i.e., renewable technologies) in their strategic agendas.
H3a. The greater the shared experience between headquarters and subsidiaries, the greater the increase in attention to alternative courses of action (i.e., renewable technologies) on their strategic agendas.
H3b. Shared experience will have a greater impact on headquarters attention versus subsidiary attention to alternative courses of action (i.e., renewable technologies) in their strategic agendas.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Regulatory uncertainty, measured primarily as years remaining until compliance with a state renewable portfolio standard; corporate structure, distinguishing headquarters from subsidiaries; complementary operating/regulatory experience; shared operating, renewable operating, and regulatory experience.
- DV(s): Renewable agenda, measured as counts of renewable electricity technology words in organizations' 10-K reports.
- Mediators: Not explicitly modeled.
- Moderators: Headquarters versus subsidiary status in the regulatory uncertainty and experience relationships; complementary and shared experience as contingencies that shape attention to renewable alternatives.

The mechanism links regulatory uncertainty to organizational attention through the attention-based view. Because headquarters and subsidiaries have different problem-solving roles, stakeholder exposure, and information access, the same regulatory uncertainty is processed differently across the corporate hierarchy. Subsidiaries show stronger uncertainty aversion, reducing attention to renewable alternatives as uncertainty increases, while headquarters are less likely to withdraw attention. Experience changes this pattern: units with complementary regulatory and operating experience, and units that share relevant experience with connected units, are better able to interpret uncertainty and place renewable technologies on their agendas.

**Data & Measures**
Archival panel data on the U.S. renewable electricity industry from 2000 to 2010. The final unbalanced panel dataset, including all control variables, describes 116 investor-owned utilities (IOUs) covering 38 headquarters and 78 subsidiaries across 32 U.S. states; 36 of the 116 IOUs are in states that do not face RPSs and 80 are in states that do. Missing data occur because 71 IOUs within U.S. electric utilities are privately owned, so no 10-Ks are available, and a further 33 IOUs lack sufficient data for a full panel because they were acquired during the analysis period (56 firms in total were acquired between 2000 and 2010). The hypothesis-testing models in Tables 2, 3, and 4 report 1,005 observations; the controls-only model in Table 2 reports 1,015. All independent variables and controls are lagged one year, except 10-K size, which is lagged to match the year of the dependent variable.

- DV: Renewable agenda, a count of renewable electricity technology words in each organization's 10-K report for each year. Atlas.ti was used to read the 10-Ks and generate word counts of all occurrences of the dictionary terms (hydro, hydroelectric, hydroelectricity, hydel, wind, solar, nuclear, biomass; Table A1), and these words were summed to create the dependent variable. Validation: 20% of the 10-Ks were manually counted and checked for distorted context (96% accuracy), and a Microsoft Word search for an additional 20% of the sample returned the same counts (100% accuracy).
- IV: Regulatory uncertainty, a count of the number of years remaining until an organization must be compliant with its state's RPS. Time to compliance may range from 1 to 20 years, and the variable takes the value 0 for all observations that do not face an RPS, so it ranges from 0 to 20. A second, effects-coded measure used for robustness codes regulatory uncertainty as 1 for organizations in the years following RPS passage, 0 for organizations in the pre-RPS years, and -1 for organizations that never face RPSs (Appendix B).
- IV: Headquarters, equal to 1 if the unit is a headquarters and 0 if it is a subsidiary. The 38 headquarters include 20 solo headquarters without subsidiaries and 18 headquarters supervising an average of 1.67 subsidiaries.
- Moderators (complementary experience): Complementary experience is coded 1 for units possessing both operating experience (production of any type of electricity) and regulatory experience (located in a state that has undergone deregulation), and 0 for units possessing one type or neither. Complementary renewable experience is coded 1 for units possessing both renewable operating experience (production of renewable electricity) and regulatory experience, and 0 otherwise.
- Moderators (shared experience): Shared regulatory experience is a four-level count of deregulation experience across a focal unit and the unit it is directly connected to (0 if neither the headquarters nor its subsidiaries are in a deregulated state, 1 if only the alter unit has experienced deregulation, 2 if only the focal unit has, 3 if both have). Shared operating experience applies the same 0-3 scheme to production of any type of electricity, and shared renewable operating experience applies it to production of renewable electricity; Appendix C clarifies the coding structure.
- Controls: three time-variant state-level measures (a count of Sierra Club members, the average price of electricity in U.S. dollars, and state GDP per capita in U.S. dollars) and six unit-level measures (annual performance measured as net income; size measured as total assets; total U.S. dollars spent on lobbying; 10-K size, the total number of words in the current report; renewable experience, total megawatts of renewable electricity produced; and total nonrenewable experience, total megawatts from sources not identified by RPSs). Year dummies and state dummies are included in all analyses.

Because the outcome is a count measure, the analyses use count models for panel data with year and state dummies. The data exhibit overdispersion (alpha = 1.39, significantly different from 0), which would ordinarily call for negative binomial models, but because the data are a panel and paneled Poisson models in Stata are fully robust, paneled Poisson models with state dummies and robust standard errors are used in every analysis. Following Edwards (1995), the raw counts are used as the dependent variable with the total number of words in each document controlled for, rather than a ratio measure. The design is associational: the paper describes its results as associations and correlations and reports no causal identification strategy. Robustness checks include ordinary least squares and negative binomial specifications, the effects-coded regulatory uncertainty measure, a broader renewable word list, and models adding headquarters' agenda similarity, distance from headquarters, and corporate family dummies.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. In Table 2, regulatory uncertainty is negatively associated with the renewable agenda (Model 2: -0.009, p < .1); interpreting the incidence rate ratios, a one-unit increase in regulatory uncertainty is associated with a statistically significant 1% reduction in attention to renewable electricity technologies. Headquarters, as compared to subsidiaries, pay 43% less attention to renewable electricity technologies (Model 2: -0.536, p < .01). The Uncertainty x Headquarters interaction is not significant (Model 3: 0.022), but the marginal-effects graph (Figure 1) shows headquarters exhibit no significant change in their renewable agendas as uncertainty rises while subsidiaries show a statistically significant decline of 57% in renewable words; the authors read this as support for Hypothesis 1. In real terms the average subsidiary dedicates 106 words to renewables and headquarters dedicate 52, and these level differences persist across high- and low-uncertainty states.
- H2a fully supported across Models 1-4 of Table 3. Complementary experience is positively associated with the renewable agenda (Model 1: 0.135, p < .05), with units possessing both operational and regulatory experience paying 14% more attention to renewable technologies than units possessing only one or neither type. Complementary renewable experience is likewise positive (Model 3: 0.151, p < .05), with those units paying 16% more attention.
- H2b supported only in the case of complementary renewable experience. The Complementary Experience x Headquarters interaction is not significant (Model 2: -0.069), and Figure 2a shows roughly a 9% increase for headquarters against a 16% increase for subsidiaries, which are not statistically different. The Complementary Renewable Exp. x Headquarters interaction is positive and significant (Model 4: 0.382, p < .01), implying headquarters with complementary renewable experience pay 46% more attention to renewables relative to all other units; Figure 2b shows a 57% change for headquarters against 4% for subsidiaries, a significant difference. In the fully saturated Model 5 the complementary experience terms change sign and are described as fragile (Complementary Experience -0.076, ns; interaction 0.375, ns), while the complementary renewable experience interaction remains (0.321, p < .1).
- H3a strongly supported. Each shared-experience measure is positively associated with the renewable agenda: shared operating experience (Table 4 Model 1: 0.095, p < .1), where one additional unit increases attention to renewables by 10%; shared renewable operating experience (Model 3: 0.069, p < .1), a 7% increase for all units possessing it; and shared regulatory experience (Model 5: 0.953, p < .01), a 160% increase, or about 240 words.
- H3b mixed. For shared operating experience the interaction with headquarters is not significant (Model 2: 0.081), and Figure 3a shows significant positive trends for both headquarters (30% increase in word count) and subsidiaries (20% increase) with no statistically significant difference between them, which the paper reads as support for Hypothesis 3a but not Hypothesis 3b. For shared renewable operating experience the interaction is positive and significant (Model 4: 0.404, p < .01), an additional increase for headquarters units of 50% relative to subsidiaries or to headquarters lacking that experience, and Figure 3b shows a 142% change in headquarters attention against 15% for subsidiaries, a statistically significant difference. For shared regulatory experience the interaction is negative and insignificant (Model 6: -0.135), and in those models the significance of the headquarters main effect is replaced by shared regulatory experience, so headquarters are not significantly different from subsidiaries once shared regulatory experience is controlled. In the fully saturated Model 7 the main effects hold (shared operating 0.104, p < .05; shared renewable operating 0.070, p < .1; shared regulatory 0.912, p < .01) while all three headquarters interactions are insignificant. The authors summarize these results as strong support for Hypothesis 3a but mixed support for Hypothesis 3b.

Robustness tests of the headquarters-subsidiary relationship (Table 5) find that headquarters' agenda similarity is not significantly related to subsidiaries' renewable agendas (-0.015; 675 observations), which the authors attribute to the reduced sample size of a subsidiary-only analysis; that distance from headquarters is significantly and negatively correlated with the renewable agenda (-0.000, p < .01; 828 observations), so the further away the subsidiary, the less attention paid to renewables; and that with corporate family dummies added, the main effects of regulatory uncertainty (-0.006, p < .1) and headquarters (-0.692, p < .01) remain significant. Ordinary least squares and negative binomial specifications, the effects-coded regulatory uncertainty measure, and an expanded renewable word list all return largely consistent results.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on organizational responses to uncertainty by showing that responses are not homogeneous within firms: corporate structure creates systematic variation between headquarters and subsidiaries. It extends the attention-based view by identifying regulatory uncertainty as an external condition that shapes strategic agendas through internal attention structures. It also contributes to organizational learning and nonmarket strategy research by showing how complementary and shared experience condition attention to environmental regulation and renewable technologies.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not assume that headquarters and subsidiaries will interpret regulatory uncertainty in the same way. Corporate managers can support better attention to renewable alternatives by improving knowledge flows from subsidiaries, especially when headquarters lack direct operational experience. Policy makers should recognize that legally related headquarters and subsidiary units may respond differently to the same regulation, so reducing uncertainty and communicating regulatory information to subsidiary managers may improve consistency in organizational responses.

**Limitations**
The study examines a relatively homogeneous empirical context: U.S. investor-owned electric utilities subject to renewable portfolio standards. This helps isolate regulatory uncertainty but limits generalizability to less regulated or more heterogeneous settings. The authors also note that the structure of the data cannot fully separate headquarters' influence on subsidiary agendas from top management team preferences, and that the regulatory uncertainty measure may also capture broader environmental change.

**Future Research**
The authors position their findings as applying to regulated contexts such as pharmaceuticals, telecoms, and information and communications technology, which share an oligopoly structure with large, uncertain investments, while noting they are not generalizable to all contexts and that a different setting might reveal additional variation in attentional responses and a greater impact of the headquarters-subsidiary relationship. Further work could distinguish regulatory uncertainty from more general environmental uncertainty and use data that more directly identify headquarters' control over subsidiary agendas and the role of top management preferences. Research could also study how formal corporate structure and cognitive divisions of labor jointly shape attention under different environmental contingencies.
