# Collective Emotions in Institutional Creation Work

**Abstract**
In this paper, we explain how and why collective emotions enable institutional creation work. Based on an ethnography in Limonade, a Haitian community affected by the 2010 earthquake, we identify social practices that elicit collective emotions through the creation of new institutions across the three disaster recovery phases. Our study’s key insight is that new institutions converge collective emotions such that they in turn justify ongoing, as well as motivate engagement in new, institutional creation work practices. Theorizing from our findings, we develop a generative model that describes the justifying and motivating function of collective emotions in the establishment of embedded institutions. In conclusion, our paper advances theory on collective emotions in institutional work and generates implications for post-disaster management practice.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how collective emotions enable the creation of institutions in a disaster-affected local community. It examines how social practices by local agents in Limonade, Haiti, elicited shared emotional responses that sustained and expanded institutional creation work after the 2010 earthquake.

**Mechanism Process**

- Process / phases: Disaster response, disaster recovery, and disaster reconstruction.
- Practices: Improvising, assessing needs, advocating emergent roles, leading, collaborating, valorizing, reflecting, educating, mobilizing engagement, and synergizing.
- Emergent arrangements: Collective awareness, collective trust, collective intentions, temporary material solutions, collective vision, collective alertness, and enduring physical infrastructure.
- Emotional dynamics: Feeling of security, compassionate empathy, affective solidarity, collective hope, collective confidence, and harmonious passion.

The process is that local agents create temporary, symbolic, and material institutional arrangements through situated practices. These arrangements elicit and converge collective emotions among community members. Those emotions first justify the continuation of current practices and then motivate engagement in new practices, allowing temporary post-disaster arrangements to become more embedded institutions.

**Data & Measures**
The data come from a punctuated five-year ethnography of a single geographical site, the town of Limonade in Haiti (pre-earthquake population size 14,886, substantially increased to 20,281 after post-earthquake internal refugee flows), with empirical materials gathered between 2011 and 2016 by one of the authors, referred to throughout as "the ethnographer." Table 1 sets out four data-collection phases — Phase 1 (2010–June 2011), Phase 2 (July–December 2011), Phase 3 (2012), and Phase 4 (2013–2016) — with participant observation of 8 days (April 2011), 10 days (October 2011), 11 days plus 4 days (March–April 2012), and 8 days (January 2016); the text states that the ethnographer spent 41 days in total investigating post-disaster institutional work practices in Limonade, living with Steve and Gabi's families. Table 1 reports 18, 7, 12, and 16 interviews across the four phases, and media secondary data of 710 pages (292 articles), 129 pages (37 articles), 228 pages (74 articles), and 315 pages (106 stories) respectively. Formal semi-structured interviews were conducted with entrepreneurs, employees, and affiliates of Sonje Ayı̈ti, the mayor, and local experts; most interviews with local staff and community members were conducted in Creole with the help of a foreign translator who later provided the transcriptions. In all, 1,897 photographs and 12 hours of film were compiled and edited into a short film. Documentary sources comprise 833 emails and 13 folders of project documents about Sonje Ayı̈ti, SAPENSA, and other recovery initiatives, a total of 12,000 pages of reports by UN-led international development agencies, more than 1,500 media articles during 2011–2016, and the Haiti Relief and Reconstruction Watch blog; the ethnographer also researched the Limonade recovery situation full time over 18 months between January 2011 and July 2012, and gained access to high-level governmental meetings and to project meetings with a group of 40 entrepreneurs, as well as university students in Limonade.

Constructs were coded, not measured. The analysis combined interpreting ethnographic insights with inductive theory-building procedures, going back and forth between data, emerging themes, and existing theoretical concepts. It proceeded in four named steps. First, sketching the observation ground: several rounds of discussion around the ethnographer's photos, videos, interviews, and field notes to build a shared understanding of Limonade's post-disaster recovery. Second, identifying new institutional arrangements, using an "insider-outsider" coding method in which the ethnographer and an author who had not been involved in coding the original field data recoded data into categories, yielding cognitive, symbolic, and physical arrangements as second-order themes (Table 2a). Third, identifying post-disaster social practices, using temporal bracketing of the data into disaster response (phase 1), recovery (phase 2), and reconstruction practices (phase 3), followed by an open and axial coding process in accordance with those phases; with the help of NVivo10 this iterative process resulted in 10 post-disaster social practices key to creating new institutional arrangements (Table 2b). Fourth, tracing shared affective responses: through axial coding (Table 2c) and constant comparison with the literature on emotions, the authors revealed six distinct collective emotions in the data — confidence, feeling of security, compassionate empathy, affective solidarity, hope, and harmonious passion — with each construct sourced to a cited emotions literature. Because the mechanisms of collective emotions among community members could not always be directly observed, the authors employed a narrative strategy, constructing detailed stories or "realist tales" from the data to support analytical inferences. The design is interpretive and inductive; the paper claims no causal identification strategy and reports no statistical tests.

**Key Findings**
The study's key insight is that new institutions converge collective emotions such that they in turn justify ongoing, as well as motivate engagement in new, institutional creation work practices. In each of the three post-disaster phases the authors identify a bundle of social practices that generates new institutional arrangements; the new arrangements in turn reinforce and sustain those social practices but also enable the emergence of new institution-building practices. Going back and forth between the data and theory, the authors report that feeling of security, affective solidarity, and collective confidence primarily justify, and thereby legitimize, current social practices, whereas compassionate empathy, collective hope, and harmonious passion motivate community members' engagement in new institutional creation practices.

By phase (Figure 9): in the disaster response phase, improvising, assessing needs, leading, and advocating on emergent roles are practices that build new institutional arrangements by providing temporary material solutions and building awareness among community members; Gabi and Steve developed a shared feeling of security that in turn justified continuing with those cognitive and material response practices, while shared compassionate empathy motivated villagers to engage with new practices. In the recovery phase, collaborating, valorizing, and reflecting enable the creation of a new collective vision for, and community members' trust in, the future development of their community; collective trust and a shared vision unleashed symbolic power to elicit affective solidarity that approved the very same practices, and a collective vision for a collaborative food production chain led to collective hope among local subsistence farmers, which motivated the mobilization of community engagement through newly founded farmer associations. In the reconstruction phase, educating, mobilizing engagement, and synergizing generate greater collective alertness and intentions to act, and thereby foster the establishment of permanent physical infrastructure embedded in the community; the villagers' shared confidence approved sustaining practices that develop collective intentions and collective alertness, and justified the collective intentions and alertness that led to the emergence of a passion for self-sufficiency among villagers.

In sum, the authors find that key actors initiate and drive the creation of disaster-specific, temporary institutional arrangements, and that as institution building progresses the disaster becomes less of a reference point while key institutional workers increasingly emphasize new embedded routines, realities, and physical infrastructure in the community. They witnessed this shift during the second and third phases, when additional agents such as Ernise, Patrick, and Vilna stepped up and joined with Gabi and Steve in shaping daily routines. They conclude the institution creation process when the efforts of a smaller group of actors to create temporary and symbolic arrangements (for example, Gabi, Steve, and the mayor) becomes a larger group effort among community actors to more strongly embed new permanent institutions in the community. The findings are idiographic and interpretive: no hypotheses are tested and no effect sizes are reported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional work theory by specifying how collective emotions operate inside the relationship between social practices and institutional arrangements. It argues that emotions are not only individual experiences of institutional actors but also collective affective responses that become constitutive of institutional creation work.

The paper also contributes to theory on collective emotions by showing two functions in institutional creation: a justifying function that legitimizes ongoing practices and a motivating function that generates new joint action. This model extends institutional work research from fields and discursive spaces to physically proximate local communities where shared emotions are more visible.

**Practical Implication**
Post-disaster management should give stronger attention to locally embedded actors who can mobilize trust, hope, solidarity, and confidence over time. The study suggests that external aid organizations may meet emergency needs but often lack the proximity and long-term ties needed to build emotionally grounded local institutions.

For policy makers and international actors, the implication is to resource local organizations and partnerships beyond the immediate response phase. Locally trusted organizations can help communities move from temporary relief toward durable education, health, food-production, and infrastructure arrangements.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the ethnography is idiographic and trades theoretical generality for contextual particularity. Its strongest boundary condition is physical proximity: the model is most directly applicable where actors are co-present and jointly attend to shared local objects of emotion.

The study also focuses primarily on positive collective emotions and therefore underemphasizes negative or traumatogenic collective emotions. It does not fully examine strategic emotion work by institutional workers or how actors may intentionally create specific emotions to influence others.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how negative collective emotions such as despair interact with hope, solidarity, confidence, and passion in institutional work. Scholars could also investigate how community agents purposefully create or manage emotions, including differences in emotional competence, emotional investments, and agentic orientation.

The authors also call for research that links people-centered, strategic, and structural-institutional perspectives on emotions. Such work could clarify how particular institutions shape particular collective emotions and how collective emotions, in turn, shape institutions beyond disaster-affected settings.
