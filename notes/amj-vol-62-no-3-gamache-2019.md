# Responding to Bad Press: How CEO Temporal Focus Influences the Sensitivity to Negative Media Coverage of Acquisitions

**Abstract**
Management scholars have demonstrated that CEOs look to cues provided from external stakeholders when determining the direction and timing of strategic action. Research has focused on “hard” forms of external performance feedback, primarily in the form of stock market reactions. To understand both whether and to what degree “soft” external performance feedback of strategic actions influences the subsequent strategic decisions of firms, we build our arguments from and contribute to upper echelons theory. We argue that negative media reactions to the announcement of a major acquisition will shape the degree to which the firm will engage in subsequent acquisition activity. However, our theory suggests that an important individual attribute, CEO temporal focus, will influence how sensitive CEOs are to media coverage. Using a sample of 747 large acquisitions made between 2006 and 2011, we find strong support for our hypotheses. Additionally, our supplemental analysis demonstrates an important difference between the influence of stock market and media reactions following an acquisition. While CEO temporal focus shapes which CEOs will be influenced by media reactions, it appears that CEOs’ propensity to be influenced by stock market reactions is not moderated by their temporal focus.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether soft external performance feedback from the media influences subsequent strategic action after a major acquisition announcement. It also asks whether CEO temporal focus shapes how strongly negative media reactions affect later acquisition spending.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Negative media reactions to the announcement of an acquisition will be negatively related to subsequent acquisition spending.
H2. The negative relationship between negative media reactions to acquisition announcements and subsequent acquisition spending will be moderated by CEO past focus such that the relationship will be stronger for CEOs with high past focus compared to CEOs low in past focus.
H3. The negative relationship between negative media reactions to acquisition announcements and subsequent acquisition spending will be moderated by CEO future focus such that the relationship will be weaker for CEOs with high future focus compared to CEOs low in future focus.

No hypothesis is developed for CEO present focus. The authors control for it and note that present focus is likely to have a mixed effect on CEOs' attention and response to media evaluations; it is examined only in supplemental analysis.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Negative media reactions to the focal acquisition announcement.
- DV(s): Subsequent acquisition spending during the 365 days after the focal acquisition.
- Mediators: No measured mediator; the theorized process is CEO attention to, interpretation of, and aversive learning from negative media feedback.
- Moderators: CEO past focus and CEO future focus; CEO present focus is examined in supplemental analysis and is not significant.

The mechanism is that negative media coverage acts as soft external performance feedback that can make CEOs more cautious about repeating acquisition activity. CEOs high in past focus are expected to attend more to negative prior feedback and carry it into later decisions, strengthening the negative media reaction-subsequent acquisition spending relationship. CEOs high in future focus are expected to emphasize future opportunities and long-term possibilities, weakening the influence of negative media reactions on later acquisition spending.

**Data & Measures**
The sampling frame is the firms listed on the S&P 500 as of January 1, 2006, and the study covers all large acquisitions (greater than $100 million) announced by these firms from 2006 until the end of 2011. CEO-level data come from ExecuComp; firm- and industry-level controls from Compustat and the Compustat Segments database; acquisition data from the SDC Mergers and Acquisitions database; stock market reactions from the Eventus database provided by the Center for Research in Securities Pricing; and media data from Factiva. CEO temporal focus was measured from annual reports collected primarily from Mergent and company corporate websites, with additional checks in the Buckmaster database, ABI/Inform, and Google searches. Data collection identified 958 acquisition announcements with media coverage; 34 were removed because multiple acquisitions were announced by the same firm on the same day, one because the announcement occurred on the last day of the CEO's tenure, 89 because no annual reports were found, and 87 for other missing data, leaving 747 acquisitions for the final analyses. Acquisitions removed from the sample were still included in the calculation of the dependent variables. The dependent variable, subsequent acquisition spending, is the total value of all large acquisitions announced during the 365 days following the focal acquisition, log-transformed because of skewness. The independent variable, negative media reaction, is the percentage of negative words captured in the media coverage of the acquisition, derived from LIWC content analysis of articles in Forbes, the Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg BusinessWeek, Barron's, Associated Press Newswires, Dow Jones Newswires, and Gannett News Service over a 21-day window running from three days before to 17 days after the announcement, after manual removal of articles not directly about the focal company and focal acquisition, duplicates, and exact reprints of company press releases. The moderators, CEO past focus and CEO future focus, come from LIWC content analysis of the letter to the shareholders from the year prior to the acquisition announcement, using the 145-word past-focus and 48-word future-focus dictionaries; each measure is the number of dictionary words captured divided by the total number of words in the letter, and the two are treated as independent constructs rather than as the relative strength of past versus future focus. Controls are CEO present focus (169-word LIWC dictionary), CEO acquisition experience (prior acquisition spending by the CEO at the focal firm over the four years, 1,460 days, before the focal acquisition), CEO total compensation (ExecuComp TDC1), stock market reaction (cumulative abnormal returns over a seven-day event window from three days before to three days after the announcement, with a 250-day estimation window from 295 to 45 trading days before the announcement), positive media reaction (percentage of positive words), media count, relative size of target, foreign target, public target, hostile takeover, multiple bidders, firm size (natural log of sales), firm performance (return on assets), leverage (debt to equity ratio), high reputation, capital expenditures, R&D expenditures, firm investment horizon, diversification (entropy measure), industry dynamism, industry munificence, industry acquisition activity, and year dummy variables. All variables were standardized before the interaction terms were created, and all predictor variables except controls related directly to the focal acquisition were lagged to the year before the year of the acquisition announcement. Estimation is Tobit regression, chosen because acquisition spending is a continuous nonnegative number with a minimum value of zero, with standard errors clustered by firm. The design is associational archival rather than causally identified; the authors address the omitted-variable threat by calculating the impact threshold for a confounding variable, which indicates that an omitted variable would need to be correlated r > 0.17 with both negative media reaction and subsequent acquisition spending to invalidate the findings.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 is supported: the coefficient for negative media reaction is negative and significant (p < .05), reported in Table 2 as -0.235 (SE = 0.101) in Model 2 and -0.238 (SE = 0.100) in the full Model 5 (n = 747). In practical terms, a one standard deviation increase in negative media coverage surrounding an acquisition is associated with a 21.2% decrease in subsequent acquisition spending, which at the mean value of subsequent acquisition spending represents a $449 million reduction in large acquisition spending in the year following the focal acquisition.
- Hypothesis 2 is supported: the CEO past focus x negative media reaction interaction is negative and significant (p < .01), reported as -0.256 (SE = 0.097) in Model 3 and -0.270 (SE = 0.092) in Model 5. Simple slopes analysis shows the relationship between negative media coverage and subsequent acquisition spending is negative at mean and high levels of CEO past focus (p < .05) but not significant at low levels of CEO past focus (p > .05). For CEOs with a high past focus (+1 SD), an acquisition with high negative media reaction (+1 SD) results in $2.3 billion less subsequent large acquisition spending than an acquisition with low negative media reaction (-1 SD).
- Hypothesis 3 is supported: the CEO future focus x negative media reaction interaction is positive and significant (p < .05), reported as 0.234 (SE = 0.109) in Model 4 and 0.252 (SE = 0.113) in Model 5. Simple slopes analysis shows the relationship is negative and significant at mean and low levels of CEO future focus (p < .05) but not significant at high levels of CEO future focus (p > .05). Among CEOs with a low future focus (-1 SD), an acquisition with high negative media reaction (+1 SD) results in $2.1 billion less subsequent large acquisition spending than an acquisition with low negative media reaction (-1 SD).
- Among the controls, stock market reaction is positive and significant (p < .01), while media count is not significant, indicating that the simple volume of media coverage does not influence subsequent acquisition spending. Descriptively, mean positive media reaction (2.562) is much higher than mean negative media reaction (0.458), and CEO past focus and CEO future focus are uncorrelated (r = .001).
- Supplemental analyses. With the number of large acquisitions as the dependent variable, Hypotheses 2 and 3 were both supported (p < .01), but the main effect of negative media reaction (Hypothesis 1) was negative and not quite significant (p = .125). When CEO past focus and CEO future focus were interacted with stock market reactions, the main effect of stock market reactions remained positive and significant (p < .01), and although both interaction terms were in the expected direction (CEO past focus x market reaction positive, CEO future focus x market reaction negative), neither was significant. Interactions of negative media reaction and positive media reaction with stock market reaction were positive but not statistically significant. The CEO present focus x negative media reaction interaction was not significant, and the interactions of CEO past focus and CEO future focus with positive media reactions were not significant in either case. Adding general negative and general positive media coverage controls, after dropping 127 confounded cases, produced results completely consistent with the main analysis.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends upper echelons theory by showing that CEOs respond not only to hard, quantifiable external feedback such as stock market reactions, but also to softer stakeholder evaluations in media coverage. It specifies CEO temporal focus as a cognitive attribute that shapes which executives attend to and act on negative media feedback.

It also contributes to acquisition research by showing that subsequent acquisition behavior is influenced by subjective media evaluations of prior acquisitions, not only by economic indicators. The supplemental analyses further distinguish media reactions from stock market reactions because temporal focus moderates the former but not the latter.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that CEOs' responses to media evaluations can shape subsequent strategic action, not only reactions to stock-market feedback. Negative media coverage is associated with lower subsequent acquisition activity, especially when the CEO is strongly past focused, whereas CEOs high in future focus appear less influenced by negative media reactions.

**Limitations**
The authors acknowledge that an omitted variable could influence both negative media reaction and subsequent acquisition spending, although their confounding-threshold analysis suggests this would need to be a relatively strong omitted correlate. The study also does not show whether CEOs' responsiveness to negative media coverage benefits the firm or moves it away from better strategic actions.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine other forms of soft performance feedback and whether they fit within an aspirational framework of CEO decision making. The authors also call for more research on CEO temporal focus as a moderator of stakeholder feedback, including feedback from institutional investors and market analysts.

Future studies could extend the acquisition logic beyond subsequent deal activity to post-acquisition integration, such as whether negative media reactions lead CEOs to invest more effort in proving the acquisition successful or to pursue faster cost reductions. They also call for work on whether attending to media coverage improves firms' future actions or distracts CEOs from the best strategic course.
