# It’s Not Just What is Said, but When it’s Said: A Temporal Account of Verbal Behaviors and Emergent Leadership in Self-Managed Teams

**Abstract**
“Emergent leadership”—the ascription of informal leadership responsibilities among team members—is a dynamic phenomenon that comes into place through social interactions. Yet, theory remains sparse about the importance of verbal behaviors for emergent leadership in self-managed teams over a team’s lifecycle. Adopting a functional perspective on leadership, we develop a temporal account that links changes in task-, change-, and relations-oriented communication to emergent leadership in early, middle, and late team phases. We test the hypothesized relationships in 42 teams that provided round-robin emergent leadership ratings and videotapes of their first, midterm, and final meetings. Team members’ verbal behaviors were captured using finegrained empirical interaction coding. Multilevel modeling showed that task-oriented communication was a stable positive predictor of emergent leadership at all time points. Change-oriented communication predicted emergent leadership at the start of a project and diminished in relevance at the midterm and final meetings. Relations-oriented communication gained importance, such that an increase in relations-oriented behaviors toward the project end predicted emergent leadership. We discuss theoretical implications for conceptualizing the behavioral antecedents of emergent leadership from a timeand context-sensitive perspective.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when changes in task-, change-, and relations-oriented verbal behaviors predict emergent leadership at different points in a self-managed team’s lifecycle. It focuses on whether the behavioral antecedents of informal leadership vary across the first, midterm, and final project meetings.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The importance of task-oriented verbal behaviors for predicting emergent leadership decreases over the course of a team’s lifecycle, such that task-oriented verbal behaviors are positively associated with emergent leadership at the start and midterm meetings, but not at the final team meeting.
- H2: The importance of change-oriented verbal behaviors for predicting emergent leadership decreases over the course of a team’s lifecycle, such that change-oriented verbal behaviors are positively associated with emergent leadership at the first but not at the midterm and final team meetings.
- H3: The importance of relations-oriented verbal behaviors for predicting emergent leadership increases over the course of a team’s lifecycle, such that relations-oriented verbal behaviors are positively associated with emergent leadership at the final but not at the first and midterm team meetings.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team members' task-oriented, change-oriented, and relations-oriented verbal behaviors, coded from videotaped meetings.
- DV(s): Emergent leadership ratings assigned by fellow team members.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Meeting time point across the team lifecycle, operationalized as first, midterm, and final meetings.

The mechanism is temporal fit between a member's communication and the team's changing functional needs. Task-oriented statements help teams accomplish work and remain positively associated with emergent leadership throughout the lifecycle. Change-oriented communication signals direction and initiative early, when the team is establishing its project approach, but loses relevance later. Relations-oriented communication becomes more important near project completion, when social cohesion and support help teams manage final task pressure.

**Data & Measures**
- Data: 42 self-managed project teams of three to five members (M = 3.26, SD = 0.54) videotaped at three meetings — week 1 (first), week 5 (midterm), and week 7 (last meeting before the final project presentation) — of an eight-week consulting project for a large automobile manufacturer, producing 126 videotaped team meetings. One hundred and thirty-six students from an international university in Germany signed up for the project work; the study's sample was 53.7% male, average age 20.27 years (SD = 1.23), with an international background of 30 nationalities. Junior consultants were randomly assigned to teams and were not previously acquainted, so the teams had neither a pre-established hierarchy nor a formal leader. Three teams declined to take part in the data collection; 42 teams gave informed consent.
- IVs: task-, change-, and relations-oriented verbal behaviors, operationalized by a quantitative interaction coding procedure applied to the videotapes with an a priori behavioral coding scheme (Table 1), run on the aggregated behaviors at the meta-categories' level. Four extensively trained research assistants coded every sense unit — the smallest speech segment expressing a complete thought — using the Interact software, with mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive codes; each research assistant double-coded eight complete videos with an expert coder and reached a Cohen's kappa of at least .70. Because meeting lengths varied, each person's category frequency was standardized per hour (codes per category divided by minutes present, multiplied by 60). The procedure produced 39,966 coded behaviors for the project-start meetings, 56,504 for the midterm meetings, and 43,622 for the final meetings.
- DV: emergent leadership, measured with a round-robin rating design in which all team members rated one another on four items adapted from Lanaj and Hollenbeck (2015) and McClean et al. (2018), answered on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = completely disagree, 6 = completely agree). Cronbach's alpha was .87, .91, and .92 at the first, midterm, and final meetings; ICC(1) was 0.66, 0.67, and 0.52 and ICC(2) was 0.80, 0.80, and 0.70 for the same three time points.
- Moderator: meeting time point (first, midterm, final), with meeting time point 1 as the reference point.
- Estimation: multilevel modeling in SPSS (mixed command, restricted maximum-likelihood with listwise exclusion of missing data) on a three-level structure of repeated measures (level 1) nested within individuals (level 2) nested within teams (level 3), using the repeated command with an unstructured variance–covariance matrix at level 2 and a random intercept at the team level. Table 2 reports N = 125 at meeting time point 1, N = 123 at meeting time point 2, and N = 127 at meeting time point 3. Neither age nor gender correlated significantly with emergent leadership and, for parsimonious reasons, they were not carried into the analyses. The design is associational — the paper names no causal identification strategy and frames the verbal behaviors as predictors of leadership ascriptions. As a robustness check, an alternative growth curve model with linear and quadratic effects for time (Appendix A) obtained essentially the same result pattern.

**Key Findings**
- H1 was not supported; the paper states that the findings contradict it. The interaction between meeting time point and task-oriented verbal behaviors was not significant, F(2, 190.44) = 0.88, p = .42, so the post hoc tests used a time-invariant estimator. Task-oriented behaviors were a positive predictor of emergent leadership, Est. = 0.08 (SE = 0.03), t(253.84) = 2.64, p < .05, and were a stable positive predictor at all meeting time points rather than losing relevance at the final meeting as H1 had posited.
- H2 was supported. The interaction between meeting time point and change-oriented behaviors was significant, F(2, 195.57) = 3.99, p < .05. Change-oriented verbal behaviors significantly predicted emergent leadership at the beginning of the project, Est. = 0.92 (SE = 0.27), t(134.75) = 3.40, p < .01, but not at the midterm meeting, Est. = 0.26 (SE = 0.31), t(122.22) = 0.84, or the final meeting, Est. = 0.00 (SE = 0.28), t(124.39) = 0.01. The interaction estimates relative to meeting time point 1 were negative; the difference was significant for meeting time point 3, t(215.06) = -2.50, p < .05, but not for meeting time point 2, t(186.14) = -1.72, p = .09.
- H3 was supported. The interaction between meeting time point and relations-oriented behaviors was significant, F(2, 181.04) = 4.68, p < .05. Relations-oriented behaviors significantly predicted emergent leadership at the final meeting, Est. = 0.45 (SE = 0.09), t(127.13) = 4.76, p < .01, but not at the first, Est. = 0.06 (SE = 0.09), t(133.99) = 0.73, or the midterm meeting, Est. = 0.07 (SE = 0.06), t(133.20) = 1.15. The changed relationship ran between meeting time point 1 and meeting time point 3, t(213.89) = 3.11, p < .01, whereas the change between meeting time points 1 and 2 was not significant, t(197.45) = 0.10, p = .92.
- In the overall test of fixed effects (Table 3), task-oriented behaviors (F = 5.81, p = .02), change-oriented behaviors (F = 4.72, p = .03), and relations-oriented behaviors (F = 12.98, p = .00) were significant, while meeting time point alone was not (F = 0.92, p = .40). The adjusted R² (η²) reported in Table 5 was .2845, .2354, and .2244 at meeting time points 1, 2, and 3.
- The authors summarize that the results of the fine-grained interaction analysis mostly provided support for the hypothesized relationships, with task-oriented communication the one category whose relevance for emergent leadership did not change over the teams' lifecycles.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends emergent leadership research by shifting behavioral antecedents from a static account to a time-sensitive account of social influence in self-managed teams. It links functional leadership theory, discursive leadership, team development, and interaction coding to show that leadership ascription depends not only on what members say but also on when they say it. The findings refine prior debates by showing that task-oriented behavior remains consistently relevant, change-oriented behavior matters early, and relations-oriented behavior matters most at the end.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations and leadership-development programs should train members of self-managed teams to read the temporal context of team interaction and adapt their verbal behavior flexibly. The authors caution against teaching employees to use one behavior mechanically at one point in time; instead, they emphasize situational awareness, behavioral flexibility, feedback from videotaped meetings, coaching, and reflective practice.

**Limitations**
The conceptual model assumes a particular lifecycle pattern in which teams align goals early, focus on task execution, and concentrate on goal attainment near the final meeting; other group-development perspectives could imply different timing. The study used consulting project teams embedded in a university setting, so the findings may not generalize to other organizational contexts where relational communication is more central from the start. The a priori coding scheme also captures each statement's primary function but cannot fully represent the simultaneous relational meanings of communication.

**Future Research**
Future research could experimentally vary team activities, goal clarity, social conflict, or time pressure across lifecycle phases to test whether different temporal demands change which behaviors predict emergent leadership. Scholars could examine project teams in other settings, such as caregiving work, where relations-oriented communication may matter earlier. Future work could also use finer-grained within-meeting analyses or relational-control coding to capture both content and interpersonal meaning in ongoing communication.
