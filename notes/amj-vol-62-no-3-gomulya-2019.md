# Crossed Wires: Endorsement Signals and the Effects of IPO Firm Delistings on Venture Capitalists’ Reputations

**Abstract**
Signaling theorists have paid a great deal of attention to the costs of acquiring characteristics that can serve as signals, such as endorsements from reputable third parties. However, limited attention has been devoted to the penalty costs associated with providing inaccurate signals and the factors that can exacerbate or attenuate the penalties. We examine the effect of negative feedback loops on venture capital (VC) firms’ reputations that result from the failures (delistings) of the newly public firms they once endorsed. Drawing on signaling and attribution theories, we argue that endorsements by reputable VC firms create high expectations that, when violated, cause stakeholders to look for scapegoats, resulting in reputational damage to the endorsing VCs. We find empirical support for this argument, and for the attenuating effect of both post-IPO market performance and survival. Our study contributes to the conversation about endorsements as signals, and empirically tests the implicit assumption that endorsements place the reputation of the endorser at risk.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether endorsers suffer reputational penalties when the firms they endorsed later fail to meet expectations. Specifically, it examines whether negative delistings by newly public VC-backed firms damage lead VCs' subsequent reputations, and whether IPO underpricing, post-IPO performance, and post-IPO survival duration change the size of that penalty.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Delistings by newly public, VC-backed firms will be negatively related to the lead VC's subsequent reputation.
- H2: The negative relationship between delisting and the lead VC's subsequent reputation will be stronger the higher the portfolio firm's underpricing.
- H3: The negative relationship between delisting and the lead VC's subsequent reputation will be weaker the higher the portfolio firm's performance in the years following its IPO.
- H4: The negative relationship between delisting and the lead VC's subsequent reputation will be weaker the longer the portfolio firm survives following its IPO.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Negative delisting of a newly public VC-backed portfolio firm within five years of IPO.
- DV(s): Lead VC reputation at t+1, measured with a modified LPJ VC Reputation Index.
- Mediators: Stakeholder disappointment, negative expectancy violation, and scapegoating are theorized attribution processes but are not directly measured as mediators.
- Moderators: IPO underpricing, post-IPO industry-adjusted ROA, post-IPO market-to-book ratio, and post-IPO survival years.

The mechanism combines signaling and attribution logics. Reputable lead VCs endorse startups by financing and taking them public, which raises stakeholder expectations about the startups' quality and prospects. When those firms later delist for negative reasons, stakeholders search for a blameworthy actor and attribute part of the failure back to the salient lead VC, damaging its reputation. The penalty is attenuated when post-IPO market performance is stronger and when the firm survives longer after IPO, because these conditions cognitively decouple the portfolio firm from its VC; IPO underpricing does not significantly strengthen the penalty.

**Data & Measures**
The data combine Jay Ritter's IPO dataset (offering dates, offering prices, filing price ranges, closing prices, SIC codes, underwriter prestige rankings), VC investment data from Securities Data Corporation's VentureXpert covering investments annually from 1990 to 2010, market performance data from CRSP, firm financial data from COMPUSTAT, and firms' annual 10(k) and 8(k) filings where COMPUSTAT data were missing. After requiring VC backing, delisting for negative reasons (acquisitions excluded), and available VC reputation data, missing COMPUSTAT and CRSP data reduced the sample to 151 unique lead VCs; the panel runs from the IPO year through the fifth post-IPO year (up to six yearly observations per firm), giving 1,587 VC-firm-year observations, and 31 of the 370 firms in the final sample (8.4%) were delisted. DV: the lead VC's reputation index value at t+1, measured with a modified LPJ VC Reputation Index (Lee, Pollock, & Jin, 2011) formed from five standardized and summed formative indicators over the prior five years — amount of funds under management, number of funds under management, number of companies invested in, investment amount, and number of IPOs — converted to a 100-point scale, with VC firm age excluded from the original index. IV: Delisting, a time-varying dichotomous measure coded 1 in the year a VC is affected by a portfolio firm's delisting within five years of its IPO (CRSP delisting codes 500-587, i.e., negative reasons) and 0 otherwise. Moderators: IPO underpricing, computed from CRSP as first-day closing price minus opening price divided by opening price, multiplied by 100, then log-transformed; post-IPO performance operationalized two ways, industry-adjusted ROA (adjusted for capital expenditures and Winsorized at 5% in each tail) and the market-to-book ratio (market value over total assets, logged), each entered as a rolling three-year average; and surviving years, the current year minus the IPO year. Time-varying controls include the VC's lagged reputation at t, VC status (Bonacich's beta centrality from five-year co-investment networks), structural holes (1 minus network constraint), investment preference for early stage, VC ownership, focal-firm company age and revenue, portfolio-firm averages of company age, revenue, ROA, MTB and VC ownership excluding the focal firm, cumulative prior delistings of other portfolio firms over a rolling five-year window, and year dummies. Estimation uses the Arellano-Bond dynamic panel GMM estimator (xtabond2 with the collapse option, Stata 14) with robust standard errors clustered at the VC level; VC and firm fixed effects are absorbed through orthogonal deviation and potentially endogenous covariates are instrumented with their own lags. The design is observational panel data: the authors invoke the AB estimator to address dynamic panel bias from the lagged dependent variable, possible reverse causality, and group-wise heteroskedasticity, not an experiment or exogenous shock.

**Key Findings**
H1 is supported: delisting has a significant and negative relationship with the lead VC's reputation at t+1 in all models, with p ranging from 0.006 to 0.035; evaluated at mean covariate levels in the full model, a portfolio firm delisting decreases VC reputation by 9.75, from 30.77 to 21.01, which the authors describe as roughly a 50% drop for the thirtieth-ranked VC in 1995, about 30% in 2000, and about 13% for the highest-reputation VCs. H2 is not supported: underpricing has no significant moderating effect in Models 3 or 7 (its main effect is dropped by the AB models because it is time-invariant, so the test runs through the time-varying interaction term). H3 is supported only in part: the delisting-by-ROA interaction is not significant in either Model 4 or Model 7, while the delisting-by-MTB interaction is positive and significant in Models 5 and 7 (4.25, p = 0.039; 4.03, p = 0.067) — the paper states the hypothesis is supported when using market performance but not when using accounting performance. H4 is supported: post-IPO surviving years positively moderates the relationship in Models 6 and 7 (1.26, p = 0.025; 1.04, p = 0.047). Plotting the interactions, moving portfolio-firm MTB from one standard deviation below to one standard deviation above the mean shrinks the delisting penalty from about 35% to about 22%, attenuating roughly 38% of the damage; extending post-IPO survival from one year to five years shrinks it from 40.3% to 25.5%, attenuating roughly 37%. In robustness tests, the delisting relationship is not significant in the non-lead VC subsample, and a Wald test rejects equality of the lead and non-lead delisting coefficients at p < 0.01, indicating delistings are more damaging for lead VCs. Decomposing the reputation index, delisting hurt the number of startups invested in and the total dollar amount invested in startups (p = 0.098 and p = 0.009) and MTB and survival duration partially attenuated that effect, but the moderating hypotheses found no support when the dependent variables were number of funds under management, total funds under management, or number of firms taken public, although the delisting main effect held (p = 0.099, 0.071, and 0.066, respectively). Thirty-one jackknife replications, each excluding one delisting event, produced coefficients the same as or close to the original AB estimates and supported H1 at p < 0.05 in all replications, H3 at p < 0.10 in 27 of 31, and H4 at p < 0.05 in 29 of 31, with H2 remaining unsupported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling theory by empirically testing the often-assumed penalty cost of inaccurate endorsement signals. It shows that negative feedback can travel from a failed signal recipient back to the signal sender, even when the sender has limited ongoing control over the recipient.

It also contributes to research on organizational reputation by showing that reputation can be damaged by distant events involving affiliated firms, not only by a focal firm's own misconduct or proximal crises. The robustness tests further refine endorsement theory by showing that lead VCs, rather than non-lead syndicate members, bear the reputational penalty.

**Practical Implication**
For venture capital firms and other professional service firms, the findings imply that endorsements create long-tail reputational exposure. Firms that raise client or portfolio-company expectations can be penalized later if those expectations are violated, even when the subsequent failure is only partly under their control.

The study also suggests that endorsers should manage expectations and attend to portfolio firms' post-endorsement viability, not only the short-term gains from successful IPO exits. Stronger post-IPO market performance and longer survival help reduce, but do not eliminate, reputational damage from later failure.

**Limitations**
The study is limited to delistings of VC-backed firms in the United States, so institutional, regulatory, and VC-market differences may affect generalizability to other countries. The reputation measure is objective and formative rather than a direct longitudinal measure of stakeholder perceptions, making the test conservative but leaving the perceptual process indirectly observed.

The design also cannot fully determine whether particular delistings were caused by inadequate VC due diligence, premature VC exit pressure, or external forces beyond both the VC's and portfolio firm's control. The authors note that such distinctions could matter for whether stakeholder punishment is reasonable.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether and how VCs repair reputational damage after portfolio-firm delistings, including which repair mechanisms work and how long recovery takes. Studies could also test whether current successes offset past failures in stakeholders' evaluations.

The authors also call for research on the nature of negative events behind delistings. Future work could distinguish failures attributable to VC actions from failures driven by external shocks, and test whether stakeholders respond differently across these causal conditions and national contexts.
