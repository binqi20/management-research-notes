# The Voice Bystander Effect: How Information Redundancy Inhibits Employee Voice

**Abstract**
Employees often remain silent rather than speak up to managers with work-related ideas, concerns, and opinions. As a result, managers can remain in the dark about issues that are otherwise well known to, or universally understood by, frontline employees. We propose a previously unexplored explanation for this phenomenon: Voice is prone to "bystander effects," such that, the more certain information is shared among employees, the less any particular employee feels individually responsible for bringing up that information with managers. We theorize that such bystander effects are especially likely to occur when peers of focal employees, on average, enjoy high-quality relationships with managers and thereby have adequate relational access to voice up the hierarchy. Using a correlational study involving managers and employees working in teams in a Fortune 500 company, as well as two experimental studies (a laboratory study involving undergraduate students working in a hierarchical setting, and a scenario study with a sample of U.S.-based workers), we provide evidence for our conceptual model. We also discuss the theoretical and managerial implications of our findings.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why employees may remain silent about work-related ideas, concerns, and opinions that are widely known among peers. Specifically, it examines whether information redundancy reduces employee voice by diffusing responsibility, and whether this effect is stronger when peers have high-quality relationships with the manager.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Information redundancy is negatively related to voice.
- Hypothesis 2. Average peer-LMX moderates the relationship between information redundancy and voice such that the relationship is more negative when average peer-LMX is higher.
- Hypothesis 3. The negative relationship between information redundancy and voice is mediated by diffusion of responsibility.
- Hypothesis 4. The interactive relationship between information redundancy and average peer-LMX on voice is mediated by diffusion of responsibility.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Information redundancy, operationalized in Study 1 through workflow structural equivalence and manipulated directly in Studies 2 and 3.
- DV(s): Employee voice to managers or higher-level decision makers.
- Mediators: Diffusion of responsibility.
- Moderators: Average peer-LMX, meaning the average quality of peers' relationships with the manager.

The mechanism is a voice bystander effect. When employees believe peers possess the same work-related information, they feel less personally responsible for raising that information to managers. This diffusion of responsibility lowers voice, especially when those peers appear capable of speaking up because they have strong relational access to the manager. Across the three studies, the authors distinguish this account from psychological safety, efficacy, and instrumentality explanations.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 used multi-source survey data from the India branch of a Fortune 500 electronics company: 144 employees from 25 teams were invited, and the final sample comprised 132 employees (92% response rate) and 25 managers (100% response rate), with team size ranging from 3 to 8 and all participants male. Voice was manager-rated on a 4-item scale (Tangirala & Ramanujam, 2012, derived from Van Dyne & LePine, 1998; 1 = "never" to 7 = "always"). Information redundancy was operationalized as the average degree of structural equivalence in an employee's job with respect to peers on the team, computed in UCINET from round-robin workflow-interaction ratings using a Euclidean-distance algorithm and reverse-scored so that lower distance represents greater redundancy. Average peer-LMX was each employee's average of the self-reported LMX scores (7 items from Scandura & Graen, 1984) of his teammates, excluding the employee himself. Regressions used cluster-robust standard errors with a maximum-likelihood estimator in Mplus; main analyses were run without controls, and robustness checks added age, tenure, education, self-LMX, psychological safety, efficacy, and instrumentality, an alternative redundancy operationalization (network constraint), and workflow centrality. The paper describes this as a correlational study and attributes the addressing of causality questions to the Study 2 experiment.

Study 2 was a laboratory experiment with 163 undergraduate business students at a large Mid-Atlantic university in the United States (mean age 21.36; 81 males, 82 females) participating for course credit. Information redundancy was manipulated between subjects by telling participants that their fellow Student Affairs Committee members were either fully aware (n = 84) or not at all aware (n = 79) of a university shuttles issue. Voice was a binary behavioral choice (0 = "no", 1 = "yes") of whether to personally raise the issue with the taskforce director; diffusion of responsibility used 5 purpose-developed items (alpha = .80); psychological safety (3 items, alpha = .75), efficacy (3 items, alpha = .88), and instrumentality (3 items, alpha = .83) were measured as alternative explanations. Mediation used indirect-effects analysis for a binary dependent variable with a continuous intervening variable, plus bootstrapped indirect effects (2,000 replications).

Study 3 was a scenario experiment with 440 U.S.-based workers recruited through the market research firm ClearVoice (average age 45.16; average work experience 23.15 years; 43% female), randomly assigned to a 2 (information redundancy: low vs. high) x 2 (average peer-LMX: low vs. high) between-subjects design in which they adopted the role of a game-testing technician at a mobile game development company. Both manipulations were delivered as scenario text and passed manipulation checks. Voice was measured with 5 items (alpha = .90), diffusion of responsibility with the same 5-item scale adapted to the scenario (alpha = .77), and psychological safety, efficacy, and instrumentality again served as alternative explanations. Analyses used regression plus bootstrap-based indirect effects (2,000 replications, bias-corrected 95% CIs). The Table 4 correlations are reported for n = 440, while the note to the Table 5 regressions reports n = 286.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 supported Hypotheses 1 and 2; the mediator was not measured, so Hypotheses 3 and 4 were not tested there. Information redundancy had a negative influence on voice (b = -0.13, SE = 0.06, p < .05), and information redundancy interacted significantly with average peer-LMX (b = -0.14, SE = 0.06, p < .05): there was no effect of information redundancy on voice when average peer-LMX was low (b = -0.02, SE = 0.08, ns) and a significant negative effect when average peer-LMX was high (b = -0.22, SE = 0.04, p < .01). The pattern held with controls and with network constraint as an alternative redundancy operationalization, and, as predicted, workflow centrality was positively associated with voice (b = 0.03, SE = 0.01, p < .05).

Study 2 supported Hypotheses 1 and 3; average peer-LMX was not manipulated in this study. Information redundancy had a negative influence on voice (b = -.92, SE = .34, p < .01), with the odds ratio of voice in the high versus low redundancy condition at .40. Information redundancy was positively associated with diffusion of responsibility (b = 0.91, SE = 0.12, p < .01), diffusion of responsibility was associated with reduced willingness to voice (b = -2.20, SE = 0.37, p < .01), and information redundancy was no longer associated with voice in the presence of the mediator (b = 0.80, SE = 0.49, ns); the bootstrapped indirect effect was significant (b = -0.83, 95% CI [-1.13, -0.59]), with diffusion of responsibility explaining 75% of the estimated total effect.

Study 3 supported all four hypotheses. Information redundancy was negatively related to voice (b = -0.37, SE = 0.08, p < .01), supporting Hypothesis 1. The information redundancy by average peer-LMX interaction on voice was significant (b = -0.34, SE = 0.16, p < .05), with no significant voice difference between low and high redundancy under low peer-LMX (contrast = 0.19, ns) but lower voice under high redundancy when peer-LMX was high (contrast = 0.53, p < .01), supporting Hypothesis 2. Information redundancy was positively related to diffusion of responsibility (b = 0.77, SE = 0.07, p < .01), which was negatively related to voice controlling for redundancy (b = -0.76, SE = 0.04, p < .01), and the indirect effect was significant (b = -0.58, SE = 0.06, bias-corrected 95% CI [-0.71, -0.46]), supporting Hypothesis 3. Information redundancy and peer-LMX also interacted to predict diffusion of responsibility (b = 0.32, SE = 0.13, p < .05), and the negative indirect effect of redundancy on voice was stronger when peer-LMX was high (coeff. = -0.70, SE = 0.09, 95% CI [-0.87, -0.53]) than when it was low (coeff. = -0.46, SE = .08, 95% CI [-0.61, -0.32]), a significant difference (coeff. = 0.24, SE = 0.11, 95% CI [0.04, 0.45]), supporting Hypothesis 4.

Taken across the set, the negative main effect of information redundancy on voice appeared in all three studies; the field (Study 1) and vignette (Study 3) studies established the moderating role of average peer-LMX, and the laboratory (Study 2) and vignette (Study 3) studies established diffusion of responsibility as the explanatory mechanism. The effects held after accounting for efficacy, psychological safety, and instrumentality, and in Study 1 neither self-LMX, relative LMX, nor variation in peers' LMX interacted with information redundancy to predict voice (p > .10).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends employee voice research by showing that shared information can inhibit, rather than enable, speaking up. It challenges the assumption that common knowledge creates safety or confidence in numbers, arguing instead that information redundancy can create diffusion of responsibility.

It also extends bystander-effect theory by moving the phenomenon into workgroups with ongoing relationships. The paper shows that the relational capacity of other observers, captured through average peer-LMX, shapes whether a focal employee passes responsibility for action to peers.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not assume that widely shared awareness of a problem makes voice more likely. Practices that spread information broadly and build strong manager-employee ties can still leave each employee waiting for someone else to speak up.

The authors suggest that managers reduce this risk by emphasizing each employee's unique contribution and making individual voice feel important rather than redundant. This is especially relevant for issues that many employees know about but no one escalates.

**Limitations**
The authors note that Study 1 did not measure diffusion of responsibility directly, even though Studies 2 and 3 supported that mechanism experimentally. Study 1 also used a relatively small field sample of 132 employees nested in 25 teams and lacked gender diversity because all participants were male.

The paper further limits its tested moderator to average peer-LMX. The authors identify other possible moderators, including personality traits such as conscientiousness, peers' voice-relevant competencies, and whether the issue involves challenging voice or a clear and unambiguous threat to the team.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the mediation process in field settings and examine the model in larger and more gender-diverse team samples. Such work would clarify whether diffusion of responsibility operates similarly in naturally occurring organizational contexts.

The authors also call for research on additional boundary conditions of the voice bystander phenomenon. Future studies could examine personality, peer competence, and issue type to determine when shared information decreases voice and when employees remain personally responsible for speaking up.
