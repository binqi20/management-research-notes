# The Social Practice of Coevolving Strategy and Structure to Realize Mandated Radical Change

**Abstract**
This paper draws on a longitudinal interpretive case study to develop a theoretical model of how actions by people across an organization coevolve strategy and structure in order to realize a mandated radical change. Mandated change, imposed by a powerful external actor, extends understanding of the dynamics of radical change. While other studies have examined how unintended consequences shape the way radical change is realized, under mandated change, actors focus on bringing about the change in ways that will be, collectively, considered to realize the intended mandate. Our study, grounded in a practice approach concerning how actions bring about radical change, identifies three different action cycles: performing action cycles, reinforcing action cycles, and reflecting action cycles. Shifts in cycles are triggered by unintended consequences that escalate into breakdowns. Cumulative cycles, arising from the escalation of unintended consequences that lead to breakdowns, are necessary for bringing about a mandated change in strategy and structure. Following breakdown, actors switch to reflecting actions in which they consider the underlying intent of the mandate and how to modify the espoused strategy and structure to bring about that intent.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how people's actions bring about a mandated radical change of strategy and structure. It focuses on how senior, middle, and frontline actors at Telco enacted, reinforced, reflected on, and modified an espoused strategy and structure under a legally binding regulatory mandate.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Mandated radical change, espoused strategy and structure, realized radical change, performing action cycles, reinforcing action cycles, reflecting action cycles, unintended consequences, breakdowns, and the "spirit" of the mandate.
- Process / cycles: Actors perform the initial espoused strategy and structure; unintended consequences emerge; actors reinforce existing actions because the mandate is legally binding; unintended consequences escalate into breakdowns; actors shift into reflecting action cycles; modified strategy and structure are enacted through new performing cycles.
- Strategic and structural elements: Independence, industry supplier, equivalence, and products, each carrying intertwined strategic objectives and structural arrangements.
- Managerial levels: Frontline employees, middle managers, and top managers all participate in action cycles, with problems escalating upward and revised understandings cascading back through the organization.
- Boundary conditions: The process is theorized for a detailed, externally imposed, legally binding mandate where failure carries financial penalties and possible breakup.

The central process is cumulative rather than linear. Under mandated change, actors initially treat unintended consequences as signs that they should work harder to enact the espoused strategy and structure, not as permission to reinterpret the change. Only when these reinforced actions produce breakdowns do actors step back from everyday performance, reflect on the mandate's intent, and modify the espoused strategy and structure in ways that still comply with the mandate. Strategy and structure therefore coevolve through situated action across organizational levels.

**Data & Measures**
Longitudinal, real-time interpretive case study of a single organization, Telco, a communications provider enacting a legally binding change mandated by its regulator; qualitative data were collected over 28 months at the Corporate Centre and across all Telco divisions. Table 1 reports the corpus. Meetings (n = 254) audio-recorded meeting observations with associated field notes: group implementation board 72 (weekly), AccessCo implementation board 34 (fortnightly), Wholesale implementation board 14 (monthly), Retail implementation board 26 (fortnightly), International retail implementation board 59 (two to three times per month), and 49 additional meetings across divisions including product meetings and briefings. Interviews (n = 130): 53 senior managers and 77 middle managers. Field observations (n = 16): 9 telephony engineers and 7 service center employees, reported in the text as 16 days of work shadowing. Documents (n = 1,597), described in the text as more than 1,500 documents: 589 company reports, 54 internal communications, 489 organizational meeting notes and presentations, 91 press releases, 274 other internal documents, 68 industry reports, and 32 newspaper articles. Documents were used to triangulate findings from other sources and ensure the timeline was accurate. The first author, who had prior research engagement with Telco during the policy formation leading to the Mandate, underwent internal training to be awarded "special status," corresponding to a structural group allowed to span divisions.

Analysis was iterative, moving between data and theory and applying process analysis tools to progressively emerge theory from data. The analytic constructs are defined and illustrated in Table 2: Mandate, espoused strategy, espoused structure, performing action cycle, unintended consequence, reinforcing action cycle, breakdown, reflecting action cycle, and realized radical change. Five analytic steps are reported. First, a rich chronological case story of the mandated radical change was written, starting from the group-level implementation board meetings and then expanded to the full data set. Second, repeated and serious breakdowns were identified and defined in the data as an inability to continue with the radical change process — people literally could not deliver on a critical goal or meet a deadline — with a skeletal chronology built around each breakdown from the meeting data and then fleshed out with additional observation, interview, and documentary data. Third, looking across the 28-month observation period, four ways in which the espoused strategy and structure (ESS) shifted were identified and tabulated in Table 3. Fourth, within-case dynamics were examined to explain how breakdowns arose each time efforts to meet the ESS generated unintended consequences that were escalated upward and then reinforced. Fifth, the link from breakdowns to reflecting action cycles and to modification of the ESS was traced. The design is interpretive and processual; the paper reports no statistical estimation and makes no causal-identification claim.

**Key Findings**
The study surfaces three action cycles — performing, reinforcing, and reflecting — through which people across Telco coevolved strategy and structure to realize the Mandate. Performing action cycles enacted the initial ESS: top managers articulated independence to internal and external stakeholders, divisional managers produced separation through distinct reporting lines and separated information systems and work processes, and AccessCo engineers wore AccessCo-branded uniforms, drove AccessCo vans, and announced at end users' doors that they were "from AccessCo." Enacting the ESS as espoused generated unintended consequences: end users unfamiliar with AccessCo were reluctant to let engineers into their homes, delaying or preventing service supply. Because the Mandate was legally binding and the ESS was perceived as consistent with it, actors did not reinterpret the espoused change; they signaled problems by escalating them upward, and the original performing actions were deemed in line with the Mandate and reinforced at multiple levels all the way to top managers — checking with peers, consulting training material, further rounds of engineer training, reiterating communications, and a personalized chief-executive message sent to every person in the organization. Reinforcing action cycles thus kept actors performing the same actions with increased effort and exacerbated rather than relieved the unintended consequences; engineers struggled to supply service for some ten months.

Escalation produced breakdown. Load accrued in the service schedule until engineers could not catch up despite new hires; AccessCo became known as the "supplier of no choice" and was depicted as a failure in the national media; divisional managers were forced to forgo or fail to implement elements needed for the "early release" deadline for Product B and to jeopardize the "mandatory use" deadline for Product A; and corporate managers had to negotiate a new mandatory-use deadline for Product A accompanied by a hefty fine. Breakdown functioned as an enabling trigger rather than only as a cost: it showed managers that even performing the ESS exactly as specified was not meeting the intent of the Mandate, and it prompted reflecting action cycles in which people stopped trying to perform the ESS and instead ran "deep dives" with engineers, project teams, and divisional managers and consulted the regulator and industry about the "spirit" of the Mandate. From that reflection corporate managers specified a modified ESS in which AccessCo was acknowledged as part of Telco — separate but connected — and confirmed with the regulator and industry members that this was faithful to the Mandate's intent. A new performing cycle then enacted the modification: colocation of the Telco and AccessCo brands on vans, uniforms, customer-facing spaces, and full-page advertisements, permitted structural linkages such as colocation of engineering and technical workers, and scripted introductions in which engineers said they were "from AccessCo, a part of Telco Group, here on behalf of [Retailer]" — after which engineers could get into houses to deliver services.

The same reflective shifts recur across the four entwined elements of the Mandate, emerging cumulatively from multiple action cycles rather than as discrete or phased episodes. "Independence" shifted from strict separation to simultaneous separation and collaboration, keeping functional separation between AccessCo and the RetailDivs while working together where necessary to support the Mandate's strategic goals. "Industry supplier" shifted from interacting with external industry retailers while ignoring internal ones to interacting with all retailers, internal and external, in the same way. "Equivalence" shifted from equal — standardizing information, products, and services so that all retailers had access to the same products — to fair, offering a common suite of differentiated products with matching price differentiation, transparently priced by quality and service level. "Products" shifted from making separate and equivalent products and services merely available to making high-quality, fit-for-market products and services available. The paper's directional claim is that, under a mandate, the unintended consequences of action do not necessarily lead to deviations from, reinterpretations of, or failures of the change initiative; rather they can support realizing the change against its higher-level objectives as intended, and cumulative cycles arising from the escalation of unintended consequences into breakdowns are necessary for bringing about the mandated change. At the 28-month strategic review, conducted by the regulator on behalf of industry and government, Telco was confirmed to have met the Mandate and fulfilled its legal obligations, despite modifications of the ESS throughout the change process. These are findings from a single, theoretically salient case, developed as process theory rather than as tests of hypotheses.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends process theories of radical change by showing that breakdowns can be necessary triggers for reflective action under mandated change. It contributes to practice-based strategy and organization theory by explaining how performing, reinforcing, and reflecting action cycles produce the coevolution of strategy and structure. It also expands accounts of top managers in change processes by showing them not only as designers of change but also as active participants in working out how the mandate should be realized.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that managers implementing mandated change should treat breakdowns as diagnostic moments for examining whether current actions realize the intent of the mandate, not only as execution failures. It also suggests that top managers need to stay actively engaged with middle managers, frontline employees, and regulators, because the meaning of compliance may need to be worked out through practice. For organizations under regulatory mandates, faithful implementation may require modifying the espoused strategy and structure while preserving the mandate's underlying intent.

**Limitations**
The authors present Telco as an extreme case of externally imposed, detailed, legally binding mandated change, so the process may differ when mandates are weaker, less specific, internally imposed, or easier to abandon. The study is a qualitative single-case process analysis, designed for theory development rather than statistical generalization. The paper also notes that the case involved top managers who had helped negotiate the mandate, which may shape the reflecting cycles observed.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether less detailed or less binding mandates generate the same reinforcing action cycles and breakdowns, or whether actors adapt more flexibly before breakdown occurs. Studies could also compare externally imposed mandates with internally imposed mandates from powerful corporate actors or parent companies. The authors further suggest examining how different degrees of constraint, legal consequence, and top-manager responsibility shape managerial engagement with radical change.
