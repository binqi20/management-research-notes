# Stuck in the Past? The Influence of a Leader’s Past Cultural Experience on Group Culture and Positive and Negative Group Deviance

**Abstract**
Extant research on the antecedents of cultures has posited that cultures result from the internal and external changes experienced by the group (the functionality perspective) and from a group leader’s personal values and personality traits (the leader–trait perspective). The current study proposes another important but neglected antecedent of cultures: a leader’s past cultural experience. Specifically, we theorize that group leaders enact cultures based on their past cultural experiences, essentially transferring cultures from their former groups to their current groups. Two studies, one in the field (Study 1) and another in the laboratory (Study 2), reveal that the levels of cultural tightness in current groups are predicted by group leaders’ past experience with cultural tightness in their former groups in which they were followers. This relationship becomes stronger when the group leaders identified with or had longer tenure in their former groups. In addition, the cultural tightness that leaders transferred from their former groups to their current groups influences negative (counterproductive work behavior) and positive (promotive and prohibitive voice) forms of group deviance. The theoretical and managerial implications of our findings for leadership and culture are discussed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks where group cultures come from and whether leaders transfer cultural tightness from groups in which they previously participated as followers to groups they later lead. It also asks when this transfer is stronger and whether transferred cultural tightness reduces both negative group deviance and constructive forms of voice.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. A group leader transfers past cultural experience to his or her group such that cultural tightness experienced in the past is positively related to cultural tightness in the leader’s group.
Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between the cultural tightness of a leader’s former group and current group is stronger when the leader has higher identification with the former group.
Hypothesis 3. The positive relationship between the cultural tightness of a leader’s former group and current group is stronger when the leader had longer tenure in the former group.
Hypothesis 4. The cultural tightness of the leader’s current group mediates the negative relationships between the cultural tightness of the leader’s former group and counterproductive work behavior, promotive voice, and prohibitive voice in the current group.
Hypothesis 5a. A group leader’s identification with a former group moderates the indirect relationships between the cultural tightness of the leader’s former group, the cultural tightness of the leader’s current group, and both positive and negative group deviances such that the indirect relationships become stronger when the identification is high.
Hypothesis 5b. A group leader’s tenure in a former group moderates the indirect relationships between the cultural tightness of the leader’s former group, the cultural tightness of the leader’s current group, and both positive and negative group deviances such that the indirect relationships become stronger when the tenure is long.
Study 1 tests Hypotheses 1 through 5. Study 2 re-tests Hypothesis 1, pitting the leader’s past cultural experience against followers’ past cultural experience and against information about the effectiveness of countercultures.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader’s past experience with cultural tightness in a former group.
- DV(s): Current group cultural tightness; counterproductive work behavior; promotive voice; prohibitive voice; and, in Study 2, number of rules set by groups.
- Mediators: Current group cultural tightness mediates the relationship between leader former-group cultural tightness and group deviance outcomes.
- Moderators: Leader identification with the former group and leader tenure in the former group strengthen cultural transfer and the indirect relationships through current group tightness.

The proposed mechanism is cultural transfer under bounded rationality and career-history imprinting. Leaders responsible for group functioning do not simply optimize culture from current contingencies; they rely on familiar cultural solutions learned in prior groups, especially when those prior cultures are internalized through identification or tenure. Transferred tightness then creates strong norms and conformity pressure, reducing counterproductive work behavior but also suppressing promotive and prohibitive voice.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (field survey): three-wave, multisource data on 404 employees in 91 sales groups at a start-up manufacturing company in South Korea, with hypothesis tests run at the group level (n = 91). There were 108 sales groups at the time of the study and 99 leaders agreed to participate; seven were excluded (four had been group leaders in their former companies, three were internally promoted), leaving 92 leaders in Wave 1, and one leader left the company before Wave 2, leaving 91 groups. The overall response rate across the three waves was 71.63% (404 of 564 employees).
- Cultural tightness of the leader’s former group (Wave 1, leader-rated) and of the current group (Wave 2, member-rated): the 6-item Gelfand et al. (2011) scale, originally developed for nation-level culture and modified to represent group-level culture (α = .92 and α = .95 respectively). Member ratings were aggregated to the group level, justified by ICC(1) = .47, ICC(2) = .78, and an average rWG of .88 (SD = 0.10, range .60 to 1.00), with responses from an average of 3.44 members per group (SD = 0.77).
- Leader identification with the former group (Wave 1, leader-rated): Priesemuth, Schminke, Ambrose, and Folger’s (2014) group identification scale (α = .93). Leader tenure in the former group: a single-item measure of length in months (M = 35.14, SD = 11.29).
- Counterproductive work behavior (Wave 3): six division heads rated the groups they oversaw over the previous eight months on a modified 12-item Bennett and Robinson (2000) organizational deviance scale (α = .98). Promotive and prohibitive voice: objective archival HR records of the number of task-related suggestions and the number of grievances each group submitted over the previous two months.
- All survey items used a 7-point Likert scale, translated following Brislin (1990). Controls were leader age and gender, the Big Five personality traits measured with the mini-IPIP, and group size.
- Analysis: multiple hierarchical regressions with the former-group tightness predictor and the two moderators mean-centered, plus Hayes’ (2013) PROCESS macro with 10,000 bias-corrected bootstrap samples for the mediation and moderated-mediation tests. The design is an associational multi-wave, multisource field survey; the paper positions Study 2, not Study 1, as the study that establishes causality.

Study 2 (laboratory experiment): 527 undergraduate students at a large North American university in 176 three-person groups (567 students took part in Wave 1; 40 of them did not attend Wave 2). Participants were randomly assigned to one condition of a 2 (a leader’s past experience with cultural tightness: tight vs. loose) × 2 (members’ past experience with cultural tightness: tight vs. loose) × 2 (the perceived effectiveness of countercultures: no information vs. information supporting the effectiveness of countercultures) factorial design.
- Wave 1 manipulated past experience with cultural tightness across three rounds of a group ideation task using three HR issues from Shalley’s (1991) in-basket exercise: in the tight condition a confederate discussion leader enforced 11 rules and deducted a lottery ticket for each observed violation; in the loose condition the leader acted as a facilitator with no rules or norms and full discretion over ticket distribution.
- Wave 2, approximately 10 days later, assigned participants who had experienced a tight (loose) culture in Wave 1 to the leader or the follower role, producing four group compositions; the experimenter verbally delivered the counterculture-effectiveness information when handing out task instructions.
- Wave 2 dependent variables: group cultural tightness, rated by the two followers on the 6-item Gelfand et al. (2011) scale adapted to the group context (α = .85) and aggregated to the group level (ICC(1) = .84, ICC(2) = .91, average rWG = .77); and the number of rules set by the group, recorded by asking leaders which of the 11 presented rules the group collectively enacted, with the additional rules set by six teams included in the measure.
- Analysis: ANOVA for the manipulation checks, then multiple hierarchical regressions on the 176 groups entering the three conditions in step 1, the two-way interactions in step 2, and the three-way interaction in step 3. Random assignment of past cultural experience and of the leader role is the identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 91 groups) supported all five hypotheses.
- Hypothesis 1 supported: the cultural tightness of a leader’s former group was positively related to the cultural tightness of the group the leader currently leads (b = .33, SE = .09, β = .36, t = 3.52, p = .001).
- Hypothesis 2 supported: the interaction between former-group cultural tightness and leader identification with the former group was significant (b = .19, SE = .08, β = .22, t = 2.31, p = .023); the simple slope was non-significant when identification was low (.15, SE = .13, p = .250) and significantly positive when identification was high (.52, SE = .11, p < .001).
- Hypothesis 3 supported: the interaction between former-group cultural tightness and leader tenure in the former group was significant (b = .03, SE = .01, β = .35, t = 3.62, p = .001); the simple slope was non-significant when tenure was low (.06, SE = .11, p = .587) and significantly positive when tenure was high (.61, SE = .12, p < .001).
- Hypothesis 4 supported: former-group cultural tightness had significant negative relationships with counterproductive work behavior (b = −.27, SE = .09, β = −.29, t = −2.93, p = .004), promotive voice (b = −1.35, SE = .56, β = −.26, t = −2.40, p = .019), and prohibitive voice (b = −.94, SE = .34, β = −.29, t = −2.75, p = .007), and current-group cultural tightness significantly mediated all three relationships (indirect effects −.12, 95% CI [−0.26, −0.03]; −.58, 95% CI [−1.29, −0.11]; and −.48, 95% CI [−1.02, −0.14], respectively).
- Hypothesis 5 supported: the indirect relationships were significant when leader identification with the former group was high (counterproductive work behavior −.21, 95% CI [−0.40, −0.06]; promotive voice −1.01, 95% CI [−2.00, −0.20]; prohibitive voice −.80, 95% CI [−1.57, −0.26]) and non-significant when identification was low, and significant when leader tenure in the former group was high (counterproductive work behavior −.24, 95% CI [−0.43, −0.09]; promotive voice −1.17, 95% CI [−2.18, −0.24]; prohibitive voice −.93, 95% CI [−1.66, −0.34]) and non-significant when tenure was low.

Study 2 (n = 176 groups) re-tested Hypothesis 1 and found further support for it; Hypotheses 2 through 5 were tested only in Study 1.
- A leader’s past experience with cultural tightness had significant positive effects on the cultural tightness scale (b = 1.49, SE = .16, β = .57, t = 9.06, p < .001) and on the number of rules set by groups (b = 3.49, SE = .48, β = .49, t = 7.33, p < .001).
- Followers’ past experience with cultural tightness was not significant for the cultural tightness scale (b = .01, SE = .16, β = .00, t = 0.08, p = .937) or the number of rules set by groups (b = .12, SE = .48, β = .02, t = 0.26, p = .794).
- The perceived effectiveness of countercultures was not significant for the cultural tightness scale (b = .07, SE = .16, β = .03, t = 0.43, p = .666) or the number of rules set by groups (b = −.02, SE = .48, β = .00, t = −0.03, p = .972), and none of the two-way or three-way interactions was significant.
- Manipulation checks were successful: Wave 1 participants in the tight culture condition perceived their group’s culture as tighter than those in the loose culture condition (M = 5.07, SD = 1.07 vs. M = 3.60, SD = 1.39; F(1, 565) = 199.37, p < .001, η² = .26), and 485 participants (92%) answered the counterculture manipulation check correctly.

The authors report that their studies did not find support for the leader–trait or the functionality perspective: Study 1 found no significant relationships between the Big Five personality traits and cultural tightness, and in Study 2 the counterculture-effectiveness manipulation did not influence group cultures.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends culture-formation theory by introducing leaders’ past cultural experience as an antecedent distinct from both functionality and leader-trait explanations. It argues that leaders shape cultures through remembered cultural solutions, not only through current environmental adaptation or stable personal values and traits.

It also contributes to cultural tightness-looseness research by moving the construct to the group level in organizational settings and linking it to both antecedents and consequences. The evidence frames cultural tightness as a double-edged sword: it can reduce harmful deviance while also discouraging constructive challenge and voice.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations hiring or assigning group leaders should consider the cultures those leaders may import from prior groups, especially when leaders strongly identified with or spent longer time in those groups. Explicit cultural expectations and incentives may be needed when the desired culture differs from the leader’s prior cultural template.

The findings also imply that tight and loose cultures should be matched to task demands. Tight cultures may fit work requiring protocol compliance and stability, whereas looser cultures may be better for work that depends on constructive deviance, dissent, and new ideas.

**Limitations**
The authors focus on cultural tightness rather than cultural values such as individualism, collectivism, or power distance, so it remains unclear whether the same transfer logic applies to other cultural dimensions. Study 1 and Study 2 also examine newly formed groups, leaving open how cultural transfer operates in ongoing groups where past and present cultural experience are harder to separate.

The laboratory study uses students and brief group interaction, so it cannot capture the full complexity of workplace culture formation over time. The counterculture manipulation in Study 2 also gives participants indirect information about effectiveness rather than repeated direct experience with cultural success or failure.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether cultural values interact with cultural tightness and whether voice norms can make tight cultures increase, rather than suppress, employee voice. The authors also call for work on cultural transfer in ongoing groups, particularly the role of early-career cultural experience and career imprinting.

Additional studies should examine culture formation in self-managing groups, where no formal leader holds disproportionate authority. Future work could also test whether groups adjust transferred cultures after repeated direct feedback that an imported cultural solution is ineffective.
