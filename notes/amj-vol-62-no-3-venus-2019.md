# Visions of Change as Visions of Continuity

**Abstract**
Despite wide consensus that leader vision is a key vehicle for leaders to motivate followers to support organizational change, it remains far from clear what characterizes an effective vision of change. Research on organizational change that has used a social identity perspective has asserted that one important reason why followers resist change is because change may pose a threat to their subjective sense of continuity of organizational identity. Accordingly, we hypothesize that leaders that communicate visions of change can address this resistance by assuring followers that the essence of the organizational identity will remain unchanged—making their vision of change also a vision of continuity. In line with our proposition that collective continuity is valued because it serves an uncertainty-reduction function, such visions should be more predictive of vision effectiveness the higher follower work-related uncertainty. Across a field study and an experimental study we find support for these predictions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what characterizes an effective leader vision of organizational change. It examines whether visions that assure followers of organizational identity continuity generate greater support for change, especially when followers experience high work-related uncertainty.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Communicating a vision of change that emphasizes collective continuity results in more follower support for change, which is stronger in the context of higher follower uncertainty.
- Hypothesis 2. Perceived collective continuity mediates the effect of a vision of change that emphasizes collective continuity on follower support for change, moderated by follower work uncertainty, such that the indirect effect of communicating a vision of change that emphasizes collective continuity on support for change, mediated by perceived collective continuity, is stronger with higher follower work uncertainty.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader communication of a vision of change that emphasizes collective continuity.
- DV(s): Follower support for change, measured through supervisor ratings in Study 1 and behavioral support in Study 2.
- Mediators: Perceived collective continuity.
- Moderators: Follower work-related uncertainty or environmental uncertainty.

The mechanism is that change can threaten employees' subjective continuity of organizational identity. A vision of continuity frames the future as preserving the organization's defining features, increasing perceived collective continuity; this matters most under uncertainty because social identity provides an uncertainty-reduction function.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (field survey). Students working on a research project contacted organizations that had recently informed their members about a plan to implement organizational change, and survey packages containing coded questionnaires for both employees and their supervisors were distributed among leader-follower dyads. In total 209 completed leader-follower matched surveys were received, representing 74 leaders; data were collected in two waves, the second taking place a year after the first in order to increase the sample size. Employees were 33.22 years old on average and had been employed at their respective employer for 4.9 years on average, and the most common planned changes were relocations and business expansions (39.2%) and reorganizations (21.1%). All constructs except the control variables were measured on 5-point agreement scales, with employees rating the independent variable, moderator, and mediator, and supervisors rating the dependent variable. Vision of continuity (IV) used three items the authors developed (e.g., "My direct supervisor often communicates in his or her vision that our team in the future will be a continuation of what we stand for"), α = .87. Employee work uncertainty (moderator) used the 4-item measure developed by Colquitt et al. (2012), α = .87. Perceived collective continuity (mediator) used the 12-item perceived collective continuity scale developed and validated by Sani et al. (2007), modified to fit an organizational context, α = .89. Support for change (DV) used six supervisor-rated items: three items representing behavioral support for change with the organization as target (Herold, Fedor, & Caldwell, 2008) plus three items the authors developed to capture participation, facilitation, and contribution, α = .94. Perceived job impact (the 4-item scale of Caldwell, Herold, and Fedor, 2004) and wave of data collection were retained as controls, while age and tenure with employer were considered but not included. Because employees were nested within leaders, estimation used multilevel models in R with the intercept treated as a random component: hierarchical regression with a mean-centered vision × work uncertainty product term to test Hypothesis 1, and Monte Carlo resampling confidence intervals (200,000,000 simulated draws) for the indirect effect. The authors state that Study 1 does not allow causal inferences to be drawn.
Study 2 (laboratory experiment). Study 2 was a controlled laboratory experiment in which student participants were informed about planned changes in the bachelor education program of their university, with vision of continuity and uncertainty as manipulated variables and participants randomly assigned to one of four conditions. A total of 208 international business students from a Dutch business school completed the experiment; analyses were based on 199 participants after three students were judged not to have participated in a serious way and six indicated they had failed to understand the English content of the experimental material. Participants ranged in age between 17 and 27 (M = 19.40, SD = 1.44). The scenario framed the school as suffering from budget deficits, requiring it to provide education with fewer teachers, eliminate certain courses, and shift to another teaching method. Vision of continuity was operationalized as a vision statement ostensibly written by the dean of the business school, with continuity references embedded in the final three paragraphs of the vision-of-continuity condition and absent from a control condition equalized in length. Uncertainty was manipulated by varying the communicated uncertainty regarding the degree of impact of the changes for the school. Both manipulations were pretested in separate pilot studies of employed participants recruited via an eLancing marketplace (40 participants for the uncertainty manipulation, 39 for the vision manipulation), and both pilot ANOVAs indicated the manipulations were successful. Perception of collective continuity (mediator) again used the perceived collective continuity scale of Sani et al. (2007), with items modified to fit the current context, α = .83. Behavioral support for change (DV) used two objective behavioral indicators of helping the faculty council write a draft letter requesting other students' support for the change: time spent in seconds writing the letter (M = 273.40, SD = 196.68) and the number of characters used to write the letter (M = 460.75, SD = 332.50); maximum normality was attained with Box-Cox parameters of 0.50 for time spent and 0.51 for character length, and the two transformed indicators were standardized and averaged into a single behavioral support measure. Estimation used ANOVA with one-sided planned contrasts, and bias-corrected confidence intervals for the indirect effect estimated from 1,000 bootstrap estimates using the MODMED macro of Preacher, Rucker, and Hayes (2007). The authors treat the controlled experiment as the design that allowed them to establish causality, complementing the field study.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (209 employees nested within 74 leaders). In step 1 of the multilevel regression, support for change was positively impacted by vision of continuity (coefficient = .17, t = 2.80, p = .01) and negatively impacted by work uncertainty (coefficient = -.23, t = -3.56, p = .00). In step 2 the predicted interaction effect emerged as significant (coefficient = .12, t = 2.30, p = .02, Δ pseudo R² = .02), supporting Hypothesis 1. Simple slope analysis showed that the impact of vision of continuity on follower support for change was stronger when employee work uncertainty was high, 1 SD above the mean (coefficient = .29, t = 3.67, p = .00), than when employee work uncertainty was low, 1 SD below the mean, where it was not significant (coefficient = .06, t = .68, p = .50). Consistent with Hypothesis 2, the indirect effect through perceived collective continuity equalled .31 × .40 = .12 at high levels of work uncertainty (99% CI = .02 to .24), whereas at low levels of work uncertainty it equalled .31 × .19 = .06 (90% CI = -.01 to .13). Supplemental analyses produced comparable results when type of change was controlled for, when the data were aggregated to the level of the 74 leaders, and when the aggregated analysis was restricted to the 33 leaders rated by at least two followers.
Study 2 (199 participants). An ANOVA of the behavioral support for change measure yielded neither a main effect of vision, F(1, 196) = .24, n.s., nor a main effect of uncertainty, F(1, 196) = .55, n.s. As in Study 1, a significant interaction was found between vision and uncertainty, F(1, 195) = 4.45, p = .04, η² = .02, supporting Hypothesis 1. One-sided planned contrasts showed that when uncertainty was high participants showed more behavioral support for change in the vision of continuity condition (M = .23, SD = .88) than in the control condition (M = -.12, SD = 1.04), t(195) = 1.83, p = .04, η² = .02; when there was no uncertainty the planned contrasts did not indicate a significant difference between the vision conditions, t(195) = 1.16, p = .25. Supporting Hypothesis 2, the indirect effect through perceived collective continuity was significant when uncertainty was high (.39 × .44 = .17; 95% CI = .01 to .39) but not when there was no uncertainty (.39 × .05 = .02; 90% CI = -.14 to .17). In robustness and exploratory analyses, controlling for perceived change size left the interaction significant, F(1, 194) = 3.86, p = .05, η² = .02, with the high-uncertainty indirect effect still significant (.34 × .38 = .13, 90% CI = .01 to .29), and collective identification did not moderate the effect of vision of continuity on change support, F(1, 194) = .00, p = .96 — a null finding the authors report explicitly and link to conflicting evidence on organizational identification and change reactions.
Overall, both hypotheses were supported in each of the two studies, and the paper concludes that across a field study and an experimental study it finds support for these predictions; the qualification is that Study 2 produced no main effect of either vision or uncertainty on behavioral support, so the vision-of-continuity advantage there appeared only under high uncertainty.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to visionary leadership research by specifying a content feature of effective change visions: assuring continuity rather than only emphasizing dissatisfaction with the status quo or an attractive future. It also contributes to organizational change and social identity research by showing that leader vision communication can shape perceived collective continuity, which helps explain support for change under uncertainty.

**Practical Implication**
Managers leading change should not only rationalize why change is needed or portray the future positively. They should also identify what employees understand as central to the organization's identity and frame planned change as preserving, reaffirming, or continuing those defining features.

**Limitations**
Study 1 raises possible common method and common source concerns for the self-reported antecedent and mediator, although the authors argue these concerns are offset by Study 2 and by supervisor-rated support in Study 1. The paper also calls for tests of whether the findings generalize to other change outcomes, such as satisfaction, trust in management, and turnover intentions, and to other change contexts.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine the framing strategies leaders use to create perceived continuity, including linking past and future, framing change as preserving identity, and using organizational symbols or work design. The authors also suggest examining leader-member exchange, value congruence, change size perceptions, industry turbulence, and cases where change itself targets identity.
