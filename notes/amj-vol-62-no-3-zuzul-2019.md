# “Matter Battles”: Cognitive Representations, Boundary Objects, and the Failure of Collaboration in Two Smart Cities

**Abstract**
In this paper, I present a longitudinal study of two smart city projects that brought together experts from diverse knowledge domains. Both projects structured collaboration around the development of boundary objects that could integrate actors’ expertise. In both projects, however, the objects sparked conflicts that exacerbated rather than attenuated differences. I develop a process model exploring how and why the development of boundary objects can manifest as divisive conflict that derails collaboration. In both projects, extreme novelty gave rise to concept ambiguity, a lack of shared ideas about what smart cities were, and process ambiguity, a lack of shared ideas about how smart cities should be developed. Ambiguity led actors from diverse domains to form divergent cognitive representations about smart cities. As they developed boundary objects, actors made decisions that violated some cognitive representations, while reifying others into material outcomes. Their efforts to develop the objects manifest as matter battles: high-stakes conflicts about material outcomes that, over time, set the stage for collaboration failure. In advancing these ideas, I provide an alternative perspective to the literature on collaboration across boundaries, which has primarily treated boundary objects as tools of integration rather than weapons of division.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why and how attempts to develop boundary objects, usually treated as tools of integration, can become divisive conflicts that derail collaboration. It examines this question in two smart city projects where actors from different knowledge domains tried to transform ambiguous ideas into material urban-development outcomes.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Extreme novelty, concept ambiguity, process ambiguity, divergent cognitive representations, boundary objects, materiality, matter battles, negative emotions, stereotyping, and collaboration failure.
- Process / phases: Extreme novelty creates ambiguity; actors form divergent smart and city representations; boundary object development forces concrete choices; choices reify some representations while violating others; repeated matter battles produce negative emotional and cognitive dynamics; actors withdraw from collaboration.
- Boundary objects: SmartBlock contract, SmartBlock master plan, SmartBlock pro forma, SmartTown financial deck, and SmartTown master plan.
- Emergent outcomes: Conflict over material decisions, simplified us-versus-them stereotypes, reduced interaction, disengagement, project withdrawal, and abandonment of collaboration.
- Boundary conditions: The process is theorized for extremely novel, cross-boundary innovation projects that require diverse experts to create material outcomes without settled templates or accepted authority structures.

The process begins when novelty leaves actors without shared definitions of what smart cities are or how they should be built. Actors from technology, design, urban engineering, architecture, real estate, and consulting domains develop different cognitive representations of the same projects. When boundary objects require concrete decisions about materials, financial assumptions, or development plans, those choices become consequential because the resulting city is physical, durable, and difficult to reverse. The conflicts therefore become matter battles: high-stakes disputes over material outcomes that intensify emotions, stereotyping, and withdrawal rather than integration.

**Data & Measures**
The study pools data from two pseudonymous smart city projects, SmartTown and SmartBlock (Bechky & Okhuysen, 2011), an approach the author distinguishes from planned multiple-case studies (Eisenhardt, 1989): the stated purpose was not to compare the two projects' outcomes but to examine the patterns that replicated across them. Field research ran at SmartTown from late 2009 to mid-2012 and at SmartBlock from early 2011 to mid-2012, following a round of preliminary SmartBlock interviews in 2010; SmartTown was observed unfolding in real time from the project's starting point, whereas SmartBlock's earliest phases were tracked through archival sources triangulated with retrospective interviews. Data collection ended when both projects were placed on indefinite hold in mid-2012 and the interviews had reached theoretical saturation. Project-level data were gathered through interviews, observation, and document review across the 10 firms studied. The author conducted 104 in-depth interviews with 59 individuals across the projects, ranging from 60 minutes to three hours, all tape recorded and transcribed; Table 2 reports this as 70 interviews with 41 individuals at SmartTown and 34 interviews with 18 individuals at SmartBlock. Observation comprised six one-week to one-month visits to SmartTown and three week-long visits to SmartBlock, with full days spent onsite at SmartTown and about five hours per day at SmartBlock, supported by extensive field notes and informal interviews at casual gatherings; Table 2 reports 6 versus 3 field visits, 40 versus 2 meetings, 10 versus 1 presentations and press events, and approximately 800 versus 100 hours in the field for SmartTown and SmartBlock respectively. Archival documents at SmartTown included successive business plans, presentations, and master plans, plus 105 emails exchanged between the multi-firm leadership team on which the author was copied; at SmartBlock they included project analyses, an internal blog written by DesignCo employees, and reports written by designers and urban engineers. Industry-level data added 14 interviews with industry stakeholders including technologists, architects, urban engineers, and governmental officials, attendance at smart city conferences, and analysis of smart city articles and reports from companies and think tanks.

Constructs were coded rather than measured. The analytical approach was inductive and open-ended (Strauss & Corbin, 1997) and unfolded in five stages. First, the author constructed thick descriptions (Langley, 1999): lengthy case studies of each project's aims, history, and actors, chronological accounts of all major events, decisions, and changes, and narrative analysis (Boje, 2001) capturing the stories told by the diverse actors involved. Second, QSR NVivo was used to engage in open coding of interview transcripts, field notes, and archival data, generating nearly 100 emergent codes that were condensed and clustered into a set of 14 second-order categories, among them Cognitive Representations and Emotions. Third, revisiting the raw data, case studies, and NVivo codes produced a set of "episodes of break-down" — five conflicts, three at SmartBlock and two at SmartTown, of which the author had fully or partially observed three while two were described in interviews and archival documents. Fourth, comparing across the conflicts revealed that each involved the creation or manipulation of an artifact meaningful to all actors, which connected the analysis to the boundary objects literature and led the author to revise the research question. Fifth, iterating between the data and the cross-boundary collaboration literature integrated the earlier codes into a single process model, which was validated by reinterviewing and presenting results to two insiders, a designer at SmartBlock and a consultant at SmartTown. The design is inductive and interpretive: the paper reports no statistical tests, no hypothesis tests, and no causal identification strategy, and the author states the study was designed to generate rather than test theory.

**Key Findings**
The paper reports an emergent process model (Figure 2) in which developing boundary objects manifests as divisive conflict rather than integration. In both projects, extreme novelty gave rise to two early and simultaneous forms of ambiguity: concept ambiguity, a lack of clarity about what smart cities are and how their success should be measured, and process ambiguity, a lack of clarity about how the process of smart city development should unfold, including who should lead it. Ambiguity in turn allowed two divergent cognitive representations to form in each project. Technologists, designers, urban engineers, and architects formed the "smart" representation, seeing smart cities as "living laboratories," "reference projects," or "sandboxes" for developing and demonstrating replicable smart and sustainable solutions, and believing that technologists and sustainable development experts should lead development. Real-estate developers and consultants formed the "city" representation, seeing each project as an attempt to build an attractive, livable, and hence sellable urban development, and believing that the requirements of the land and buildings should determine which technological solutions were developed rather than the reverse.

As actors structured collaboration around five boundary objects — the SmartBlock contract (2009), master plan (2011), and pro forma (2011), and the SmartTown financial deck (2010) and master plan (2011) — the divergent representations produced incompatible preferences about concrete choices, which drove lengthy and heated discussions. Facing temporal pressure to complete each object before planning could continue, actors resolved those discussions by making divisive decisions that reified some representations into material outcomes while violating others. Table 4 gives the implemented choice for each object: on the SmartBlock contract the real-estate developers conceded and the contract was embedded with few formal real-estate targets; on the SmartBlock master plan actors on both sides acquiesced on certain points, deciding to construct one building in timber and the others in concrete; the SmartBlock pro forma was not developed before the board rejected the project, because of the failure to agree; on the SmartTown financial deck the consultants agreed to benchmark on typical technology projects; and on the SmartTown master plan the technologists decided not to develop real-estate targets and requirements in detail. Because the intended outcomes were physical, permanent, and difficult to reverse, these disagreements acquired deeper significance and are categorized as matter battles — a term the author takes from an architect (Boyer, 2011) and proposes can be usefully applied, in organizational settings, to high-stakes conflicts about material outcomes.

The reification of representations into material outcomes evoked strong frustration, irritation, disappointment, anger, and even contempt and hostility, after which actors began describing each other through simplifying "us" versus "them" stereotypes and blame: smart-representation actors described collaborators as "stuck in traditional ways of thinking and working," while city-representation actors blamed collaborators for their lack of understanding of real-estate development or construction. Repeated conflicts led actors to question the viability of the collaborations and even the projects themselves. The author reports intermediate successes that did not counterbalance the conflicts — SmartBlock actors convinced their country's major energy provider to create a clean energy mix specifically for the block, and SmartTown actors won a number of awards for their innovative ideas about smart cities. At SmartBlock, actors limited their work to the minimum requirements set out by the contracts and their aims shifted from innovating to simply finishing; in mid-2012 DesignCo's board rejected the final project proposal, including the master plan, and SmartBlock was called off. At SmartTown, both the consultants and the real-estate developers stopped working on the project entirely, and TechCo's CEO categorized those two relationships as "lowlights" of the project.

Comparing the two settings, the author reports that despite differences in size, timeframe, cost, leadership structure, and stage of development at the start of the research, analysis of the projects' devolution revealed a strikingly robust pattern. The paper proposes that matter battles comprise a kind of intractable conflict, but one that differs from the intractable conflicts described in prior work: rather than enduring clashes between individuals from different ethnicities, nationalities, and societies, or long-standing disputes between occupational groups, matter battles occur among individuals without histories of prior clashes who come together to create materiality amid ambiguity. The findings are inductive and interpretive; no hypotheses are tested and no effect sizes or significance levels are reported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on cross-boundary collaboration by showing that boundary objects can become weapons of division, not only tools of integration. It links cognitive representations and boundary objects by explaining how ambiguous settings allow divergent mental maps to form and then become reified through material choices. It also develops the construct of matter battles as a form of intractable conflict that can emerge in novel organizational projects even without long-standing group divisions.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that leaders of novel cross-boundary innovation projects should not assume that boundary objects will automatically integrate diverse expertise. They need to surface and compare actors’ cognitive representations before object development forces irreversible choices. The paper suggests that pre-prototypes or other low-stakes draft objects may help reveal divergent representations before decisions become embedded in high-stakes material outcomes.

**Limitations**
The study was designed to generate rather than test theory, so the process model should be treated as analytically grounded but not statistically tested. The evidence comes from two deviant smart city projects that both ended in collaboration failure; the author argues that the differences between the cases amplify the generalizability of the findings, allow boundary conditions to be theorized, and strengthen confidence in the process uncovered. The author also notes that the cases did not allow systematic comparison of which kinds of boundary objects are more or less likely to generate matter battles.

**Future Research**
Future research can test whether the matter-battle process appears in settings with clearer leadership, more established power dynamics, or less expert collaborators. Studies can also examine which kinds of boundary objects are especially likely to produce matter battles, including whether flexibility, reversibility, or optionality reduce conflict. The paper further calls for work on interventions, such as practices that reveal or integrate cognitive representations before ambiguity is transformed into materiality.
