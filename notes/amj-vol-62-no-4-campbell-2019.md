# Born to Take Risk? The Effect of CEO Birth Order on Strategic Risk Taking

**Abstract**
The importance of birth order has been the subject of debate for centuries, and has captured the attention of the general public and researchers alike. Despite this interest, scholars have little understanding of the impact birth order has on CEOs and their strategic decisions. With this in mind, we develop theory that explains how CEO birth order may be associated with strategic risk taking. Drawing from evolutionary theory arguments related to birth order, we theorize that CEO birth order is positively associated with strategic risk taking; that is, earlier-born CEOs will take less risk than later-born CEOs. As evolutionary theory proposes that birth order effects are driven by sibling rivalry, we also argue that this relationship is moderated by three factors related to sibling rivalry: age gap between a CEO and the closest born sibling, CEO age, and the presence of a sibling CEO. Our results provide support for our theorizing and suggest that birth order may have important implications for organizations. We believe this study helps advance strategic management research, the broader multidisciplinary “family science” literature, and the much-needed cross-pollination of ideas between the two.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether CEO birth order is associated with strategic risk taking, and whether this relationship is strengthened or weakened by conditions tied to sibling rivalry. It specifically examines whether closest-sibling age gap, CEO age, and the presence of a sibling CEO condition the birth order-risk taking relationship.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: CEO birth order is positively associated with strategic risk taking; that is, later-born CEOs will take more risk than earlier-born CEOs.
- H2: The positive relationship between CEO birth order and strategic risk taking is moderated by the age gap between a CEO and the closest-born sibling, such that the relationship is strengthened when the age gap is smaller and weakened when the age gap is larger.
- H3: The positive relationship between CEO birth order and strategic risk taking is moderated by CEO age, such that the relationship is strengthened when CEOs are younger in age and weakened when CEOs are older in age.
- H4: The positive relationship between CEO birth order and strategic risk taking is moderated by the presence of a sibling CEO, such that the relationship is strengthened when a CEO has a sibling who is a CEO and weakened when a CEO does not have a sibling who is a CEO.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO birth order, coded as the order in which CEOs were born to their parents.
- DV(s): Strategic risk taking, measured as the logged aggregate of capital expenditures, R&D spending, and acquisitions.
- Mediators: No direct mediator is tested; sibling rivalry is the proposed theoretical mechanism.
- Moderators: Closest sibling age gap, CEO age, and presence of a sibling CEO.

The theorized mechanism is that birth order shapes childhood sibling rivalry and niche differentiation, which in turn forms durable risk-taking tendencies that later carry into CEO strategic decision making. Later-born CEOs are expected to take more strategic risk because later-born siblings historically faced stronger incentives to use riskier behavior to attract parental investment. The results support the main positive association, show stronger effects when sibling age gaps are smaller and when a sibling is also a CEO, and do not support CEO age as a weakening moderator.

**Data & Measures**
The primary data are family CEOs of publicly traded firms affiliated with the largest South Korean Chaebols as identified by the Korea Fair Trade Commission; after identifying cases in which members of the Chaebol families served as CEOs of these firms during 1999-2015 and accounting for missing data, the authors arrived at 71 CEOs across 67 firms and a final sample size of 503 CEO-years, all of whom are male. Birth order and family characteristics came primarily from The Chaebol of Korea: The Management Structure and Personal Network of Korean Chaebols (Kim et al., 2005), supplemented by news articles, obituaries, and other sources to keep the family trees current; other firm- and CEO-level data came from the Korea Information Service (KIS) and Korea Listed Companies Association (KLCA) databases, and acquisition data from SDC platinum. CEOs were identified using the KISLINE database as the person ranked number 1 within each company. The dependent variable, strategic risk taking at t + 1, is an index formed by logging the sum of three outlays associated with uncertain and potentially negative returns: capital expenditures and R&D spending (both obtained from the KLCA database) and acquisitions (the transaction value of all completed acquisitions as reported in SDC platinum); the three were aggregated rather than modeled separately because these spending categories often substitute for each other. CEO birth order is coded as the order in which CEOs were born to their parents, taking the value 1 for a first-born, 2 for a second-born, 3 for a third-born, and so on, and is treated as a continuous variable; Wald tests comparing this treatment against a seven-category discrete measure and a three-category first-born/middle-born/last-born measure found deviations from linearity not to be statistically significant. Closest sibling age gap is the number of years between the focal CEO and the closest sibling, determined by birthdates (for a first-born CEO the closest sibling is the immediate younger sibling). Presence of sibling CEO is a binary indicator taking the value 1 when a sibling of the focal CEO was simultaneously a CEO of another independently traded firm, which occurred for 37% of the CEO-year observations, determined from the executives list in the KIS database. CEO age is the focal year minus the CEO's birth year. Controls include number of siblings (logged), number of female siblings (logged), CEO stock ownership, a dummy for being born during an economic upswing (1960s-1970s), presence of a sibling executive in the same firm, firm size (logged total assets), return on assets, Tobin's Q, board size (logged number of board members), proportion of outside board members, presence of a female director, financial leverage, stock market beta, and year and industry fixed effects (industry classification based on two-digit codes). Estimation follows a Heckman two-stage model adopted to account for potential bias from the nonrandom CEO sampling: the first-stage probit predicted the presence of a family CEO in the full collection of 1,170 CEO-year observations, which includes family and nonfamily CEOs of the Chaebol-affiliated firm sample, using the business group chairman's number of children and whether the chairman's first child was a son as the two exclusion restrictions, plus firm performance, firm size, firm age, and industry and year dummies with robust standard errors; the inverse Mills ratio from that model entered the second-stage GEE models. The second-stage hypothesis tests use generalized estimating equations with an identity link function, Gaussian family, and first-order autoregressive correlation structure, with robust standard errors to account for potential misspecification of the correlation structure and heteroskedasticity. All predictor variables were lagged by one year to mitigate concerns of reverse causality, and the continuous variables in the interaction terms (CEO age and closest sibling age gap) were mean-centered. The design is an observational panel with a sample-selection correction rather than an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy; the authors present the Heckman model and lagged predictors as steps to mitigate endogeneity concerns that can never be ruled out completely. A separate supplemental analysis used a U.S. sample of 36 CEOs, assembled from individuals who were CEOs in Execucomp in 1992 and born before 1940 so that they could be matched to the 1940 U.S. census (or the 1930 census, for older CEOs) through Ancestry.com; most of the same controls were available except some board controls, and data on whether siblings were simultaneously employed as CEOs could not be gathered.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. According to Model 1 of Table 4, CEO birth order has a positive and statistically significant influence on strategic risk taking (b = .17, p < .05), and this holds across Models 2-4, which include the interaction terms, and Model 5, which includes all interaction terms; because the outcome is log-transformed, exponentiating the coefficient implies that for each increase in birth order, risky spending increases by 19%. Hypothesis 2 is supported. The moderating effect of closest sibling age gap is negative and significant in Model 2 (b = -0.09, p < .05) and the result also holds in Model 5; a one-unit increase in CEO birth order is associated with a 33% increase in strategic risk taking when the age gap is small (one standard deviation below the mean), whereas the increase is 9% when the age gap is large (one standard deviation above the mean). Hypothesis 3 is not supported. CEO age is not a statistically significant moderator in Model 3 (b = -.01, n.s.), and this nonsignificance is repeated in Model 5. Hypothesis 4 is supported. Model 4 shows the presence of a sibling CEO to be a positive and significant moderator (b = .19, p < .05), and the significant finding holds in Model 5; a one-unit increase in CEO birth order is associated with a 33% increase in strategic risk taking when there is a sibling who is a CEO in another firm, whereas there is a 15% increase when there is no such sibling CEO. All five models are estimated on 503 observations.

Supplemental analyses on the same South Korean sample report that the zero-order correlation between CEO birth order and strategic risk taking is insignificant, which the authors attribute to the presence of correlates of both the independent and dependent variable that should be controlled for; consistent with number of siblings acting as a suppressor variable, the effect of CEO birth order on strategic risk taking was not statistically significant when number of siblings was dropped and became significant when it was included, in both the South Korean and the U.S. sample. Replacing the linear age-gap measure with a dichotomous indicator of a closest-sibling age gap of less than five years also yields statistically significant support for Hypothesis 2 (b = .27, p < .05), and the result holds with a less-than-three-years cutoff. Winsorizing the dependent variable at the 1% and 99% levels left the results substantively unchanged. In the separate supplemental U.S. sample of 36 CEOs, the main effect of birth order on CEO risk taking was replicated (p < .05) and the moderating effect of closest-sibling age gap was replicated (p < .05), while the moderating effect of age was nonsignificant as in the main analyses; the sibling-CEO moderator could not be tested there because data on whether siblings were simultaneously employed as CEOs were unavailable, so only three of the four hypotheses were retested in that sample.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends upper echelons research by shifting attention from work-related executive experiences to early-life family-domain experiences. It contributes to family science and birth order research by theorizing boundary conditions around sibling rivalry, and it advances strategic risk-taking research by identifying CEO birth order as an observable ex ante correlate of strategic risk preferences.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that CEO birth order may be one observable input when organizations think about CEO strategic risk-taking tendencies. In family business groups, the presence of sibling CEOs may make sibling rivalry especially salient, so early-life family-domain experience can be relevant when interpreting executives' risk preferences.

**Limitations**
The authors note endogeneity and sample-selection concerns, although they use a Heckman two-stage correction and lagged predictors to mitigate them. The sibling rivalry mechanism is not directly measured, the primary sample is male CEOs in South Korean Chaebols, and the U.S. supplemental sample is small and constrained by historical census data. They also could not control for other executives' birth order or CEO personality traits such as the Big Five.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether CEO birth order influences other strategic decisions and could test more directly how family experiences shape executive psychological properties such as risk-taking propensity. The authors also call for studies in other countries, larger datasets using sources such as census and Ancestry.com records, samples with enough female CEOs to test gender differences, and designs that can control for other top-management-team and personality factors.
