# Identity Affirmation as Threat? Time-Bending Sensemaking and the Career and Family Identity Patterns of Early Achievers

**Abstract**
We develop a model of dual identity affirmation through which professionals make sense of their career and family identities by incorporating past and future identity enactment into present self-concepts, and by enacting suspended career or family identities in fleeting momentary ways. The model emerged from a qualitative inductive study of professionals whose career identities had been affirmed by achieving early career success recognized by a 40 Under 40 Award. Identity affirmation often sparked career and family identity threat that individuals resolved through time-bending sensemaking. During times of family focus, early achievers attained a sense of dual identity affirmation by believing that they had in the past (identity residue), or would again in the future (identity projection), focus on the career role, mitigating career identity threat. During periods of career focus, they similarly projected future or remembered past family involvement to mitigate family identity threat. Early achievers also found more fleeting ways to maintain their identities in identity bursts that helped them resolve threat to their career or family identity while the other was taking precedence. Three identity patterns emerged: identity bursts anchored chronic accelerating, while residue was key to lane switching; seasonalizing was anchored by both residue and projecting.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how career identity affirmation from external recognition can create identity threat for individuals with salient career and family identities. It also asks how those individuals construe time to make sense of career and family identities, mitigate threat, and achieve internal dual identity affirmation.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Career identity affirmation, dual identity affirmation, career identity threat, family identity threat, and the difficulty of enacting valued career and family identities simultaneously.
- Triggering condition: Early career success recognized through 40 Under 40 awards affirmed participants' career identities but also made future career expectations and family sacrifices more salient.
- Time-bending sensemaking strategies: Identity residue links present self-concepts to past identity enactment; identity projecting links present self-concepts to imagined future enactment; identity bursts provide brief, intense enactments of a threatened identity.
- Identity patterns: Chronic accelerating relies heavily on family identity projecting and bursts while maintaining high career focus; lane switching uses accumulated career residue to shift into more family-focused cruising; seasonalizing alternates career and family focus across shorter or longer seasons.
- Emergent outcomes: Participants sought dual identity affirmation by redefining balance over time rather than only in the present moment.

The process is temporal and interpretive rather than a variable-based causal model. External recognition can affirm career identity while simultaneously threatening both career and family identities. Early achievers respond by stretching the time horizon of identity enactment into the past, present, and future, allowing them to feel connected to identities that cannot always be enacted at the same time.

**Data & Measures**
Two waves of semi-structured interviews analyzed inductively; no variables were measured, no statistical estimation was performed, and no causal identification strategy is claimed. Wave 1: the first two authors jointly conducted 20 (60-minute) face-to-face interviews with professionals who worked a minimum of 30 hours per week, recruited from a professional development conference in a large Midwestern city and compensated with $50 cash or donation. Wave 1 participants were 7 men and 13 women, age M = 46 (SD = 9.6), five nonparents and 15 parents, 14 married, two divorced, three in committed relationships and one single, and 18 of 20 White; the protocol asked about work and personal life roles, choices made to fulfill those roles, and strategies for managing them (Appendix A), with follow-up questions on comments that opened new terrain. Wave 2: the first two authors conducted 45 semi-structured interviews with recipients of 40 Under 40 awards (1994–2015); information on award winners was publicly accessible, and the first contact was an awardee closely networked with other awardees who helped recruit participants using a snowball method. Nine Wave 2 interviews were conducted face to face and 36 over telephone or video conferencing. Wave 2 participants were age M = 41.2 (SD = 7.0), 16 men and 29 women, eight nonparents and 37 parents, 33 married, one remarried, three divorced, three in a committed relationship and five single, and 39 of 45 White; seven had jobs in academic settings, 18 were corporate executives, 15 were entrepreneurs, and five were attorneys. The Wave 2 protocol asked about the affirmation and identity threat from the award and how career and family identities had been maintained over time in the face of identity threat (Appendix B).

Constructs were coded rather than measured. The first two authors jointly coded all Wave 1 interviews; through open coding the first three authors developed a list of first-order concepts that converged into second-order themes, including a "temporal perspective" code covering statements about ebbs and flows, a current situation being only for a short time, or how the past was or the future would be different. Data analysis then used grounded theory methodology (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), similar to the Wave 1 approach, in a three-stage process (Pratt et al., 2006) running from first-order concepts, to second-order themes, and then aggregate dimensions. Stage 1 identified statements about participants' dual identities via open coding, selecting common statements to create temporary categories and first-order concepts, documented in contact summaries (Miles & Huberman, 1994) that were used to revise and guide later interviews. Stage 2 compared distinctions within and across waves to capture change in the constructs of interest (identity threat and time-bending sensemaking strategies), moving from open to axial coding — for example, statements about future changes in identity enactment divided into two categories of identity projecting, finite situation and envisioning the future. Stage 3 had all four authors aggregate the second-order themes into theoretical dimensions, comparing them with extant literature and examining the "narrative causality" in longer quoted passages (Abell, 2004). Interviews were conducted until new data did not provide additional insights and the authors determined they had reached theoretical saturation.

**Key Findings**
Career identity affirmation from external recognition engendered feelings of being validated, affirmed, legitimate, credible, honored, or proud, but rather than generating a sense of contentment or serenity it led many early achievers to question who they were, who they had been, and who they were becoming in both career and family domains. Affirmation therefore acted as a catalyst rather than an endpoint, and because the affirmed career identity was highly valued and the family role difficult to exit, participants did not resolve the resulting threat by exiting or downgrading either identity.

Instead, early achievers described a troika of time-bending sensemaking strategies aligned with identity enactment in the past, present, and future: identity residue (past), identity bursts (present), and identity projecting (future). Residue and projecting are belief-driven — residue is the use of memories of affirming, more ideal past enactment of an identity, while projecting rests on believing that the current threatened enactment is a finite situation and envisioning more ideal enactment in the future. Identity bursts are action-driven, defined as enacting identities in more fleeting, episodic, and intense ways to mitigate threat in the present, letting individuals invest smaller amounts of time to stay in touch with the identity currently in suspension.

Three identity patterns emerged from the data. Chronic accelerating — sustained higher career and lower family focus — was revealed by family identity projecting as well as elaborate identity bursting strategies, and often carried a sense of regret and loss. Lane switching originally follows a path of career accelerating before switching to career cruising and was revealed by career identity residue strategies, with participants responding with joy, relief, and gratitude suggestive of dual identity affirmation. Seasonalizing showed a pattern of toggling back and forth between cruising and accelerating and was revealed by reliance on both identity residue and projecting. The abstract states the anchoring as: "identity bursts anchored chronic accelerating, while residue was key to lane switching; seasonalizing was anchored by both residue and projecting." Seasons unfolded over months or years (macro seasons) as well as over the course of a day or week (micro seasons), differing in the frequency and magnitude of the shifts.

The authors report emotional outcomes that they say provide only some evidence, allowing them to speculate about the relative efficacy of the patterns: seasonalizers often felt blessed, grateful, and lucky; lane switching often yielded joy, relief, and gratitude after switching, though individuals may endure a long and uncertain period of family identity projecting while accumulating career residue; and chronic accelerating may require the most energy to continually enact family identity bursting strategies, causing stress and anxiety and risking burnout. Microseasonalizing may be the most effective strategy for creating a sense of balance but requires discipline to actively manage work–family boundaries. Comparing award winners by cohort, participants who won 10 or more years earlier were more likely to have the perspective needed to recognize identity seasons playing out over years or to have built enough residue to switch lanes; those who won approximately 5–10 years prior commonly used seasonalizing strategies over shorter periods such as daily, especially if they had children; and those who won in the most recent five years were often younger and had more success with family projecting but were less sure how the pattern of their lives would unfold into larger seasons. Overall, the findings illustrate how balance may be achieved cognitively, if not actually, over time.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to identity and work-family theory by showing that identity affirmation can be an instigator of identity work, not only a stabilizing outcome. It develops the time-bending sensemaking concepts of identity residue, identity projecting, and identity bursts, and links them to three career-family identity patterns: chronic accelerating, lane switching, and seasonalizing. It also reframes work-family balance as something individuals may construct across time through past and future selves, not only through present-time role allocation.

**Practical Implication**
The study suggests that organizations should not assume early achievers always want a straight career ladder to the top. Flexible work, vacation time, sabbaticals, and career systems that allow acceleration, cruising, and renewed acceleration may help professionals sustain both career and family identities. Awards and other public recognition may also require coaching, peer discussion, or mentoring support because affirmation can create new identity pressure.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study relies on retrospective interview accounts rather than repeated interviews with the same informants over time. Generalizability is also limited because the focal sample consists of early achievers who received public external recognition at a young age, so the same patterns may differ among less publicly affirmed professionals or among people whose career identities are less central.

**Future Research**
The authors call for panel data or experience sampling research to track how time-related sensemaking strategies shape identity management over time. They also suggest future work on typologies of identity affirmation, the emergence of additional patterns such as chronic cruising, and gender differences in how men and women enact time-bending identity strategies.
