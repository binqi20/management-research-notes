# Does Losing Temporary Workers Matter? The Effects of Planned Turnover on Replacements and Unit Performance

**Abstract**
This study examines the performance consequences of planned turnover. In particular, we investigate the departure of temporary workers due to the expiration of their contracts. We reconcile the contradictory predictions of collective turnover research—that any type of worker’s exit impairs organizational performance—and of contingent work research—that temporary workers’ exits improve organizational performance because they provide flexibility. We argue that the planned turnover of temporary workers has an inverted U-shaped relationship with unit performance because it combines flexibility benefits and disruption costs. We also argue that the costs of planned temporary worker turnover are moderated by the proportion and the firm-specific experience of their replacements. We test these arguments using longitudinal monthly data from a leading multinational company in the food and beverage industry, and find support for our hypotheses. The study suggests that managers hiring temporary workers should consider the cost of losing them and challenges the widespread scholarly and managerial assumption that temporary workers are disposable resources.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether the planned departure of temporary workers due to contract expiration improves unit performance through numerical flexibility or impairs performance through operational disruption. It also asks how the proportion of replacements and replacements' firm-specific experience shape that planned turnover-performance relationship.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. The planned turnover of temporary workers has an inverted U-shaped relationship with unit performance.
H2. The planned turnover of temporary workers and the proportion of replacements among temporary workers interact in predicting unit performance: an additional unit of planned turnover decreases performance more when the proportion of replacements among temporary workers is high than when it is low.
H3. The planned turnover of temporary workers and the firm-specific experience of replacements interact in predicting unit performance: an additional unit of planned turnover decreases performance more when replacements are novices than when they are experienced.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Planned temporary worker turnover, measured as temporary workers leaving because of contract expiration divided by average temporary workers in the store in the prior month.
- DV(s): Unit financial performance, measured as the logarithm of store net controllable profit.
- Mediators: Not directly measured; the proposed mechanisms are flexibility benefits from adjusting workforce size and disruption costs from lost firm-specific human capital, changed routines, and training demands.
- Moderators: Proportion of replacements among temporary workers; experienced replacements; novice replacements.

Planned temporary worker turnover can initially improve performance by reducing slack and labor costs when units adjust staffing to demand. Beyond an optimal level, exits deplete collective human capital and disrupt routines, so the performance effect turns negative. Replacements can restore productive capacity at low levels of planned turnover, but high replacement intensity, especially with novice replacements, increases disruption when turnover is already high.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses a matched unit-employee dataset of monthly personnel and performance records for the Italian sales network of a multinational chain in food and beverage services for travelers, covering the years 2007-2014 (96 months). The unit of analysis is the store-month-year, and only basic operators are considered because managers are always permanent employees. These records were manually matched with regional macroeconomic indicators from the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT) and with highway traffic volumes from the Italian Association of Highways (AISCAT). The initial database includes 19,340 store-month-year observations for 256 stores with complete information on the unit, the workers, and the manager; after excluding September observations, the final database used in the analyses includes 13,280 store-month-year observations for 255 stores. The dependent variable, unit performance, is proxied by store profitability, measured as the logarithm of store net controllable profit in month t (total sales minus cost of goods sold, labor cost, and other store costs), with actual profits multiplied by a decimal constant (X/1,000) for confidentiality. The independent variable, planned temporary worker turnover, is the number of temporary workers leaving the unit because of contract expiration in month t-1 divided by the average number of temporary workers in the store in month t-1; workers rehired within the same month are not counted as turning over. Replacements is the total number of temporary workers who enter the unit in month t divided by the average number of temporary workers in the store in month t. Experienced replacements is the ratio of temporary workers entering through rehiring or transfer from another unit in month t to the same denominator, with rehires constituting 98% of experienced replacements on average. Novice replacements is the ratio of first-time temporary hires entering in month t to the same denominator. Controls are the trimestral regional unemployment rate in month t-1, hours worked by temporary workers and by permanent workers in month t, permanent workers' company tenure in years in month t-1, and the company's store classification from 1 (little complexity) to 6 (strong complexity); a subsample of 9,041 observations for 167 stores adds volume of demand, measured as the total number of vehicles traversing the highway segment where the store is located in each trimester. Estimation uses store fixed effects models with standard errors clustered at the store level, plus month, year, and manager fixed effects, and a one-month lag between the independent and dependent variables. Hypothesis 1 is tested with a quadratic term for planned temporary worker turnover; Hypotheses 2 and 3 are tested with a two-part spline that splits planned turnover at the empirically derived optimal level of 29.57%, generating separate low and high planned temporary worker turnover variables. The design is observational rather than a named causal identification strategy: the paper treats endogeneity as a potential concern and addresses it through month and manager fixed effects, exclusion of September observations, and subsample analyses controlling for demand volume. The Table 3 interaction models that include the replacement measures are estimated on 13,187 store-month-year observations, while Model 1 of Table 3 and the Table 2 models report 13,280. Supplementary qualitative evidence comes from interviews with three managers, three temporary workers, and three permanent workers, one each in three units selected in partnership with the organization; each interview lasted about half an hour, was recorded, and followed the same protocol, with questions tailored to the type of respondent.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. In the linear model (Table 2, Model 2) planned temporary worker turnover is negatively and significantly related to unit performance (b = -0.05, p < .01), while in the quadratic model (Table 2, Model 3) the linear term is positive and significant (b = 0.12, p < .01) and the squared term is negative and significant (b = -0.21, p < .001). The optimal point lies at 29.57% (0.30), well within the range of the data (mean = 0.30, SD = 0.52); the slope below the optimum is positive (0.12; t = 2.67, p < .01) and the slope beyond it is negative (-7.26; t = -3.22, p < .001). The curvilinear pattern persists in the subsample controlling for volume of demand (linear term b = 0.11, p < .05; squared term b = -0.18, p < .01). The two-part spline (Table 3, Model 1) reproduces the pattern: low planned temporary worker turnover is positive and significant (b = 0.10, p < .05) and high planned temporary worker turnover is negative and significant (b = -0.14, p < .001). Replacements have a positive and significant main effect on unit performance (b = 0.04, p < .001; Table 3, Model 2). Hypothesis 2 is only partially supported. Contrary to Hypothesis 2, the interaction between low planned temporary worker turnover and replacements is positive and significant (b = 0.26, p < .01), so below the optimal level an additional unit of turnover is more beneficial when the proportion of replacements is high than when it is low. Above the optimal level the interaction between high planned temporary worker turnover and replacements is negative and marginally significant (b = -0.10, p < .10), so the authors conclude that Hypothesis 2 is supported only for high levels of planned turnover. Hypothesis 3 is likewise supported only beyond the optimal level. At low planned turnover both interactions are positive and significant (experienced replacements b = 0.20, p < .05; novice replacements b = 1.27, p < .05). At high planned turnover the interaction with novice replacements is negative and significant (b = -0.56, p < .01), whereas the interaction with experienced replacements is not statistically significant (p > .10), and a Wald test rejects the hypothesis that the two coefficients are equal (F(1, 254) = 5.43, p < .05). The magnitudes are economically meaningful: for a store with average profitability (79.78) and a high proportion of replacements (0.83), moving from zero to optimal turnover raises performance by 4.79 monetary units (0.07 standard deviations; a 6% increase in profits), whereas moving from the optimal level (0.30) to a high level (0.82) lowers it by 7.98 monetary units (0.11 standard deviations; a 10% decrease in profits); with a high rate of novice replacements (0.24) the corresponding changes are a gain of 8.78 monetary units (0.12 standard deviations; an 11% increase) and a loss of 11.17 monetary units (0.16 standard deviations; a 14% decrease). The interviews corroborate the theorized mechanism: informants reported that temporary workers' exits force the remaining workers to redistribute and take on more work, and that teaching replacements implies a lot of additional work for them.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconciles collective turnover research and contingent work research by showing that planned temporary worker turnover is neither uniformly harmful nor costless. It develops and tests an inverted U-shaped account in which numerical flexibility benefits coexist with operational disruption costs. It also extends context-emergent turnover and capacity theory by distinguishing the quantity of replacements from their firm-specific human capital.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should treat temporary workers as resources that can accumulate firm-specific human capital rather than as disposable plug-in labor. The paper suggests that staffing flexibility has hidden costs when high planned turnover forces remaining workers to absorb departures and train replacements. Managers can reduce disruption by avoiding excessive waves of novice replacements after high temporary-worker turnover.

**Limitations**
The evidence comes from one company in Italy, a context with high employment protection for permanent workers and unusually low non-temporary turnover. The jobs studied are low-skill food and beverage service roles, so generalizability to more complex knowledge work remains uncertain. The archival design does not directly measure operational disruption or remaining workers' reactions, and the study is not experimental.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine settings with less employment protection, more simultaneous forms of turnover, and different uses of temporary work. Studies could test whether unit-level HR practices, climate, and managerial discretion moderate the effects of planned temporary worker turnover. Additional work could directly measure disruption, overwork, training burdens, and replacement learning after specific turnover events.
