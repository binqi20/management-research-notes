# Bringing the Firms (and Forms) Back In: Organizational Form Identity and Individual Turnover

**Abstract**
Ideas about the role of the organization in models of voluntary turnover remain scant. We posit that the social audience in general, and people working in organizations in particular, sort organizations into categories (“organizational forms”) and rely on knowledge of the taken-for-granted external identities of firms in these categories to interpret their experience as employees. Although many of these interpretations revolve around size, categorical distinctions between “small” and “large” firms pack concepts and ideas that extend significantly beyond a simple distinction based on size, and would be better understood as an organizational form distinction between specialist and generalist firms. Focusing on the organizational form of a specialist, we claim that two features of its form identity work against each other to influence turnover: employees of specialists realize that it is hard to develop careers internally (a push factor) but value the more relaxed environment, meaningful work, and flexibility of their jobs (a pull effect). We propose a theory to parse out the two effects, find empirical support for it in analyses of managerial turnover in the Korean TV advertising industry, and conclude that the construct of organizational form offers an opportunity to extend models of career mobility and individual turnover.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how organizational form identity, especially the distinction between specialist and generalist firms, shapes individual voluntary turnover beyond the structural effect of firm size. It examines whether specialist employment increases turnover through perceived weak internal career prospects while also reducing turnover for employees whose experience, rank, or educational prestige makes specialist work more appealing.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The effect of employment in a specialist organization on turnover is positive.
Hypothesis 2. The positive effect of employment in a specialist organization on turnover is amplified as a function of the employee's waiting time to promotion.
Hypothesis 3a. The positive effect of employment in a specialist organization on turnover is tempered and likely reversed for employees with significant industry experience.
Hypothesis 3b. The positive effect of employment in a specialist organization on turnover is tempered and likely reversed for employees in high-rank positions.
Hypothesis 3c. The positive effect of employment in a specialist organization on turnover is tempered and likely reversed for employees with high educational prestige.

The authors state that, given the paucity of research linking organizational form identity and individual turnover, they were constrained in grounding predictions in extant theory and resorted to an exploratory approach; the formal hypotheses emerged from considerations of actual labor market dynamics during the observation period, systematized through theoretical segments from research on turnover, resource partitioning, and form identity.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Specialist organizational form identity; promotion waiting time; employee industry experience; employee rank; educational prestige.
- DV(s): Individual voluntary turnover, defined as external mobility from one advertising agency to another within the Korean TV advertising industry.
- Mediators: Not modeled as formal mediators; the theorized process runs through employees' interpretations of specialist firms as offering limited internal career progression but greater flexibility, meaningful work, and intrinsic appeal.
- Moderators: Promotion clock, high industry tenure, high rank, and educational prestige moderate the specialist employment effect.

The mechanism is cross-level: employees interpret their own career experience through the social identity attached to the employing organizational form. Specialist firms create a push toward turnover because employees expect weaker internal labor markets and slower advancement; long promotion waiting times intensify that push. At the same time, specialists have identity-based appeal because they are viewed as flexible, less bureaucratic, and more meaningful workplaces, so experienced, high-ranking, or high-status employees are less likely to leave specialists than employees without those career buffers.

**Data & Measures**
Career-history data on all managerial employees in the advertising industry in Korea during 1980-1997, coded from the annual Advertising Directory compiled by the Korean Advertisers' Association. The data are limited to the market for TV advertising, because obtaining information on small agencies serving regional newspapers and local customers is described as intractable and very costly, and because the top three agencies in the data account for more than half of total TV commercial revenues. The directory lists only employees whose ranks are at least at the basic manager level (Gwajang) or above; managers' career paths were reconstructed by compiling annual directories from different years through matching on first and last name, birth year, rank, and educational background. The qualitative component is guided, open-ended interviews with four well-regarded industry executives who were active in the industry during the observation period; the interviews informed the hypotheses and were also used to shore up the validity of the specialist-generalist measure, with the industry experts agreeing with the coding and recommending no corrections.

- DV: Individual voluntary turnover (or external mobility), which took the value of 1 when an individual moved from one firm to another within the advertising industry in Korea, and 0 otherwise. Because the directories do not identify job transitions between agencies, turnover was coded by comparing each individual's position between two consecutive years and identifying job changes involving a transition between agencies. The paper reports 1,684 firm-to-firm job transitions observed during the study period, 247 transitions in which employment ceased in one agency without an immediate hire by another agency and which are therefore not marked up as part of the dependent variable, and a resulting number of transitions analyzed in the models of 1,437; auxiliary analyses using all 1,684 transitions obtained similar results. The notes to Tables 2 and 4 report n spells = 17,810 and n turnover events = 1,449, and Table 3 reports 569 turnover events to generalists and 880 to specialists.
- IV: Specialist form identity, an indicator variable assigned through an interpretive categorical distinction based on the authors' assessment of form identity, incorporating public perceptions of market dominance (reflected in a widely disseminated annual list of the "top ten firms" in the industry) and affiliation with a large parent conglomerate. Three researchers coded organizational forms and reached full agreement on all members of the categories.
- Moderators: Promotion clock, a duration variable counting the waiting time in years until promotion in the current firm, started at the individual's entry in the firm and reset to zero at each promotion or transition to a new agency. High industry tenure, the top quintile of a continuous measure of years worked in the industry, a cutoff corresponding to employees with eight or more years of industry experience. High rank, the top quintile of Korea's conventional seven-level position system, corresponding to those in positions of managing director (level 4) or higher. Educational prestige, annual university reputation scores computed from the average national college entrance examination scores of all students matriculated in the focal university's department of business administration, matched to each employee-year observation and marked up at the top quintile.
- Controls: at the individual level, individual age, prior turnover (individuals with two or more interfirm job transitions), cumulative number of promotions in the focal agency, and number of employees in the agency in the same rank as ego; at the organization level, the natural log of organizational size (number of employees) and organizational age; at the market and industry level, counts of firm foundings and firm failures and a measure of collective turnover (the total annual number of firm-to-firm job transitions in the industry); plus three macroeconomic controls (yearly gross national product, industry resources as the percentage of advertising expenditures by client firms from GNP, and top 100 budgets). All independent and control variables were lagged by a year.

Estimation uses generalized estimating equations (GEE) on an unbalanced panel structured at the individual-year level, with a logit link function, an exchangeable correlation matrix selected on fit, and cluster-robust standard errors based on the Huber-White sandwich estimator. Because GEE does not allow likelihood estimation and so makes model fit hard to evaluate, the authors also estimate the turnover rate in an event-history specification as a second method, reporting those estimates as almost identical to the GEE estimates. The paper names no causal identification strategy: the models are associational panel estimates with one-year-lagged predictors. Robustness analyses split the dependent variable by destination state (transitions to generalists versus to specialists, Table 3), relegate specialism to a control and make continuous size the key explanatory variable in all interaction terms (Table 4), add three-way interactions of promotion wait time and specialist with high rank, high experience, and educational prestige, and substitute two continuous niche-width measures (the variance in employee age and in educational prestige within each agency-year) for the categorical specialism measure.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported. The baseline effect of specialist employment on turnover is positive (Model 2.2: 0.29, p < .05), which the paper reads as the deleterious effect of perceived weak career incentives dominating the beneficial effect of identity-based appeal. Ceteris paribus, employees are 1.34 (exp 0.29) times more likely to quit a specialist than a generalist.
- Hypothesis 2 supported. The interaction of specialist employment with promotion waiting time is positive and significant (Model 2.3: 0.09, p < .05; Model 2.7: 0.16, p < .01), suggesting that the higher turnover odds in specialists are buttressed by time elapsed without promotion. Plotting the saturated model, for an employee who has not been promoted in 10 years the odds of quitting a specialist are almost twice as high as quitting a generalist.
- Hypothesis 3a. In Model 2.4 the specialist by high-industry-tenure interaction is negative (-0.32) and, in the paper's words, "only significant in a one-tailed test (or significant at the .10 level in a two-tailed test)." In the saturated Model 2.7 the same interaction becomes highly significant (-0.54, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 3b supported. The specialist by high-rank interaction is negative and significant (Model 2.5: -1.23, p < .01; Model 2.7: -1.16, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 3c supported. The specialist by educational-prestige interaction is negative and significant (Model 2.6: -0.37, p < .05; Model 2.7: -0.37, p < .05), consistent with the paper's expectation.
- In the saturated Model 2.7 all effects hold and the paper concludes that all hypotheses were supported. Employees with significant industry experience are only 0.58 (exp -0.54) times as likely to quit a specialist as those without it, employees in a high-rank position are only 0.31 (exp -1.16) times as likely to turn over as those holding lower positions in specialists, and employees with high educational prestige are 0.69 (exp -0.37) times as likely to quit specialists as those with lower status. Based on the size of the coefficients, high industry tenure, senior rank, and high status each more than offset the baseline positive effect of specialist employment on turnover, so employees with at least one of these characteristics are less likely to quit a specialist than a generalist employer.
- Robustness. When the dependent variable is split by destination (Table 3), the predicted effects of the theory hold in each of the two transitions; because there are fewer transitions from specialists to generalists, not all effects are significant, but those that are significant are in the predicted direction, and differentiating between the two destination states does not improve model fit. When continuous size replaces the specialist indicator as the key explanatory variable (Table 4), the interaction coefficients of size with promotion clock, industry tenure, rank, and educational prestige are all in the predicted direction with reversed signs (since increasing size signifies movement away from specialism) and highly significant, while the main effect of size continues to be insignificant and the effect of the specialist indicator remains positive and highly significant. Three-way interactions of promotion wait time and specialist with rank, experience, and educational prestige were negative, with two of the three highly significant, and results held when the categorical specialism measure was replaced by either niche-width measure.
- The qualitative interviews converge with the quantitative pattern. Interviewees defined quitting a specialist as driven primarily by career concerns, while all of them tied the image of the small specialist to a more desirable place to work, and they treated the generalist-specialist distinction as categorical rather than merely a matter of size.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to turnover research by bringing organizational form identity into models that usually emphasize individual-level psychological, demographic, or career variables. It extends resource partitioning and organizational form theory by showing how macro-level distinctions between specialist and generalist firms shape individual career mobility. It also clarifies why firm size alone is theoretically underspecified: size becomes consequential partly because it is encoded in categorical identities that employees use to interpret work and career prospects.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that retention practices should be tailored by organizational form. Specialist firms may retain employees more effectively by clarifying promotion schedules while emphasizing autonomy, creativity, purpose, flexibility, and intrinsic rewards. They may also gain more from disproportionate investment in higher-echelon, experienced, or high-status employees, who are most likely to fit specialist employment.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that the single-industry Korean advertising context limits generalizability, even though similar patterns may occur in professional service industries such as investment banking or management consulting. They also note that the data lack fine-grained information on formal and informal organizational structures and employment policies inside each agency. The study lacks gender data, which could meaningfully affect observed career patterns.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether the observed specialist-generalist turnover dynamics generalize to other industries, countries, and cultural settings. The paper also points to work that can directly observe organizational employment policies, internal structures, and individual perceptions of organizational form identities. More broadly, future turnover models should examine how organizational forms shape attachment, commitment, satisfaction, and other psychological mechanisms usually studied at the individual level.
