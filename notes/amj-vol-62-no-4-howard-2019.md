# The Spawning of Ecosystems: How Cohort Effects Benefit New Ventures

**Abstract**
Research has examined the process of “entrepreneurial migration,” whereby employees from successful industry incumbents move to new ventures. This phenomenon has been linked to direct benefits to entrepreneurial firms, offering valuable knowledge and routines obtained by employees during their tenure at incumbent firms. We propose a theoretical framework in which shared experience—a common background in beneficial knowledge management practices—creates a “cohort effect,” facilitating direct ties and innovation benefits to new ventures receiving inventors from the same incumbent firms. Analyzing longitudinal data on inventor migration to 658 new biotech ventures tracked from 1990 to 2013, we find shared migration ties increase knowledge and market overlap between firms, enhancing their likelihood of direct engagement through alliances and employee hiring as well as the quality of knowledge they develop. Our core theoretical contribution is the identification of a migration cohort effect, in which inventors who share contemporaneous experiences at incumbent firms and migrate to new ventures create unique relationships between those ventures. Where prior research on spawning and migration has focused primarily on the direct benefit of human capital transfer from incumbents to new ventures, we explore the broader network implications of similarities and interactions between firms receiving this human capital.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how the spawning of knowledge from incumbent firms, represented by inventors moving from incumbents to new ventures, creates a cohort-based ecosystem of firms that can share knowledge, engage in direct exchange, and create higher-quality knowledge. It focuses on whether shared migration cohort ties shape knowledge overlap, market overlap, alliances, inventor hiring, and knowledge quality among new biotech ventures.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a: New ventures with shared migration cohort ties are likely to have greater knowledge overlap.
- Hypothesis 1b: New ventures with shared migration cohort ties are more likely to pursue overlapping markets than firms lacking such ties.
- Hypothesis 2a: New ventures with shared migration cohort ties are more likely to form alliances than firms lacking such ties.
- Hypothesis 2b: New ventures with shared migration cohort ties are likely to have greater exchange through direct inventor migration.
- Hypothesis 3: New ventures with shared migration cohort ties are more likely to achieve greater knowledge quality.
- Hypothesis 4: The positive relationship between new ventures with shared migration cohort ties and knowledge quality is reduced as prior collaboration between cohort employees increases.
- Hypothesis 5: The positive relationship between new ventures with shared migration cohort ties and knowledge quality is greater when teams migrate to the new venture.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Shared ecosystem migration ties between new ventures; number of shared ecosystem migration ties in firm-year knowledge-quality models.
- DV(s): Knowledge overlap, market overlap, alliances, inventor migration, and knowledge quality.
- Mediators: Not modeled as formal mediators; the theorized process runs through shared knowledge management practices, common frames of reference, broader knowledge search, and direct exchange through alliances and inventor movement.
- Moderators: Employee incumbent collaboration, hypothesized to weaken the shared-tie effect on knowledge quality; team migration from incumbent, hypothesized to strengthen it but not supported.

The mechanism is that inventors who share contemporaneous experience at the same incumbent carry similar knowledge management practices, technological understandings, and social connections into different new ventures. These shared migration cohort ties make ventures more likely to recognize similar technologies and markets, form alliances, and hire from one another. Cohort ecosystem membership also broadens a new venture's knowledge search by making other ecosystem firms' knowledge developments more observable and usable. However, prior direct collaboration among the migrating inventors can reduce novelty because firms may be building from overly similar prior knowledge.

**Data & Measures**
The study draws on four archival sources covering new ventures founded in the biotech industry from 1990 to 2000 and tracked until 2013. A search of the Dow Jones VentureOne (now Thomson One) database, which collects information on entrepreneurial ventures, their founders, and their funding history for all U.S. venture capital investments, yielded the baseline sample of 658 VC-backed biotech ventures. Migration ties were built from U.S. patent records: each venture's inventors were mapped to the disambiguated inventor IDs developed by the Harvard Business School project (Li et al., 2014), and for each patent that lists a venture inventor and most recently pre-dates the venture's founding date, the patent assignee is treated as an incumbent to which the venture has an entrepreneurial migration tie; inventor histories reach back to 1975, and technology links to incumbents occurring ten years or more before founding are excluded. Recombinant Capital supplied licensing-agreement data (more than 4,000 agreements involving the 658 firms, with nine agreements directly between sample firms omitted), and SDC Platinum supplied alliance data. Two panel structures were constructed: a dyad-year data set pairing each venture with all possible combinations of other sampled firms over the focal firm's first ten years of operation, omitting years in which the alter firm was not yet in operation and observations in which one firm had been acquired by a third party, covering 1.6 million dyad-year observations (Tables 2, 3, and 7 report a sample size of 1,621,171 firm dyads); and a firm-year data set capturing each sampled firm's first ten years after founding, with 5,167 firm-years in the knowledge-quality models. Shared Ecosystem Migration, the independent variable for Hypotheses 1 to 3, is a binary variable equaling 1 beginning in the year migrating inventors are observed to begin patenting at the destination firm and for subsequent dyad-years; a shared tie occurs when individuals from the two firms are listed as inventors on patents filed with a common incumbent organization as assignee prior to the founding of their new ventures, and the cohort window is restricted to plus or minus five years. In the firm-year panel used for Hypotheses 3 to 5 the independent variable is instead the total count of shared ecosystem ties. Knowledge Overlap is a linear transformation, ranging from 1 to 0, of the Euclidean distance between the two dyad firms' knowledge-base vectors, whose dimensions are the unique USPC codes on the firm's patents and whose magnitudes are the counts of patents using each code, prepared on a three-year rolling aggregation. Market Overlap is a count of dyad-firm licensing agreements with third parties that matched both the same disease category and the same technology category. Alliances is a count of alliances announced by dyad pairs in the observation year, limited to those categorized as focusing on research and development. Inventor Migration is the total count of inventor moves between focal and alter firm in each observation year, which in practice never exceeded one event per year and so has a binary distribution. Knowledge Quality is the number of patent forward citations by external organizations within five years after application, aggregated as the sum across all focal-firm patents with application dates in the observation year; the authors note that the lag in observing forward citations makes this field-level assessment not immediately observable to sample firms, reducing potential concerns of reverse causality. Employee Incumbent Collaboration, the Hypothesis 4 moderator, was measured as the count of prior collaborations directly connecting inventors of the two firms through concurrent listing on parent-firm patents, and Team Migration from Incumbent, the Hypothesis 5 moderator, as the total number of teams of two or more inventors observed to migrate from an incumbent to the new venture in the observation year (no more than a single team per venture-year was observed, and the largest team observed was three). Dyad-level controls include Direct Patent Citation, focal and alter firm Achieved IPO, Cumulative Rounds and Cumulative Amount of VC Funding, Non-Dyad Inventor Migration and Non-Dyad Alliances, Number of Inventors, Star Scientists, Focal, Alter Firm Same Primary Technology Area, and Focal, Alter Firm Geographic Distance; firm-year controls add Number of Patents, Number of Alliances, Citation of University Patents, Total External Backward Citations, Number of Firm Technology Categories, Industry Number of VC Deals, Industry Amount of VC Investment, and Number of U.S. Drug Patents. Model specification is matched to each outcome: panel-based ordinary least squares for the continuous knowledge overlap measure; rare events logistic regression implemented through the relogit command in Stata for inventor migration, which occurs in less than 0.1% of observations; and panel-based negative binomial regression for the overdispersed count outcomes of market overlap, alliances, and knowledge quality, with firm-level fixed effects incorporated in the Hypotheses 3 to 5 tests. The paper does not claim a causal identification design for its baseline models; it addresses endogeneity through additional robustness tests, namely GMM instrumental-variable regression (instruments: Total Inventor Migration and State R&D Spending for the firm-year knowledge-quality test, reported on 4,929 firm-years; Focal, Alter Firm in Non-Compete Enforcement States and Citations of Parent Firm Technology for the dyad-level tests, with Focal Firm State R&D Spending substituted for the second instrument in the inventor-migration model), a propensity-score matching approach in Stata (psmatch2) that matched 201,311 dyad-year observations with no shared migration links to the 201,311 observations of firms that do share such a relationship, and stochastic actor-oriented models estimated in R SIENA on 658 x 658 annual sociomatrices for the period 2000 through 2005, with knowledge overlap and market overlap dichotomized at one standard deviation above the mean.

**Key Findings**
- Preliminary t tests show that new ventures drawing human capital from industry parents receive 61% more VC investment, t(657) = 4.24, p < .001, and typically have one additional round of funding, t(657) = 3.58, p < .001, relative to non-ecosystem counterparts.
- Hypothesis 1a is supported: the coefficient on shared ecosystem migration ties is positive and highly significant (b = 0.007, p <= .001, Model 2), and the presence of ecosystem ties increases the level of knowledge overlap by 165%; the paper describes this as strong support for Hypothesis 1a.
- Hypothesis 1b is supported: the coefficient associated with shared ecosystem migration ties is again positive and significant for market overlap (b = 0.895, p < .001, Model 4), with shared ecosystem ties increasing the level of market overlap by 66%.
- Hypothesis 2a is supported: the shared ecosystem migration coefficient on alliances is positive and significant (b = 1.461, p < .001, Model 6), and having a shared migration relationship increases the likelihood of forming an alliance by 39%.
- Hypothesis 2b is supported: the effect on direct inventor migration is positive and significant (b = 1.716, p < .001, Model 8), increasing the likelihood of direct inventor migration by 22%.
- Hypothesis 3 is supported: in the firm-year fixed-effects models the coefficient for number of shared ecosystem migration ties is positive and significant (b = 0.070, p < .05, Model 10), with an additional shared ecosystem tie leading to a 3.1% increase in knowledge quality for the focal firm.
- Hypothesis 4 is supported: the interaction between shared ecosystem ties and employee incumbent collaboration is negative and significant (b = -0.030, p < .001, Model 11), so prior direct collaboration among migrating inventors reduces the positive tie-to-knowledge-quality relationship.
- Hypothesis 5 is not supported: the interaction between shared ecosystem ties and team migration is not significant (b = -0.142, SE = 0.235, Model 12; b = -0.107, SE = 0.234, in the fully loaded Model 13), and the authors report finding no significance for this effect and no support in their empirical sample for the prediction that migrating teams would further enhance knowledge quality.
- Robustness tests are consistent with the baseline pattern. Standardizing external forward citations by primary USPC code and year and estimating generalized linearized models yields significant support for Hypotheses 3 and 4, with no observed significance for Hypothesis 5. The instrumental-variable model of knowledge quality again supports Hypothesis 3 (b = 0.029, p < .05, Model 19). The dyad-level instrumental-variable models are consistent with the baseline findings for knowledge overlap, market overlap, alliances, and direct migration, and the propensity-score-matched models again find positive, significant effects of shared ecosystem migration ties on all four Hypothesis 1 and 2 outcomes and on the Hypothesis 3 knowledge-quality outcome. The stochastic actor-oriented models find consistent support for shared ecosystem migration ties in the formation of high knowledge overlap relationships (b = 0.292, p < .001) and high market overlap relationships (b = 0.157, p < .001).
- One qualification the authors state: in the instrumental-variable test of direct inventor migration (Model 23) the Sargan statistic is significant (4.41, p < .05), and they report being unable to fully rule out the potential for endogeneity in their empirical results for Hypothesis 2b.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes the concept of a migration cohort effect: inventors with shared contemporaneous incumbent experience create unique relationships between the new ventures they join. It extends employee migration and organizational spawning research beyond dyadic parent-to-progeny knowledge transfer by showing that multiple progeny firms can form an intermediate ecosystem structure within an industry. It also contributes to innovation research by linking migration-based affiliations to knowledge overlap, market overlap, direct exchange, and knowledge quality.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that entrepreneurial ventures may benefit not only from hiring knowledge workers from successful incumbents, but also from understanding the broader cohort ecosystem created by those workers' shared histories. For managers in technology-intensive fields, migration cohort ties can identify likely alliance partners, talent pools, and firms pursuing related knowledge trajectories. For policymakers, the results suggest that regulations governing hiring practices and labor mobility can affect interorganizational knowledge flows and innovation dynamics, not just individual employment moves.

**Limitations**
The authors note that founders and migrating knowledge workers may leave incumbents precisely to pursue different technologies, products, or markets, which could limit or negate shared incumbent imprinting. They also acknowledge that larger incumbents may expose employees to only partly overlapping knowledge management practices, and that cohort ecosystem effects may diminish as ventures grow, diversify, hire new personnel, change alliance portfolios, or move into other technological areas. The robustness tests address several alternatives, but the instrumental-variable analysis could not fully rule out endogeneity for direct inventor migration.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how migration cohort ecosystems affect the evolution and pace of technological advancement across industries, including whether strong early incumbents eventually create societal benefits or stifle field-level development. Scholars could study technological complexity, modularity, coordinated action among ecosystem firms, comparisons across parent-firm ecosystems, and parent-level conditions that make some incumbents better spawners of successful generational spin-offs. The authors also call for work on additional outcomes such as survival, growth, overall performance, product launches, successful IPOs, firm characteristics, capabilities, and power imbalances between firms.
