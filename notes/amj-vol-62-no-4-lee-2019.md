# A Functional Model of Workplace Envy and Job Performance: When do Employees Capitalize on Envy by Learning from Envied Targets?

**Abstract**
We integrate the social functional view of emotions with recent developments in workplace envy research to develop and test a novel theoretical framework showing that envious employees can use their envy to promote self-enhancing actions rather than other-diminishing behaviors. We theorize that enviers’ core self-evaluations and friendship ties will attenuate the extent to which enviers undermine envied targets and promote the extent to which enviers actively learn from their envied targets through observational learning and advice seeking. Using two data sets from round-robin surveys of employees in the cosmetic (Study 1) and the financial (Study 2) industries, we show that, while enviers undermine envied targets, they also capitalize on their envy by seeking advice from the envied targets, and enviers who have higher core self-evaluations and friendship with the envied targets are more likely to seek advice from those targets; higher core self-evaluation also decreases the likelihood of undermining the envied targets. We found limited support for the role of envy in triggering enviers’ observational learning. Furthermore, enviers who learn from envied targets perform better and advice seeking is more influential for enviers’ performance than observational learning.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how and when workplace envy can improve job performance rather than only producing destructive behavior. It specifically examines whether envious employees learn from envied targets through observational learning and advice seeking, and whether core self-evaluations and workplace friendship make constructive responses more likely than social undermining.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. A's envy of B is positively related to A's undermining of B.
H2a. A's envy of B is positively related to A's observational learning from B.
H2b. A's envy of B is positively related to A's advice seeking from B.
H3. CSE moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's undermining of B: the relationship is less positive when A has high CSE.
H4a. CSE moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's observational learning from B: the relationship is more positive when A has high CSE.
H4b. CSE moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's advice seeking from B: the relationship is more positive when A has high CSE.
H5. Friendship moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's undermining of B: the relationship is less positive when A and B are friends.
H6a. Friendship moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's observational learning from B: the relationship is more positive when A and B are friends.
H6b. Friendship moderates the relationship between A's envy of B and A's advice seeking from B: the relationship is more positive when A and B are friends.
H7a. The degree to which employees learn from observing envied targets is positively related to job performance.
H7b. The degree to which employees seek advice from envied targets is positively related to job performance.

Study 1 tests Hypotheses 1-6; Study 2 tests Hypotheses 2, 4, 6, and 7.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): A's envy of B.
- DV(s): A's social undermining of B, A's observational learning from B, A's advice seeking from B, and individual job performance.
- Mediators: None at the same level. Footnote 7 states that the model "does not depict mediation process at the same level"; the link to performance is cross-level, with each employee's dyadic-level envy–learning slope (observational learning and advice seeking) theorized to predict individual-level job performance.
- Moderators: Core self-evaluations and friendship between the envier and the envied target.

The mechanism is that envy signals an unfavorable status comparison and creates pressure to reduce the gap between the envier and the envied target. Employees may respond destructively by undermining the target, but they may also use the target as a source of performance-relevant information. Higher core self-evaluations and friendship make it more likely that envy is handled through advice seeking, and friendship also helps in Study 2 for observational learning. Advice seeking is the more robust learning channel and remains a stronger predictor of performance when observational learning is considered at the same time.

**Data & Measures**
Two two-wave round-robin surveys of intact work teams in South Korea, with all survey items prepared through Brislin's (1990) translation and back-translation procedure. The design is associational: the paper names no causal identification strategy, and the reported coefficients are survey-based associations estimated with social relations modeling and multilevel regression.

- Study 1 sample and procedure: sales employees at a large branch of a cosmetic company located in a metropolitan city of South Korea. At Time 1 all employees within the branch were contacted, 73 employees from 14 teams; 70 employees from 14 teams participated in the first survey (96% participation rate) and 58 employees from 11 teams completed the second (83% overall participation rate). Missing data on the study variables reduced the main analysis sample to 52 employees in 11 teams, producing 267 directed dyadic ratings. Average team size was 7.17 (SD = 3.14), average age 48.35 years (SD = 8.96), average organizational tenure 5.15 years (SD = 4.78), and all participants were women. Time 1 measured envy, CSEs, advice seeking, and friendship; Time 2, approximately a month later, measured observational learning and undermining.
- Study 1 measures: because a roster method was used, envy, observational learning, advice seeking, and undermining were each measured with a single item. Envy (T1) used Kim and Glomb's (2014) single-item scale, with four Schaubroeck and Lam (2004) items supplied as examples; advice seeking (T1) used a single item from Klein et al. (2004), "I asked this coworker for work-related advice"; observational learning (T2) was based on Van Kleef, Steinel, and Homan's (2013) scale; social undermining (T2) was based on Duffy et al.'s (2002) scale. CSEs (T1) used Judge, Erez, Bono, and Thoresen's (2003) 12-item scale (alpha = .77, seven-point response format). Friendship (T1) followed Zagenczyk et al. (2010), binary coded and counted only for reciprocated ties. A separate validation survey of U.S. full-time employees recruited via Mechanical Turk (600 distributed; n = 582 after removing attention-check failures) reported partial correlations between the single-item and full-item scales of .78 (envy), .57 (observational learning), .60 (advice seeking), and .69 (undermining), all p < .01, controlling for a marker variable.
- Study 2 sample and procedure: employees at branches of a bank and an insurance company in a South Korean metropolitan area. At Time 1 all employees working in the city were contacted, 229 employees from 30 teams (152 employees from 18 teams for the insurance company; 77 employees from 12 teams for the bank); 220 employees from 30 teams participated in the first survey (96% participation rate: 148 insurance employees from 18 teams, 97%; 72 bank employees from 12 teams, 94%), and all Time 1 participants completed the second survey. Missing data reduced the main analysis sample to 215 employees in 30 teams, producing 1,688 directed dyadic ratings. Average team size was 9.02 (SD = 3.71), average age 36.90 years (SD = 6.32), average organizational tenure 3.32 years (SD = 3.73), and about 50% of participants were women. Time 1 measured envy and CSEs; Time 2, approximately a month later, measured observational learning, advice seeking, and friendship, and supervisors rated job performance. Monthly sales performance was obtained from the insurance company for the three months following the Time 2 survey and was available for 134 employees.
- Study 2 measures: Study 2 used the original full items of each scale. Envy (T1) used Schaubroeck and Lam's (2004) four-item scale (alpha = .88); observational learning (T2) adapted Van Kleef et al.'s (2013) three-item scale (alpha = .98); advice seeking (T2) used Sparrowe, Liden, Wayne, and Kraimer's (2001) two-item scale (alpha = .97); friendship (T2) used the Zagenczyk et al. (2010) scale with only reciprocated ties coded as friendship. CSEs (T1) used the same 12-item scale as Study 1 (alpha = .88). Supervisor-rated job performance comprised task performance (Bono & Judge, 2003; three items, alpha = .99), affiliative performance (Tsai, Chen, & Liu, 2007; three items, alpha = .97), and proactive performance (Grant, Parker, & Collins, 2009; three items, alpha = .97). Archival sales performance was recorded in points that take into account factors such as monthly premiums and the number and recency of deals, and was z-standardized from original values. Organizational membership was entered as a control dummy (0 = insurance company, 1 = bank).
- Estimation: both studies used social relations modeling (Kenny, 1994; Snijders & Kenny, 1999) for the dyadic hypotheses, with envy centered around the person's mean and CSEs grand-mean centered. Because Hypothesis 7 involves individual-level relationships, the authors first extracted individual-specific envy-learning slopes as empirical Bayes parameter estimates from Model 1 of Tables 8 and 9, then regressed job performance on those slopes using multilevel regression models to account for team nestedness. Table 10 reports n = 215 individuals in 30 teams for task, affiliative, and proactive performance, and n = 134 individuals in 18 teams for sales performance.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 267 directed dyadic ratings from 52 employees in 11 teams) tested Hypotheses 1-6.

- H1 supported: envy was positively related to undermining (.19, p < .01).
- H2a not supported: envy was not significantly related to observational learning (.11, ns).
- H2b supported: envy was positively related to advice seeking (.39, p < .01).
- H3 supported: the envy by CSE interaction on undermining was significant and negative (-.24, p < .05); envy was positively related to undermining under low CSE (simple slope = .46, p < .01) but the relationship was not significant under high CSE (simple slope = .12, ns).
- H4a not supported: the envy by CSE interaction on observational learning was not significant (.05, ns).
- H4b supported: the envy by CSE interaction on advice seeking was significant and positive (.44, p < .01); envy was positively related to advice seeking under high CSE (simple slope = .46, p < .01) but not under low CSE (simple slope = -.15, ns).
- H5 not supported: envy did not significantly interact with friendship in predicting undermining (-.13, ns).
- H6a not supported: the envy by friendship interaction on observational learning was not significant (-.07, ns).
- H6b supported: the envy by friendship interaction on advice seeking was positive and significant (.29, p < .05); envy was more positively related to advice seeking when A and B were friends (simple slope = .42, p < .01), and the relationship was nonsignificant when they were not friends (simple slope = .15, ns).

Study 2 (n = 1,688 directed dyadic ratings from 215 employees in 30 teams) tested Hypotheses 2, 4, 6, and 7.

- H2a not supported: envy was not significantly related to observational learning (-.04, ns).
- H2b supported: envy was positively related to advice seeking (.08, p < .05).
- H4a not supported: the envy by CSE interaction on observational learning was significant and positive (.10, p < .01), but envy was not significantly related to observational learning under high CSE (simple slope = -.01, ns) and the relationship was negative under low CSE (simple slope = -.15, p < .01).
- H4b supported: the envy by CSE interaction on advice seeking was significant and positive (.13, p < .05); envy was positively related to advice seeking under high CSE (simple slope = .14, p < .05) and nonsignificant under low CSE (simple slope = -.05, ns).
- H6a supported: the envy by friendship interaction on observational learning was positive and significant (.26, p < .01); envy was positively related to observational learning when A and B were friends (simple slope = .18, p < .05) but the relationship was negative when they were not friends (simple slope = -.08, p < .05).
- H6b supported: the envy by friendship interaction on advice seeking was positive and significant (.20, p < .05); envy had a more positive relationship with advice seeking when A and B were friends (simple slope = .26, p < .05) and the relationship was nonsignificant when they were not friends (simple slope = .05, ns).
- H7a supported: the individual envy-observational learning slope was positively related to task performance (.93, p < .05) and affiliative performance (.87, p < .01), marginally related to proactive performance (.59, p < .10), and, for the insurance company, also predicted sales performance (.81, p < .05).
- H7b supported: the individual envy-advice seeking slope was positively related to task performance (1.66, p < .01), affiliative performance (1.21, p < .01), proactive performance (1.03, p < .05), and sales performance (.71, p < .05).

Cohen's f-squared values for the envy-observational learning slope ranged from .01 to .04 and those for the envy-advice seeking slope from .03 to .07, mostly indicating small effect sizes. When both learning slopes were entered in the same equation (Model 3 of Table 10), the envy-observational learning slope was not a significant predictor for any type of performance, while the envy-advice seeking slope remained a significant predictor for task performance (1.47, p < .01), affiliative performance (.99, p < .05), and proactive performance (.91, p < .05), although it was not significantly related to sales performance (.48, ns). Across the two studies, envy had a significant positive main effect on advice seeking but no significant main effect on observational learning; the authors note that CSE and friendship moderated the envy-observational learning link only in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes a functional model of workplace envy that treats envy as capable of both destructive and constructive transmutation. It extends workplace envy research by identifying peer-based learning, especially advice seeking from the envied target, as a mechanism through which envy can improve performance. It also refines social functional emotion theory by showing that personal and relational resources shape whether envy serves competitive and coordinating functions or turns into social undermining.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should not treat envy only as a toxic emotion to suppress. Managers can help employees convert upward comparisons into development by encouraging learning from high performers and by using appraisal conversations to identify relative weaknesses constructively. The findings also imply that building positive self-regard and workplace friendships can make advice seeking from envied colleagues more likely.

**Limitations**
The authors did not test whether learning and performance benefits eventually reduce envy, so the feedback loop from constructive action back to emotional experience remains unclear. Several non-performance constructs were self-reported, although the authors used temporal separation, supervisor ratings, archival sales data, and marker-variable checks to reduce common-method concerns. Study 1 also relied on single-item roster measures, and both studies were conducted in South Korea, which may limit generalizability to more individualistic cultural settings.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether successful learning from envied targets reduces envy over longer repeated episodes, or whether persistent comparison gaps shift envy toward admiration or other targets. Scholars could also test curvilinear envy effects, since very high envy may stop increasing constructive advice seeking or undermining. Further studies should examine whether the model generalizes across cultures, especially individualistic cultural settings.
