# Career Resourcing and the Process of Professional Emergence

**Abstract**
We theorize a career resourcing process that explains how individuals can create a new profession. Using historical archives, we trace the emergence of health services research as a new research profession through the career actions of early practitioners. We find that career resourcing can lead to the institutionalization of a new profession by (a) a process of accretion, wherein people pursuing fulfilling careers generate resources that contribute to institutionalization, or (b) institutional work to deliberately build the professional community and infrastructure. We contribute to research on institutional change by specifying career actions that can lead to the institutionalization of a new profession, and by developing theory that accounts for the motivations and the means of individuals to act in ways that result in the institutionalization of a new profession.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what process links individuals' career actions to the emergence of a new profession. Empirically, it examines how early health services researchers' job moves, research projects, mentoring, funding work, and institution-building actions helped create and institutionalize health services research.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / process elements: Career resourcing, career actions, assets, resources, personal career schemas, profession-building schemas, accretion, institutional work, professional community, professional infrastructure, and professional emergence.
- Career actions: Individuals moved into jobs, produced knowledge, mentored students and colleagues, obtained funding, created research centers, formed professional associations, and built tools or programs that later supported further career and profession-building actions.
- Assets / resources: Career actions generated stature, knowledge, material support, and community ties; these assets became resources when actors used them to enable subsequent actions for themselves and others.
- Schemas: Personal career schemas motivated actors to pursue interesting, fulfilling, or impactful work, while profession-building schemas oriented some later actions toward deliberately creating HSR infrastructure and community.
- Emergence pathways: Professional emergence occurred through accretion across individuals and generations, and through deliberate institutional work that built the professional community and infrastructure.

The process is recursive and temporal. Career actions generated assets that actors later converted into resources, and those resources enabled further career actions, including actions by other people. Over time, dispersed career-motivated actions accumulated into a professional community, body of knowledge, and infrastructure; in some cases, those accumulated resources also made deliberate profession-building institutional work possible.

**Data & Measures**
The data are archival and secondary sources on the history of HSR, assembled as an analytical case study. The core is a set of archived oral history interviews with 19 individuals (20 interviews) collected as part of the History of Health Services Research project by the National Library of Medicine and NICHSR, supplemented by an interview with John Wennberg published in Health Affairs and career history interviews with Gail Wilensky, Bruce Vladeck, and William Roper conducted as part of a separate oral history project on the then-Health Care Financing Administration; together these give a core data set of 24 interviews with 23 people who played leading roles in creating and institutionalizing HSR. Table 1 reports the full corpus: 24 oral career histories (23 people), 47 faculty bios, CVs, obituaries and tributes, 14 interviews (13 people) and a panel discussion on the creation of the AHSR, 17 first-hand accounts of HSR organizations, 13 Institute of Medicine reports, and 4 secondary histories of HSR organizations.

Constructs were coded inductively rather than measured. Both authors inductively coded all data sources that could shed light on the research question and generated first-order codes reflecting grounded, close-to-the-data actions and processes, alongside codes serving as indicators of institutionalization; both authors also independently created narrative summaries for the 23 people in the 24 career history interviews (Charmaz, 2006; Langley, 1999). Codes were then grouped into the data structure presented in Table 3: career actions (job moves, producing knowledge, mentoring), assets or potential resources (stature, knowledge assets, material assets, community assets), schemas (personal career, profession building), and professional emergence (professional community, professional infrastructure). Sensitized by prior models of resourcing, the authors returned to the narratives to identify how one career action enabled subsequent career actions, what schemas people drew on, and what linked resourcing to professional emergence. The design is interpretive and inductive and the paper claims no causal identification strategy; the authors state that their analysis involved informed judgment based on imperfect data, and they triangulate the retrospective interview accounts against university biographies, CVs, obituaries, Institute of Medicine panel rosters, and published first-hand accounts.

**Key Findings**
The study finds that individuals' career actions established a new profession over time through a career resourcing process in which career actions create assets that are used as resources to enable subsequent career actions, with shared schemas guiding how people mobilize and use those assets. Three forms of career action — job moves, knowledge production, and mentoring — generate four types of asset — stature, knowledge, material, and community. The process is temporal within an individual's career, social in that one person's career actions generate assets usable by others, and temporal across generations of scholars.

Career resourcing led to professional emergence by two routes. First, accretion, defined as the cumulative effect of diverse people's career actions on professional emergence, accumulated assets within individual careers, across individuals, and across generations, even though the underlying career actions were typically motivated by personal career schemas — for example, doing high-quality, interesting research; public service as part of a fulfilling career; and preferring jobs with prestigious employers — rather than by institution building. Second, deliberate institutional work, explicitly motivated and granted value by profession-building schemas, directly constituted elements of the profession: examples include Kerr White lobbying to rename the NIH study section to "Health Services Research" and invoking a schema of attracting new members, the creation of HSR training programs, departments, and centers, and the formation of the AHSR. The paper reports that career actions oriented toward profession building were almost always preceded and enabled by career actions guided primarily by personal-career oriented schemas earlier in individuals' careers, and concludes that both accretion and deliberate institutional work are essential to explaining how the new profession emerged.

Table 4 documents the outcome across seven decades: in the pre-emergence period of the 1940s there was no HSR label or identity, limited academic knowledge, limited data sets, no identifiable group of researchers doing HSR, no dedicated departments, training programs, or professional association, no dedicated funding, and only ad hoc use of HSR in the federal government. By the institutionalized period of the late 1990s onward there was a recognized HSR identity, key texts and paradigms, core methods and major data sets, dedicated HSR journals, academic departments, centers, and training programs at many major universities, the AHSR founded in 1981 as a professional association, dedicated funding through bodies including the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, the National Institute of Mental Health, the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation, and the Commonwealth Fund, and HSR units in insurers, hospital systems, federal agencies, and state-level governments.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional change and professional emergence theory by explaining a bottom-up process through which individual careers can shape institutions. It extends institutional work theory by showing that institutional consequences can arise not only from purposive institution-building but also from dispersed, personally motivated career actions that accumulate over time. It also brings resourcing theory into a multilevel institutional setting, showing how assets become resources across careers, generations, and professional fields.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that new professions can be built by supporting career pathways, research infrastructure, mentoring relationships, funding opportunities, and jobs that allow dispersed actors to generate and reuse resources. For actors trying to develop an emerging profession, the findings suggest that personal career moves and institution-building actions are not separate domains: career development can gradually create the stature, knowledge, material support, and community needed for professional infrastructure.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the retrospective interviews at the heart of the data create limitations because respondents may imperfectly recall their career actions and may present themselves as more heroic or coherent than they were at the time.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for research on how career resourcing interacts with other professional-emergence mechanisms, including ethos, identity, discourse, work practices, and institutional work. It also suggests examining the coevolution of new professions, careers, jobs, and work in organizations, and comparing career resourcing processes across existing professions, different institutional events, and complex multilevel systems.
