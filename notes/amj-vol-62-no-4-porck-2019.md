# Social Identification in Multiteam Systems: The Role of Depletion and Task Complexity

**Abstract**
Organizations construct multiteam systems to address complex challenges that require the joint efforts of multiple teams. Taking an uncertainty perspective and integrating social identity theory with depletion research, we theoretically and empirically examine the role of social identification in multiteam system performance. In contrast to general assumptions in the literature regarding the need to develop identity at the highest level of a system, we argue that within a multiteam system, identification with that system negatively relates to multiteam system performance, whereas identification with the component team positively relates to multiteam system performance. Our uncertainty perspective suggests that identification with the multiteam system introduces uncertainty regarding the appropriate norms and interdependencies in the system, which leads to more depletion, and consequently lower system performance. Conversely, identification with the component team offers less uncertainty, resulting in less depletion and higher multiteam system performance. Thus, our integrated theoretical framework suggests that depletion mediates the negative effects of multiteam system identification and the positive effects of component team identification on multiteam system performance. Moreover, consistent with our uncertainty perspective, the indirect effect of identification on multiteam system performance via depletion is stronger when task complexity is high and weaker when task complexity is low.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how identification with a component team versus identification with the larger multiteam system affects multiteam system performance. It further asks whether depletion mediates those effects and whether task complexity strengthens the indirect relationships.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1a. There is a negative relationship between multiteam system identification and multiteam system performance.
H1b. There is a positive relationship between component team identification and multiteam system performance.
H2a. Depletion mediates the negative relationship between multiteam system identification and multiteam system performance.
H2b. Depletion mediates the positive relationship between component team identification and multiteam system performance.
H3a. Task complexity moderates the negative relationship between multiteam system identification and multiteam system performance, such that the negative total effect of multiteam system identification on multiteam system performance is stronger when the task is more complex and weaker when the task is less complex.
H3b. Task complexity moderates the positive relationship between component team identification and multiteam system performance, such that the positive total effect of team identification on multiteam system performance is stronger when the task is more complex and weaker when the task is less complex.
H4a. Task complexity moderates the mediated relationship between multiteam system identification and multiteam system performance, such that the negative indirect effect of multiteam system identification on multiteam system performance via depletion is stronger when the task is more complex and weaker when the task is less complex.
H4b. Task complexity moderates the mediated relationship between component team identification and multiteam system performance, such that the positive indirect effect of team identification on multiteam system performance via depletion is stronger when the task is more complex and weaker when the task is less complex.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Multiteam system identification and component team identification.
- DV(s): Multiteam system performance, measured as standardized points earned in the 10-round Leadership Development Exercise simulation.
- Mediators: Post-session depletion, controlling for pre-session depletion.
- Moderators: Task complexity, manipulated through low-complexity and high-complexity simulation grids.

The proposed mechanism is that multiteam system identification directs members toward an abstract higher-order identity that increases uncertainty about norms, interdependencies, and appropriate action, thereby depleting cognitive resources and reducing performance. Component team identification instead gives members a more concrete identity prototype tied to specialized team tasks, reducing uncertainty and depletion. Task complexity intensifies both pathways because complex multiteam-system tasks make information cues, interdependence, and environmental change harder to manage.

**Data & Measures**
Laboratory multiteam-system simulation data from 289 undergraduate students attending a large university in the United States, recruited from a select business-college program in which they took a teamwork class and competed in team-based computer simulations. 155 students participated in program year one and 134 in year two; mean participant age was 18.08 years (SD = .37) and 49.1% of the sample was male. Teams first trained and played two simulations as stand-alone teams over two months. The 289 participants were nested in 66 four- to five-person component teams, which were nested in 22 multiteam systems; each of the 22 multiteam systems (level 2 observations) played three 10-round Leadership Development Exercise (LDX) simulations over approximately four months, producing 66 performance episodes (level 1 observations). Each multiteam system comprised a Support Team (intelligence), a Point Team (operations), and a Leadership Team, with roles assigned by relative performance during training and held constant across the study and across conditions.

- DV: Multiteam system performance, measured as the total number of points earned during the 10-round simulation and standardized within each of the three performance episodes.
- IVs: Component team identification and multiteam system identification, measured with a modified 8-item version of the Identification with Group Scale (Roccas, Sagiv, Schwartz, Halevy, & Eidelson, 2008) covering importance of and commitment to the component team or multiteam system, on a five-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Coefficient alpha was .94 for component team identification and .95 for multiteam system identification. Responses were averaged to the multiteam system level; for team identification, members' scores were first aggregated to the team level and then averaged across the three component teams.
- Mediator: Depletion, measured with Johnson, Lanaj, and Barnes' (2014) five-item scale before each 10-round session (pre-session) and at the end of each session, on a five-point scale from 1 = very slightly or not at all to 5 = very much. Coefficient alpha was .95 for pre-session and .97 for end-of-session depletion, and responses were averaged to the multiteam system level. Pre-session depletion is controlled in the depletion model to assess change in depletion over the performance episode and to assuage concerns over causal order.
- Moderator: Task complexity, manipulated by changing the game grid in the third and final simulation: two low-complexity grids were used in the first two sessions and a high-complexity grid in the third, created by adding 22 unknown enemy targets from four new unknown enemy target categories. Because the authors wanted objective rather than perceived complexity, four LDX experts rated the three grids on Vashdi, Bamberger, and Erez's (2013) three-item measure of component, coordinative, and dynamic complexity (alpha = .98). Mean complexity was 2.50 (SD = 1.00) and 2.75 (SD = 1.00) for the two low-complexity grids and 4.75 (SD = .50) for the high-complexity grid, with paired-samples t-tests confirming the manipulation.
- Control: The program year (level 2), included because recruitment procedures varied slightly from year to year.

Aggregation of identification and depletion to the multiteam system level was justified with ICC(1), ICC(2), and median rwg(j) statistics, and a multilevel confirmatory factor analysis (run at item level and again with item parcels) showed component team identification and multiteam system identification to be distinguishable constructs. Hypotheses were tested with multilevel path analysis in Mplus 7, clustering by multiteam system, centering level 1 variables on the sample's mean, and including the direct effect when modeling mediation. Moderated mediation followed Preacher, Zyphur, and Zhang (2010), with bias-corrected confidence intervals for the conditional indirect effects constructed from a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 replications. Only task complexity is experimentally manipulated, and it is manipulated within subjects so that each multiteam system serves as its own control on that variable; identification and depletion are aggregated survey measures, and the paper claims no causal identification strategy for the identification-to-performance relationships. A footnote reports a PinT power analysis giving average power of .74 (range = .51-.91) for the effects examined, which the authors describe as near the typical recommended level of .80.

**Key Findings**
- H1a received partial support. Multiteam system identification was negatively associated with multiteam system performance, but only at a marginal level of statistical significance (γ = -1.25, p < .10), which the paper reports as partial support for Hypothesis 1a.
- H1b supported. Component team identification was positively associated with multiteam system performance (γ = 1.60, p < .05).
- H2a supported. At average task complexity, multiteam system identification was positively associated with depletion (γ = .53, p < .05) and depletion was negatively associated with multiteam system performance (γ = -.87, p < .05); the indirect effect was -.47, 95% CI [-.95, -.18], which did not include zero.
- H2b supported. Component team identification was negatively associated with depletion (γ = -.50, p < .05), and the indirect effect on performance via depletion was .44, 95% CI [.16, .88], which did not include zero.
- H3a supported. Task complexity moderated the negative relationship between multiteam system identification and multiteam system performance (γ = -6.11, p < .05): the negative effect was stronger when the task was more complex (simple slope = -5.32, p < .05) and weaker, and not significant, when the task was less complex (simple slope = .78, n.s.).
- H3b supported. Task complexity moderated the positive relationship between team identification and multiteam system performance (γ = 6.65, p < .05): the positive effect was stronger when the task was more complex (simple slope = 6.03, p < .05) and weaker, and not significant, when the task was less complex. The baseline path model explained 21.0% of the level 1 variance in multiteam system performance.
- H4a supported. Task complexity moderated the multiteam system identification to depletion path (γ = 1.17, p < .05), depletion remained negatively associated with performance (γ = -.87, p < .05), and the 95% CI for the index of moderated mediation excluded zero (index = -1.02, 95% CI [-2.46, -.21]). The negative indirect effect held under high complexity (the results text reports -1.15 and Table 2 reports -1.14, both with 95% CI [-2.39, -.38]) but not under low complexity (-.13, 95% CI [-.51, .19]).
- H4b supported. Task complexity moderated the team identification to depletion path (γ = -1.58, p < .05), and the 95% CI for the index of moderated mediation excluded zero (index = 1.38, 95% CI [.31, 3.19]). The positive indirect effect was 1.36, 95% CI [.39, 2.78] under high complexity and -.02, 95% CI [-.54, .25] under low complexity. The moderated mediation model explained 55.1% of the level 1 variance in depletion and 41.1% of the level 1 variance in multiteam system performance.

A supplemental analysis prompted by a reviewer tested relative (component team to multiteam system) identification through the slope of the incongruence line of a response surface. Relative identification was positively related to multiteam system performance (incongruence line slope = 2.85, p < .05) and negatively related to depletion (incongruence line slope = -1.04, p < .05), and the 95% CI for the indirect effect via depletion excluded zero (indirect effect = .91, 95% CI [.37, 1.81]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the assumption that higher-order system identification should always improve performance in multiteam systems. It extends social identity theory by showing that nested identities can have opposite performance implications when the higher-order target is abstract and uncertainty-inducing. It also links multiteam-system theory with depletion and task-complexity research by specifying depletion as the resource mechanism connecting identification to system performance.

**Practical Implication**
Managers of complex multiteam systems should not assume that cultivating identification with the largest collective is always beneficial. When task complexity is high, the paper suggests that performance may improve when members identify strongly with their component teams and trust leadership teams to coordinate the broader system. Leaders should reduce uncertainty by clarifying component-team roles, interdependencies, and the system-level coordination picture.

**Limitations**
The study uses undergraduate students in a laboratory simulation, so generalizability to experienced employees and field multiteam systems remains uncertain. The level-1 sample of 66 performance episodes is relatively small, although the authors note that this is sizable for multiteam-system research and use a within-subject design to improve precision. The laboratory task offers psychological realism, but the authors note that how its goal hierarchy maps to real-world goal hierarchies is debatable.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether the findings generalize to field settings, older and more experienced working adults, larger samples, and multiteam systems with different levels of task complexity. Additional studies could examine how different component-team roles, especially boundary-spanning leadership teams, alter the costs and benefits of social identification. The paper also points to future work on how multiple identities combine, conflict, overlap, or shift over time in multiteam systems.
