# Holier than Thou? Identity Buffers and Adoption of Controversial Practices in the Islamic Banking Category

**Abstract**
Existing scholarship on categories has frequently highlighted how some category members violate codes that others diligently abide by. In this paper, we consider differences in identity across category members, and ask how these relative differences determine their response to a code-violating change. Taking a case where category members are identified as “insiders” and “outsiders,” we argue that insiders’ reaction to a code violation depends upon the extent to which they believe their identity is distinct from the code violator’s, who might be an insider or an outsider. We suggest that it is the presence or absence of an “identity buffer”—i.e., a relative identity advantage—that determines insiders’ reaction. We hypothesize that when a fellow category insider introduces a code violation, the focal insider will be more likely to refrain from the practice. When an outsider introduces the violation, insiders will be more likely to adopt the violation provided they can retain an identity buffer. We posit that when outsiders adopt code-preserving behavior, thus narrowing the identity buffer between insiders and outsiders, it will mitigate insiders’ likelihood of code violation adoption. We find support for our hypotheses using data on the Islamic banking industry in 12 countries (2003–2014).

**Research Question**
The paper asks how the relative difference between a code violator's identity and an insider's identity affects the focal insider's decision to adopt a new code-violating practice. Empirically, it asks when full-fledged Islamic banks adopt derivatives, and whether that likelihood differs when derivatives are first adopted by peer insiders versus CB-owned outsider banks.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Insider-initiated code violation is negatively associated with the likelihood of a focal insider to violate the same code.
- H2: Outsider-initiated code violation is positively associated with the likelihood of a focal insider also violating the same code.
- H3: When an outsider violates one code while preserving another, the positive relationship between outsider-initiated code violation and the likelihood of focal insider code violation will be weakened.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Peer insider derivative use; outsider derivative use.
- DV(s): Focal full-fledged Islamic bank derivative use.
- Mediators: Not modeled as formal mediators.
- Moderators: Outsider zakat payment as outsider code-preserving behavior that narrows the identity buffer.

The mechanism is an identity-buffer process. When a peer insider violates a code, other insiders avoid imitation because endorsing a fellow insider's deviance threatens the collective insider identity. When an outsider violates a code, insiders can adopt the profitable controversial practice while retaining their relative identity advantage over outsiders. When outsiders simultaneously preserve another salient code through zakat payment, that advantage narrows, so insiders become less willing to follow the outsider's code violation.

**Data & Measures**
The full data set consists of 143 Islamic banks in 23 countries; of the 143, 101 are full-fledged Islamic banks and 42 are CB owned. The main analyses were run only on countries including at least one full-fledged Islamic bank (insider) and one CB-owned Islamic bank (outsider), a subsample of 66 full-fledged Islamic banks and 42 CB-owned Islamic banks in 12 countries. The main data sources were annual reports, websites, and the Bankscope database. The dependent variable is a full-fledged Islamic bank's likelihood of using derivatives, a dummy variable equal to 1 if the bank adopts derivatives and 0 if the bank does not; the paper reports that only 12.8% of derivative usage among full-fledged Islamic banks was observed out of the total observations. The first independent variable, Peer derivative use, was built by first coding whether a full-fledged Islamic bank i used derivatives at time t and then counting full-fledged Islamic banks using derivatives in country c at time t. The second independent variable, Outsider derivative use, was built the same way from whether a CB-owned Islamic bank i used derivatives at time t, then counting CB-owned Islamic banks using derivatives in country c at time t. The moderator, Outsider zakat payment, follows the same steps for code-preserving behavior: whether a CB-owned Islamic bank i paid zakat at time t, then the count of CB-owned Islamic banks paying zakat in country c at time t. Use of derivative products is taken as the code violation and giving of zakat as the code preservation. Controls include performance differences between the focal bank and its competitors, with competitors categorized between insiders and outsiders, yielding Below outsider performance, Above outsider performance, Below insider performance, and Above insider performance; return on assets is the main performance variable, and the difference is computed by subtracting the competitor's average performance from focal firm performance, lagged by one year. Further controls are Focal insider public listing, Focal insider payment of zakat, Insider zakat payment (the count of insiders that paid zakat), Profitability as standardized net income, Size as the natural log of assets, Product count change as a proxy for how innovative the bank was in new product development, Percentage of conventional financial institution ownership, Percentage of Islamic ownership, global ultimate ownership dummies for a government or private entity, Total outsider banks and Total insider banks in country, Muslim percentage of population, GDP growth, and Bank density (total commercial bank branches in a country divided by 100,000 adults, from The World Bank database), plus year and country fixed effects to account for general macroeconomic conditions and country-specific regulation. Estimation uses a random-effects logit model with "Islamic bank" as the cross-sectional unit and "year" as the temporal unit; the authors state that a fixed-effects approach would have been inappropriate because their predictors had very low within variance, which meant that derivative adoption for more than half of the full-fledged Islamic banks did not vary over time. Standard errors are clustered-robust by bank group (banks in different countries under single common ownership), one level above the unit of analysis. Multicollinearity was checked with the Belsley, Kuh, and Welsch regression collinearity diagnostic (a score of 23.10, below the acceptable threshold of 30) and variance inflation factors (average VIF of 7.93, below the standard cutoff of 10). All independent and moderator variables were lagged by one year, with data collected from 2003 to construct lagged independent and control variables for the first year of the observed dependent variable (2004); the authors describe this lagging as intended to enhance causal inference, but the design is an observational panel and no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy is claimed. Robustness analyses introduce additional measures: the Mundlak (1978) procedure, which estimates random-effects models by adding entity means of variables; an alternative proportion measure of Outsider zakat payment (the count of outsiders paying zakat in country c in year t divided by the total count of outsiders in country c in year t), whose correlation with Outsider derivative use is 0.26 against 0.71 for the count measure; a rare-events logit specification for a subsample of insider-only countries in which 35 Islamic banks were spread across 11 countries between 2003-2014 and derivative adoption occurs in only 6.01% of observations; Derivatives legitimacy, measured from the Factiva media dataset by searching the keywords "derivative" or "derivatives" within the Islamic banking industry, which returned 729 articles between 2007 and 2014 (the authors note they were not able to gather articles for all the countries and all the years), coding article tone by country-year with 93.75% judgment matching between two coders on a random sample of 80 articles, and taking the proportion of positive and neutral articles; and Bank status, measured as the summed normalized Bonacich centrality scores of a bank's sharia supervisory board members per bank year, computed in UCINET from a per-year symmetrical matrix of all 380 scholars in the data set. The qualitative component comprises 21 open-ended interviews with Islamic bankers, lawyers, regulators, and scholars, together with books, newspaper articles, industry magazines, and bank filings; Table 2 presents representative interview quotes illustrating aspects of the industry, and this material is used to establish that derivative use is treated as code-violating and zakat payment as code-preserving in this category.

**Key Findings**
Table 5 reports the random-effects logit models. Model 1 is a base model with controls only. Model 2 adds Peer insider derivative use, which the authors find to be negative and significant (b = -1.95, p = 0.03); they therefore find support for Hypothesis 1, which predicts a negative relationship between peer insider-initiated code violation and the likelihood of the focal insider to code violate. Model 3 adds Outsider derivative use (b = 2.04, p = 0.07), which the authors report as supporting Hypothesis 2; Table 5 marks this coefficient at the p < 0.1 level, and the authors note that the marginal effects of insiders and outsiders on the baseline probability are similar in magnitude. Model 4 tests Hypothesis 3 through the interaction of Outsider derivative use with Outsider zakat payment; the interaction is negative and significant (b = -0.38, p < 0.01), lending support to Hypothesis 3, and Figure 5 shows that as more outsiders pay zakat it would take more outsiders using derivatives for a focal insider to follow suit. All four models in Table 5 are estimated on 536 bank-year observations covering 66 banks.

Robustness checks leave the pattern unchanged. Model 5 applies the Mundlak procedure and the authors report obtaining the same results (536 bank-year observations, 66 banks; the interaction of Outsider derivative use with Outsider zakat payment is -0.17, p < 0.01), and Models 1-3 rerun with the Mundlak procedure in models not shown are also confirmed unchanged. Substituting the proportion measure of Outsider zakat payment for the count measure also produced the same results in models not shown. In insider-only countries (Model 6, a rare-events logit on 367 bank-year observations covering 35 banks), Peer insider derivative use remains negative and significant (b = -3.21, p = 0.03). Controlling for Derivatives legitimacy (Model 7, 309 bank-year observations, 58 banks) leaves the main results holding, with Derivatives legitimacy itself positive but insignificant (b = 0.04, p = 0.99). Controlling for Bank status (Model 8, 405 bank-year observations, 59 banks) also leaves the main results holding, with a positive but insignificant effect of a bank's status (b = 0.01, p = 0.84); the authors report that they were unable to gather complete information on all banks' sharia supervisory board members for all the bank years in the sample, especially for the earlier years (2003-2006), which is why status was not controlled for in the main models and why the number of observations in Model 8 is lower. Two supplementary analyses address whether identity buffers also exist among insider peers. A test on the count of the word "zakat" in all Islamic banks' annual reports in 2013-2014 finds a mean of 7.15 mentions per annual report for full-fledged Islamic banks against 14.8 for CB-owned Islamic banks, a statistically significant difference (p = 0.000) that the authors read as indicating insiders are less likely to parade their code-preserving efforts compared to outsiders. Adding to Model 2 the interaction of Focal insider zakat payment with Insider derivative use (unreported model) yields no significance of the interaction, which the authors take as support for their argument that payment of zakat does not redeem insiders when they code violate.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to organizational category research by showing that insiders' responses to code violation depend on relative identity differences between the violator and potential adopter, not simply on whether a practice violates a category code. It develops the concept of an identity buffer to explain why outsider-initiated violations can diffuse to insiders while insider-initiated violations are resisted. It also extends social identity theory by showing that in-group loyalty can make insiders stricter toward in-group deviants rather than uniformly favoring them.

**Practical Implication**
For organizations in identity-sensitive categories, controversial practice adoption depends on who introduces the practice and whether identity differences remain visible to audiences. Incumbents may have more room to copy outsider practices when their insider identity remains distinct, but that room narrows when outsiders credibly display code-preserving behavior. Outsider entrants can therefore shape insider behavior not only by violating codes but also by selectively preserving other salient codes.

**Limitations**
The authors note that their measures of code violation and code preservation are binary, even though identity and code adoption are multidimensional. They were also unable to fully examine how outsiders' social position in their original conventional banking category shaped their entry into Islamic banking. A further boundary condition is that derivatives are profitable financial products, so the findings may differ for code violations whose economic payoff is neutral or uncertain.

**Future Research**
Future work could measure gradations in code violation and preservation, including how a firm's grade of category membership affects the type and extent of code violation it can undertake. Researchers could also examine how outsiders' prior-category identities shape whether they enter a new category as code violators, code preservers, or both. Another avenue is to study code violations with uncertain economic value, such as emerging technological standards, to see whether identity-buffer dynamics operate similarly when adoption is risky rather than clearly profitable.
