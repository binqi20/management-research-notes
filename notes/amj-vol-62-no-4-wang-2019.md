# Defending Mao’s Dream: How Politicians’ Ideological Imprinting Affects Firms’ Political Appointment in China

**Abstract**
Prior studies on corporate political strategies have taken an exchange view to examine these strategies’ benefits and costs for firms and politicians, but have paid less attention to how politicians’ political values shape their perceptions of, and willingness to engage in, these exchanges. We investigate how politicians’ imprinted political ideologies affect the likelihood of firms’ political appointments. Examining 760 city mayors across 242 Chinese cities from 2001 to 2013, we find that cities have fewer private firms appointed to local councils if the mayor—the key decision maker for such appointments—is more strongly imprinted with an orthodox communist ideology that opposes capitalism. The intensity and evolution of such an ideological imprint are influenced by contextual factors. The imprint’s strength is shaped by the mayors’ prior exposure to intense ideological experiences, such as experiencing the Cultural Revolution at a young age. Working in an environment consistent with the ideology (e.g., a province with a greater communist legacy) sustains and even strengthens the imprint, whereas working in an environment inconsistent with the ideology (e.g., a city with greater economic development) attenuates it. We discuss the implications of these findings for political strategy research, imprinting theory, and nascent research on political ideology.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how city mayors' imprinted political ideologies affect the likelihood that private firms in their jurisdictions receive political appointments in China. It further examines when that ideological imprint is intensified by prior Cultural Revolution exposure and when it persists or decays depending on communist legacy and economic development in the mayor's working environment.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. If the focal city mayor has been imprinted with a stronger communist ideology, fewer private firms headquartered in that city are appointed to political councils.
Hypothesis 2. The negative effect of a stronger communist ideology on private firms' political appointment is strengthened if the focal city mayor experienced the Cultural Revolution at a young age.
Hypothesis 3. The negative effect of a stronger communist ideology on private firms' political appointment is strengthened if the focal province has a stronger communist legacy.
Hypothesis 4. The negative effect of a stronger communist ideology on private firms' political appointment is reduced if the focal city has a higher economic development level.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Mayor's communist ideology, coded by whether the mayor joined the Communist Party before 1978.
- DV(s): Ratio of political appointments held by private firms, measured from executives' deputyship in the PC or PPCC at the city level or above.
- Mediators: Not directly modeled as measured mediators; the theory specifies ideological values, anti-capitalist perceptions, and party identity as the process logic.
- Moderators: Mayor's Cultural Revolution cohort, regional communist legacy, and city GDP.

The mechanism is that joining the Communist Party during the Mao era imprints mayors with orthodox communist ideology and party identity that make private entrepreneurs appear less legitimate as political insiders. Mayors with stronger imprints therefore endorse fewer private-firm executives for PC or PPCC deputyship. The imprint is stronger when mayors experienced the Cultural Revolution at a young age, persists or strengthens in provinces with stronger communist legacies, and attenuates in economically developed cities where market-oriented demands make the old ideology less compatible with current governance tasks.

**Data & Measures**
The dataset is built from multiple sources. The politician sample consists of all city mayors in China who served in the position from 2001 to 2013; the authors manually collected the CVs of 760 mayors from 242 cities that have fully available data on all variables of interest, yielding 2,402 city-year observations. Each city-year observation was matched with one incumbent mayor and his or her personal data; where mayoral turnover occurred within a year, the observation reflects the mayor who served for more than six months in that city-year. The paper reports that the average mayor age was 57, the average tenure in the mayor position was 30 months, mayors were predominantly male, and 40% joined the communist party prior to 1978. City-level institutional and economic development data come from the China City Statistical Yearbook published annually by the National Statistics Bureau of China. The firm-level sample covered all publicly listed firms that were privately held on the Shenzhen and Shanghai Stock Exchanges from 2001 to 2013, with 2001 chosen because the data quality of Chinese public firms improved significantly after that year. Firm-level political appointment was manually coded from companies' annual reports; other firm-level information (tax contribution, government procurement, existing political resources) was obtained from the WIND database. Because the analyses are conducted at the city-year level, firm data were aggregated to the city level based on where the firms were headquartered.

- DV: Political appointment, identified as having a top executive serve as a deputy in the People's Congress (PC) or the People's Political Consultative Conference (PPCC) at the city level or above in a given year. It is coded at the firm level as a continuous variable considering both the CEO's and the chairman's service, ranging from 0-2 for each firm; the total number of deputyships obtained by all private firms in the focal city in a given year is then summed and divided by the total quota of deputyships in the focal city. The average total quota of PC and PPCC deputyships for the sampled cities is 892; where cities do not disclose current or prior deputy numbers the authors either estimated the number based on cities of similar size or used the current value. The ratio of private firms' political appointment varies from 0 to 6% of total deputyships, with an average of 0.12%. Of the sample firms, 15.9% reported having such a deputyship; at the city level the average number of private listed firms is 38 and the average number of deputyships held by private firms is 1.07.
- IV: Mayor's communist ideology, coded 1 if the focal mayor joined the party before 1978 and 0 if the focal mayor joined in the economic reform era after 1978.
- Moderators: Cultural Revolution cohort, coded 1 if the focal mayor was born between 1950 and 1959 (7 to 16 years old when the movement started) and 0 otherwise. Regional communist legacy, measured by the timing of each province's liberation by the People's Liberation Army and coded as the number of months liberated before (or after) the founding date of the PRC in October 1949, ranging from 23 months before the founding to 19 months after. Economic development level, measured by the focal city's GDP in a given year.
- Controls: mayor's local tenure (years the mayor had worked in any position in the same city prior to the current mayoral tenure), mayor's education (1 if postgraduate degree or above), mayor's promotion (1 if the mayor had a promotion), and mayor's gender (1 for female); the aggregate tax contribution by private firms in the focal city; how many private firms in the focal city have the local government as a top customer; private firms' existing political resources (how many private firms in the focal city have party members as top executives); and year dummies.

Because the dependent variable is continuous, the hypotheses were tested with ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions, employing city fixed effects (Stata code xtreg) to take account of time-invariant city heterogeneity and to examine within-city variation originating from mayoral turnover, together with year dummies. The paper claims no causal identification for these main models; they are associational panel estimates. To address the alternative explanation that politicians' stronger ideological inclination reflects self-selection into the party rather than imprinting, the authors use an instrumental-variable approach: a first-stage selection model predicts a mayor's likelihood of joining the party, and its predicted residuals proxy for communist ideology in the second stage. The two instruments are the number of party members in the birth province of the focal mayor in the year 1952 and the GDP volume of the focal mayor's birth province in 1952. Post-estimation tests comprise a Bayesian under-identification test, a weak identification test reporting a Kleibergen-Paap Wald F-statistic of 21.58, and an over-identification test with a p-value of 0.58.

Further robustness models add the provincial party secretary's age, repeat the analyses using the city party secretary's characteristics (n = 2,465), aggregate the data from mayor-year to mayor-tenure and average the ratio across each mayor's entire tenure (n = 814), and run firm-year tests with firm fixed effects and cluster-robust standard errors on 7,670 observations while adding CEO age, tenure, gender, and compensation. Additional checks use the total number of deputyships instead of the ratio as the dependent variable while controlling for either city population or the total number of firms, recode the ratio as a categorical 0/1/2 variable estimated with a multinomial logit model, winsorize the dependent variable, code communist ideology as 1 if mayors joined the party during the Cultural Revolution period (1966-1976), and define the Cultural Revolution cohort as people born between 1948 and 1957.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported. In Model 2 of Table 2 the effect of communist ideology is negative and significant; the results text reports p < .01, while Table 2 shows the coefficient as -0.20 marked with a single asterisk (p < .05). With other conditions equal and held at their mean, having a mayor with a strong communist ideology reduces the ratio of private firms' political appointments by 0.20% compared with firms headquartered in cities whose mayors are not imprinted with the ideology. The paper states that Hypothesis 1 receives strong empirical support.
- Hypothesis 2 supported. In Model 3 of Table 2 the coefficient for the interaction between communist ideology and the mayor's Cultural Revolution cohort is negative and significant (-0.23, p < .01). Communist ideology reduces the ratio of private firms' political appointments by 0.40% for mayors born between 1950 and 1959, whereas the reduction is only 0.17% for those not born during that period.
- Hypothesis 3 supported. In Model 4 the interaction between communist ideology and regional communist legacy is negative and significant (-0.19, p < .01). In regions with a stronger communist legacy, communist ideology reduces the ratio of private firms' political appointments by 0.34%, in contrast with 0.15% for regions with a weaker communist legacy.
- Hypothesis 4 supported. In Model 5 the coefficient for the interaction between communist ideology and the focal city's GDP is positive and significant (0.03, p < .01). In regions with above-mean-level GDP the communist ideology reduces private firms' political appointments by 0.14%, while in regions with below-mean-level GDP the reduction is 0.17%.
- Model 6 presents the full model, with all results remaining the same.
- Selection check (Table 3). Model 1 shows that the two instruments are not related to the dependent variable, which the authors read as meeting the exclusion restrictions. In the first stage (Model 2), the number of party members in 1952 positively relates to a mayor's joining the party during Mao's era, while the GDP of the birth province in 1952 negatively relates. Models 3 to 6 show that the main results remain the same after considering the selection issue using the first-stage residuals.
- Other robustness checks (Table 4). The effect of the provincial party secretary's age is negative but not significant, and all interactions maintain their effects after controlling for higher-level government considerations. The authors find no evidence that the city party secretary's political ideology affects private firms' political appointment. The mayor-tenure analysis with a reduced sample of 814 leaves all results substantially the same, and the firm-level tests on 7,670 observations are largely consistent with the city-level analyses. Using the total number of deputyships instead of the ratio still holds; the multinomial logit on the recoded categorical ratio is robust; winsorizing the dependent variable does not change the results; coding communist ideology by joining during 1966-1976 yields a stronger effect; and measuring the Cultural Revolution cohort as those born between 1948 and 1957 leaves results substantively the same.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to corporate political strategy research by shifting attention from firms' demand for political ties to politicians' receptivity to those ties. It shows that politicians' ideological values are antecedents of firms' political participation, not merely background context for instrumental exchange.

It also contributes to individual-level imprinting theory by showing that imprint strength and persistence vary with prior ideological experiences and later imprint-environment fit. The paper extends political ideology research beyond the U.S. liberal-conservative framing by examining Chinese communist ideology and party affiliation as both a value system and an in-group identity.

**Practical Implication**
For firms, political appointment depends not only on their own resources, contribution, or willingness to pursue political ties, but also on the ideological background of local political decision makers. Private firms operating in authoritarian or transitional settings may face different political-access prospects across jurisdictions because officials interpret the legitimacy of business participation differently.

For policymakers and analysts, the findings imply that state-firm interactions should be evaluated through the attributes of state agents, not only through formal institutions or aggregate state interests. The same political appointment channel can carry different consequences depending on the decision maker's ideological imprint.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the study remains theoretically silent on firm-level dynamics, even though supplementary analyses suggest firms benefit less from appointments when mayors have strong communist ideology. They also note that the paper does not theorize interaction patterns between mayors and party secretaries in China's dual power structure.

The study's China setting is powerful for examining communist ideological imprinting, but the authors caution that its institutional arrangements limit generalizability. The communist ideology imprint is most applicable to transitional economies such as those in Eastern Europe, Russia, and Asia.

**Future Research**
Future research could theorize how firms respond strategically to politicians' biased attitudes and how different types of firms are affected by politicians' ideology. Further work could examine power sharing between mayors and party secretaries and how leader experience or discretion changes firms' political participation.

The authors also encourage research on how city-level economic and institutional conditions shape mayor turnover and the antecedents of mayor ideology. Comparative work could examine other political ideologies and how they affect firms' strategies in different institutional contexts.
