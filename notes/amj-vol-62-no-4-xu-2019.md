# Deviant versus Aspirational Risk Taking: The Effects of Performance Feedback on Bribery Expenditure and R&D Intensity

**Abstract**
Combining the theses of “problemistic search” and “slack search,” past research in the behavioral theory of the firm suggests that both lowand high-performing firms may engage in the same type of risk-taking activity. We counter this view with a consistent, motivation-based logic in the theory: low-performing firms are fixated on finding shortterm solutions to immediate problems, so they have an increased probability of exhibiting deviant risk-taking behavior such as bribery, whereas high-performing firms are concerned about sustaining their competitive advantage in the long run and will more likely engage in aspirational risk taking such as research and development (R&D). Using a sample of 9,633 firm-year observations covering 2,224 listed companies in China, we find that, as a firm’s performance falls further below its aspiration level, it has larger abnormal entertainment spending, an implicit measure of bribery expenditure, but not higher R&D intensity. However, as a firm’s performance rises further above its aspiration level, it has greater R&D intensity, but not more bribery expenses. Legal development and industry competition moderate the relationship between performance feedback and risk-taking behavior.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether low- and high-performing firms differ in their risk-taking behavior when performance feedback places them below or above aspiration levels. It examines whether negative feedback motivates deviant risk taking through bribery expenditure while positive feedback motivates aspirational risk taking through R&D intensity, and whether legal development and industry competition condition those relationships.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: When a firm's performance is below its aspiration level, there is a negative relationship between performance and bribery.
- H2: When a firm's performance is above its aspiration level, there is a positive relationship between performance and R&D.
- H3a: When a firm's performance is below its aspiration level, legal development weakens the negative relationship between performance and bribery.
- H3b: When a firm's performance is above its aspiration level, legal development strengthens the positive relationship between performance and R&D.
- H4a: When a firm's performance is below its aspiration level, industry competition strengthens the negative relationship between performance and bribery.
- H4b: When a firm's performance is above its aspiration level, industry competition strengthens the positive relationship between performance and R&D.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Performance below and above social aspiration; performance below and above historical aspiration.
- DV(s): Bribery expenditure, measured through abnormal entertainment and travel expenses; R&D intensity, measured as industry-adjusted R&D expenses scaled by sales.
- Mediators: Not directly modeled as measured mediators; the theory specifies motivation-based problemistic search and aspirational search as the process logic.
- Moderators: Provincial legal development and industry competition.

The mechanism is that performance below aspirations creates pressure for quick remedies, making low-performing firms more willing to use myopic and deviant actions such as bribery. Performance above aspirations reduces immediate strain and directs firms toward future-oriented capability building, making high-performing firms more likely to invest in R&D. Strong legal development weakens the bribery path and strengthens the R&D path, while high competition intensifies both the bribery response among low performers and the R&D response among high performers.

**Data & Measures**
The sample covers Chinese listed companies on the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges from 2007 to 2013; 2007 was chosen because information-disclosure quality improved significantly when new accounting standards became effective in February 2006, and 2013 because an anti-corruption campaign launched by the Chinese government propelled listed companies to hide entertainment expenses starting in 2014. B-share firms, financial-sector firms (different accounting standards), and firms listed for one year or less were excluded, and firm-year observations with missing values were dropped, yielding a final sample of 9,633 firm-year observations from 2,224 listed companies; sample firms had on average been listed 12.6 years, held assets of RMB 2.24 billion, and had 1,817 employees. Entertainment and travel expenses come from firms' annual reports, provincial-level legal development from the marketization index compiled by the National Economic Research Institute, and other firm- and industry-level data from the China Securities Market and Accounting Research database and the Wind Datafeed Service. Bribery expenditure (measured at t+1) is abnormal entertainment expenses: entertainment and travel expenses are first regressed on total sales, total assets, marketing expenses divided by total sales, capital intensity, and the average compensation of the three highest-paid executives (following Cai et al., 2011; adjusted R-squared 0.70), and the residual is taken as the bribery proxy, an approach the authors say does not depend on self-reporting by managers. R&D intensity (also at t+1) is industry-adjusted R&D expenses scaled by total sales, calculated as a firm's R&D expenditure divided by its sales minus industry R&D intensity, where industry R&D intensity is the average R&D intensity of all firms in a three-digit China Securities Regulatory Commission industry category. Performance is return on assets, net income divided by the average of beginning and ending total assets. Social aspiration is the average ROA of the focal firm's three-digit CSRC industry peers in a year; historical aspiration is the firm's own ROA in the previous year. Following prior BTOF research, a spline function separates performance below and above each aspiration: performance (when below social aspiration) equals firm ROA minus industry average ROA if firm ROA is lower and 0 otherwise; performance (when above social aspiration) equals the same difference if firm ROA is at or above the industry average and 0 otherwise; the two historical-aspiration splines are built the same way against the firm's prior-year ROA. Legal development is the National Economic Research Institute index subdimension on market institutions' development and legal protection, containing three items: development of market and legal intermediaries (shares of lawyers and independent accountants in the local population), legal environment for businesses, and legal protection of intellectual property rights. Industry competition is industry concentration measured with the Herfindahl-Hirschman index, reverse-coded so that higher scores indicate higher competition. Controls are CEO age, CEO tenure, political ties, board size, firm size (natural logarithm of total employees), listing age, three slack measures (SGA/sales, current ratio, equity ratio), SOE status, foreign sales percentage, organizational change, donation, and the logged number of firms in the city; VIFs range from 1.01 to 1.30. Hypotheses are tested with panel analysis, standard errors clustered at the industry level, and year, industry, and city fixed effects (five year dummies, 70 industry dummies, and 337 city dummies), with the independent, moderating, and control variables lagged by one year so that their ending year is 2012. The authors frame that lag as an attempt "to establish the causal link" and as one of several initiatives to address potential endogeneity, alongside the extensive control set and the fixed effects, but the design is an observational panel rather than an experiment, instrumental-variable, difference-in-differences, or regression-discontinuity study. Reported robustness checks include return on equity and return on sales as alternative performance measures, two-digit rather than three-digit industry classification, seemingly unrelated regressions allowing the bribery and R&D error terms to correlate, single-item substitutes for the composite legal development measure, standardized coefficients compared against the slack indicators, subgroup analysis using continuous industry-adjusted ROA on the below- and above-aspiration subsamples, a two-stage Heckman selection model with an inverse Mills ratio, an impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable calculation, and multilevel analyses with firm at level 1 and province or industry at level 2.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses are supported. Because the results based on social and historical aspirations are highly consistent, the authors discuss hypothesis testing from the social-aspiration models in Tables 2 and 3 (n = 9,633 in every model). In support of Hypothesis 1, Model 2 of Table 2 shows that the impact of prior performance (when below social aspiration) on bribery expenditure is negative (b = -0.73, p < .01), so that the further a firm falls below its aspiration level, the larger its abnormal entertainment spending; the effect of performance (when above social aspiration) on bribery is not significant. In support of Hypothesis 2, Model 2 of Table 3 shows that the relationship between performance (when above social aspiration) and R&D intensity is positive (b = 1.10, p < .01), while performance (when below social aspiration) does not have a significant effect on R&D intensity. Consistent with the paper's assumption that bribery and R&D are fundamentally different risk-taking behaviors, prior R&D intensity negatively affects bribery expenditure (b = -2.78, p < .01) and prior bribery expenditure negatively affects R&D intensity (b = -0.07, p < .01). Hypothesis 3a is supported: in Model 5 of Table 2 the interaction between performance (when below social aspiration) and legal development is positive (b = 0.14, p < .01), and in the low-legal-development subsample performance below aspiration is negatively associated with bribery expenditure, whereas in the high-legal-development subsample that negative effect disappears and the relationship is insignificant. Hypothesis 3b is supported: in Model 5 of Table 3 the interaction between performance (when above social aspiration) and legal development is positive (b = 0.09, p < .05), with the positive performance-R&D relationship holding in the high-legal-development subsample and disappearing in the low-legal-development subsample. Hypothesis 4a is supported: the interaction between performance (when below social aspiration) and industry competition is negative (b = -1.54, p < .05), with performance below aspiration not significantly related to bribery expenditure under low competition but negative and significant under high competition. Hypothesis 4b is supported: the interaction between performance (when above social aspiration) and industry competition is positive (b = 3.16, p < .01), with performance above aspiration not significantly related to R&D intensity under low competition but positive and significant under high competition.

The robustness tests are reported as consistent with the main results, including the subgroup analysis that replaces the spline with a continuous industry-adjusted ROA measure, where Hypotheses 1, 3a, and 4a are supported on the below-aspiration subsample and Hypotheses 2, 3b, and 4b on the above-aspiration subsample. An additional analysis using organizational moderators finds that the effect of performance (when below aspirations) on bribery is strengthened for non-SOEs (b = 0.82, p < .01), smaller firms (b = 0.54, p < .01), and firms with younger or shorter-tenured CEOs (b = 0.07, p < .01; b = 0.33, p < .01), and that performance (when above aspirations) has a stronger effect on R&D for non-SOEs than for SOEs (b = -0.98, p < .01).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the behavioral theory of the firm by using a consistent motivation-based logic for both below- and above-aspiration performance rather than combining problemistic search for poor performance with slack-capacity logic for strong performance. It distinguishes deviant risk taking from aspirational risk taking and shows that firms at different points relative to aspirations are not simply more or less risk seeking, but tend toward different types of risky action. It also extends BTOF by theorizing legal development and industry competition as environmental boundary conditions.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that regulators and investors should watch low-performing firms more closely for bribery risk, especially where legal development is weak or industry competition is high. They also suggest that strong legal institutions can both deter corruption among troubled firms and encourage R&D among high-performing firms. Competition is more ambiguous as a policy lever because it may stimulate innovation among strong performers while also increasing bribery pressure among weak performers.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the bribery expenditure measure is a proxy based on abnormal entertainment and travel expenses, and that it cannot perfectly separate bribes from conventional lobbying or relationship-building expenses. They also state that the study cannot concurrently examine morality or ethics as a moderator of deviant risk taking. The China setting provides useful institutional variation, but the authors caution that bribery, guanxi, and gift-giving may operate differently from developed-economy contexts.

**Future Research**
Future research should develop more objective and accurate measures of bribery. It should examine moral and ethical factors as additional boundary conditions for the relationship between performance feedback and bribery. The authors also call for studies across wider national contexts, including both developed and emerging economies, to test whether the same contrasting risk-taking patterns generalize beyond China.
