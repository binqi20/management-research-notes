# Structuring Reality Through the Faultlines Lens: The Effects of Structure, Fairness, and Status Conflict on the Activated Faultlines–Performance Relationship

**Abstract**
We investigate how activated team faultlines represent an informal sensemaking structure through which teammates interpret their social reality. Constructed from intersubgroup comparisons, activated team faultlines likely result in ambiguous or illegitimate status perceptions. Thus, activated faultlines threaten team justice climate, which drives status conflict, impairing team performance. We explore the effects of team structure clarity in providing certainty or legitimacy around status and structure, ameliorating the negative effect of activated faultlines on team justice climate. We test our model using a multi-source (three sources), multi-wave cross-lagged design (four waves) on a sample of 271 employees and 41 leaders in 41 teams. We find that the negative relationship between activated faultlines and team performance is mediated by the team justice climate–status conflict causal chain. We also find that team structure clarity reduces activated faultlines’ negative effect on team justice climate. Results highlight the value of using team faultlines, the social identity approach, and justice theories to understand how diverse teams interpret their social reality, which influences their performance. Furthermore, our research provides practical guidance to managers in building clear structures that minimize harmful effects of activated faultlines on justice perceptions and team performance.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how activated team faultlines affect team performance when team members use faultline-based subgroups to interpret their social reality. It specifically examines whether activated faultlines reduce team performance through lower team justice climate and higher status conflict, and whether team structure clarity weakens this process.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a: The negative relationship between activated faultlines and team performance will be mediated by status conflict.
- Hypothesis 1b: Team justice climate will mediate the relationship between activated faultlines and status conflict.
- Hypothesis 1c: The negative relationship between activated faultlines and team performance will be mediated by team justice climate and status conflict, such that activated faultlines will reduce team justice climate and therefore promote status conflict, which in turn will impair team performance.
- Hypothesis 2: Team structure clarity moderates the activated faultlines-team justice climate-status conflict mediated relationship, such that clear team structures attenuate the mediated indirect effect and unclear team structures perpetuate the existing mediated indirect effect.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Activated faultlines.
- DV(s): Team performance.
- Mediators: Team justice climate and status conflict.
- Moderators: Team structure clarity moderates the activated faultlines-team justice climate-status conflict relationship.

The argument is that activated faultlines operate as an informal sensemaking structure that makes intersubgroup status relations ambiguous or illegitimate. Those perceptions reduce team justice climate, which increases status conflict as subgroups defend or elevate perceived status. Status conflict then draws attention away from common team tasks and impairs team performance. Clear team structures provide an alternative, legitimating structure around roles, procedures, and priorities, reducing the negative effect of activated faultlines on team justice climate.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from one Spanish organization delivering social health care services, combining archival human resources records on age, gender, organizational tenure, and educational background with four survey waves administered at six-week intervals: Time 1 collected team members' faultline perceptions, team structure clarity, and controls, and supervisors' ratings of their team's structure clarity; Time 2 measured team justice climate; Time 3 measured team processes including status conflict; Time 4 collected supervisors' assessments of team performance. Questionnaires went to 512 team members at Time 1 (411 returned, 80.27%), then to those 411 at Time 2 (367 returned, 71.67%) and to those 367 at Time 3 (309 returned, 60.35%); the Time 1 supervisor survey returned 59 of 66 (89.39%) and the Time 4 supervisor survey 51 of 66 (77.27%). Teams below a 60% within-team response rate or without matched upper-level manager data were excluded, so 25 teams and 241 employees were dropped and the final sample was 271 employees in 41 teams. Activated faultlines (T1) were computed in five steps: Shaw's (2004) procedure produced four dormant faultline strength scores, each taking one attribute (educational background, gender, organizational tenure, age) as the primary attribute after continuous variables were converted into quartile categories; each attribute's strength score was then multiplied by team members' perceived split on that same attribute (items following Jehn and Bezrukova, 2010, such as "I noticed that my team split into different subgroups based on age"); and the weighted scores were averaged. Team justice climate (T2) used 14 items adapted from Li and Cropanzano's (2009) intraunit justice climate scale, covering distributive (5 items), procedural (5 items), and interactional (4 items) justice (alpha = .84; ICC1 = .23, ICC2 = .58). Team structure clarity (T1) used Bunderson and Boumgarden's (2010) five-item scale rated by supervisors (alpha = .87); the parallel team-member rating (ICC1 = .28, ICC2 = .66) correlated r = .65 (p < .01) with the supervisor score, and the supervisor score was used to separate information sources. Status conflict (T3) used Bendersky and Hays' (2012) nine-item scale (alpha = .88; ICC1 = .26, ICC2 = .56). Team performance (T4) was rated by supervisors on six criteria (efficiency, quality of innovations, productivity, adherence to schedules, adherence to budgets, and overall achievement) from 1 "far below average" to 5 "far above average" relative to teams performing similar tasks (alpha = .84). Controls were task complexity (three items adapted from Morgeson and Humphrey, 2006; alpha = .87), overall team heterogeneity (Blau's index for gender and educational background, standard deviations for tenure and age, then averaged), team size supplied by human resources, and intrateam conflict (Jehn's 1995 eight-item scale; alpha = .80). Measurement models were assessed with confirmatory factor analyses estimated by robust maximum likelihood (MLM in Mplus) on the polychoric correlation matrix, and the hypotheses were tested on n = 41 teams using bootstrapped (5,000 resamples) mediation and moderated mediation models run through Hayes's (2013) PROCESS macro. The design is associational rather than causally identified: the authors state that it does not allow manipulation of the independent variables and that, despite the cross-lagged and multisource approach, they cannot ensure causality in the results.

**Key Findings**
- Team-level correlations (n = 41) show activated faultlines negatively related to team performance (r = -.48, p < .01) and to team justice climate (r = -.55, p < .01), and positively related to status conflict (r = .49, p < .01); team justice climate is negatively related to status conflict (r = -.64, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 1a is supported: the direct effect of activated faultlines on team performance was not significant (B = -0.85, SE = 0.65, t = -1.32, p = .19), while the indirect effect through status conflict was significant (-0.96, bootstrap SE = 0.61, 95% CI -2.63/-0.15), controlling for other types of conflict.
- Hypothesis 1b is supported: the direct effect of activated faultlines on status conflict was not significant (B = 0.59, SE = 0.71, t = 0.83, p = .41), while the positive indirect effect through team justice climate was significant (0.93, bootstrap SE = 0.41, 95% CI 0.35/2.07).
- Hypothesis 1c is supported: activated faultlines were negatively related to team justice climate (B = -3.53, SE = 1.13, t = -3.12), team justice climate was negatively related to status conflict (B = -0.26, SE = 0.09, t = -2.78), and status conflict was negatively related to team performance (B = -0.58, SE = 0.16, t = -3.51). The total effect of activated faultlines on team performance was negative and significant (B = -1.82, SE = 0.74, t = -2.45, p = .01) and the standardized serial indirect effect was significant (-0.11, bootstrap SE = 0.05, 95% CI -0.27/-0.03), with a ratio of indirect to total effect of 0.29 (bootstrap SE = 12.43, 95% CI 0.06/1.66). In that model the direct effect of activated faultlines on performance (B = -0.69, SE = 0.70, t = -0.98, p = .33) and the team justice climate-performance path (B = 0.06, SE = 0.10, t = 0.66, p = .51) were not significant.
- Hypothesis 2 is supported: the activated faultlines x team structure clarity interaction term was significant (B = 3.05, SE = 1.37, t = 2.22, p = .03), reported in the text as an effect on status conflict and listed in Table 4 under the model on team justice climate. The conditional indirect effect of activated faultlines through team justice climate on status conflict was 1.60 (bootstrap SE = 0.69, 95% CI 0.50/3.20) at unclear structure (1 SD below the mean, 2.36), 0.86 (bootstrap SE = 0.37, 95% CI 0.29/1.79) at moderately clear structure (mean 3.27), and a nonsignificant 0.12 (bootstrap SE = 0.47, 95% CI -0.62/1.44) at clear structure (1 SD above the mean, 4.18); the index of moderated mediation was -0.80 (bootstrap SE = 0.50, 95% CI -1.97/-0.02).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconceptualizes activated faultlines as a sensemaking structure rather than only as a more refined diversity measure. It extends faultlines research by identifying team justice climate and status conflict as mechanisms linking activated faultlines to performance, and by showing how team structure clarity can attenuate that pathway. It also contributes a measurement approach that combines dormant faultline strength with perceived faultline activation.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not treat all possible demographic splits as equally threatening; the study suggests diagnosing whether team members actually perceive dormant faultlines as activated. For teams with activated faultlines, managers can reduce ambiguity and perceived illegitimacy by clarifying roles, procedures, priorities, and authority relations. The paper also suggests that leaders should attend to status expectations in diverse teams and use structure to support fairness perceptions before status conflict undermines performance.

**Limitations**
The authors note that most measures were perceptual, except demographic information used in the activated faultline measure. The field design did not manipulate independent variables, so the cross-lagged and multisource design does not establish causality. The results come from a specific organizational and cultural context, a Spanish health care organization, which limits generalizability.

**Future Research**
Future research could use nonobtrusive process measures and objective performance indicators to test activated faultlines and status conflict. The authors call for research designs that can test causal relationships and for replication across cultures. They also suggest longitudinal work on how activated faultlines change over time, how different diversity attributes become weighted, whether activated faultlines affect psychological safety, and whether leadership styles provide the clarity that helps manage faultline-based teams.
