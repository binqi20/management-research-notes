# The Ideator’s Bias: How Identity-Induced Self-Efficacy Drives Overestimation in Employee-Driven Process Innovation

**Abstract**
Grasping the true value of ideas is essential for corporate innovation success. When it comes to forecasting the value of one’s own innovation ideas, however, people may err systematically. In this paper, we shed light on this ideator’s bias, and examine when and why certain ideas are more prone to biased evaluations. Specifically, we argue that biased idea evaluations depend on the self-efficacy that ideators may derive from their specific role and social identity in the firm when generating a specific idea. We test our theoretical predictions of such a situation-specific perspective on overconfidence by using a corporate dataset on process innovations and their valuations. Furthermore, we triangulate our predicted mechanism and rule out alternative explanations through a series of additional interviews and four scenario-based experiments. Consistent with our situation-specific, identity-based account, we find that ideas from employees at a higher (vs. lower) organizational level, and from employees generating ideas in groups (vs. individually) are more prone to an ideator’s bias. In doing so, our study helps explain when the ideator’s bias prevails in a real-life organizational context, raises caution about some current proxies to identify high-potential ideas, and provides fresh insights to established theories around overconfidence and self-efficacy.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when and why employees overestimate the economic value of their own process innovation ideas. It focuses on whether organizational position and collaborative ideation activate role and social identities that increase self-efficacy and, in turn, intensify the ideator’s bias.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. There is a positive relationship between organizational position and ideator’s bias: ideas are valued more highly by higher-level creators than by lower-level creators.
H2. There is a positive relationship between group membership and ideator’s bias: ideas are valued more highly by collaboratively ideating than by individually ideating employees.
H3. Group membership will moderate the relationship between organizational position and ideator’s bias: ideas submitted by noncollaborating lower-level employees will be valued less highly than ideas submitted by (a) collaborating lower-level employees, (b) noncollaborating higher-level employees, and (c) collaborating higher-level employees.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Organizational position of the ideator and collaborative versus individual ideation.
- DV(s): Ideator’s bias, measured as the relative difference between the ideator’s initial value forecast and accounting-verified idea value.
- Mediators: Self-efficacy is theorized as the mechanism and is tested in the scenario experiments.
- Moderators: Group membership is theorized to moderate the organizational-position effect; the paper predicts the lowest overvaluation for noncollaborating lower-level employees.

The proposed mechanism is that salient role identities and social identities shape situation-specific self-efficacy. Higher-level employees draw more optimistic self-efficacy from managerial role identity, while lower-level employees gain additional collective self-efficacy when ideating in groups. The field data support higher overvaluation among higher-level and collaborative ideators, and the interaction shows that collaboration mainly raises overvaluation among lower-level employees rather than adding to already elevated higher-level self-efficacy. The interviews and experiments triangulate the identity-induced self-efficacy account and help rule out alternative explanations such as power, endowment effects, groupthink, and shared mental models.

**Data & Measures**
The field study draws on a proprietary dataset from the process-improvement program of a product division of a European-based automotive supplier, covering all employee-generated process innovation ideas that had been submitted or had completed their life cycle in 2009. The dataset contained 1,174 finalized ideas, and after accounting for missing values 944 ideas entered the analysis; the raw dataset additionally contained 286 uncompleted "work in progress" ideas for which verified valuations did not yet exist. The dependent variable, ideator’s bias, is (estimated value − actual value) / estimated value, that is, the relative difference between the ideator’s Stage 1 value forecast and the idea value verified by accounting at Stage 5, with positive values indicating overvaluation, negative values undervaluation, and zero a correct evaluation; nonimplemented ideas are censored from above at 100%, and the variable is Winsorized so that values outside the 5th-to-95th percentile range are set to those percentiles. Organizational position is a dummy derived manually from the firm's organizational charts, where lower-level (dummy = 0) covers frontline workers and their first- and second-level supervisors and higher-level covers upper-rank individuals such as production managers, quality managers, and plant heads; collaborative ideation is a dummy (0 = individual ideation, 1 = collaborative ideation) read from the submission system's "idea creator cell," with 79% of ideas created individually and 21% collaboratively. The two are interacted to test Hypothesis 3. Controls are idea value in thousands of euros as estimated by the ideating employee(s), upfront investment cost, an OEM-program dummy, a novelty dummy (46% of ideas novel), idea length measured as the number of characters of idea name and description with project identifiers and duplications removed, and past ideation success measured as previously accepted ideas divided by cumulative submissions. Estimation is Tobit regression with robust standard errors clustered at the ideator level, yielding 48 left-censored, 371 uncensored, and 525 right-censored observations, with an OLS full model reported alongside; the field design is associational, and the paper assigns the test of causality to its experiments rather than claiming a causal identification strategy for the field data. Two further data sources complement the field analysis: six post hoc semi-structured interviews with ideators in the same firm, and four between-subjects scenario-based experiments with participants from a U.S. online panel (Amazon Mechanical Turk) — Experiment 1 (n = 153) manipulating role identity by telling participants they were a higher-level or a lower-level employee, Experiment 1b (n = 152) priming power rather than identity following the procedure and sample of Fast et al. (2012), Experiment 2 (n = 141) manipulating social identity by telling participants they had generated the idea individually or in a group, and Experiment 2b (n = 298) varying group size. The idea description was held constant across experimental conditions, participants were asked to rate its expected value on a scale, and self-efficacy was tested as the mediator.

**Key Findings**
Model-free analysis shows that 74% of ideas were overvalued, 20% undervalued, and 6% correctly valued. Higher-level employees overvalued more frequently than lower-level employees (82% vs. 68%; z = 4.87, p < .01) and undervalued less frequently (14% vs. 24%; z = 3.94, p < .01). The collaboration split was weaker: overvaluation was 77% for collaborative versus 73% for individual ideation (z = 1.08, p = .28, not significant), while undervaluation was 13% versus 21% (z = 2.58, p = .01). In the multivariate Tobit models, Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2 were both supported: ideas from higher-level employees were significantly more overvalued (b = 1.53, SE = 0.37) and collaboratively ideated ideas were significantly more overvalued (b = 0.99, SE = 0.46) in Model 2-2, with the results text reporting p < .01 for both coefficients while Table 2 flags the collaboration coefficient at the 5% level. Hypothesis 3 was supported by a significant negative interaction in Model 2-3 (b = −2.33, SE = 0.87, p < .01): ideas submitted by noncollaborating lower-level employees were valued significantly less highly than those submitted by collaborating lower-level employees and by noncollaborating and collaborating higher-level employees. Average marginal effects show that organizational position mattered for individually generated ideas (b = 1.75, SE = 0.34; z = 5.16, p < .01) but not for collaboratively generated ones (p = .68), and that collaboration mattered only for ideas generated by lower-level employees (b = 1.90, SE = 0.47; z = 4.02, p < .01) and not for those generated by higher-level employees (p = .83); the effects of hierarchy and collaboration are therefore not additive. Results remain qualitatively identical under OLS (Model 2-4), bootstrapped clustered standard errors, department controls and a Heckman (1979) specification for missing observations, a reproduced sample drawing on rejected ideas and Stage 3 evaluations (n = 754), and an endowment-effect control, and the estimated patterns for forecast imprecision differ clearly from those for bias. The complementary evidence converged with the field results. All six interviews suggested that higher-level employees' hierarchical identity was activated and reflected in overly optimistic idea assessments, and lower-level ideators described a group "we-feeling"; no high-level informant referred to biases in collaborative ideation unprompted, whereas two of three lower-level informants did so. In the experiments, participants asked to adopt a higher-level (vs. lower-level) identity rated the idea as significantly more valuable and self-efficacy significantly mediated this effect (Experiment 1); the power manipulation, in contrast to the identity manipulation, did not produce biased evaluations (Experiment 1b); participants imagining they had created the idea in a group (vs. alone) rated it as significantly more valuable, again mediated by self-efficacy (Experiment 2); and the ideator’s bias was greater in larger groups (Experiment 2b).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes an organizationally embedded, situational micro-account of ideator’s bias based on identity-induced self-efficacy, moving beyond trait-like accounts of overconfidence. It also cautions innovation research against treating team-generated ideas as inherently higher potential without accounting for team members’ overvaluation. Finally, it links self-efficacy and overconfidence by showing how personal and collective self-efficacy can become a source of biased idea valuation in organizational settings.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest separating idea generation from idea evaluation so ideators can gain distance from identities salient during ideation. When ideator input is necessary, managers should apply additional scrutiny to valuations from groups and higher-level employees because those contexts are especially prone to overvaluation. The paper also suggests priming accurate forecasting during evaluation rather than only encouraging inventive confidence.

**Limitations**
The authors acknowledge the peculiarities of a process-innovation setting where accounting provides standardized evaluations and decision makers rely comparatively little on ideators’ own estimates. They also note that employees in the focal setting are encouraged to generate ideas, so overestimation could operate differently in organizations with greater inertia. Cultural orientation and organizational socialization may also affect whether role identity, group identity, or hybrid identities become salient.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether the findings generalize to non-Western countries, more hierarchical or more egalitarian societies, and organizations with different cultures or onboarding practices. The authors also call for work on when collective self-efficacy contributes to groupthink or escalation of commitment, and on whether identity cues can sometimes evoke moderate self-efficacy that reduces biased forecasts.
