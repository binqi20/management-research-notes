# Impression Offsetting as an Early Warning Signal of Low CEO Confidence in Acquisitions

**Abstract**
Researchers have long been interested in understanding the motives behind CEOs’ actions. On the one hand, CEOs may pursue strategic actions because they are confident they will enhance firm value. Alternatively, CEOs may take actions even when they have low confidence in the value of those actions, perhaps driven by self-interest or social pressures. Although research suggests that CEO option exercises following an acquisition announcement are an ex post behavioral outcome of low CEO confidence in the acquisition’s value-creation potential, prior research has not identified any ex ante signals shareholders can look for to assess acquiring CEOs’ confidence when the acquisition is announced. We address this concern by exploring a potential early warning signal of low CEO confidence: impression offsetting. We theorize that impression offsetting—measured as other unrelated positive announcements made by the firm in the days immediately surrounding the acquisition announcement—may serve as an ex ante signal of low CEO confidence in the acquisition’s value-creation potential, and, as such, will be positively associated with CEO option exercises, an ex post behavioral outcome of low confidence. We test our theory with a sample of 491 large acquisitions and find consistent support for our hypotheses.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether investors can gauge a CEO's confidence in an acquisition when it is announced. It also asks why CEOs use anticipatory impression management, theorizing that impression offsetting around acquisition announcements is an ex ante signal that CEOs have low confidence in the acquisition's long-term value-creation potential.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Impression offsetting around an acquisition announcement will be positively associated with the percentage of stock options exercised by the CEO in the period following the announcement.
H2. The association between impression offsetting around an acquisition announcement and subsequent stock options exercised by the CEO will be moderated by CEO age such that the association will be stronger for older CEOs.
H3. The association between impression offsetting around an acquisition announcement and subsequent stock options exercised by the CEO will be moderated by firm reputation such that the association will be stronger for firms with high reputation.
H4. The association between impression offsetting around an acquisition announcement and subsequent stock options exercised by the CEO will be moderated by industry dynamism such that the association will be stronger for firms facing more dynamic industry conditions.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Impression offsetting, measured as the count of material, positive firm-authored press releases in the three-day window centered on the acquisition announcement.
- DV(s): Subsequent CEO stock option exercises, measured as options exercised by the CEO in the quarter following the focal acquisition divided by exercisable options held at the end of the prior year.
- Mediators: No mediator is directly tested; low acquisition-related CEO confidence is inferred through the association between impression offsetting and later option exercises.
- Moderators: CEO age, high firm reputation, and industry dynamism.

The theorized mechanism is that CEOs with low acquisition-related confidence release unrelated positive information around the acquisition announcement to reduce scrutiny and buffer negative market reactions. If the acquisition later appears unlikely to create value, the CEO can protect accumulated equity value by exercising options after the announcement. The positive impression-offsetting-to-option-exercise relationship is expected to be stronger when downside risk is more salient: for older CEOs, CEOs of high-reputation firms, and CEOs operating in more dynamic industries.

**Data & Measures**
Archival event-level dataset assembled from SDC Platinum Domestic Mergers (acquisition data), PR Newswire and Business Wire via LexisNexis (press releases), Compustat (firm-level and industry-level variables), the Center for Research in Security Prices (the Annual beta variable), Thomson Reuters Insider Filing data (CEO equity holdings changes), Eventus (stock market reaction), Risk Metrics (board-level variables), and ExecuComp (compensation and demographic variables). Full data collection produced an initial sample of 770 acquisitions, reduced by missing data to a final analysis sample of 491 acquisitions; Table 3 reports n = 491 for each model. All predictor variables were measured one quarter-year before the dependent variable, except firm-level control variables, which were measured one full year before.

- DV: Subsequent Options Exercises, the ratio of (1) the options exercised by the CEO in the quarter following the focal acquisition to (2) the number of exercisable options held by the CEO at the end of the prior year, logged because of its skewed distribution.
- IV: Impression Offsetting, the count of material, positive announcements made by the focal firm in a firm-authored press release in the three-day period centered on the acquisition announcement (one day before and one day after); Table 1 gives the categorization of announcement types.
- Moderators: CEO Age, the age reported in ExecuComp for the firm's CEO in the year of the acquisition; High Reputation, a binary variable coded 1 for firms included on either Fortune's Most Admired or the Wall Street Journal's Corporate Reputation Survey; Dynamism, industry sales regressed on a year counter over a five-year window with the standard error divided by the industry's mean sales.
- Controls: the paper reports eight CEO-level and board-level controls (e.g., CEO Prior Option Exercises, CEO Total Compensation, CEO Tenure, CEO Power, Busy Board, Female Board Representation), six firm-level and industry-level controls (e.g., Acquisition Experience, Firm Size, Net Income, Munificence, Annual beta), and six acquisition-level controls (Stock Market Reaction, Hostile Acquisition, Stock Consideration, Acquisition Size, Other Material Announcements, Baseline Positive Announcements), plus year dummy variables included in all models but omitted from the tables.

Estimation is Tobit regression, used because the dependent variable is a continuous "corner solution response" bounded below at zero, with robust standard errors clustered by firm and all nonbinary variables standardized before the interaction terms were created. The design is associational: the paper describes its results as associations and claims no causal identification strategy. To address possible omitted-variable bias, the authors report a two-stage residual inclusion Tobit model whose first stage is a negative binomial regression predicting the number of impression offsetting announcements, using average top management team total compensation and acquisition unrelatedness as instruments, together with an impact threshold of a confounding variable (ITCV) test.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. Impression offsetting is positively and significantly associated with subsequent CEO option exercises in both Model 2 and the full Model 6 (p < .001; coefficients 0.0319 and 0.0243). In practical terms, a CEO who makes three impression offsetting announcements around an acquisition exercises 6.7% more options in the next quarter than a CEO of a firm that makes zero such announcements, a difference of about $220,000 at the mean value of exercisable option holdings.
- H2 supported. The CEO Age by Impression Offsetting interaction is positive and significant in Models 3 and 6 (p < .001; coefficients 0.0471 and 0.0541). Simple slopes: positive for high CEO age (+1 SD, p < .001) and negative for low CEO age (-1 SD, p < .001). Among CEOs making three offsetting announcements, higher age (+1 SD) is associated with exercising 27.62% more options than more youthful CEOs (-1 SD), roughly $850,000.
- H3 supported. The High Reputation by Impression Offsetting interaction is positive and significant in Models 4 and 6 (p < .001; coefficients 0.0817 and 0.0917). Simple slopes: positive for high-reputation firms (p < .001) but not significant for not-high-reputation firms (p > .05). When the firm makes zero impression offsetting announcements, CEOs of high-reputation firms exercise approximately 10% of the option value that CEOs of other firms exercise, a difference of nearly $3M at the mean of exercisable options.
- H4 supported. The Dynamism by Impression Offsetting interaction is positive and significant in Models 5 and 6 (p < .001; coefficients 0.0900 and 0.0992). Simple slopes: positive for high dynamism (+1 SD, p < .001) and negative for low dynamism (-1 SD, p < .001). Among firms making three offsetting announcements, CEOs in highly dynamic industries exercise 61.3% more options than CEOs in less dynamic industries, a difference of approximately $1.7M.

Supplemental analyses are consistent with the reported results: adding two measures of CEO trait overconfidence leaves the hypothesized associations intact, and both overconfidence measures are themselves negative and significant predictors of subsequent options exercised (p < .001); the two-stage residual inclusion Tobit and an alternative count-based dependent variable both return results consistent with those presented; and the ITCV test indicates an omitted variable would need to correlate at .274 with both impression offsetting and subsequent options exercises to overturn the findings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to impression management research by identifying low action-specific CEO confidence as a motivation for anticipatory impression offsetting. It extends signaling theory by showing that impression offsetting can function as an unintended signal, rather than only an intentional signal, of executive confidence. It also contributes to behavioral and governance research by offering an observable way to study CEO confidence in strategic actions that may be driven by self-interest or social pressures.

**Practical Implication**
The paper suggests that investors should treat unrelated positive announcements surrounding an acquisition as a warning signal that the CEO may lack confidence in the deal's value-creation potential. When an acquisition is accompanied by impression offsetting, investors should scrutinize the acquisition's value logic rather than interpreting the additional positive announcements in isolation.

**Limitations**
The authors cannot directly observe CEO confidence and rely on subsequent stock option exercises as an established behavioral proxy. The design is observational and cannot fully rule out omitted-variable explanations, although the authors use extensive controls, two-stage residual inclusion Tobit models, and impact-threshold analysis. The sample is limited to large completed acquisitions by public U.S.-based S&P 500 firms with public U.S.-based targets.

**Future Research**
The authors call for future research to examine alternative unobtrusive measures of CEO confidence and compare them with option-exercise signals. They also suggest extending impression offsetting research beyond acquisitions to other strategic and governance actions, and examining additional CEO-level moderators such as narcissism, prevention focus, or construal level.
