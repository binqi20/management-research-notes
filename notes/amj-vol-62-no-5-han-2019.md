# The Goldilocks Effect of Strategic Human Resource Management? Optimizing the Benefits of a High-Performance Work System Through the Dual Alignment of Vertical and Horizontal Fit

**Abstract**
Although vertical and horizontal fit in strategic human resource management are foundational to the links between a high-performance work system (HPWS) and organizational performance, little is known about how these two fits interact to affect organizational performance. We address this shortcoming while also advancing knowledge on each type of fit. We offer a more nuanced examination of vertical fit (which has typically been assessed with respect to organizations’ broad strategic types) by focusing on the alignment of an HPWS with an organization’s market entry timing mode—a key element of strategy. We propose that among organizations pursuing new product development, the effect of an HPWS on organizational performance is most positive under a fast-follower entry timing, followed by a first-mover and finally a fence-sitter entry timing. We then hypothesize that the benefit of vertical fit is magnified when the complementary human resources practices comprising an HPWS are implemented with greater internal consistency (or with similar intensities) across the ability, motivation, and opportunity domains—reflecting a positive interaction between vertical and horizontal fit in predicting the effectiveness of an HPWS. Analyses of four-wave nationally representative panel data yield strong support for our dual-alignment model of SHRM.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how vertical fit and horizontal fit jointly shape the relationship between a high-performance work system and organizational performance. Specifically, it examines whether HPWS effects differ across first-mover, fast-follower, and fence-sitter market entry timing modes, and whether those effects are strengthened when HPWS practices are internally consistent across ability, motivation, and opportunity domains.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Market entry timing modes will moderate the relationship between the use of an HPWS and subsequent product sales, such that the relationship will be most positive among organizations pursuing a fast-follower entry timing, followed by a first-mover entry timing, and subsequently a fence-sitter entry timing.
- H2: HPWS internal consistency will moderate the interactive effect between the use of an HPWS and market entry timing modes on product sales, such that the successive effects of HPWS utilization on product sales across the three market entry timing modes (most positive among fast followers, followed by first movers, and subsequently fence sitters) will be more pronounced when HPWS internal consistency is high.
- H3: Product sales will mediate the three-way interactive effects among an HPWS, market entry timing modes, and HPWS internal consistency on subsequent organizational financial performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Use of a high-performance work system.
- DV(s): Subsequent product sales and subsequent financial performance.
- Mediators: Product sales mediates the conditional indirect effect of HPWS on subsequent financial performance.
- Moderators: Market entry timing mode and HPWS internal consistency.
- Controls: Current product sales or financial performance, establishment size, total assets, capital-to-labor ratio, other entry timing, establishment fixed effects, and year fixed effects.

The mechanism is a dual-alignment argument. Vertical fit occurs when an HPWS supports the knowledge requirements of a firm's market entry timing mode; the authors argue that this fit is strongest for fast followers, weaker for first movers, and weakest for fence sitters. Horizontal fit occurs when the ability-, motivation-, and opportunity-enhancing domains of the HPWS are implemented with similar intensity, reducing bottlenecks in employees' knowledge search, sharing, and application. Product sales then transmits the dual-alignment effect to financial performance.

**Data & Measures**
The data are the Korean Labor Institute's Workplace Panel Survey (WPS), a biennial survey of business establishments, matched to a financial information set supplied by the same institute. The 2005 sampling frame drew 4,275 establishments with 30 or more employees across 17 industry sectors using stratified random sampling by industry and region, and usable data were received from 1,905 establishments (45% overall response rate). The authors used four waves (2005 to 2011) because the subsequent (t + 1) financial performance data for 2013 were not yet available, and after excluding missing data the final panel was 1,416 business establishments and 3,456 establishment-year observations, with 806, 842, 897, and 911 establishments per year. HPWS was built from 16 HR practices reported by a management representative and mapped onto the AMO model (six ability-enhancing items, six motivation-enhancing items, four opportunity-enhancing items); each practice was standardized using industry means, the ability, motivation, and opportunity domain scores were the means of their component standardized practices, and the overall HPWS index was the mean across the three AMO domain scores. HPWS internal consistency was measured as the SD among the three AMO domain scores, with the reciprocal taken so that higher scores represent higher internal consistency. Market entry timing was a single management-representative choice among four descriptions of the establishment's mode for its main products or services (first mover, fast follower, fence sitter, or none of the above), coded as four dummy variables. Product sales was the log of product sales divided by the total number of employees, and financial performance was the ratio of operating profit to average total assets (ROA) in percentage points, winsorized at the 1% level at both tails because of high skewness and kurtosis; both were modeled at t + 1 with the corresponding t value controlled. Controls were log of workforce size, log of total assets, capital-to-labor ratio, other entry timing, and establishment and year fixed effects. Estimation used fixed-effects regressions, which the authors adopted to control for nonobserved fixed sources of confounding factors and which Hausman tests preferred to random-effects specifications for both dependent variables, with indirect and conditional indirect effects tested by bootstrapping with 20,000 replications. The design is an observational panel with lagged outcomes; the paper claims no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 received partial support. With a first-mover referent (Model 2, Table 4), an HPWS was more positively associated with subsequent product sales among fast followers than among first movers (B = .20, 95% CI [.04, .37], p < .05), and with a fence-sitter referent (Model 3) it was more positively associated among fast followers than among fence sitters (B = .27, 95% CI [.05, .49], p < .05); however, the relationship was not significantly stronger among first movers than among fence sitters (B = .07, 95% CI [-.13, .27], n.s.). Examining the modes individually, the HPWS effect on product sales was significantly positive only under a fast-follower entry timing (B = .21, 95% CI [.06, .36], p < .05) and non-significant under a first-mover (B = .01, 95% CI [-.11, .13]) or fence-sitter (B = -.14, 95% CI [-.39, .11]) entry timing, which the authors report as partial support for Hypothesis 1 alongside full support for its core part concerning fast followers. The core part of Hypothesis 2 was supported: the three-way interaction of an HPWS, fast-follower (vs. first-mover) entry timing, and HPWS internal consistency predicted product sales (B = .07, 95% CI [.03, .11], p < .05), with the HPWS by fast-follower interactive effect at .61 (95% CI [.34, .88], p < .05) when internal consistency was high versus -.16 (95% CI [-.43, .11], n.s.) when it was low (difference = .77, 95% CI [.34, 1.20], p < .05); the fast-follower (vs. fence-sitter) counterpart was likewise significant (B = .07, 95% CI [.01, .13], p < .05; high .70, 95% CI [.29, 1.12], p < .05; low -.10, 95% CI [-.52, .32], n.s.; difference = .80, 95% CI [.10, 1.50], p < .05), whereas the three-way interaction involving first-mover (vs. fence-sitter) entry timing was not significant (B = .01, 95% CI [-.05, .07], n.s.). Under high internal consistency the HPWS effect on product sales was significantly positive only for fast followers (B = .54, 95% CI [.30, .78], p < .05) and non-significant for first movers (B = -.04, 95% CI [-.23, .15]) and fence sitters (B = -.11, 95% CI [-.82, .61]); under low internal consistency the effects did not significantly vary across the three entry timing modes. The core part of Hypothesis 3 was also supported: product sales were positively related to financial performance (B = 3.72, 95% CI [3.02, 4.42], p < .05), and bootstrapping showed that the three-way interaction involving fast-follower (vs. first-mover) entry timing was indirectly related to financial performance via product sales (B = .26, 95% CI [.05, .63], p < .05), as was the fast-follower (vs. fence-sitter) version (B = .27, 95% CI [.03, .72], p < .05), while the first-mover (vs. fence-sitter) three-way effect was not significantly mediated (B = .03, 95% CI [-.20, .25], n.s.). The indirect effect of an HPWS was significantly positive under a fast-follower entry timing with high internal consistency (B = 2.01, 95% CI [.66, 4.39], p < .05) and stronger than all other combinations of entry timing mode and internal consistency; the authors translate this dual-alignment condition into a 23.24% increase in product sales per employee and a 22.81% increase in financial performance for an HPWS implemented one SD (.39) above its industry mean.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to SHRM by theorizing and testing the interplay between vertical and horizontal fit rather than treating them as separate fit concepts. It sharpens vertical-fit theory by focusing on market entry timing as a specific element of strategy instead of broad strategic types. It also contributes to horizontal-fit research by operationalizing internal consistency across AMO domains and showing that consistent implementation conditions when HPWS practices produce stronger performance benefits.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that managers should not treat HPWS investments as universally beneficial. An HPWS is most useful when the knowledge needed for strategy execution is within employees' reach for absorption and leveraging, as in a fast-follower strategy, and less useful when knowledge requirements demand broad external experimentation or are already routine. The paper also implies that managers should balance investments across ability-, motivation-, and opportunity-enhancing HR practices rather than overemphasizing one domain.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the HPWS-entry-timing relationship may depend on contextual factors such as technological and market change that were not directly examined. They also did not observe the intervening knowledge-based mechanisms, leaving absorptive capacity and knowledge behaviors theoretically supported but not directly measured. Additional limitations include single-rater measurement for HPWS and entry timing, many binary HR-practice measures, and the focus on one HR system type.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the dual-alignment argument under different environmental conditions, especially where first-mover advantages vary by technological and market change. The authors call for studies that directly measure knowledge-based mechanisms and organizational flexibility as pathways linking HR systems to entry timing requirements. They also suggest examining more objective HR and strategy measures, richer response scales, and alternative HR systems that may fit other market entry timing modes.
