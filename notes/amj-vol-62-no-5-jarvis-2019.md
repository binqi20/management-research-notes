# Where the Heart Functions Best: Reactive–Affective Conflict and the Disruptive Work of Animal Rights Organizations

**Abstract**
We study the emotive aspect of institutional work performed by U.S. animal rights organizations (AROs) attempting to disrupt industrial practices in modern factory farming operations perceived to be abusive to animals. Drawing on an inductive, qualitative analysis of interviews with ARO advocates, as well as textual and visual archival data collected from AROs’ websites, we argue that the suppression of emotion plays a critical role in AROs’ disruptive work. We find that advocates are motivated to suppress their emotions by a perceived incompatibility between their reactive emotional displays and their affective commitment to institutional work, or what we label reactive–affective conflict. We show how two triggers of reactive–affective conflict—potential supporters’ investment in the status quo and emotive norms governing institutional work—encourage ARO advocates to suppress their emotions in face-to-face interactions with audiences while attempting to elicit emotions via visuals as their strategy of disruptive work. We contribute to the literature on the strategic use of emotion in institutional work by highlighting important relationships between the characteristics of potential supporters, the nature of institutional work, and institutional workers’ management of their own emotions to further their institutional projects. In doing so, we add needed nuance to extant conceptualizations of how emotion is strategically deployed as part of purposeful efforts to create, maintain, and disrupt institutions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what motivates actors to suppress their emotions in support of institutional work, and how strategies of institutional work can incorporate emotional suppression while still eliciting emotion in potential supporters. It studies this problem through the disruptive institutional work of U.S. animal rights organizations seeking to challenge factory farming practices.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Reactive emotions of moral shock, anger, and sorrow; affective commitment to animal wellbeing and AROs' disruptive work; reactive–affective conflict.
- Triggering conditions: Potential supporters' cognitive and emotional investment in the status quo, and emotive norms governing the settings where institutional work is performed.
- Process / tactics: Suppressing emotion to plant seeds in face-to-face advocacy, eliciting emotion through visuals, and suppressing emotion to gather evidence in undercover investigations.
- Emergent outcomes: ARO advocates decouple emotion elicitation from their own emotional display, reducing the risk that potential supporters dismiss them as irrational or fanatical.
- Boundary conditions: The process is developed in a setting where the target practices are highly institutionalized, negative externalities are often hidden, and change agents work from the margins.

The process centers on reactive–affective conflict: advocates' reactive emotional displays may undermine the affective commitment that motivates their disruptive work. In street-side advocacy, suppressing emotion helps advocates appear calm enough to make potential supporters receptive to seeds of information, while visuals carry the emotional force. In undercover investigations, suppressing emotion allows advocates to maintain cover long enough to gather visual evidence of factory farming practices.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: an inductive, interpretive qualitative study of the institutional work of six large U.S. AROs and their advocates. A grounded approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Strauss & Corbin, 1990) was adopted, employing the constant comparative method with iterative interplay between collected data and consequent rounds of theorizing until data saturation. The authors describe the study as exploratory and focused on inductively generating theory; no causal identification strategy is claimed and no statistical inference is drawn.
- Interview corpus: 29 semi-structured interviews with 30 ARO advocates, conducted between February and August of 2015, with two advocates requesting to be interviewed in tandem. Twenty-four participants were female and six were male, ranging in age from their thirties to their eighties, all identifying as members or volunteers of AROs for at least a decade. Interviews were conducted by phone, recorded, transcribed, and member checked; they lasted on average 45.17 minutes, yielding 321 pages of single-spaced text when transcribed.
- Three types of ARO advocate were interviewed. Rank-and-file advocates (street-side petition drives and leafleting) were identified by contacting an administrator at a large, national U.S. ARO who supplied contact information for the most successful signature gatherers on a large ballot-initiative petition drive in a southeastern state; 19 volunteers responded and one declined, resulting in 17 interviews with 18 advocates. Administrators self-identified during that same sampling, resulting in seven interviews. Undercover investigators were reached by first contacting the Director of Investigations at a U.S. ARO and then snowballing (Creswell, 2012), yielding interviews with five retired investigators.
- Interview protocol: designed to elicit narratives about both advocates' own decisions to support AROs and their attempts to get others to do the same; for investigators it also covered the emotional impact of their investigations and AROs' aims in collecting undercover footage. Chell's (2004) critical incident method was used, asking advocates to discuss their advocacy experiences in general and to identify especially significant or memorable events.
- Textual archival corpus: the websites of six large U.S. AROs — Compassion Over Killing, Farm Sanctuary, HSUS, Mercy For Animals, People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals (PETA), and Vegan Outreach — identified by interview participants as the AROs they had the most experience working for. Materials included mission statements, white papers on the effects of FFOs' practices, blog posts, e-newsletters, online magazines, leaflets, and training materials for new advocates, comprising 852 items averaging about 1.5 pages of text each.
- Visual archival corpus: 211 videos averaging 3.67 minutes in length, plus several hundred still images, taken from videos posted to AROs' websites and still images embedded in ARO educational materials and websites. The majority of the videos were "mini documentaries" resulting from undercover investigations into FFOs; the remainder included celebrity endorsements, dietary advice, recipes, and skits.
- Coding of interview and textual data: digital copies of all interview transcripts, textual archival documents, videos, and still images were imported into NVivo 11 Pro. First-order codes were gleaned using Charmaz's (2006, 2008) "line-by-line" coding method, chosen because it generates first-order codes that already suggest interconnections between categories. Following Pratt, Rockmann, and Kaufmann (2006), results were organized into three nested analytical categories: first-order codes of commonly themed statements, second-order theoretical categories derived from those statements, and third-order aggregate theoretical dimensions (the data structure is reported in Figure 2).
- Coding of visual data: a semiotic analysis following Rose's (2016) compendium of visual methodologies. The authors first watched the videos or viewed the still images and noted salient characteristics (e.g., color, sound, spatial organization), then formed collective interpretations of what AROs meant to signify about the conditions FFO animals live in, the forms and severity of abuse they endure, the regularity of that abuse, and the role FFO employees play in perpetuating it. Tables 3 and 5 report the resulting semiotic interpretations of the visuals presented in Figures 3, 5, and 6, and Table 4 those in Figure 4.

**Key Findings**
- Advocates' emotional experiences have two distinct components. Advocates recalled intense reactive experiences of moral shock, anger, and sorrow upon learning about (or, for investigators, witnessing first-hand) institutionalized FFO practices, juxtaposed against long-standing affective commitments grounded in compassion for animals. The study reports that mobilization into disruptive work was not wholly a function of either one: affective commitment to animals served as an emotional milieu that oriented advocates' reactive emotions toward FFO practices, so both are implicated in the motivation to participate.
- The paper's central finding runs against the authors' own prior expectation. "Contrary to our expectations at the outset of our study, and the institutional literature to date," they find the interactive suppression of emotion, rather than its expression, to be an integral component of AROs' strategic institutional work.
- Suppression is motivated by what the authors label reactive–affective conflict: a perceived incompatibility between displaying reactive emotions in interactions with potential supporters and pursuing affective commitments to the wellbeing of animals and AROs' disruptive work.
- Two triggers of reactive–affective conflict emerged from the data. The first is potential supporters' cognitive and emotional investment in the status quo: advocates report that supporters are too invested in everyday routines, rituals, and relationships bound up with animal products to consider AROs' messages, which generates both apathy and active resistance and lets supporters treat advocates' displays of shock, anger, and sorrow as prima facie evidence that AROs are extremist or irrational, rendering emotional displays ineffective as recruitment tactics. The second is emotive norms governing institutional work: to maintain cover, undercover investigators felt pressure to adhere to the norms for emotional expression governing normal FFO employees, including participating in standard practices they regard as abusive — a tension the authors describe as the most salient and acutely stressful aspect of the investigator role.
- The paper pairs the triggers with tactics in this order. AROs respond to the first trigger by both (1) suppressing emotion to plant "seeds" with potential supporters and (2) eliciting emotion in potential supporters using visuals. They respond to the second trigger by suppressing emotion to gather evidence.
- Suppressing emotion to plant seeds: in street-side leafleting the aim is simply to plant seeds that pique potential supporters' interest, and advocates report that a friendly, emotionally neutral demeanor is integral to doing so. Suppression was universally acknowledged as a necessary although not sufficient prerequisite for planting seeds. Educational materials mirror the same emotionally neutral register — figures and statistics, "cold" statements of fact, and everyday lifestyle tips — and advocates report that, disarmed by this neutrality, potential supporters become more likely to interact, take and read materials, and consider the merit of the project. This is advocates' reported belief about efficacy, not a measured outcome; the authors state the success or failure of emotive tactics was not their focus.
- Eliciting emotion via visuals: videos and still images carry the emotional force the advocates withhold, using graphic depictions of abuse, obtrusive audio, close-up framing of injuries and of animals' eyes, and wide framings emphasizing industrial scale. The study finds visuals' most important function is to decouple the process of eliciting emotion from the advocates performing the work, mitigating potential supporters' ability to dismiss AROs' work as irrational or extremist, because visuals are not vulnerable to charges of emotional bias in the way human advocates are.
- Suppressing emotion to gather evidence: investigators described "quieting" their reactive responses and shutting everything off in order to keep the job and collect evidence, a skill honed over careers spanning multiple investigations and often discussed as "desensitizing" themselves. Investigators clarified that desensitization did not mean they ceased feeling affective commitment to animals; it was temporary, and the psycho-emotional costs built up and were released in climactic episodes of emotional outpouring such as burnout and breaking down. One investigator described the situation as an emotional "catch-22": the advocates with the most passion for animals make the best investigators, and are precisely those for whom performing standard FFO practices is hardest.
- Gender: although interviewees were predominantly female, mirroring the demographic composition of the animal rights movement, the authors report that they found no differences between men and women in the perception of the stigma associated with displaying their emotions to potential supporters. They note evaluating differences between subgroups of advocates was not the study's primary purpose.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional work research by showing that emotion matters not only through workers' emotional displays but also through their strategic suppression of emotion. It theorizes reactive–affective conflict as an experiential mechanism linking workers' reactive emotions, affective commitments, audience characteristics, and emotive norms. It also extends research on visuality in organizations by showing how visuals can elicit emotion while decoupling that elicitation from stigmatized human advocates.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that organizations trying to mobilize support for disruptive institutional projects may need to manage advocates' emotional displays as carefully as their messages. For AROs, the findings suggest that calm interpersonal engagement, emotionally resonant visuals, and disciplined evidence-gathering can be mutually reinforcing tactics when audiences are defensive or heavily invested in the status quo.

**Limitations**
The authors state that their data collection focused on AROs and their advocates, which produces a single-sided view of the moral worth of factory farming operations and their practices. They also note that the study was not designed to test the success or failure of different emotive tactics, and that gender differences in the perception of emotional display were not the primary purpose of the research.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine both sides of institutional battles, including how targets of disruption counter advocates' emotive tactics. Researchers could also study audience reception of emotional displays and visuals, gendered perceptions of emotion in institutional work, and how victims' emotional experiences are marshaled by third parties in work on societal grand challenges.
