# Between Legitimacy and Efficiency: An Institutional Theory of Corporate Giving

**Abstract**
This study explores the cost of legitimacy management in the context of corporate giving. Specifically, how much does an organization spend in order to manage external legitimacy? We view corporate giving as a means by which firms may seek external legitimacy, and propose that, in response to evaluations of external audiences, organizations strategically manage such costs under a tension between legitimacy and efficiency concerns. We examine this argument by developing an institutional theory of corporate giving and testing our hypotheses using the corporate giving behaviors of publicly listed Korean companies from 2003 to 2011. Consistent with our proposition concerning the role of external audiences in shaping legitimacy management costs, we show that strategic decisions regarding how much a firm spends on corporate giving are related to the extent that the firm receives positive or negative media attention. Furthermore, we find that the relationship between media evaluations and corporate giving varies with a firm’s performance, which suggests that its internal efficiency condition can be an important contingency factor in determining its legitimacy management cost. We discuss the implications of these findings for the study of legitimacy management processes and the role of external audiences in such processes.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how much organizations spend to manage external legitimacy in the context of corporate giving. It examines how positive media coverage of giving, negative media coverage of lawsuits against family owners, and firm performance jointly shape the ratio of corporate giving to sales.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1a. The greater the positive media coverage a firm receives about its corporate giving, the more corporate giving it is likely to offer.
H1b. The effect of positive media coverage on a firm’s corporate giving is greater for high-performing firms, compared to low-performing firms.
H2a. The greater the negative media coverage a firm receives about the lawsuits against its family owners, the less corporate giving it is likely to offer.
H2b. The effect of negative media coverage on a firm’s corporate giving is smaller for high-performing firms, compared to low-performing firms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Positive media coverage of a firm's corporate giving; negative media coverage of lawsuits against a firm's family owners.
- DV(s): Corporate giving, measured as the ratio of corporate giving to sales in a given year.
- Mediators: No mediator is directly tested.
- Moderators: Firm performance, measured as return on assets.

The mechanism is a tension between social legitimacy and economic efficiency. Positive media attention raises external audience expectations, so firms spend more on corporate giving to maintain legitimacy; this effect is stronger among high-performing firms because they have more room to bear the cost. Negative media attention to lawsuits against family owners creates legitimacy pressure, but it also creates immediate efficiency pressure; firms therefore reduce giving, and the reduction is unexpectedly stronger among high performers, suggesting that they rely more heavily on economic efficiency logic under severe conflict.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses an unbalanced firm-year panel of all publicly listed nonfinancial firms on the KOSPI market from 2003 to 2011; the final sample contained 5,778 firm-year observations for 747 publicly listed companies. Corporate giving data, corporate governance information, and financial data were compiled and cross-checked from three databases of KOSPI-listed firms: KISLINE (NICE Information Service), TS2000 (Korea Listed Companies Association), and the DART repository of Korea’s corporate filings (Financial Supervisory Service). Media content analyses used the Korean Integrated News Database System (KINDS) operated by the Korea Press Foundation, supplemented with search results from the Chosun and Joongang websites, giving a content analysis of 12 general content newspapers and eight business-oriented newspapers; face-to-face and telephone interviews with executives, foundation staff, journalists, and certified public accountants were also conducted to investigate the robustness of the study’s findings. The dependent variable, corporate giving, is measured as the ratio of corporate giving to sales in a given year, taken from each firm’s income statement, where the reported item covers only monetary donations (direct cash contributions and the value of product donations) and excludes nonmonetary charitable activities such as employees’ volunteered time. Positive media coverage of a firm’s giving is the natural logarithm of the number of articles reporting a firm’s corporate giving in a positive way across 20 major daily newspapers: articles containing the firm’s name and any of the keywords “donation,” “social contributions,” and “social responsibility” were coded independently by two trained research assistants for whether they reported the firm’s philanthropic efforts favorably (kappa = .81, 99% agreement). Negative media coverage of lawsuits against a firm’s family owners is the natural logarithm of the number of articles reporting legal actions that different external constituents brought against the family owners of a given firm: family owners were identified from each company’s shareholders list in KISLINE, articles containing their names and the keyword “lawsuit” were retrieved from the same 20 newspapers, and two trained research assistants independently verified them (kappa = .88, 97% agreement). Firm profitability is measured as the ROA, and the two interaction terms were constructed by multiplying each of the two media independent variables with ROA to test Hypotheses 1b and 2b. Controls include a lagged ratio of corporate giving to sales, firm size (natural logarithm of total assets), firm age, the debt-to-equity ratio, advertisement intensity and R&D intensity (measured by the ratios of R&D and advertisement expenses to total sales), positive and negative media coverage of a firm’s financial performance and of a firm’s products (all logged and built through web scraping of over 6.4 million articles plus manual verification), a sustainability report dummy, family ownership (percentage of shares owned by individual family members and family-controlled firms), business group affiliation (top 30 business groups), newly listed and delisted firm dummies, logged government penalties from the Korea Fair Trade Commission Enforcement Database, ROA, and an industry average giving ratio computed after classifying sample firms into 16 industries under the Korean Standard Industrial Classification; the notes to Tables 3 and 4 state that fifteen industries and eight year dummies are included in all models. Estimation uses the generalized estimating equation (GEE) method of Liang and Zeger (1986) with an exchangeable correlation structure and Huber–White robust standard errors. The design is associational and the paper claims no causal identification strategy for its main models; reverse causality is addressed only through robustness checks — re-measuring the two media variables over earlier time windows of the year (January–April, January–May, January–June), two-stage regressions using the sum of positive and negative media attention on a firm’s financial performance and products as an instrument, and a regression of positive media coverage of a firm’s giving on the ratio of corporate giving to sales.

**Key Findings**
The results provide strong support for Hypothesis 1a: the extent to which a firm received positive media coverage about its giving behaviors was positively and significantly associated with its corporate giving ratio, both when entered alone (b = 0.032, p < .001, Model 2) and in the full model (b = 0.015, p < .05, Model 5). In Model 2, when the number of positive articles increased from the minimum (0) to 50 and to the maximum (189), the ratio of corporate giving to sales increased by 0.13% and 0.17% respectively, corresponding on average to about $1.6 million and $2.1 million USD. Hypothesis 2a was also supported: the coefficients on negative media coverage of lawsuits against a firm’s family owners were negative and statistically significant separately (b = −0.030, p < .05, Model 3) and in the full model (b = −0.025, p < .05, Model 5), and in Model 3 an increase in negative articles from the minimum (0) to 25 and to the maximum (57) decreased the giving ratio by 0.10% and 0.12%, about $1.3 million and $1.5 million USD. Hypothesis 1b received full support: the interaction between positive media coverage and firm performance was positive and statistically significant separately and in the full model (b = 0.003, p < .01, Models 4 and 5), and the plotted interaction shows that while both groups give more as positive coverage increases, the effect is greater for high performers (top quartile ROA = 7.25) than for low performers (bottom quartile ROA = 0.56). Hypothesis 2b was not supported, and the result is the opposite of what it predicted: the interaction between negative media coverage and firm performance was negative and significant (b = −0.002, p < .05, Model 5), and while both groups give less as media attention to legal conflicts increases, the decline in corporate giving is greater — the negative slope is steeper — for high performers than for low performers, implying that under worsening conflicts with external constituents high performers rely more heavily on an economic efficiency logic. Additional analyses found the interaction between the two media independent variables nonsignificant (p = .91), and a test of the null that the absolute values of the positive and negative media coefficients are equal in Model 3 was not rejected, chi-square(1, n = 5,778) = 0.04, p = .78, two-tailed, which the authors read as neither variable being strongly dominant over the other. Robustness checks left the conclusions substantially the same: measuring the two media variables only over earlier windows of the year (Table 4) supported strong relationships for Hypotheses 1a and 2a, two-stage regressions produced results consistent with Table 3, regressing positive media coverage of a firm’s giving on the giving ratio produced no statistically significant relationship for either the lagged or unlagged giving ratio variable, and adding family-ownership threshold dummies did not change the results. Among the controls, firms with a high ratio of corporate giving in the previous year, high-performing firms, and firms with higher investment in advertising tended to give more, and positive media coverage of a firm’s financial performance was associated with greater corporate giving, whereas negative media coverage of a firm’s product recall and the release of an annual sustainability report were associated with less corporate giving.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends institutional theory by treating legitimacy management cost as a variable rather than an assumed or negligible condition. It argues that conformity to institutional expectations should be analyzed not only through legitimacy benefits and illegitimacy penalties, but also through the costs organizations pay to manage external legitimacy.

It also contributes to research on external audiences by showing that positive and negative media evaluations have different dynamics in corporate giving. Positive media feedback reinforces giving, whereas negative media attention to family-owner lawsuits is associated with less giving, especially under high firm performance.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that managers should treat corporate giving as a recurring legitimacy-management decision under resource constraints, not simply as an ad hoc philanthropic act. Positive media attention can raise future audience expectations, while legal conflicts involving central owners can shift attention and resources toward immediate efficiency concerns.

For external audiences, the study implies that visibility and evaluation matter: media coverage helps shape how much firms spend on corporate giving. For firms in governance contexts where family owners are central to organizational identity, legal disputes involving those owners may materially affect social spending decisions.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that not all institutional conformity is costly, because some organizations may symbolically adopt practices without full implementation. The study's Korean KOSPI context and the prevalence of family-controlled firms also limit generalizability to other countries and unlisted private firms.

The data measure aggregate annual corporate giving and do not observe the portfolio of charitable activities or how spending is distributed across causes and stakeholders. The study also does not jointly observe legitimacy management costs, illegitimacy penalties, and legitimacy benefits in one integrated empirical design.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how legitimacy management cost shapes not only adoption of institutionalized practices but also the scope and degree of implementation. Studies in other countries, markets, and unlisted private firms could test whether the same legitimacy-efficiency dynamics hold outside the Korean listed-firm context.

Future work could also collect portfolio-level corporate giving data and integrate legitimacy management costs, illegitimacy penalties, and legitimacy benefits in the same design. Such research would clarify how organizations decide whether legitimacy management improves performance or survival.
