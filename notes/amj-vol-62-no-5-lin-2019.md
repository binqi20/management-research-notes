# The Dark Side of Transformational Leader Behaviors for Leaders Themselves: A Conservation of Resources Perspective

**Abstract**
The vast majority of theory and research to date on transformational leadership has focused on how transformational leader behaviors influence followers, portraying those behaviors as unequivocally beneficial. We pivot from this predominant focus to a focus on the detriments of transformational leader behaviors for leaders themselves. Drawing from conservation of resources theory, we propose that although transformational leader behaviors produce gains for both leaders and followers, those behaviors also trigger losses for leaders themselves. The results of two experience-sampling studies, whereby leaders and their followers completed weekly surveys for six weeks, revealed that transformational leader behaviors were associated with increases in leader emotional exhaustion and subsequent leader turnover intentions, and these detrimental consequences occurred over and above benefits to followers (Study 1) and benefits to leaders themselves (Study 2). Furthermore, the extent to which transformational leader behaviors were associated with increases in emotional exhaustion depended upon attributes of followers, such that leaders experienced greater increases in emotional exhaustion when their transformational leader behaviors were directed toward followers low in conscientiousness (Study 1) and competence (Study 2). Overall, our work provides answers to both why and under what conditions the dark side of transformational leader behaviors is likely to appear.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether transformational leader behaviors have detrimental intrapersonal consequences for leaders even while producing follower and leader benefits. It also asks when those detriments are more likely to emerge, focusing on follower conscientiousness and follower competence as boundary conditions.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Within leaders, there is a positive, indirect effect of transformational leader behaviors on leader turnover intentions through leader emotional exhaustion.
Hypothesis 2. Follower (a) conscientiousness and (b) competence moderate the positive, indirect effect of transformational leader behaviors on leader turnover intentions through leader emotional exhaustion, such that the indirect effect is weaker for followers high in conscientiousness (competence) and stronger for followers low in conscientiousness (competence).

Study 1 tested Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2a; the authors state that competence was not measured in Study 1. Study 2 tested Hypothesis 1, Hypothesis 2a, and Hypothesis 2b, examining conscientiousness and competence as moderators simultaneously.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Transformational leader behaviors.
- DV(s): Leader turnover intentions.
- Mediators: Leader emotional exhaustion.
- Moderators: Follower conscientiousness in Study 1; follower conscientiousness and follower competence in Study 2.

Drawing on conservation of resources theory, the paper argues that transformational leader behaviors require leaders to expend time, energy, emotion regulation, and attention. In the short term, those resource expenditures increase leader emotional exhaustion, and emotional exhaustion increases leader turnover intentions as leaders seek to protect remaining resources. The boundary-condition evidence differs across studies: follower conscientiousness moderated the indirect effect in Study 1, while follower competence, rather than broad conscientiousness, emerged as the significant moderator in Study 2.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 collected data from 130 pairs of leaders and followers employed in a variety of industries, including banking, communications, education, healthcare, and manufacturing. Students in management courses recruited a full-time employee, who in turn recruited his or her direct supervisor. A baseline survey one week before the experience-sampling phase captured demographics and follower conscientiousness; both leaders and followers were then sent a survey at the end of each week for six straight weeks. Of 156 dyads that initially agreed, 130 completed 591 weekly matched surveys (an average of 4.56 weekly surveys per dyad, a 76% response rate); an examination of user agent strings and timestamps uncovered no suspicious data, so all completed matched surveys were retained. Items used a 5-point Likert scale. Follower-rated transformational leader behaviors used five items from the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (Bass & Avolio, 1997; averaged α = .90). Leader and follower emotional exhaustion used a 5-item scale from Pugh, Groth, and Hennig-Thurau (2011; averaged α = .90 for leaders and .91 for followers). Leader turnover intentions used the 4-item measure of Kelloway, Gottlieb, and Barham (1999; averaged α = .91). Follower OCB used 12 items adapted from Dalal, Lam, Weiss, Welch, and Hulin (2009; averaged α = .87), follower positive affect the 5-item PANAS short form of Mackinnon et al. (1999; averaged α = .86), and follower conscientiousness Saucier's (1994) eight-word adjective checklist (α = .87).

Study 2 used a similar procedure, except that the recruited employee also recruited at least two coworkers so that each leader was rated by multiple followers; participants were employed in a variety of industries, including banking, education, healthcare, and retail. A baseline survey assessed demographics, follower conscientiousness, and follower competence, and weekly surveys were sent at the end of each week for six consecutive weeks. Of 97 groups that initially agreed, 79 leaders and 217 followers completed 394 weekly matched surveys (a response rate of 83%); after removing questionable observations, the final sample consisted of 77 leaders and 211 followers who completed 378 weekly matched surveys (an average of 4.90 weekly surveys per group). Transformational leader behaviors used all 20 MLQ items covering idealized influence, inspirational motivation, intellectual stimulation, and individualized consideration (averaged α = .97), aggregated across followers on the basis of ICC evidence. Leader emotional exhaustion used a 5-item scale adapted from Maslach and Jackson (1986; averaged α = .90) and leader turnover intentions the same items as Study 1 (averaged α = .97). Leader work engagement used three items from Rich, Lepine, and Crawford (2010; averaged α = .64), leader need fulfillment nine items from La Guardia, Ryan, Couchman, and Deci (2000; averaged α = .76), and follower-rated leader positive and negative affect the 5-item PANAS short form (averaged α = .92 for positive affect and .95 for negative affect). Follower conscientiousness used the same items as Study 1 (α = .81), and follower competence 10 items from the International Personality Item Pool (Gough, 1996; α = .82).

Both studies were analyzed with multilevel path analysis in Mplus 7.11, with within-person variables at Level 1 using random slopes for hypothesized paths and between-person moderators at Level 2. Level 2 predictors were grand-mean centered and Level 1 predictors group-mean centered, and prior levels of leader emotional exhaustion and leader turnover intentions were entered as fixed-slope controls so that coefficients are interpreted as changes. Previously established benefits were modeled as alternative mechanisms: follower OCB, positive affect, and emotional exhaustion in Study 1; leader work engagement, need fulfillment, positive affect, and negative affect in Study 2. Indirect effects were estimated with a parametric bootstrap and a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 replications. The design is associational rather than causal: the authors state that focal variables were measured at the same time and that their studies cannot establish causality.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (591 weekly observations from 130 dyads) supported Hypothesis 1. Transformational leader behaviors were associated with an increase in leader emotional exhaustion from the previous week (γ = .19, p < .05), leader emotional exhaustion was related to an increase in leader turnover intentions from the previous week (γ = .26, p < .01), and the indirect effect was positive and significant (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [.004, .104]). Transformational leader behaviors were also positively related to follower OCB (γ = .29, p < .01) and follower positive affect (γ = .14, p < .05) but, contrary to prior research, were not related to follower emotional exhaustion (γ = .04, n.s.). The total indirect effect through emotional exhaustion and these alternative mechanisms was not significant (total indirect effect = .06, 95% CI [–.021, .131]). Hypothesis 2a was supported: follower conscientiousness exhibited a cross-level moderating effect (γ = –.30, p < .05), such that the relationship between transformational leader behaviors and leader emotional exhaustion was stronger when followers were low in conscientiousness (b = .31, p < .01) than when they were high (b = .03, n.s.); the indirect effect was significant at low conscientiousness (estimate .07, 95% CI [.017, .134]) but not at high conscientiousness (estimate .01, 95% CI [–.045, .057]), and the difference was significant (estimate = –.06, 95% CI [–.139, –.007]).

Study 2 (378 weekly observations from 77 leaders) replicated Hypothesis 1: transformational leader behaviors were associated with an increase in leader emotional exhaustion (γ = .19, p < .05), leader emotional exhaustion with an increase in leader turnover intentions (γ = .28, p < .01), and the indirect effect was positive and significant (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [.009, .116]). Transformational leader behaviors were positively related to leader work engagement (γ = .27, p < .01) and leader positive affect (γ = .56, p < .01), negatively related to leader negative affect (γ = –.35, p < .01), and, contrary to prior research, not related to leader need fulfillment (γ = .01, n.s.); the total indirect effect on leader turnover intentions was again not significant (total indirect effect = –.09, 95% CI [–.247, .060]). The moderation results differed from Study 1. In contrast to Study 1, conscientiousness did not moderate the within-person relationship between transformational leader behavior and leader emotional exhaustion (γ = .30, n.s.), and the difference in indirect effects at high versus low follower conscientiousness was not significant (estimate = .03, 95% CI [–.062, .261]). Hypothesis 2b was supported: over and above follower conscientiousness, follower competence exhibited a cross-level moderating effect (γ = –.69, p < .01), such that the relationship was stronger for followers with low competence (b = .40, p < .01) than for followers with high competence (b = –.11, n.s.); the indirect effect was significant for followers low in competence (estimate .07, 95% CI [.009, .164]) but not for followers high in competence (estimate –.02, 95% CI [–.082, .029]), and the difference was significant (estimate = –.09, 95% CI [–.294, –.011]). The paper's Study 2 results section closes this passage with the sentence "Thus, Hypothesis 3 was partially supported."

Across the two studies the detrimental indirect path replicated, but the follower-personality boundary condition did not: the effect of transformational leader behaviors on leader emotional exhaustion, and its indirect effect on leader turnover intentions, were stronger for leaders with followers low in conscientiousness in Study 1 and low in competence in Study 2. Robustness checks in both studies also showed that transformational leader behaviors in the previous week were negatively related to transformational leader behaviors in the current week (γ = –.12, p < .05 in Study 1; γ = –.14, p < .05 in Study 2).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts transformational leadership research from its predominant follower-centered focus to an actor-centric account of consequences for leaders themselves. It uses conservation of resources theory to challenge the assumption that transformational leadership is uniformly positive by showing that the same behaviors can create leader benefits and leader resource losses. It also extends within-person leadership theory by showing that follower characteristics shape the intrapersonal consequences of leader behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest caution in encouraging managers to display transformational leader behaviors consistently without attention to recovery and context. Leaders may need breaks, recovery activities, and more strategic deployment of transformational behaviors when they are depleted or when followers are less likely to benefit from those efforts. Organizations should recognize that behaviors often treated as desirable can still carry resource costs for leaders.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the studies measured focal variables at the same time, so the experience-sampling and lagged controls do not establish causality. Study 1 relied on one follower report, raising concerns about selection bias and representativeness, although Study 2 included multiple followers. The paper also focuses on short-term proximal outcomes, transformational leader behaviors only, and follower characteristics rather than directly measuring whether followers waste leaders' transformational efforts.

**Future Research**
Future research could use designs that better establish causality and test long-term consequences of transformational leader behaviors for burnout, turnover, and career mobility. The authors also call for work on other positive and negative leader behaviors, leader characteristics as moderators, and direct assessment of whether followers actually waste leaders' transformational efforts or whether leaders merely perceive those efforts as wasted.
