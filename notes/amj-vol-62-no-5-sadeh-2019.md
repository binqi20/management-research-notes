# Bringing “Together”: Emotions and Power in Organizational Responses to Institutional Complexity

**Abstract**
The theoretical puzzle of how organizations deal with contradicting logics has been extensively investigated during the past two decades. This stream of research has focused on the cognitive, but has overlooked emotions and power, which are fundamental to the lived experience of logics, and to their constitution. Drawing on a 15-month ethnography of “Together,” a Jewish–Palestinian organization in a mixed city in Israel, we explore how the organization succeeds in challenging societal dominant notions of ethno-nationalism by stimulating universalistic notions, and how it occasionally fails to do so, as ethno-nationalism creeps in. Our findings indicate that emotional control allows organizations to deal with contradictory logics and achieve their desired constellation, while unbidden emotions disrupt local efforts and enable changes in desired constellations of logics. Further, systemic power mediates the very experience of logics and the prospects of changing their constellation: Social asymmetry necessitates harder emotion work on behalf of the underprivileged, differentiating their experience of logics, and limiting the extent to which emotional eruption is a viable option. Thus, our study highlights the intersection of logics, emotions, and power, and how logics are managed and failed to be managed.

**Research Question**
The paper asks what role emotions play in stabilizing or destabilizing constellations of logics inside organizations facing institutional complexity. It also asks how systemic power shapes the emotional generation of institutional stability and change.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Universalistic logic, ethno-national logic, emotional control, emotional eruption, emotion rules, emotion work, and systemic power.
- Process / routine stabilization: Material objects, texts, and interactions generate emotion rules that cultivate hope, closeness, care, love, safety, and avoidance, helping Together sustain universalism during routine periods.
- Triggering conditions: External political or violent events, especially Memorial and Independence Days and the 2012 Israel-Gaza War, activate ethno-national emotions such as fear, suspicion, anger, tension, and splitting.
- Emergent outcomes: Emotional eruptions temporarily make ethno-nationalism more salient inside the organization, even though the universalistic constellation is restored after events pass.
- Power dynamics / boundary conditions: Jewish and Palestinian members face unequal emotion work; Palestinian members must work harder to bracket discrimination and movement restrictions, while emotional eruption is more organizationally available around events salient to Jewish society.

The process is not a simple causal variable model. Together maintains a desired constellation of logics by controlling emotional content and emotional process through material, textual, and interactional arrangements. When external events pierce this routine control, unbidden emotions make the rejected ethno-national logic salient. Systemic power mediates both sides of the process by shaping which events count as disruptive and how much emotion work privileged and underprivileged members must perform.

**Data & Measures**
Data corpus (Table 1, "Data sources between March 2012 and June 2013"): 15 months of participant observation in Together, in which the first author observed the sport, education, youth, dialogue, restaurant, hotel, and cleaning and maintenance departments both in daily routines and during festive activities and formal meetings, plus interdepartmental activities such as management meetings and organizational celebrations, writing extensive field notes during or immediately after each activity that recorded the activity attended, her own emotions, and her initial interpretations. Interviews: 117 interviews, an hour and a half long on average, with 72 interviewees (61 insiders and 11 outsiders who keep close contact with Together; 28 Palestinian, 41 Jewish, 3 other nationality; 40 male, 32 female), of whom seven were central informants re-interviewed at different points across the research period. The semi-structured protocol explored positive and negative experiences at work, relational dynamics, organizational structure, the effects of joint Jewish-Arab work on the organization, and relationships between Together and its environments. Observations: 59 formal meetings, 21 team meetings, 9 various organizational events, and 93 sporadic days of ongoing observation. Archival data: email correspondence among members and with people in the organizational environment, protocols of meetings including ones the first author did not attend, letters from the CEO to employees, letters and requests from donors, and official documents. Most interviews were held in English or Hebrew and only two in Arabic, given the first author's limited knowledge of the language. Quote provenance is labeled throughout as "Int." for formal interviews, "Convrs." for informal conversation, "doc." for documents, and "Obs." for verbatim extracts from the field journal.

Constructs are coded rather than measured, and the analysis proceeded in four stages. First, institutional logics were captured with the pattern-inducing technique and content analysis, systematically generating, verifying, discarding, combining, and revising coded groupings into main categories; this initially yielded two logics, coexistence and conflict, later understood in more general terms as universalism and ethno-nationalism, and validated by iteratively comparing the empirical data against the literature on nationalism and universalism. Second, emotions were analyzed with multiple analytical tools: experienced emotions shared during interviews or with others in daily interaction; overt emotional displays such as laughter, crying, speech rate, tone of voice, and emotional trajectories within interactions; and covert emotional expressions including emotionally charged metaphors, nonverbal communication such as gestures, gazes, and body postures, and what was not said. These markers were interpreted by situating them in their context and examining their related content, their object, the interactional sequence surrounding them, and the logics they related to. Third, the enactment of logics and emotions was compared across nationality (Jewish or Palestinian) and time (event or daily routine), using a timeline of all national, political, and religious events in the research period (Figure 1) to check how, if at all, each event resonated in the organization. Fourth, the theoretical model (Figure 2) was developed. The design is inductive and interpretive and claims no causal identification. Reported validating mechanisms include sensitivity to textual markers of power relations such as "the languages of the unsayable," use of the first author's field-note reflections on experiences such as being avoided, and discussion of interpretations between the first author, who collected the data, and the second author, who had a more distant relation to it.

**Key Findings**
Universalism is the dominant logic inside Together during routine times, and its dominance is an ongoing achievement in which emotional control plays a central part. Emotion rules prescribe the emotional contents that constitute universalism, namely hope, closeness, care, love, and safety in relation to all national groups, together with an ongoing emotional process of avoidance; they simultaneously deem illegitimate the display of animosity, anger, suspicion, and fear toward members of the other nationality, as well as helplessness, alertness, and stress. These rules are formed and maintained through the design of the physical surroundings, the production of texts, and the give and take of interactions, and the authors conclude that such organizationally enforced emotion rules can effectively stabilize a desired constellation of logics in routine times even when that constellation is antithetic to the constellation of logics in society.

Emotional control is not complete. Emotions that constitute the ethno-national logic are generated externally, via intensive political or violent events at the societal level, and materialize inside the organization through informal interactions. Over the 15-month stay, three external events brought about emotions that changed the internal constellation of logics: Memorial and Independence Days, which were noted twice during the fieldwork, and the 2012 Israel-Gaza War. During these episodes unbidden emotions such as fear, suspicion, anger, and tension surfaced alongside an emotional process of splitting, and emotional eruptions temporarily undermined the strong organizational commitment to universalism and made the ethno-national logic more salient. Because the material, textual, and interactional generators of universalistic emotions are enduring and widespread, the desired constellation of logics was reestablished soon after the episodes of emotional eruption, even without additional active efforts.

Systemic power mediates both loops. Societal asymmetry means that being Palestinian entails experiencing the hardships of a discriminated minority on a daily basis while being Jewish means sharing the identity of the privileged majority, so harder emotion work is needed on behalf of Palestinian members to put aside their external reality and adhere to universalism; on this basis the authors propose that even when the same emotion rules apply to members of all subgroups, their implications differ, and that differentiated emotion work also means a different experience of logics. Power also determines which events register as disruptive: external events relating mostly to Jewish society in Israel resonated in the emotional dynamics within Together and changed the constellation of logics, whereas events involving mostly Palestinian society, including Nakba Day, Land Day, and violent incidents against Palestinians such as the one named "The Lynch," did not resonate inside the organization and were dismissed by both Jewish and Palestinian inhabitants as irrelevant. The authors further report that avoidance of ethno-national-related emotions left no room for joint reinterpretation or collective sensemaking of unbidden emotions, and, based on prior research, suggest that this lack of collective sensemaking is connected to the unwanted change in the constellation of logics once disruptive emotions erupt.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional complexity research by moving beyond cognitive mechanisms such as sensemaking and negotiation to show how emotions and power constitute the lived experience of logics. It theorizes emotional control as a mechanism through which organizations stabilize a desired constellation of logics, and emotional eruption as a mechanism through which rejected logics re-enter organizational life. It also extends institutional theory by showing that emotion rules are not neutral: systemic power differentiates members' experience of logics and the organizational consequences of emotional eruption.

**Practical Implication**
The paper implies that organizations pursuing social change against dominant societal logics cannot rely only on formal goals, inclusive symbols, or avoidance of conflict. They need to recognize that emotional control may preserve cooperation in routine periods while also suppressing the sensemaking needed when disruptive events occur. Leaders in conflict-laden or hybrid organizations should attend to unequal emotional burdens across groups, because apparently shared emotion rules can require much heavier work from underprivileged members.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the ethnographic design provides depth but is based on one organization, so analytic generalization is bound by the study context. The case involves an organization committed to a logic that challenges a dominant societal logic, and it involves highly incompatible logics, which may limit transferability to organizations where logics are more compatible or where emotion rules align with dominant institutions. The authors also discuss limits linked to the first author's Jewish identity and limited Arabic, which constrained access to some Palestinian members' experiences.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how emotional dynamics influence organizational responses to institutional complexity under different degrees of dominance between logics and in contexts where logics are more compatible. The paper also calls for work on when collective sensemaking moderates the effect of emotional eruptions on constellations of logics. More broadly, future studies should connect affective, political, and cognitive processes in institutional complexity.
