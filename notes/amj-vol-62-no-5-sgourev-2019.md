# From Montagues to Capulets: Analyzing the Systemic Nature of Rivalry in Career Mobility

**Abstract**
The paper proposes a systemic theory of rivalry that addresses a key limitation of past research by articulating how rivalry spreads across levels of analysis and between social domains. Shifting the perspective from what rivalry does to what it prevents from doing, we conceptualize rivalry as a system of relational constraints that extends beyond the level of direct rivals. Using qualitative and quantitative data on the mobility of jockeys in the Palio di Siena (1743–2011), the famous horse race in Siena, Italy, we document relational gaps in the mobility network—career moves that do not occur, even if expected on economic grounds. The analysis shows that rivalry constrains not only the moves to rivals but also to allies of rivals and rivals of allies. The effect becomes stronger over time, attesting that relational constraints persist in developed markets. Our fieldwork reveals that the constraining ability of rivalry is based on conformity to expectations of loyalty and adherence to group identities. The observed relational gaps are pertinent not only to scholarship on rivalry but also to mobility and network research.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how rivalry operates as a systemic form of relational constraint rather than only as a dyadic psychological relationship. It examines whether rivalries among Siena's contrade constrain jockey career mobility not only between direct rivals, but also through allies of rivals, rivals of allies, and increasingly professionalized market conditions.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The existence of a rivalry between two organizations reduces the likelihood of a career move between them.
- H2a: The likelihood of a career move between two organizations is reduced when one organization is a rival of the other's allies.
- H2b: The likelihood of a career move between two organizations is reduced when one organization is an ally of the other's rivals.
- H3: The negative effect of rivalry on career mobility (Hypothesis 1) becomes stronger with the development of markets.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / relational structures: Rivalry, alliances, allies of rivals, rivals of allies, professionalization, and relational gaps in mobility networks.
- Process / mechanisms: Rivalry creates expectations of loyalty and purity of affiliation; jockeys who move to a rival or adjacent rival camp risk stigmatization, damaged relationships, and possible retaliation.
- Empirical outcomes: Career moves between contrade, modeled as dyad-year mobility ties.
- Boundary / system conditions: The constraining effect is tested in a competitive labor market for Palio jockeys and examined as the market professionalizes over time.

The paper's process argument is that rivalry organizes social structure by creating categorical boundaries around friend and foe. Those boundaries make some otherwise plausible career moves socially costly, even when competitive labor-market logic would predict mobility toward attractive employers. Alliances extend the effect beyond direct rival dyads, so jockeys avoid not only rivals but also allies of rivals and rivals of allies. Professionalization does not erase these relational constraints; in the authors' account, it reinforces adherence to informal norms because reputation and professional standing depend on respecting the rivalry system.

**Data & Measures**
- Quantitative data and unit of analysis: Historical Palio data from the archives of the municipality of Siena and of individual neighborhoods by Orlando Papei (2015) and Sergio Profeti (2015), plus books recording the races over the last three centuries. The authors reconstructed the career moves of 480 jockeys between 1743 and 2011. The unit of analysis is the neighborhood-dyad year; the main models use 44,196 dyad-year observations across 272 dyads.
- DV: Move_ijt took the value of 1 if at least one career move was observed from contrada i (sender) to contrada j (receiver) in year t.
- Key IV and second-order network variables: Rival_ijt was set to 1 if a formally declared rivalry between contrada i and contrada j existed in period t, and 0 otherwise; Ally_ijt was set to 1 if a formally declared alliance existed in year t. Allies of Rivals_ij was obtained by multiplying the rivalry network by the alliance network, and Rivals of Allies_ij by multiplying the alliance network by the rivalry network. Models also included Ally of Ally, derived from the alliance network, and Rival of Rival, derived from the rivalry network. All matrices were directed and nonsymmetrical.
- Moderator: Professionalization_t, measured in two ways. The first counts the number of alternative historical horse races each year that employ jockeys from Siena, based on four identified races in Asti, Ferrara, Fucecchio, and Legnano. The second counts active regulatory constraints on jockeys' activity across four regulatory dimensions identified from expert evaluation: eligibility restrictions based on age limits, permission of kinship ties between participants, jockeys' personal liability, and the obligation for the employment of jockeys to be listed in a formal register.
- Controls: Performance Gap_ijt (difference in victories between contrada i and contrada j in a given year t), Competitive Overlap_ijt (number of races in which the two contrade competed against each other over the last five years), Status Differences_ijt (coded -1 when a jockey moved from a contrada with an honorary Nobile title to one without, 0 between contrade of equal status, and 1 for a move from a low-status to a Noble contrada), a geographic-proximity network coded 1 for a shared border, past mobility, dyad fixed effects, and a dummy variable for each year (2011 omitted).
- Estimation and identification: A dyad-level panel fixed-effects logit model with year dummy variables, with the fixed-effects specification chosen on a Hausman test. The design is observational; the authors report associations and use dyad and year fixed effects rather than claiming an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy. Robustness specifications use an alternative Performance Gap measure (Model 6), rivalry strength as the logarithm of rivalry duration (Model 7), hiring-contrada incentive dummies (Model 8), captain-level controls (Model 9, 37,810 observations, because captain data are available from the beginning of the 19th century), a linear probability model (Model 10), a pooled logit with double-clustered standard errors (Model 11, 43,866 observations), and a conditional logit with standard errors clustered by dyad (Model 12).
- Network models: Stochastic actor-oriented models estimated with rSiena on career moves aggregated into five 50-year windows (1762-1811, 1812-1861, 1862-1911, 1912-1961, and post-1961). Move counts were normalized to a z score and dichotomized at 0, yielding five 17 x 17 binary, directed, one-mode mobility networks as the dependent variable, with individual and dyadic effects the same as in Table 2 plus structural effects (density, reciprocity, transitivity, actors at distance 2, indegree and outdegree popularity).
- Qualitative data and coding: 36 interviews with active and retired jockeys, captains, priori, lieutenants, contrada members, and Palio experts, lasting between 30 and 90 minutes, recorded and transcribed. The authors attended six Palios and visited Siena eight times between 2013 and 2018. They also obtained the text of 75 interviews conducted between 2012 and 2014 by a local journalist permitted to interview all jockeys and captains before each Palio. Using QSR NVivo, they first open coded instances in the data where informants referred to rivalry, its nature, and implications for the Palio, then used refined axial coding, which guided theory development and helped validate the measures and modeling approach.

**Key Findings**
- Descriptive pattern: The data set featured 46 instances of alliance formation and 33 of alliance termination between 1743 and 2011, with 11 rivalries formed and six terminated. Of the 3,072 total career moves in the sample, only 2% occurred between rivals, and the mean probability of a move was 6.4%.
- H1 supported: the effect of rivalry on the probability of a career move is negative and significant (Model 2, b = -1.01, p < .001). The odds of a move for pairs of rival contrade over the odds for the reference group is 0.36, so the odds of a move between rivals are lower by 64%.
- H2a (rival of the other's allies): the results are in line with the expectation, with the odds of a move to a rival of an ally at 0.84 of the odds for the control group. The effect is marginally significant in Model 3 (b = -0.17, p < .10, OR = 0.84) and significant at conventional levels in Model 4 (b = -0.19, p < .05, OR = 0.83).
- H2b (ally of the other's rivals): Model 3 provides evidence in this respect (b = -0.21, p < .05). The authors state that the evidence for these second-order effects is relatively weaker compared to that for the other hypotheses, and attribute the pattern in their fieldwork to the possible emergence of unbalanced triads in the first decades of the 20th century.
- H3 supported: the interaction of Professionalization and Rivalry is negative and significant (Model 4, b = -0.22, p < .05), and the same pattern holds with the alternative regulatory-restriction measure of professionalization (Model 5, b = -0.47, p < .01). Holding professionalization at its mean, rivalry reduces the probability of a career move by 8.6%; at mean + 1 SD it reduces it by 17.9%, and at mean - 1 SD by only 1.1%. Professionalization itself has a significant positive association with the likelihood of a career move (b = 1.99, p < .001).
- Robustness for H1 and H3 (Tables 3 and 4): both hold across every alternative specification - alternative Performance Gap (Model 6, rivalry b = -0.69, p < .01; interaction b = -0.22, p < .05), rivalry strength (Model 7, b = -0.25, p < .001; interaction b = -0.05, p = .05), hiring-incentive controls (Model 8, which the authors report as corroborating Table 2), captain-level controls (Model 9, b = -0.63, p < .05; interaction b = -0.24, p < .05), the linear probability model (Model 10, b = -0.03, p < .01; interaction b = -0.01, p < .05), the pooled logit with double-clustered standard errors (Model 11, b = -0.75, p < .01; interaction b = -0.22, p < .05), and the conditional logit (Model 12, b = -0.69, p < .01; interaction b = -0.22, p < .05).
- Robustness for the second-order hypotheses is heterogeneous, not uniform: both are significant at p < .05 in Model 6 (rivals of allies b = -0.19; allies of rivals b = -0.21) and Model 7 (b = -0.19 and b = -0.22); both weaken to p < .10 in Model 9 (Ally of Rival b = -0.16; Rival of Ally b = -0.18) and Model 11 (b = -0.21 and b = -0.18); in Model 10 the authors report weak support for H2b (b = -0.01, p < .10) and state that H2a was not supported; in Model 12 H2b is reduced (b = -0.22, p < .05) while H2a receives weaker support (b = -0.19, p < .10).
- Stochastic actor-oriented models (Table 5): the effect of Rival is negative and significant (m = -0.395, p < .05), confirming relational gaps in mobility networks net of local structure. The effect of Rival of Ally is negative and weakly significant (m = -0.161, p < .10), which the authors read as lending moderate support to H2a, while the effect of Ally of Rival is in the expected direction but not significant. A sienaTimeTest rejects the null of homogeneous rivalry constraints over time; across the full 250-year frame the effect-wise joint significance chi-squared is 6.4, p = .081, while the differences over the last three windows are highly significant (p = .04 and p = .027), and the negative effect of rivalry is greatest in the post-1961 period, corroborating H3.
- Qualitative findings and convergence: the fieldwork indicates that the constraining ability of rivalry rests on conformity to expectations of loyalty and purity of affiliation and on adherence to group identities, with informants describing moves to a rival as a normative violation that risks stigmatization and collective retaliation. Most informants focused on what rivalry prevented them from doing rather than on what it enabled. The authors report alignment between the fieldwork and the quantitative analysis, including interviews completed by a third party with no research agenda.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to rivalry scholarship by shifting attention from dyadic rivalry effects to the extended relational system in which rivalry is embedded. It links rivalry, alliance networks, and career mobility by identifying relational gaps: expected but unrealized mobility ties that arise from antagonistic network structure. It also contributes to network and mobility research by theorizing missing ties as the product of systemic relational constraints rather than only lack of opportunity, information, or economic interest.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that rivalry can spill across groups and domains, so managers should not assume rivalry remains confined to direct competitors or symbolic conflict. In organizational settings, rivalry may constrain information exchange, staffing, collaboration, or mobility through adjacent relationships as well as direct rival ties. The paper suggests managerial attention is needed to prevent rivalry from diffusing into work-related tasks and adjacent social relationships.

The authors state that managers need to be aware of both the opportunities and the threats associated with rivalry. On the opportunity side, rivalry can be a source of competitive advantage when it hinders the mobility of high-performing employees or strengthens relational factors in highly competitive markets for talent, yielding stronger commitment, higher retention rates, accumulation of tacit knowledge, and less leakage of firm-specific information to a rival. The same constraints on mobility can also make it more difficult to recruit from a rival employer, resulting in higher-than-market-rate job offers.

**Limitations**
The authors identify the lack of direct data on jockey remuneration as the main limitation, because contracts between captains and jockeys are secret. They use proxies, dyad fixed effects, and year fixed effects to address resource-endowment and market-opportunity differences, but the paper cannot directly observe compensation incentives. They also note that the study's bounded historical setting raises scope conditions for contexts where crossing organizational boundaries does not carry comparable identity or professional implications.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine the interplay between relational constraints and market incentives in labor markets with more direct compensation data. The authors also call for work on individual-level factors explaining why some actors move to rivals while others do not, other forms of market exchange beyond mobility, and relational gaps in email exchanges, casual talk, co-attendance at third-party events, and other business contexts. Additional work could test whether the consequences of rivalry extend beyond direct rivals through collaborative ties.
