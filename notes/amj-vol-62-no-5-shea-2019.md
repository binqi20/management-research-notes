# Microfoundations of Corporate Social Responsibility and Irresponsibility

**Abstract**
This study examines the importance of social perception of corporate social responsibility (CSR) and irresponsibility (CSI). Drawing from social psychology literature on stereotypes, we argue that two fundamental dimensions of social perception—warmth and competence—help explain the underlying processes and conditions under which CSR leads to specific outcomes. We propose that firms engaging in CSR are perceived as higher in warmth and, by default, competence; moreover, different perceptions of the organization’s warmth and competence can moderate CSR rewards and CSI penalties. To demonstrate this, we conduct three experiments. Experiment 1 links CSR with perceptions of warmth and competence, showing that warmth perceptions mediate the relationship between CSR and important outcomes, such as purchase intentions and reputation. Experiment 2 adds information on firms’ countries of origin, revealing that CSR rewards and CSI penalties differ depending on the (mis)alignment of CSR strategy with country stereotypes. Experiment 3 replicates these findings using behavioral paradigms. We find that firms from high-warmth countries (the United States, Sweden, Portugal) receive lower CSR rewards and pay higher CSI penalties than firms from low-warmth countries (Germany, Pakistan) but this effect is moderated by competence. Our micro–macro study advances social evaluation, strategic CSR, and international management literatures.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how individuals perceive CSR and CSI, and whether warmth and competence explain when these practices translate into outcomes such as reputation, purchase intentions, subjective quality, helping behavior, and purchase price. It also asks whether country-of-origin stereotypes change CSR rewards and CSI penalties by providing independent cues about organizational warmth and competence.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Ceteris paribus, firms engaging in CSR (CSI) will be perceived as having higher (lower) levels of warmth.
H2. Ceteris paribus, firms engaging in CSR (CSI) will be perceived as having higher (lower) levels of competence.
H3. Warmth perceptions will mediate the relationship between CSR/CSI and outcomes that involve single immediate evaluation.
H4. The higher the perception of warmth of the firm, the higher the penalties for CSI.
H5. The higher the perception of warmth of the firm, the lower the rewards for CSR.
H6. The perception of competence of the firm moderates the relationships in Hypotheses 4 and 5, such that (a) the higher the perception of warmth and competence of the firm (as opposed to lower), the lower the penalties for CSI, and (b) the lower the perception of warmth and competence (as opposed to higher), the higher the rewards to CSR.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CSR, CSI, or control information about a firm or product manufacturer; in Studies 2 and 3, country-of-origin warmth and competence cues.
- DV(s): Warmth, competence, reputation, purchase intentions, subjective product quality, helping behavior measured as feedback word count, and purchase price.
- Mediators: Warmth perceptions, with competence included in mediation models but not consistently mediating CSR/CSI effects.
- Moderators: Country-of-origin warmth and competence stereotypes, represented by the United States or Sweden, Germany, Portugal, and Pakistan.

The mechanism is that CSR acts as a warmth signal and CSI as a negative warmth signal in initial social judgments of organizations. Warmth perceptions mediate the effects of CSR and CSI on immediate evaluations such as reputation and purchase intentions, while country-of-origin stereotypes create assimilation or contrast effects. Low-warmth firms can receive larger relative CSR rewards because CSR violates expectations positively, whereas high-warmth firms can face harsher CSI penalties because irresponsibility violates expectations negatively; competence sometimes buffers or amplifies these effects.

**Data & Measures**
Three randomized experiments with no archival data. The authors state that experiments allowed them to make causal statements based on control of the environment (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002) and that participants were randomly assigned to conditions; because warmth and competence are manipulated rather than measured in Studies 2 and 3, no mediation analyses were run in those two studies.

- Study 1 (102 Mechanical Turk participants, 66 males, mean age 31, paid $0.30 in Amazon credits, all based in the United States and employed full-time): one-factor between-subjects design randomly assigning participants to a CSR, CSI, or control condition. The manipulation was a vignette about a fictitious "Company X" engaging in fair or unfair overseas manufacturing processes, taken from Sen and Bhattacharya (2001). Warmth was measured with four items (tolerant, warm, good natured, sincere; alpha = .93) and competence with five items (competent, confident, independent, competitive, intelligent; alpha = .86), both on 1-5 scales; reputation with four items (favorable, good, pleasant, positive; alpha = .97) on a 1-5 scale; and purchase intentions with a single item ("If the products from Company X were available for purchase, what is your likelihood of purchasing a product from Company X?") on a 1-7 scale. Analysis: one-way ANOVAs with planned comparisons, plus Hayes's (2013) PROCESS macros and the Preacher and Hayes (2004) bootstrap estimator with 10,000 bootstrap samples for simple and sequential mediation.
- Study 2 (572 Mechanical Turk participants, 357 males, mean age 32.13, paid $0.50 in Amazon credits, all based in the United States): three-factor between-subjects design crossing CSR state (CSR, CSI, control) with country-of-origin warmth (high, low) and competence (high, low). Country of origin was manipulated by changing the organization's name in the Study 1 vignette to U.S. Tech Corp., German Tech Corp., Portugal Tech Corp., or Pakistan Tech Corp. (Company X in the control condition); Table 2a labels the four as the United States (high, high), Germany (low, high), Portugal (high, low), and Pakistan (low, low). Warmth and competence used the same items as Study 1 (warmth alpha = .94; competence alpha = .84), and reputation (1-5) and purchase intentions (1-7) were the same two dependent variables. A pure control condition with no country-of-origin information was also run but was not included in the final analyses because it specifies no country and hence no warmth or competence. Analysis: a three-way ANOVA (condition by warmth by competence) followed by two-way ANOVAs and pairwise comparisons.
- Study 3 (100 participants recruited from a paid student research lab at a private Midwestern university; one excluded for expletive language and 11 for failing the manipulation check): mixed design with 3 (CSR, CSI, control) by 2 (order: pricing first, feedback first) between-subjects factors and country of origin as a within-subjects factor, labelled in Table 3a as Sweden (high, high), Germany (low, high), Portugal (high, low), and Pakistan (low, low). Sweden replaced the United States as the high-warmth, high-competence country to mitigate in-group bias, following a separate pre-test in which 95 Mechanical Turk workers rated the warmth and competence of seven countries against the BIAS map. Participants received $8 for participation plus $1 for a purchase decision and evaluated four types of pens bought at $0.78-$0.83 from a wholesaler and labelled "Made in Sweden," "Made in Germany," "Made in Portugal," and "Made in Pakistan" in a 1-millimeter font; the CSR, CSI, and control information was delivered as fact sheets covering labor and environmental performance. Helping was measured as the number of words written as feedback on the pen designs (M = 21.87, SD = 10.42), adapted from Porath and Erez (2007); subjective quality with two items ("The pen is of high quality," "The pen is desirable"; alphas = .72-.76) on a 5-point scale; and purchase price with the Becker-DeGroot-Marschak method on a sliding scale between $0.01 and $1.00 (M = $0.33, SD = $0.17). Warmth and competence were single-item manipulation checks. The feedback and purchasing tasks were counterbalanced and order was controlled for. Analysis: repeated-measures ANOVAs with planned comparisons and paired-samples t tests.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 102) supported H1, H2, and H3. CSR firms were judged warmer (M = 4.33, SD = 0.58) than CSI firms (M = 2.02, SD = 0.61), F(1, 67) = 265.25, and than control firms (M = 3.20, SD = 0.59), F(1, 67) = 66.71 (H1). CSR firms were judged more competent (M = 4.14, SD = 0.48) than CSI firms (M = 3.30, SD = 0.88), F(1, 67) = 24.68, but the CSR-versus-control difference was only marginal (control M = 3.93, SD = 0.50), F(1, 67) = 3.18, p = .08 (H2); the authors read this as competence being tied to the absence of CSR rather than its presence. Warmth mediated the effect of CSR on reputation (indirect effect b = 0.91, 95% CI [0.59, 1.19]) and of CSI on reputation (b = -0.95, 95% CI [-1.28, -0.67]), and likewise for purchase intentions (CSR b = 0.83, 95% CI [0.15, 1.46]; CSI b = -0.98, 95% CI [-1.84, -0.33]); competence did not consistently mediate, and models placing competence before warmth produced no indirect effects excluding zero, supporting H3.
- Study 2 (n = 572) supported H4, did not support H5, and produced mixed results for H6. Main effects reproduced the reward/penalty pattern: CSR firms attained higher reputation (M = 4.27, SD = 0.69) than CSI firms (M = 1.67, SD = 0.79) and control firms (M = 3.58, SD = 0.69), and higher purchase intentions (CSR M = 5.43, SD = 1.28; CSI M = 2.43, SD = 1.42; control M = 4.59, SD = 1.01). For H4, the CSI by warmth interaction was significant for reputation, F(1, 324) = 4.37, p = .04, with high-warmth firms facing a larger reputation penalty (Δ2.07) for CSI than low-warmth firms (Δ1.74), and for purchase intentions, F(1, 324) = 4.41, p = .04 (high-warmth Δ2.45 vs. low-warmth Δ1.88). For H5, the CSR by warmth interaction was not significant for reputation, F(1, 328) = 1.18, p = .28, or purchase intentions, F(1, 328) = 1.84, p = .18. H6a was not supported for either outcome: at low country warmth the CSI by competence interactions were significant (reputation F(1, 157) = 5.00, p = .02; purchase intentions F(1, 157) = 6.18, p = .01) but showed competence failing to buffer CSI losses, and at high country warmth neither interaction was significant. H6b was supported for both outcomes: at low country warmth the CSR by competence interaction was significant for reputation, F(1, 159) = 12.59, p = .001, and purchase intentions, F(1, 159) = 5.11, p = .03, with Pakistan (low, low) making larger relative gains from CSR than Germany (reputation ΔPakistan = 1.14 vs. ΔGermany = 0.41; purchase intentions ΔPakistan = 1.41 vs. ΔGermany = .59), while at high country warmth neither interaction was significant.
- Study 3 (100 recruited, 12 excluded) replicated H1 and H2, did not support H4, gave indirect support to H5, and produced mixed results for H6. On the warmth manipulation check, CSR participants rated the pen manufacturers warmer (M = 4.56, SD = 1.41) than CSI participants (M = 3.33, SD = 1.42), F(1, 53) = 10.18, p = .002; on competence, CSR (M = 5.56, SD = 0.82) exceeded CSI (M = 4.70, SD = 1.26), F(1, 53) = 8.56, p = .005, while CSR and control did not differ, F(1, 52) = 1.08, p = .30. Participants in the CSR condition wrote marginally more feedback words (M = 26.28, SD = 14.10) than those in the CSI condition (M = 20.53, SD = 7.22), F(1, 53) = 3.81, p = .05, and those in the control condition (M = 19.45, SD = 8.48), F(1, 52) = 4.80, p = .03, with no variation across country of origin. H4 failed: in the CSI condition high-warmth firms received marginally higher subjective quality ratings (M = 3.09, SD = 0.45) than low-warmth firms (M = 2.71, SD = 0.50), t(12) = 2.10, p = .06, and significantly higher purchase prices (M = 0.40, SD = 0.07 vs. M = 0.28, SD = 0.03), t(12) = 2.44, p = .02 — the opposite of the predicted penalty. H5 received indirect support: low-warmth firms gained significantly in subjective quality moving from CSI (M = 2.71, SD = 0.50) to CSR (M = 3.29, SD = 0.54), F(1, 23) = 7.78, p = .01 (Δ = .58), while high-warmth firms did not (Δ = .12); on purchase price, high-warmth firms' prices fell when switching from CSI to CSR (Δ = .11) while low-warmth firms' prices rose (Δ = .08). For H6a, in the CSI condition Sweden held a higher purchase price (M = 0.43, SD = 0.25) than Germany (M = 0.25, SD = 0.12), t(12) = 2.69, p = .02, and than Pakistan (M = 0.31, SD = 0.17), t(12) = 2.70, p = .02, which the authors read as some support, whereas the subjective-quality comparisons did not provide clear support for H6a. For H6b, in the CSR condition Pakistan's pens were rated higher in subjective quality (M = 3.58, SD = 0.87) than Portugal's (M = 2.83, SD = 0.65), t(11) = 2.37, p = .04, and marginally higher than Sweden's (M = 3.00, SD = 0.90), t(11) = 1.83, p = .09, supporting H6b, but no purchase-price differences emerged in the CSR condition, all ts < 1.55, ps > .14, providing no evidence for H6b on that outcome.
- Table 4 summarizes support as follows: H1 supported in all three experiments; H2 supported in Experiments 1 and 3 and not applicable in Experiment 2; H3 supported in Experiment 1 only (via sequential mediation), with mediation not run in Experiments 2 and 3 because warmth was manipulated; H4 supported in Experiment 2 but not Experiment 3; H5 not supported in Experiment 2 but supported in Experiment 3 (willingness to pay, perceived quality); and H6 mixed in both Experiments 2 and 3. The authors state that they found mixed support for some of their hypotheses and that the effects were most consistent when firms exhibited negative behaviors (CSI) and when countries of origin were low in warmth.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a microfoundational account of CSR and CSI by linking corporate social strategy to individual-level social perception. It extends CSR research beyond macro-level performance links by showing warmth and competence as psychological mechanisms and boundary conditions for CSR/CSI effects. It also connects the stereotype content model and BIAS map to strategy and international management by showing how country-of-origin stereotypes shape evaluations of firms' social behavior.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should account for how stakeholders perceive both the firm's CSR/CSI behavior and other warmth-competence cues, including country of origin. CSR may be especially useful for firms initially perceived as low in warmth, including new multinationals or firms from low-warmth country contexts, because it can improve warmth and competence perceptions. The paper also warns that high-warmth firms may be penalized more sharply for CSI because irresponsibility conflicts with the warmth expectations attached to them.

**Limitations**
The authors note that controlled experiments provide causal leverage but limit ecological scope; future work should test other scenarios, industries, stakeholders, CSR/CSI activities, outcomes, and methods. The country manipulations use actual countries, which improves realism but reduces experimental precision and limits the number of warmth-competence combinations. The study was tested in technology and manufacturing contexts, and it could not directly address decoupling or greenwashing dynamics.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine stakeholder-specific judgments, organizational-level outcomes, and additional firm or country attributes such as size, status, and industry stereotypes. The authors suggest testing these ideas with real-world data, including country-level social evaluations and cross-border mergers and acquisitions. They also call for work on how different CSR actions generate warmth or competence perceptions and how stakeholders interpret symbolic versus substantive CSR.
