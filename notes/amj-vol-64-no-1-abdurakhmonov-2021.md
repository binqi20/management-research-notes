# Unpacking Firm External Dependence: How Government Contract Dependence Affects Firm Investments and Market Performance

**Abstract**
We advance resource dependence theory (RDT) by answering calls for theorizing on both how being dependent on external entities for resources affects firms and the heterogeneous nature of such dependencies. Specifically, we theorize how internal firm dynamics and performance are affected by resource dependence by integrating resource orchestration (RO) into RDT. Building on RO, we argue that as the degree to which a firm is dependent upon a single source of revenue increases (a core tenet of RDT), the firm adjusts internally by reducing investments and the market responds negatively as well. Further, we argue that the heterogeneous nature of such dependence—namely, dependence scope (the spread of dependence throughout the entity with which the dependence relationship resides), dependence supplementation (the addition of new relationships within the prior dependence relationship), and dependence stability (the expected length of the dependence relationship)—moderates the relationship between the level of dependence and these outcomes. We find broad support for our theorizing in a longitudinal sample of manufacturing firms’ dependence upon governmental contracts for revenue. Collectively, we advance understanding of RDT by adding nuance to both explanatory mechanisms underlying dependence and conceptualizations of firms’ external dependence relationships, posing theoretical and practical implications.

**Research Question**
How does dependence on government contracts affect firms' internal investment decisions and market performance, and how do the scope, supplementation, and stability of that dependence condition these relationships?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Government dependence relates negatively to market performance.
- H1b: Government dependence relates negatively to firm investment.
- H2a: Government dependence scope weakens the negative relationship between government dependence and market performance.
- H2b: Government dependence scope weakens the negative relationship between government dependence and firm investment.
- H3a: Government dependence supplementation weakens the negative relationship between government dependence and market performance.
- H3b: Government dependence supplementation strengthens the negative relationship between government dependence and firm investment.
- H4a: Government dependence stability weakens the negative relationship between government dependence and market performance.
- H4b: Government dependence stability strengthens the negative relationship between government dependence and firm investment.
- H5: Firm investment mediates the relationship between government dependence and market performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Government dependence, measured as government-sourced contract revenue divided by total sales.
- DV(s): Market performance, measured by Tobin's Q; firm investment, measured by capital expenditures and R&D expenditures.
- Mediators: Firm investment, with capital investment tested as a partial mediator between government dependence and market performance.
- Moderators: Dependence scope, dependence supplementation, and dependence stability.

The mechanism integrates RDT and resource orchestration: as firms become more dependent on one government revenue source, resource configurations become more tailored to that customer's idiosyncratic demands, reducing autonomy, narrowing search for alternative investments, and lowering investors' expectations of future market opportunities. The results generally support a negative association between government dependence and Tobin's Q, and support a negative association with capital expenditures but not directly with R&D. Scope weakens the negative market-performance relationship; supplementation and stability weaken the negative market-performance relationship but strengthen the negative investment relationship, with capital investment partially mediating the dependence-performance link.

**Data & Measures**
The 2009–2014 firm-year panel combines government-contract data from USASpending.gov, subsidy and regulatory-violation data from GoodJobsFirst.org, political-activity data from the Center for Responsive Politics, and other variables from Compustat. Government dependence is government-sourced contract revenue divided by total sales; dependence scope is the number of distinct major U.S. government agencies contracted with; supplementation is the number of new unique contracts secured in the focal year; and stability is the average years remaining across ongoing and new contracts. Capital investment and R&D are the logs of capital expenditures and R&D expenditures, respectively; market performance is Tobin's Q, calculated as common-stock value, liquidating preferred-stock value, long-term debt, and debt maturing within one year, divided by total assets. Dependent variables are measured at t+1 and predictors at t; the fixed-effects models use robust standard errors and time dummies, with nearest-neighbor propensity-score matching and an inverse Mills selection control.

**Key Findings**
H1a received general support: a one-standard-deviation increase in government dependence corresponded to an approximately 2.7% decrease in Tobin's Q. H1b received partial support in the main models: capital investment fell by approximately 0.8%, whereas the R&D result was not supportive; a full-sample two-step selection correction weakened the capital-expenditure direct effect to nonsignificance. H2a was supported because wider dependence scope weakened the negative market-performance relationship, but H2b was not supported for either capital investment or R&D. H3a and H4a were supported: supplementation and stability each weakened the negative relationship with Tobin's Q. H3b and H4b were supported for both investment measures: supplementation and stability each strengthened the negative relationship with capital investment and R&D. H5 received limited support: capital investment mediated 15.9% of the total effect (p < .05), whereas R&D did not mediate it; with additional temporal spacing, capital-investment mediation fell to 9.51% and p < .10.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends RDT by moving beyond the external management of dependence to examine the internal firm dynamics that accompany dependence relationships. By integrating resource orchestration, it explains how dependence can shape resource search, selection, configuration, and deployment, and it treats dependence as heterogeneous rather than homogeneous through the concepts of scope, supplementation, and stability.

**Practical Implication**
The paper reports concrete performance and investment implications of greater government dependence: a one-standard-deviation increase corresponds to an approximately 2.7% decrease in Tobin's Q and a 0.8% decrease in capital investment. The magnitude varies with the structure of the dependence relationship: wider scope, supplementation, and stability weaken the negative market-performance relationship, while supplementation and stability strengthen the negative investment relationship.

**Limitations**
The paper focuses on U.S.-based manufacturing firms and on government contracts as one specific source of external dependence, so the findings may not generalize directly to other industries or dependence relationships. The authors also note that other contextual factors, such as industry concentration or firms' ability to resist inertial forces, may add further nuance to how government dependence affects investment and market perceptions.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine government dependence beyond manufacturing, including how industry concentration, political shocks, consumer boycotts, and market position shape the risks attached to contract stability. The authors also call for work on other dependence relationships, such as dependence on major business customers, and on other RDT approaches such as acquisitions and alliances.
