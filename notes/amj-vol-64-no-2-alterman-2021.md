# Best Not to Know: Pay Secrecy, Employee Voluntary Turnover, and the Conditioning Effect of Distributive Justice

**Abstract**
Building on uncertainty management theory, we develop and test a model explicating how and when secrecy in pay communication may affect employee turnover-related outcomes (i.e., employee turnover intentions and firm voluntary turnover rates). Underlying this model is the notion that employees triangulate perceptions of pay secrecy (i.e., a pay-related procedural justice cue that also reflects uncertainty) with their own or others' perceptions of distributive justice as a basis for assessing organizational trustworthiness, with the latter serving as an important driver of voluntary turnover intentions and behavior. Results of two studies (Study 1 at the individual level and Study 2 at the firm level) indicate that, rather than being universal, the relationship between pay secrecy and turnover is contingent upon perceptions of distributive justice, with turnover intentions (at the individual level via organizational trustworthiness) and voluntary turnover rates (at the firm level) differentially affected by pay secrecy under conditions of higher and lower levels of distributive justice. These findings suggest an important extension to organizational justice theories; namely that, when procedural justice cues are confounded with uncertainty (as they are with pay secrecy), the assumed compounding interaction between procedural and distributive justice cues may be replaced by a more antagonistic interaction.

**Research Question**
How and when does pay secrecy affect employee turnover-related outcomes, and does distributive justice condition the relationship between pay secrecy, organizational trustworthiness, turnover intentions, and firm voluntary turnover rates?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Distributive justice perceptions moderate the relationship between pay secrecy perceptions and organizational trust, such that, in the context of high distributive justice perceptions, the effect of pay secrecy perceptions on organizational trust is negative, but, in the context of low distributive justice perceptions, the effect of pay secrecy perceptions on organizational trust is positive.
H2. Distributive justice perceptions moderate the indirect effect of pay secrecy perceptions on turnover intentions via organizational trust, such that, in the context of high distributive justice perceptions, the indirect effect is positive, but, in the context of low distributive justice perceptions, the indirect effect is negative.
H3. Distributive justice climate moderates the relationship between pay secrecy practice and firm voluntary turnover rate, such that, when distributive justice climate is high, the relationship is positive, but, when distributive justice climate is low, the relationship is negative.

The authors state that Hypotheses 1 and 2 are tested at the individual level in Study 1, and Hypothesis 3 at the firm level in Study 2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Pay secrecy perceptions in Study 1 and pay secrecy practice in Study 2.
- DV(s): Turnover intentions in Study 1 and firm voluntary turnover rate in Study 2.
- Mediators: Organizational trust in Study 1.
- Moderators: Distributive justice perceptions in Study 1 and distributive justice climate in Study 2.

The mechanism is that pay secrecy functions as a procedural justice cue that is also confounded with uncertainty. Under uncertainty management theory, employees triangulate pay secrecy with distributive justice to assess organizational trustworthiness. When distributive justice is high, lower pay secrecy provides clearer evidence of fair pay-related treatment and is associated with higher trust and lower turnover-related outcomes; when distributive justice is low, pay secrecy can preserve trust at the individual level by allowing employees to discount aversive fairness information, although this protective pattern did not become significant for firm voluntary turnover rates in Study 2.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 is a two-wave paper survey of employed students enrolled in an executive MBA program at a large university in southern China, with the waves separated by a one-month interval: control, independent, and moderator variables (pay secrecy and distributive justice perceptions) were measured in Wave 1, and the mediator and dependent variables (organizational trust and turnover intentions) a month later in Wave 2. Of the 231 students surveyed, 196 responded, a response rate of 84.85%; average age was 32.14 years (SD = 6.21), average organizational tenure 4.53 years (SD = 4.79), and 64.30% of the sample was female, employed in such fields as medicine, manufacturing, education, and banking. Pay secrecy perceptions used five items the authors selected from previously established scales on the basis of their own conceptualization of pay secrecy (Arnold et al., 2018), with two items capturing procedural transparency and three capturing communication openness, rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree (Cronbach's alpha = .81); a third possible component, pay level transparency, was not assessed because the authors describe that practice as extremely rare in China. Organizational trust used the seven-item scale of Schoorman and Ballinger (2006) (alpha = .78), distributive justice used four items adapted from Colquitt (2001) (alpha = .95), and turnover intentions used three items from Cammann, Fichman, Jenkins, and Klesh (1983) (alpha = .80), all on the same 5-point Likert scale. Controls were Time 1 organizational trust and turnover intentions, employee age, gender, organizational size (employee headcount), organizational tenure, and procedural and informational justice measured with Colquitt's (2001) scales, plus four employee monthly salary dummies (reference category "over 10,000 Yuan") and 16 industry dummies (reference "public administration"). Analysis used multiple regression with all predictors grand-mean centered and the interaction formed as the product of centered pay secrecy perceptions and distributive justice, the Johnson-Neyman technique to locate regions of significance, and a Monte Carlo test of the moderated mediation index with 20,000 bootstrap replications; Tables 1 and 2 report n = 196. All surveys were conducted in Chinese following a translation-back translation procedure (Brislin, 1970).

Study 2 uses an archival firm-level data set collected for a separate project in 2013. Data were solicited from 200 firms (state owned, private, and multinational) drawn from the registered firm list of Dongguan City's (Guangdong Province, China) Industrial and Commercial Bureau, randomly sampled as 67 small firms (100 or fewer employees), 67 medium-sized firms (101-500 employees), and 66 large firms (more than 500 employees); 107 firms agreed to participate (35 small, 33 medium sized, and 39 large). The CEO or HR manager in each participating firm completed a firm-level survey, and a maximum of 40 nonmanagerial employees per firm were sampled randomly with equal probability of selection to complete an employee survey; employee-level response rates ranged from 5% to 100%, with a median of 60%. After matching the firm-level and employee-level data and excluding observations with missing values on the focal variables, 86 firms and 2,035 employees remained; among those employees, 48% were male, average age was 30.21 years (SD = 7.48), and average organizational tenure was 4.79 years (SD = 4.82). Pay secrecy practice used the same five items as Study 1, answered by CEOs or HR managers on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree (alpha = .75). Distributive justice climate used the same four-item Colquitt (2001) scale, rated by employees on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree (alpha = .94) and aggregated from individual ratings to the firm level following the direct consensus composition model, supported by a median rwg(j) of .82, ICC(1) = .11, ICC(2) = .75, and a one-way analysis of variance showing significant firm-level differences in distributive justice climate ratings, F(85, 1949) = 4.03, p < .001. Firm voluntary turnover rate was assessed by having the CEO or HR manager record the firm's annual voluntary turnover rate in the previous year as a proportion of its workforce, and these annual rates ranged from 0.00 to 0.94. Controls were organizational age, organizational size (headcount), and 13 industry dummy variables with the services industry as the referent. Analysis used Tobit regression, chosen because voluntary turnover rate is a censored variable, with grand-mean centered predictors, an interaction term formed as the product of centered pay secrecy practice and distributive justice climate, and the Johnson-Neyman technique; Tables 3 and 4 report n = 86.

The design is associational in both studies. No variable was manipulated and the authors claim no causal identification strategy; Study 1 separates predictors from outcomes by a one-month time lag and Study 2 uses a manager-reported turnover rate, but the authors state that they cannot rule out reverse causality and encourage future research to test causal effects using more sophisticated longitudinal designs.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 196; multiple regression, Table 2) fully supported Hypothesis 1. Distributive justice significantly moderated the relationship between pay secrecy perceptions and organizational trust (B = -0.08, p = .01), and this interaction explained an additional 2% of the variance in organizational trust beyond the control variables and the main effects of pay secrecy perceptions and distributive justice (R2 = .45). The Johnson-Neyman analysis showed that the relationship between pay secrecy perceptions and organizational trust was significant and positive when grand-mean centered distributive justice was lower than -1.61, and significant and negative when grand-mean centered distributive justice was higher than 0.14; that is, pay secrecy was negatively related to trust when distributive justice was high, but positively related to trust when distributive justice was low. In the descriptive statistics, pay secrecy perceptions were inversely correlated with organizational trust (r = -.15, p = .04) and were not significantly correlated with turnover intentions (r = .09, p = .21) or distributive justice perceptions (r = .00, p = .98).

Study 1 also fully supported Hypothesis 2. The moderated mediation effect was significant (index of moderated mediation = 0.02, 95% CI [.001, .056], Monte Carlo method with 20,000 bootstrap replications), and across the full observed centered range of the moderator [-2.17, 1.83] the conditional indirect effect was negative and significant when centered distributive justice was lower than -0.91 and positive and significant when centered distributive justice was higher than 0.54. The authors summarize this as showing that at high (low) levels of distributive justice, pay secrecy perceptions were positively (negatively) related to turnover intentions via lower (higher) levels of organizational trust. A supplemental dual-stage test found that distributive justice moderated the pay secrecy perceptions to organizational trust path (B = -0.08, p = .01) but did not significantly moderate the organizational trust to turnover intentions path (B = -0.03, p = .65), and further supplemental analyses found that neither the interaction of pay secrecy perceptions with procedural justice nor the interaction with informational justice predicted organizational trust.

Study 2 (86 firms; Tobit regression, Table 4) only partially supported Hypothesis 3. Distributive justice climate moderated the relationship between pay secrecy practice and firm voluntary turnover rate (B = 0.16, p = .03), and this interaction explained an additional 5% of the variance in firm voluntary turnover rate beyond the control variables and the main effects of pay secrecy practice and distributive justice climate (R2 = .35). Across the full range of observed grand-mean centered distributive justice climate values [-1.17, 1.42], the Johnson-Neyman analysis showed that the relationship between pay secrecy practice and firm voluntary turnover rate was significant and positive when centered distributive justice climate was higher than 0.18, but that this relation was not significant for any distributive justice climate values smaller than 0.18. The predicted positive relationship under a high distributive justice climate therefore held, whereas the predicted negative relationship under a low distributive justice climate did not, which the authors report as partial support for Hypothesis 3. Pay secrecy practice was not significantly correlated with firm voluntary turnover rate (r = .14, p = .24) or with distributive justice climate (r = .14, p = .20). To illustrate magnitude, the authors report that, holding controls and all else equal, a 1 SD decrease in pay secrecy practice from the mean combined with a 1 or 2 SD increase in distributive justice climate from the mean equates to about a 12% voluntary turnover rate, against roughly 19% at average levels of both variables, a decrease of 7 percentage points.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends organizational justice theory by showing that procedural and distributive justice cues do not always compound when the procedural cue is also uncertain. It positions organizational trustworthiness as a global fairness judgment linking pay secrecy and turnover-related outcomes, and it extends pay secrecy research from individual attitudes to firm-level voluntary turnover rates.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations considering pay transparency should attend to employees' distributive justice perceptions rather than assuming that transparency or secrecy has a uniform turnover effect. The findings suggest that higher pay secrecy is especially risky when employees generally perceive pay outcomes as fair, whereas additional pay-range or pay-procedure information may help reduce uncertainty when full transparency is not feasible.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study excludes pay level transparency because of lack of variance, relies partly on self-report measures, and may have limited generalizability beyond Chinese or East Asian contexts. They also acknowledge possible reverse causality, a single manager rating for firm pay secrecy practice, limited firm-level sample size, and the absence of firm-level organizational trust as a mediator.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine pay level transparency, multiple justice dimensions, cross-cultural generalizability, other employee populations, enterprise ownership differences, and additional firm-level controls such as union status, diversity, pay-for-performance policies, and organizational climate. The authors also call for stronger longitudinal designs, multiple manager ratings, firm-level mediating mechanisms, justice climate strength, supervisor-focused justice climate, other outcomes such as performance and counterproductive behavior, and turnover quality among good versus poor performers.
