# How Technological Overlap between Spinouts and Parent Firms Affects Corporate Venture Capital Investments in Spinouts: The Role of Competitive Tension

**Abstract**
Drawing on the literature on entrepreneurship and competitive dynamics, we investigate how technological overlap between spinouts and parent firms influences corporate investors’ decisions regarding investments in spinouts. We suggest that a high level of technological overlap between a spinout and its parent firm deters potential corporate investors from making an investment in the spinout because of competitive tension arising from anticipated hostile actions by the parent firm. We further suggest that these negative effects are contingent on the tradeoffs between competitive risks and benefits. Our findings show that the negative effects are amplified when parent firms have a strong litigiousness in claiming their intellectual property rights. However, we find that the negative effects are mitigated when corporate investors can expect benefits from gaining indirect access to parent firms’ technological knowledge. Using a sample of corporate venture capital investments in the U.S. medical device industry, we find evidence that supports our hypotheses.

**Research Question**
How does technological overlap between a spinout and its parent firm affect a corporate investor's likelihood of investing in the spinout? The paper also asks when this negative relationship is amplified by competitive risks or mitigated by competitive benefits from access to parent-firm knowledge.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Baseline Hypothesis. Spinouts that have a high level of technological overlap with their parent firms (TOSP) are less likely than other ventures (nonspinouts and spinouts with a low level of overlap) to receive an investment from corporate investors.
- Hypothesis 1 (negative). The level of technological overlap between a spinout and its parent firm is negatively associated with the likelihood that a corporate investor will invest in the spinout.
- Hypothesis 2 (negative moderation). The negative effect of TOSP on the likelihood of a corporate investor's investment in the spinout is amplified by the level of the parent firm's litigiousness for intellectual property rights.
- Hypothesis 3 (positive moderation). The negative effect of TOSP on the likelihood of a corporate investor's investment in the spinout is mitigated by the level of technological overlap between the corporate investor and the parent firm (TOCP).
- Hypothesis 4 (positive moderation). The negative effect of TOSP on the likelihood of a corporate investor's investment in the spinout is mitigated by the level of the parent firm's innovativeness.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Technological overlap between a spinout and its parent firm (TOSP); in the baseline comparison, spinout status with high versus low TOSP.
- DV(s): Corporate venture capital investment in the venture or spinout.
- Mediators: No formal mediator is estimated; competitive tension from anticipated hostile parent-firm actions is the theorized mechanism.
- Moderators: Parent firm litigiousness amplifies the negative TOSP effect; technological overlap between the corporate investor and parent firm (TOCP) and parent firm innovativeness mitigate it.

The proposed mechanism is a competitive-tension process. When a spinout's technology overlaps strongly with its parent firm's knowledge base, corporate investors anticipate that the parent firm may retaliate through legal or market-based actions, making the investment riskier. That risk is stronger when the parent firm is litigious, but it can be offset when the corporate investor expects strategic benefits from indirectly accessing a competitor's knowledge through the spinout.

**Data & Measures**
Setting and data sources. The study covers CVC investments in the U.S. medical device industry over 1995-2015. Fund-level CVC deal data come from Thomson ONE's VentureXpert; the corporate parent of each CVC fund was identified manually using sources such as Bloomberg Businessweek, Capital IQ, and LexisNexis, and corporate investors were restricted to public firms with at least one medical device approved by the FDA, yielding 45 industry incumbents as corporate investors. VentureXpert also yielded 838 U.S.-based, investor-backed ventures in medical device subsectors founded between 1995 and 2010; founder career histories were compiled from Bloomberg Businessweek executive profiles, Capital IQ, Crunchbase, Factiva, Relationship Science, LinkedIn, and company websites, identifying 254 spinouts under the 10-year window. Restricting ventures to those with at least one granted U.S. patent produced the final sample of 679 ventures (215 spinouts and 465 nonspinouts). Patent data come from the U.S. Patent and Trademarks Office, parent-firm and corporate-investor litigation history from Lex Machina and MaxVal's Litigation Databank, product data from the FDA's medical device approval database, acquisition and alliance information from Thomson ONE's Mergers & Acquisitions and Securities Data Company databases respectively, and accounting information from Compustat.

Two dyad-year datasets. (a) A spinout and nonspinout sample of 190,315 potential dyad-years between corporate investors and all ventures, of which 259 are realized CVC investment deals; this dataset tests the Baseline Hypothesis. (b) A spinout sample of 69,815 potential dyad-years between corporate investors and spinouts, of which 91 are realized CVC investment deals; this dataset tests Hypotheses 1-4.

Measures.
- DV: CVC investment(ijt), a binary variable equal to 1 for a realized CVC investment dyad by corporate investor i in venture (or spinout) j in year t, and 0 otherwise.
- IV: TOSP, measured with Jaffe's (1986) technological overlap formula comparing the spinout's patent portfolio (patents from the founding year t1 through the end of the sample period) with the parent firm's patent portfolio over the 10 years before the founder left (t0 - 10 to t0). For the Baseline Hypothesis, a binary spinout variable (1 for ventures founded by former employees of established firms) plus two nested binaries splitting spinouts at the mean of TOSP into spinout with high TOSP and spinout with low TOSP.
- Moderators: parent firm litigiousness, the number of unique patent infringement lawsuits filed by a parent firm during the five years prior to the dyad year (Hypothesis 2); TOCP, Jaffe's (1986) overlap between corporate investor and parent firm over the five years prior to the dyad year (Hypothesis 3); and parent firm innovativeness, operationalized as parent firm new product, the number of medical device products approved by the FDA during the 10 years prior to the dyad year, counting products approved through both the 510(k) and premarket approval processes and log-transformed (Hypothesis 4). All explanatory variables were mean centered before creating interaction terms.
- Spinout definitions use one-, three-, five-, and 10-year windows between leaving the parent firm and founding the venture.
- Controls span the venture/spinout (patent count, age, founder PhD or MD, time since left parent firm, number of founders, collaborative relationship between spinout and parent firm, manufacturing resource need, marketing resource need), the corporate investor (patent count, new products, R&D intensity, patent infringement, size, cash, financial orientation, strategic orientation), the dyad (technological overlap and cross-citations between spinout and corporate investor, geographic distance), and the parent firm (patent count, alliance between parent firm and corporate investor).

Estimation and identification. The binary dependent variable is estimated with probit regression models including industry segment and year fixed effects, with robust standard errors clustered at the dyad level. Table 3 reports 190,315 observations and Table 4 reports 69,815 observations for the 10-year window (52,290, 45,659, and 35,755 for the five-, three-, and one-year windows). Specifications adding corporate investor fixed effects use 130,259 observations in Table 3 and 30,492 in Table 5 for the 10-year window; the paper states this approach reduces the sample because observations associated with corporate investor dummies are dropped when they have no variation in the dependent variable. Because interaction effects in nonlinear models cannot be read from the interaction coefficient alone, the moderating effects are also interpreted through the marginal effect of TOSP at low, medium, and high values of each moderator. The main design is associational; the paper separately addresses endogeneity of TOSP with an instrumental variable (IV) probit regression using state-level noncompete agreement enforceability as the instrument (Appendix B), estimating the moderating effects through high/low median-split subsamples. Further checks include rare events logistic regression, a randomly matched sample pairing each realized dyad with 10 nonrealized dyads, alternative product-market measures of TOSP (news-based market-segment coding, shared 4-digit SIC code, FDA product-class overlap), dynamic TOSP measures, an ordered categorical dependent variable based on investment amount, and alternative parent-firm innovativeness measures based on patents and forward citations. Interviews with corporate venture capitalists, independent venture capitalists, and founders of spinouts are used illustratively; the paper reports no interview count.

**Key Findings**
- Baseline Hypothesis supported. In the spinout and nonspinout sample (Table 3), the spinout indicator alone is negative but insignificant (b = -0.02, p = 0.776, Model 1). Splitting spinouts at the mean of TOSP, spinout with high TOSP is negative and significant (b = -0.43, p = 0.000) while spinout with low TOSP is positive and significant (b = 0.14, p = 0.057), and the two coefficients differ significantly from each other (p = 0.000).
- Hypothesis 1 supported. TOSP is negative and statistically significant (b = -1.11, p = 0.000, Table 4 Model 2), and the average marginal effect of TOSP is -0.004 (z-stat = -3.40, p = 0.001). When TOSP increases from its mean to one standard deviation above the mean, the likelihood of CVC investment decreases by 49.3%.
- Hypothesis 2 supported. The interaction between TOSP and parent firm litigiousness is negative and significant (b = -0.09, p = 0.066, Model 3). In Table 6 Panel A the marginal effect of TOSP is smaller at a low level of parent firm litigiousness than at medium and high levels, and Figure 3 shows a one-standard-deviation increase in TOSP reduces the likelihood of CVC investment by 60.3% at a low level of parent firm litigiousness versus 94.2% at a high level.
- Hypothesis 3 supported. The interaction between TOSP and TOCP is positive and statistically significant (b = 2.09, p = 0.008, Model 4). Figure 4 shows a one-standard-deviation increase in TOSP reduces the likelihood of CVC investment by 73.9% when TOCP is low but by 40.2% when TOCP is high.
- Hypothesis 4 supported. The interaction between TOSP and parent firm new product is positive and significant (b = 0.39, p = 0.041, Model 5). Figure 5 shows a one-standard-deviation increase in TOSP reduces the likelihood of CVC investment by 78.4% at a low level of parent firm new product but by only 35.0% at a high level.
- Across spinout-definition windows, the paper reports that these findings remain consistent in Models 7-9 and that the corporate investor fixed-effect models are generally consistent (Table 5). Reading the tables directly, the TOSP main effect and the litigiousness and TOCP interactions keep their signs and significance in every reported window in Tables 4 and 5, while the parent firm new product interaction is positive but insignificant in the one-year window models (0.29 in Table 4 Model 9; 0.28 in Table 5 Model 4).
- Alternative explanations (Table 7). In Cox proportional hazard models, TOSP is positive but insignificant for independent venture capital investment (b = 0.37, p = 0.366, Model 1) while negative and significant for CVC investment (b = -2.12, p = 0.029, Model 2). In a spinout year-level random-effects panel regression, TOSP has a positive but insignificant effect on spinouts' innovation performance (b = 0.05, p = 0.411, Model 3), so the paper concludes TOSP has no significantly negative effect on spinout innovation performance.
- Measurement robustness. The three alternative product-market overlap measures generally support the arguments, but the models using the FDA product-class measure (Appendix A, Models 5 and 6) show directions consistent with Hypotheses 3 and 4 that are insignificant.
- IV probit (Appendix B). TOSP remains negatively associated with the likelihood of CVC investment (b = -4.17, p = 0.000, Model 2). In median-split subsamples, the negative TOSP effect is significant for high parent firm litigiousness (b = -5.55, p = 0.000, Model 3) but not for low litigiousness (b = -0.74, p = 0.839, Model 4); TOSP is negative and significant for both high and low TOCP (b = -3.44, p = 0.047, Model 5; b = -5.08, p = 0.000, Model 6) and for both high and low parent firm new product (b = -2.70, p = 0.071, Model 7; b = -3.59, p = 0.012, Model 8), with the marginal effects of TOSP greater when TOCP and parent firm new product are low, which the paper reads as supporting Hypotheses 3 and 4.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends employee entrepreneurship research by showing that technological inheritance can be a liability for spinouts, not only a resource endowment or reputational advantage. It identifies a downside of spinout-parent similarity: overlap may trigger competitive tension that reduces external corporate investment.

The paper also contributes to competitive dynamics by theorizing the transitivity of competitive tension through interfirm networks. Rather than treating competitive tension only as a dyadic rival relationship, it shows how tension between a parent firm and a spinout can shape a third party's decision to form an investment relationship.

**Practical Implication**
For corporate investors, the findings indicate that spinout investments involve a balance between access to useful external technologies and competitive risks from parent-firm reactions. Spinout-parent technological overlap, parent-firm litigiousness, and the investor's potential benefit from indirect access to parent-firm knowledge are central conditions in that balance.

For spinouts, the results imply that close technological inheritance from a parent firm may make CVC funding harder to secure when corporate investors anticipate retaliation risks. Parent firms' reputations for enforcing intellectual property rights can shape how attractive their spinouts look to outside corporate investors.

**Limitations**
The empirical setting is the U.S. medical device industry, where intellectual property protection and patent litigation are especially salient, so the risk-benefit balance may differ in industries with weaker formal protection regimes. The paper also cannot provide decisive performance implications for whether corporate investors actually create value by accessing spinout knowledge.

The analysis focuses on patented technological knowledge and CVC investment ties, so it does not capture the full range of competitive actions, tacit knowledge conflicts, or governance choices in other forms of external knowledge acquisition. The dyad-level data also cannot fully represent the network structure of triadic competitive tension.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether the same mechanism operates in industries where parent-firm retaliation has weaker deterrent effects or where corporate investors are less sensitive to intellectual property litigation. It could also test when expected benefits from spinout knowledge translate into better corporate-investor performance.

Additional work could study competitive tension across other governance modes, such as R&D alliances, acquisitions, or other external knowledge acquisition relationships. The paper also points to future research on ongoing post-founding knowledge inheritance through founders' social ties with former colleagues at parent firms.
