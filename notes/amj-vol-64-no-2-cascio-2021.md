# Antecedents and Consequences of Employee and Asset Restructuring

**Abstract**
We examine two forms of employment and asset restructuring: downsizing and upsizing. Guided by institutional theory, we focus on two important questions: (a) are the antecedents of restructuring common across downsizing and upsizing? and (b) are the performance consequences of such restructuring related to prior firm performance? To answer these questions, we use primary data from companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange over a 37-year period (1980–2016). Our results suggest that six key antecedents play a prominent role in both downsizing and upsizing, although they differ in magnitude and direction in how they affect each form of restructuring. However, in both instances they reflect efficiency considerations (company performance and managerial foresight), as well as institutional forces (the economy, political uncertainty, industry, and technology), just as neo-institutional theory suggests. In addition, prior performance plays a central role in restructuring decisions, in both the upsizing and downsizing of assets and employees. The evidence suggests that it is not just current-year performance but also the trend in company performance over the prior two years that forecasts subsequent firm performance; thus, our findings extend and refine institutional theory.

**Research Question**
Are the antecedents of restructuring common across downsizing and upsizing, and are the performance consequences of employee and asset restructuring related to prior firm performance? The paper asks these questions for two restructuring forms (downsizing and upsizing) and two restructuring types (employees and assets).

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Company performance, managerial foresight, the economy, political uncertainty, industry competition, and technology affect firm-level decisions to restructure through downsizing or upsizing.
- Hypothesis 2a: Within downsizing categories (employees, assets, or both), the trend in company performance will be least negative for employee downsizing, more negative for asset downsizing, and most negative for combination downsizing.
- Hypothesis 2b: Within upsizing categories (employees, assets, or both), the trend in company performance will be least positive for employee upsizing, more positive for asset upsizing, and most positive for combination upsizing.
- Hypothesis 3a: Among downsizers, ex post company performance will be most positive when the prior trend in company performance has been most negative.
- Hypothesis 3b: Among upsizers, ex post company performance will be most positive when the prior trend in company performance has been least positive.

Table 1 states the expected sign of each antecedent. For downsizing, managerial foresight, company performance, the state of the economy, political uncertainty, and industry performance are all expected to be negative. For upsizing, managerial foresight, company performance, the state of the economy, and industry performance are expected to be positive, while political uncertainty stays negative. Technology is the one antecedent whose predicted sign is left open (positive or negative) for both forms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Company performance, managerial foresight, performance of the economy, political uncertainty, industry performance/competition, technology, and prior firm-performance trends.
- DV(s): Firm-level decisions to downsize or upsize employees, assets, or both; ex post industry-adjusted stock market returns after restructuring.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper as a separate moderation model. The paper compares hypotheses and regressions across restructuring categories and prior-performance trends to explain ex post performance patterns.

Guided by institutional theory, the authors argue that restructuring is shaped jointly by efficiency considerations and institutional forces. Company performance and managerial foresight capture normative/efficiency pressures; the economy and political uncertainty capture coercive pressures; and industry effects plus technology capture mimetic processes. The evidence links these antecedents to downsizing and upsizing decisions, then links prior performance trends to subsequent industry-adjusted returns: restructuring after difficult performance is associated with stronger ex post performance than restructuring under more benign conditions.

**Data & Measures**
- Data source and window: panel data on corporations listed on the New York Stock Exchange from 1980 through fiscal year 2016, taken from the Compustat database, sampled at fiscal year-ends until calendar year 2017. Because the tests are primarily based on stock returns in the first or second year subsequent to a restructuring event, the accounting and employment data are curtailed either to 1980-2015 or to 1980-2014.
- Sample construction: the raw data comprise 21,230 companies and 190,008 pooled data observations. Eliminating companies for which employee numbers, total assets, long-term assets, or net income is unavailable leaves 6,334 companies and 98,701 pooled observations. The authors then eliminate companies identified as non-U.S.-based or not reporting in U.S. dollars (157 firms) and those with histories shorter than five years (1,550 firms). The final sample is 61,853 sets of observations for 4,627 firms, an unbalanced panel; most tests are based on subsamples of these data.
- Outcome measure: industry-adjusted return, IAR(i,t) = R(i,t) - IR(i,t), where R(i,t) = ln((P(i,t) + D(i,t)) / P(i,t-1)) using the stock's price and annual dividends, and IR(i,t) is the weighted-average return of the S&P industry component (Global Industry Classification) to which firm i belongs. R and IAR are computed on each company's annual fiscal calendar, and the consequences tests use IAR at t+1, at t+2, and averaged over t+1 and t+2.
- Restructuring classification, two approaches. The "cut-off" approach applies a 5% threshold to year-over-year changes and defines seven categories: Employee Downsizers (Cat1), Asset Downsizers (Cat2), Combination Downsizers (Cat3), Employee Upsizers (Cat4), Asset Upsizers (Cat5), Combination Upsizers (Cat6), and Stable Employers (Cat7), with Cat7 serving as the baseline. The "deciles" approach sorts year-by-year percentage changes in employees and in total assets into deciles, treating deciles 1-4 as downsizing, deciles 7-10 as upsizing, and deciles 5-6 as stable; conditional employee (asset) changes are those whose corresponding asset (employee) changes fall in the fifth or sixth decile, and combination changes are those falling in the identical decile on both dimensions. Because a single firm may restructure on multiple occasions over the sample period of 1981 through fiscal year-end 2015, it may appear in multiple deciles and multiple categories.
- Antecedent measures (Equation 2), each entered at t0, t-1, and t-2, and all dichotomous except the trend term: TECH is the trend variable equal to the year (1, 2, ..., n); managerial foresight (MGF) equals 1 if the percentage change in dividends exceeds the industry average, 0 otherwise; company performance (COM) equals 1 if the percentage change in net income exceeds the industry average, 0 otherwise; economic performance (ECO) equals 1 if the percentage change in GDP exceeds 2.3%, 0 otherwise; political risk (POL) equals 1 if the intrayear standard deviation of the Partisan Conflict Index exceeds 13, 0 otherwise; and excess industry return (IND) equals 1 if excess industry return exceeds 0, 0 otherwise.
- Controls, also dichotomous and left unranked in the logistic regressions: firm size (SIZ) equals 1 if total assets fall in the upper third of the sample; market-to-book (MB) equals 1 if the ratio is greater than the industry average market-to-book ratio; leverage (LEV) equals 1 if the debt-to-total-assets ratio is greater than the industry average.
- Estimation: logistic models of restructuring category on the antecedents, estimated separately for downsizing and upsizing. The reported specification restricts the downsizing sample to combination downsizers (Cat3) versus stable employers (Cat7) and the upsizing sample to combination upsizers (Cat6) versus Cat7, giving 9,591 and 13,798 observations respectively (Table 2). Equation 2 is estimated with and without MGF, and only the specification with MGF is reported. Ex post consequences are estimated with dummy-variable regressions of IAR on the restructuring deciles with standard errors adjusted for heteroskedasticity of unknown form (White, 1980), plus a variant (Equation 3a) that also controls for autocorrelation in IAR; Table 4 reports the Equation 3a results. Five-year rolling regressions over the 37-year period are used to examine time variation.
- Decile-based sample statistics (Table 3) cover a total of 57,226 observations, comprising 7,229 employee downsizers, 5,477 asset downsizers, 4,603 combination downsizers, 4,146 employee upsizers, 4,386 asset upsizers, and 4,404 combination upsizers.
- Identification: the design is associational rather than causal. The authors state that because the study uses archival data and is not a true experimental design, the best they can do is identify statistically significant associations between variables.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 is supported. The authors report that each coefficient carries the expected sign, that almost all antecedents have opposing directional effects across downsizing and upsizing, and that taken together the evidence provides strong support for Hypothesis 1.
- Antecedents of downsizing (combination downsizers versus stable employers, 9,591 observations): current company performance is the top-ranked predictor and is negative, COM(t0) = -1.02 (t = -13.99), with COM(t-1) = -0.62 (-9.04) and COM(t-2) = -0.45 (-6.58) also negative and significant. Managerial foresight ranks second, MGF(t0) = -0.48 (-5.42), while its two lags are insignificant. ECO(t0) = -0.22 (-2.76) ranks third and POL(t0) = -0.18 (-2.21) ranks fourth. The current-year industry estimate is insignificant, IND(t0) = 0.02 (0.35), but IND(t-1) = -0.25 (-3.70) is negative and significant. TECH = 0.01 (2.88) is positive and significant. Cragg-Uhler R2 = 0.11, with 90% of predictions correct.
- Antecedents of upsizing (combination upsizers versus stable employers, 13,798 observations): the signs reverse. COM(t0) = 0.58 (14.88) is again the top-ranked predictor, with COM(t-1) = 0.41 (10.28) and COM(t-2) = 0.20 (5.14) also positive and significant. Managerial foresight ranks second and all three of its estimates are positive and significant, MGF(t0) = 0.68 (16.75), MGF(t-1) = 0.20 (4.78), and MGF(t-2) = 0.17 (4.20). ECO(t0) = 0.20 (4.35) and ECO(t-1) = 0.25 (5.01) are positive and significant. POL(t0) = -0.10 (-2.39) is negative, so political discord discourages both forms of restructuring. IND is positively related to upsizing but is the least important of the ranked predictors, and TECH = 0.01 (1.15) is insignificant for upsizing. Cragg-Uhler R2 = 0.14, with 67% of predictions correct.
- Hypothesis 2a is supported. Mean industry-adjusted returns in the restructuring year are -2.03% for employee downsizers, -4.85% for asset downsizers, and -10.30% for combination downsizers; at t-1 the values for employee, asset, and combination downsizing are -4.96, -5.22, and -14.63. The same pattern holds for ROA and industry-adjusted ROA, and the authors state that this evidence lends strong support to Hypothesis 2a.
- Hypothesis 2b is supported. Mean industry-adjusted returns in the restructuring year are 0.51% for employee upsizers, 5.38% for asset upsizers, and 7.42% for combination upsizers; at t-1 the values for employee, asset, and combination upsizing are 0.58, 5.54, and 13.33, and at t-2 they are 0.42, 5.23, and 12.87. Each difference is statistically different, and the authors state that the evidence strongly supports Hypothesis 2b.
- Hypotheses 3a and 3b are supported. Summing IAR averaged over t+1 and t+2 across the restructuring deciles gives 7.49% for employee downsizing, 20.57% for asset downsizing, and 45.08% for combination downsizing; the ordering reverses for upsizing, at 5.99% for employee upsizing, -5.89% for asset upsizing, and -19.74% for combination upsizing. The paper reports all six cross-category differences as significant: within downsizing, combination minus employee = 37.59 (t = 7.24), combination minus assets = 24.51 (4.28), and assets minus employees = 13.08 (2.93); within upsizing, combination minus employee = -25.73 (-5.62), combination minus assets = -13.85 (-3.04), and assets minus employees = -11.88 (-2.41).
- Downsizers versus upsizers: combination downsizers outperform combination upsizers by 64.82 (t = 13.98) and asset downsizers outperform asset upsizers by 26.46 (5.50), but the employee downsizing versus employee upsizing difference is smallest, 1.50 (0.39), and is insignificant.
- The authors summarize the ex post pattern as returns being highest for combination downsizing, followed by asset downsizing, and lowest for employee downsizing, while among upsizers performance is best for employee upsizing, followed by asset upsizing, and then combination upsizing. They read this as evidence that restructuring undertaken during difficult financial conditions, on average, outperforms restructuring under more benign conditions.
- Time variation: in five-year rolling regressions, upsizing industry-adjusted returns are no longer statistically different from zero in the latter half of the sample (after roughly the year 2000), which the authors suggest may explain inconsistencies in the pattern of performance results across studies of restructuring.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends institutional theory by showing that efficiency considerations and institutional forces jointly predict both downsizing and upsizing, not only employee downsizing. It also refines institutional theory by showing that trends in prior company performance, not only contemporaneous performance, forecast subsequent firm performance after restructuring. The design further highlights time as a research-design issue because market responses to restructuring vary across the 37-year sampling period.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should consider company performance, managerial foresight, the economy, political uncertainty, industry competition, and technology before restructuring, with attention to recent and prior performance trends. The paper cautions against treating employee downsizing as an easy lever to restore profitability because it is frequent in the sample but has the lowest payoff among downsizing categories. It also suggests that upsizing does not automatically produce better stock market performance when undertaken from high profitability.

**Limitations**
The study uses archival data rather than an experimental design, so it identifies statistically significant associations rather than causal effects. The sample consists of publicly traded firms, and privately held firms may behave differently. The authors also could not observe different downsizing motives and goals, such as declining demand versus efficiency improvement.

**Future Research**
Future studies should account explicitly for sampling periods because restructuring-performance relationships vary over time. Qualitative research using in-depth case studies and interviews with senior executives could provide richer evidence about unmeasured variables, tacit knowledge, and firm-specific restructuring decisions. Future work could also examine motives and goals for downsizing more directly.
