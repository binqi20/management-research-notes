# Leading by Doing: Does Leading by Example Impact Productivity and Service Quality?

**Abstract**
This paper presents a research model for the creation of organizational value through leading by example. I argue that leading by example serves as a unique value-creation driver by enhancing engagement, productivity, and service quality at the business level. Using a strategy-based resource management framework, I also argue that there are synergies between leading by example and the manifestation of organizational core values. My theory is grounded in the strategy-based notion that combining organizational resources—leading by example (practical element) and organizational core values (conceptual element)—optimizes employee engagement, which in turn enhances productivity and service quality. I examine my model empirically using two-time-point panel data derived from three different sources (employees, financial data, and secret shoppers) in a sample of 233 retail stores. The findings provide evidence that leading by example improves productivity and service quality (after taking into account the potential influence of charismatic leadership). The indirect effect of leading by example on productivity and service quality (through engagement) is even stronger when manifestation of organizational core values is high. Contributions to theory, research, and practice are presented.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether leading by example creates business-level value by improving productivity and service quality at the store level. It also asks whether employee engagement mediates this effect and whether written manifestation of organizational core values strengthens the engagement pathway.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: LBE enhances productivity and service quality at the store level, beyond the beneficial effect of charismatic leadership.
- H2: LBE increases employee engagement at the store level.
- H3: There is an interactive effect of LBE and core value manifestation on employee engagement at the store level; specifically, when core value manifestation is higher, the positive effect of LBE on employee engagement is enhanced.
- H4: The positive indirect effect of LBE on productivity and service quality (through employee engagement) is stronger when core value manifestation is higher.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leading by example at the store level.
- DV(s): Store-level productivity and service quality.
- Mediators: Store-level employee engagement.
- Moderators: Core value manifestation, defined as employees' perceptions that written workplace statements communicate the retail chain's core values.

The mechanism combines strategy-based resource management with leadership and social learning arguments. Leading by example is treated as a practical organizational resource: by visibly performing employee activities, leaders clarify desired behavior, support social learning, and encourage employees to invest more fully in their roles. Core value manifestation adds a conceptual resource that explains why the demonstrated behavior matters for the organization's goals, strengthening engagement and, through engagement, productivity and service quality.

**Data & Measures**
The data are a three-year panel on one Israeli retail chain drawn from three sources: an annual web-based employee survey run in the same months each year, accounting-based business performance records from the chain's headquarters, and secret-shopper evaluations by an outside assessment agency. Of the chain's 260 retail stores, 18 were eliminated because complete responses across the three years were not available, 6 were omitted because their leaders were replaced during the period examined, and 3 were omitted because their leader had assumed the position less than six months before the first wave of employee surveys, leaving 233 stores (a response rate of 88.50%) and 8,137 employees (a 75.34% response rate). The unit of analysis is the retail store and the unit of observation is the retail store year; all reported models use 699 store-year observations. Business-level outcomes were obtained from headquarters three months after the employee survey stage was completed, and productivity and service quality were each averaged over the six months starting after that three-month lag. All measures except productivity and service quality used a 5-point Likert scale.

- IV: leading by example (LBE) = the 5-item exemplary behavior subscale of Podsakoff et al.'s (1990) leadership behavior measure, rated by employees from 1 (not at all) to 5 (all the time) and averaged over employees within each store (ICC1 = .13, rWG(J) = .90, Cronbach's alpha = .93).
- Competing leadership construct entered alongside LBE: charismatic leadership = 7 items based on the multifactor leadership questionnaire (Bass, 1985; Bass & Avolio, 1990), rated from 1 (very rarely) to 5 (very often) and averaged within store (ICC1 = .12, rWG(J) = .87, alpha = .95).
- Mediator: employee engagement = Rich et al.'s (2010) 6-item scale, rated from 1 (almost never) to 5 (very often) and averaged within store (ICC1 = .10, rWG(J) = .86, alpha = .87).
- Moderator: core value manifestation = employees' ratings, on a 5-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (all the time), of the extent to which the chain's core values (integrity, service quality, excellence, market leadership, and familial-like ties) are communicated through five types of written material: marketing materials, sales promotions, internal memos and documents, store emails, and posters (ICC1 = .11, rWG(J) = .96, alpha = .83). Interviews with 10 store managers confirmed a wide variety of manifestation approaches across stores.
- DV: productivity = store revenue divided by employees' overall work hours.
- DV: service quality = monthly secret-shopper evaluation of five aspects (service provider appearance, willingness to provide prompt service, provision of professional information, degree of courtesy, and degree of personal attention), each rated from 1 (poor) to 10 (excellent) and averaged into a single monthly score per store.
- Controls: store tenure, store size (number of employees), whether outsourced workers are employed, the prior year's productivity and service quality, leader general tenure, tenure as a leader, leader age, employee tenure, and employee age.

Estimation uses pooled year and retail-store fixed-effects panel regression with standard errors clustered at the store level; Breusch-Pagan and Hausman tests confirmed the appropriateness of fixed effects rather than ordinary least squares or hierarchical linear modeling regressions. The moderation is tested in a year and retail-store fixed-effects moderating regression, with slopes plotted at high (+1 SD) and low (-1 SD) core value manifestation. The conditional indirect effects use simulation-based bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for average indirect causal effects (Imai, Keele, & Yamamoto, 2010) with 10,000 sample draws. Confirmatory factor analyses assessed the four-factor structure and the discriminant validity of LBE from charismatic leadership. The author states that this design does not fully facilitate a causal inference of the proposed effects.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: with charismatic leadership included in the regression and year and retail-store effects fixed, LBE had a significant positive effect on store productivity (estimate = 44.20, SE = 20.040, p = .008; Table 2, Model 2, column 2) and on service quality (estimate = 0.062, SE = 0.032, p = .006; Table 2, Model 5, column 2). Table 2 reports charismatic leadership coefficients of 33.86 for productivity and -0.019 for service quality in those same models, neither carrying a significance marker.
- H2 supported: LBE had a significant positive effect on store-level employee engagement (estimate = 0.326, SE = 0.064, p ≤ .001; Table 3, Model 8, column 2).
- H3 supported: the interactive effect of LBE and core value manifestation on employee engagement was positive and significant (estimate = 0.158, SE = 0.081, p = .045; Table 3, Model 9, column 2), and Figure 2 shows store workforce engagement was enhanced when a stronger combination of LBE and written manifestation of core values was exhibited.
- H4 supported: the indirect effect of LBE on productivity through engagement was 39.870 at lower (-1 SD) and 49.116 at higher (+1 SD) core value manifestation, with 95% confidence intervals of 25.250 to 59.347 and 31.807 to 70.295 respectively; the indirect effect on service quality was 0.073 at the lower and 0.092 at the higher level, with 95% confidence intervals of 0.045 to 0.110 and 0.058 to 0.128 (p < .05). Both indirect effects were significant at both levels because the confidence intervals excluded zero, and both were strongest when manifestation of organizational core values was higher.

Supplementary checks: in reverse-causality fixed-effects regressions, engagement (0.083), productivity (0.000), and service quality (-0.002) did not significantly predict LBE (Appendix A, Table A1). LBE remained significant when the prior year's productivity and service quality were controlled (Table 2, Models 3 and 6), and the squared LBE term was not significant for either outcome, so an inverted U-shaped relationship was not supported (Appendix B, Table B1). All of these models use 699 store-year observations from 233 stores.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops leading by example as a distinct leadership construct rather than treating it as a catchphrase or a subset of charismatic leadership. It extends leadership research by showing that a doing-oriented leadership practice has business-level associations with productivity and service quality beyond a rhetorically oriented leadership style.

The paper also contributes to resource management theory by showing how practical and conceptual resources can work together. Leading by example and core value manifestation jointly enhance engagement, linking micro-level employee motivation to store-level performance outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should treat leading by example as a substantive leadership practice rather than a peripheral personal style. The findings imply that leaders can support productivity, service quality, and employee engagement by visibly demonstrating expected frontline behaviors.

The paper also suggests that leading by example works better when paired with clear written communication of organizational core values. Leadership training and promotion criteria should therefore value managers who both model desired behaviors and connect those behaviors to organizational purpose.

**Limitations**
The study uses multisource, three-year panel data and a three-month lag, but the author states that this design still does not fully support causal inference. Employees' subjective ratings may also imperfectly capture actual leading by example and may be affected by social desirability or employee-leader relations, although anonymity and confidentiality were used to reduce these concerns.

The study examines leading by example mainly in relation to charismatic leadership, while other leadership characteristics such as ethical leadership and behavioral integrity may also affect business performance. It is also limited to one retail chain of medium-sized stores in Israel, which constrains direct generalization to larger organizations, other industries, and other national contexts.

**Future Research**
Future research could use experimental designs to test the causal effects of leading by example more decisively. Researchers could also examine how leading by example differs from or interacts with ethical leadership, behavioral integrity, and other leadership constructs.

The author calls for work on other ways of manifesting core values, such as leader speeches, conferences, and videos, and for further refinement of the core value manifestation construct. Future studies could also test cascading processes at higher organizational levels and examine leading by example in other contexts, such as high technology or cultures with different authority norms.
