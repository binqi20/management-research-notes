# How Voice Emerges and Develops in Newly Formed Supervisor–Employee Dyads

**Abstract**
We develop and test theory regarding how voice emerges and develops in new supervisor– employee dyads. We propose that employees higher in proactive personality speak up more in early stages of interactions in their dyads. The subsequent path that their voice takes—that is, increasing in a virtuous positive trajectory or decreasing in a vicious negative trajectory—is determined by whether their proactive personality matches their supervisors’. For instance, when paired with supervisors who are not similarly proactive, they confront low congruence on change-related goals in the working relationship; hence, rather than building off their initial voice and developing a positive voice trajectory, frustration might lead their voice to decay in a negative trajectory. This theory is supported in a sample of 403 newly formed dyads. Employees higher in proactive personality were more likely to speak up to their supervisors immediately after beginning to work together. Supervisor–employee congruence in proactive personality predicted how voice changed in the dyads in the ensuing six months. Dyadic (in)congruence in proactive personality drove the linear (negative) positive trend in voice and enabled voice growth to pick up momentum and nonlinearly (decelerate) accelerate over time. We discuss our findings’ implications for theory and practice.

**Research Question**
How does voice emerge and develop over time in newly formed supervisor-employee dyads? The paper asks whether employees' proactive personality predicts initial voice and whether supervisor-employee congruence in proactive personality shapes later linear and nonlinear voice trajectories through perceived change goal congruence.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. An employee's proactive personality is positively related to their initial level of voice in a newly formed supervisor–employee dyad.
H2a. An employee whose proactive personality is more aligned with that of the supervisor (i.e., higher congruence) will exhibit a more positive overall trend of voice development over time (i.e., more positive linear slope) in a newly formed dyad.
H2b. An employee whose proactive personality is more aligned with that of the supervisor (i.e., higher congruence) will exhibit a more positive nonlinear trend of voice development over time (i.e., more positive quadratic slope) in a newly formed dyad.
H3a. An employee who is aligned with the supervisor at higher (versus lower) levels of proactive personality will exhibit a more positive overall trend of voice development over time (i.e., more positive linear slope) in a newly formed dyad.
H3b. An employee who is aligned with the supervisor at higher (versus lower) levels of proactive personality will exhibit a more positive nonlinear trend of voice development over time (i.e., more positive quadratic slope) in a newly formed dyad.
H4a. Change goal congruence mediates the relationship between supervisor–employee congruence in proactive personality and the overall trend of voice development over time (i.e., linear slope) in a newly formed dyad.
H4b. Change goal congruence mediates the relationship between supervisor–employee congruence in proactive personality and the nonlinear trend of voice development over time (i.e., quadratic slope) in a newly formed dyad.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employee proactive personality for initial voice; supervisor-employee congruence and level of congruence in proactive personality for later voice trajectories.
- DV(s): Employee voice at Time 1 and the linear and quadratic slopes of voice across Times 1-3.
- Mediators: Change goal congruence, measured from supervisors after Time 1.
- Moderators: Supervisor proactive personality as the matching or mismatching dyadic trait; high-versus-low congruent levels of proactive personality.

The paper argues that newly formed dyads initially create uncertainty and opportunity, activating proactive employees to speak up before dyadic norms have solidified. Over time, supervisors and employees become social situations for each other: congruent proactive personalities create mutual trait activation and shared change goals, supporting voice growth and acceleration. Incongruence produces frustration, perceived mismatch in change priorities, and self-deflating voice trajectories. Change goal congruence explains why proactive personality fit translates into both positive linear and nonlinear voice development.

**Data & Measures**
The primary data are four waves of surveys collected from a large manufacturing firm in China. Working with the firm's HR department, the authors identified new supervisor–employee dyads formed when new or reassigned employees joined a work unit, when new or reassigned supervisors joined a work unit, or when new work units were constituted with new or reassigned supervisors and employees. Participation was voluntary and was solicited after HR staff and top supervisors left the room; participants completed surveys in private spaces during work hours and returned them in sealed envelopes, receiving small incentives per wave and a larger bonus (about a $15 gift card) for completing all waves. Proactive personality was collected from both employees and supervisors at Time 0, just before they started working together. Employees reported their voice in three later waves (Times 1-3) spaced three months apart across the first six months of the dyad, which the socialization and LMX literatures identify as the critical window; the equal spacing produced balanced data for the temporal change models. Change goal congruence experienced in the dyad was reported by supervisors a little after Time 1. The final sample consisted of 403 employees out of 521 targeted across time and sources (77.35% response) nested within 82 supervisors; average age was 30.41 years and 82.13% were male. The employees were engaged in production lines or in functions directly supporting production, were skilled workers such as machine operators, had at least two years of general manufacturing experience (an average of 4.4 years in the final sample), and had specialized technical-school plus in-house training.

Measures were administered in Chinese and developed using the translation-back-translation procedure from original English measures (Brislin, 1980). Proactive personality of employees and of supervisors was measured with the 6-item scale from Parker (1998) (example items: "If I see something I don't like, I fix it," and "I am always looking for better ways to do things"). Change goal congruence was captured from supervisors with a 4-item measure adapted from De Clercq, Thongpapanl, and Dimov (2011) (example item: "[Employee's name] and I always share the same views towards improving work practices"). Voice was measured from employees with a 3-item scale adapted from Detert and Burris (2007) (example item: "I always give [manager's name] suggestions about how to make this work unit better, even if others disagree"). Controls were conscientiousness of employees and supervisors plus their congruence on that trait (4-item short-form scale, Donnellan, Oswald, Baird, & Lucas, 2006), employee-rated LMX collected in all three voice waves (7-item scale, Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995) with its linear and nonlinear trends controlled, employees' professional work experience, and task routinization as a surrogate for situational strength (5-item scale adapted from Jehn, 1995). The authors report that results remained the same with or without these controls.

Analysis proceeded in two stages. Empirical estimates of linear and nonlinear voice development were first generated for each employee from mixed-effects growth models (Bliese & Ployhart, 2002) regressing voice scores on Time (measurement waves coded 0, 1, and 2) and its square, using unstructured errors. Those estimated linear and quadratic slopes were then exported as dependent variables into cross-level polynomial regressions (Jansen & Kristof-Brown, 2005) in conjunction with response surface modeling (Edwards & Parry, 1993) in SAS, accounting for the nesting of multiple employees within a single supervisor. The regressions entered controls plus employee proactive personality (E), supervisor proactive personality (S), their squared terms (E2 and S2), and the E x S product, all centered around the pooled grand mean. Congruence effects (Hypotheses 2a and 2b) were considered supported when the curvature along the incongruence line (E = -S; composite coefficient b3 - b4 + b5) differed from 0, indicating an inverted U-shape, and level-of-congruence effects (Hypotheses 3a and 3b) when the slope along the congruence line (E = S; composite coefficient b1 + b2) was significantly positive. Mediation (Hypotheses 4a and 4b) was tested with a block variable approach (Edwards & Cable, 2009) in which the five polynomial terms were combined into a single weighted linear composite representing the overall congruence effect. The design is a longitudinal observational survey with no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy; the authors state that they cannot definitively establish causality via their study.

Two auxiliary samples, separate from the main Chinese manufacturing sample, support the paper. A validation study for the task routinization control used a Prolific panel of 194 working adults from the United States (average age 34.03 years, average work experience 5.79 years, 49% male) from a wide variety of industries. A supplementary qualitative study used two Qualtrics panels of U.S. working adults in full-time jobs across multiple industries such as consulting, healthcare, information technology, and retail (average work experience 5.63 years, average age 36.6 years, 75.26% women): one panel of employees who had less than two months of experience with their supervisors (n = 63) and one panel of employees who had between two and eight months of experience with their supervisors (n = 68). Participants wrote about a voice incident they had been involved in and what made them feel knowledgeable about the issue; two coders independently coded the narratives, beginning from three assumed bases of voice and expanding the scheme to allow new emergent bases, yielding the six categories summarized in Tables 5 and 6. In a further supplementary analysis on the main sample, supervisors' performance was rated by their own managers one level above at Time 1 and Time 3 using a 5-item scale from Greenhaus, Parasuraman, and Wormley (1990) covering efficiency, quality, productivity, judgment, and overall goal achievement on a 7-point Likert scale.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported. In Model 1, employee proactive personality was significantly related to voice at Time 1 (b = 0.22, p < .01), whereas supervisor proactive personality was not (b = -0.03, p > .05), and the omnibus test for the three second-order terms (E2, E x S, and S2) was not significant (p > .05), indicating no substantive supervisor-employee congruence effects on voice at Time 1. The authors read this as showing that initially in a newly formed dyad the employee's proactive personality is an independent (unconditional) predictor of voice. ICC(1) values indicated that 50.9% of the variance in voice was accounted for by between-employee variance and 13.2% by between-supervisor variance.

Hypothesis 2a was supported. For the linear slope of voice (Model 4) there was significant variance around the slope (p < .05), the omnibus test for the three second-order terms was significant (F = 14.51, p < .01), and the surface along the incongruence line curved downward in an inverted U-shaped manner (curvature = -0.15, p < .01), so the linear slope of voice was higher when there was a fit in proactive personality and any deviation from the congruence line decreased it. Hypothesis 2b was also supported. Variance around the quadratic slope of voice was not significant (p > .05), but the authors allowed it to vary across employees on theoretical grounds (Raudenbush & Bryk, 2002); in Model 5 the omnibus test for the three second-order terms was significant on the nonlinear slope (F = 4.14, p < .01) and the incongruence-line curvature was again negative (curvature = -0.02, p < .01), so the nonlinear slope of voice was likewise higher under proactive personality fit.

Hypotheses 3a and 3b were supported. The slope along the congruence line was significant and positive for the linear slope of voice (0.22, p < .01) and for the quadratic slope of voice (0.02, p < .01), indicating that congruence in proactive personalities at higher levels leads to stronger linear and nonlinear voice trajectories than congruence occurring at lower levels. The response surfaces showed both the linear trajectory (Figure 3) and the quadratic trajectory (Figure 4) of voice higher at the rear corner (high-high congruence) than at the front corner (low-low congruence).

Hypotheses 4a and 4b were supported. Change goal congruence itself showed the congruence pattern (Model 6: omnibus F = 5.18, p < .01; incongruence-line curvature = -0.30, p < .01), and change goal congruence predicted both the linear (0.05, p < .01) and the nonlinear (0.02, p < .01) slopes of voice (Models 7 and 8). In the block variable test, the effect of the block variable on the mediator was significant (1.23, p < .01), and the indirect effects via change goal congruence were significant for the linear slope of voice (0.06, p < .01, 95% CI = [.02 .11]) and for the quadratic slope of voice (0.02, p < .01, 95% CI = [0.01 0.04]).

Supplementary analyses supported the claim that voice development is distinct from LMX development. Controlling for LMX growth did not impact the pattern of results for voice growth, and the correlation between the linear slopes of LMX and voice was modest (b = 0.25, p < .01), as was the correlation between their nonlinear slopes (b = .12, p < .05). LMX steadily increased during the period of study (coefficient = 0.43, p < .01) and plateaued toward the end (coefficient = -0.11, p < .01); employees' proactive personality did not predict initial levels of LMX (b = 0.07, p > .05) as it did voice, and although congruence in the dyad did enhance the positive linear slope of LMX, congruence in proactive personality did not predict the quadratic slope of LMX. Change in supervisors' performance between Time 1 and Time 3 was not predicted by the nonlinear slope in voice (p > .05) but was, the authors report, positively associated with the linear slope in voice, and goal congruence and linear voice growth serially mediated the effect of proactive personality fit on the change in supervisor performance (b = 0.42, p < .05). In the supplementary qualitative study all participants were able to describe an instance of voice; comparing the two panels showed that employees with two to eight months of experience emphasized their ongoing experience with the job substantially more (73% vs. 30%) and noted their prior experience (35% vs. 51%), training (12% vs. 21%), and intuitive sense of right and wrong (3% vs. 27%) less than employees with less than two months of experience.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts voice research from a snapshot view to a temporal dyadic model, showing that employee traits have stronger independent effects early in a relationship and later operate jointly with supervisor traits. It extends supervisor-employee dyad research beyond LMX by showing that voice can follow self-amplifying or self-deflating trajectories rather than merely stabilizing as affiliative relationships do. It also identifies change goal congruence as a proximal mechanism linking proactive personality fit to voice development.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should attend to the social fit between supervisors and employees, not only the technical fit between employees and job demands, because shared proactivity can support sustained voice. Supervisors cannot assume early voice will persist automatically; the paper suggests they need to cultivate repeated successful voice exchanges and recognize whether their change goals align with employees'. The findings also imply that proactivity at only one hierarchical level may be insufficient to sustain voice when adjacent-level partners do not share similar change orientations.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the China setting may shape the results because higher power distance could make supervisor traits more salient than in lower power-distance contexts. They also state that the study cannot definitively establish causality and that random assignment in quasi-experimental designs would strengthen causal inference. The temporal design modeled linear and nonlinear trends across the first six months, but voice may also shift after critical events or follow cyclical ebb-and-flow patterns not captured here.

**Future Research**
Future research should compare proactive personality fit effects across national contexts that vary in power distance and use designs with random assignment of supervisors and employees where possible. Researchers should study voice over longer horizons to identify when it reaches equilibrium and what events disrupt or reset that equilibrium. The authors also encourage work on how the basis, content, quality, and novelty of voice shift as employees move from outsider-like early perspectives to insider on-the-job experience.
