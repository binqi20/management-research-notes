# Strength and Weakness in Numbers? Unpacking the Role of Prevalence in the Diffusion of Reverse Mergers

**Abstract**
A common prediction in research on practice diffusion is a “strength in numbers” effect (i.e., that a growing number of past adopters will increase the number of future adopters). We advance and test a theoretical perspective to explain when and how practice prevalence may also generate a “weakness in numbers” effect. Specifically, to explain the diffusion of reverse mergers—a controversial practice that allows a private firm to go public by merging with a publicly listed “shell company”—we suggest that prevalence affects their diffusion in a complex way based on two divergent social influence pathways, creating (a) a direct, positive effect of practice prevalence on potential adopters, who view prevalence as evidence of the practice’s value; and (b) an indirect, negative effect mediated through third-party evaluators (i.e., investors and the media), who view prevalence as a cause for concern and skepticism. We also highlight the utility of this theoretical framework by analyzing how a decline in the status of past adopters exerts a negative effect on diffusion through both social influence pathways. Employing structural equation modeling techniques, we find support for the hypothesized relationships, and we discuss the implications of the study for future research on practice diffusion.

**Research Question**
What does the prevalence of a controversial practice imply for its subsequent diffusion? The paper asks whether reverse merger prevalence creates only a direct "strength in numbers" effect among potential adopters or also a mediated "weakness in numbers" effect through negative third-party evaluations by investors and the media.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: An increased number of past adopters of RMs exerts a positive direct influence on the subsequent diffusion of RMs.
- H1b: An increased number of past adopters of RMs exerts a negative mediated influence on the subsequent diffusion of RMs, mediated by negative third-party evaluations of RMs (i.e., the media and stock markets).
- H2a: An increased proportion of lower-status adopters of RMs exerts a negative direct influence on the subsequent diffusion of RMs.
- H2b: An increased proportion of lower-status adopters of RMs exerts a negative mediated influence on the subsequent diffusion of RMs, mediated by negative third-party evaluations (i.e., the media and stock markets).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Number of past reverse merger adopters; proportion of past lower-status reverse merger adopters.
- DV(s): Weekly number of new reverse mergers.
- Mediators: Stock market returns for reverse merger firms; negative media coverage of reverse mergers.
- Moderators: No formal moderator is estimated in the main model; supplementary analyses test but do not support moderation by third-party evaluations.

The proposed mechanism is a competitive mediation process. Growing prevalence directly informs potential adopters that reverse mergers may be valuable, increasing adoption through informational social influence. At the same time, because reverse mergers are controversial and challenge accepted IPO norms, growing prevalence attracts skeptical attention from investors and the media, lowering stock-market evaluations, increasing negative coverage, and indirectly reducing later adoption through normative social influence.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: the authors built a population database of U.S. reverse mergers (RMs) from Deal Flow Media, the Securities Data Company database, and RM announcements in Factiva, manually checking SEC filings (the latest 10-K or 10-Q before the transaction, and often the subsequent 8-K or 10-B/10-SB) to confirm that the public counterparty was a shell company; the data consist of 1,904 RMs from 2001 to 2012. Media data come from Factiva's Major News and Business Publications for 2001-2012 (267 articles identified: 148 neutral, 113 negative, 6 positive; inter-rater reliability kappa = 0.88, with negative articles contrasted against neutral plus positive ones). Share prices come from DataStream for 1,466 firms, auditor data from Audit Analytics and SEC filings in Edgar, and IPO data from the Securities Data Company and Jay Ritter's website.
- DV: Number of new RMs = the weekly count of new RMs, dated by the day of the RM announcement, from August 1, 2002 to December 31, 2012 (521 weeks in total, the n reported for every model in Table 2). RMs were aggregated weekly because it takes approximately two weeks to consummate an RM; the analysis start date was set so that stock market data existed for at least 10 RMs.
- IV for H1a and H1b: Number of past RMs = the logarithm of the cumulative past number of RMs, with the count starting in 2001.
- IV for H2a and H2b: % of past lower-status RMs = the past number of RMs by lower-status firms divided by the total number of past RMs. Following Siegel and Wang (2013), adopters were deemed lower status if they hired trivially small auditing firms with fewer than five partners and 10 professional staff members.
- Mediators: Stock market returns = an equally weighted portfolio of the weekly abnormal returns of all traded RMs, where abnormal return is the market-adjusted ARit = Rit - Rmt with Rmt the Nasdaq Composite Index return and an assumed market beta of 1. Negative media coverage = the weekly count of media articles depicting RMs negatively (negative tenor, following Fiss et al., 2012), lagged to avoid simultaneity bias.
- Controls: two regulatory dummies (the period after the SEC's July 2005 rules and the period after the SEC's June 9, 2011 investor warning letter), Available shells (logged weekly count of listed shell companies), Number of new IPOs, % of past small RMs (adopters with a market value of $15 million or less at the time of going public, orthogonalized), lagged weekly counts of buy, hold, and sell analyst recommendations, Investor sentiment (the bull-bear spread from the weekly American Association of Individual Investors survey), Neutral media coverage, a lagged RM portfolio return, and year dummies.
- Estimation and identification: generalized structural equation modeling (GSEM) estimating the diffusion, stock-return, and media-coverage equations simultaneously, with a negative binomial specification for the diffusion and media coverage of RMs and a linear specification for the stock market valuations; mediated effects were computed with Stata's nlcom command using nonparametric bootstrapping with 1,000 replications. The two independent variables were orthogonalized using Stata's orthog command to reduce multicollinearity and lagged "to allow for temporal ordering and to avoid simultaneity bias." The paper names no causal identification strategy - there is no instrument, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, or experiment - so the design is an observational weekly time-series of the U.S. RM population and the estimates are associational. The authors state that the empirical analyses did not allow them to adequately model the mediating role of regulatory actions, so regulatory periods enter as control dummies instead.

**Key Findings**
- Models 4A, 4B, and 4C (all controls plus both independent variables; n = 521 weeks) are the models the authors use to interpret the findings.
- H1a supported: a positive direct effect of RM prevalence on subsequent diffusion (b = 0.61, p < 0.001).
- H2a supported: a negative direct effect of the proportion of lower-status adopters on subsequent RM adoptions (b = -0.11, p < 0.01).
- Prevalence to third-party evaluations: an increase in the number of RMs was followed by more negative stock market returns (b = -1.49, p < 0.05, Model 4B) and by an increase in negative media coverage (b = 0.97, p < 0.05, Model 4C).
- Adopter status to third-party evaluations: a higher proportion of lower-status adopters was followed by more negative stock market reactions (b = -0.81, p < 0.001) and more heightened negative media coverage (b = 0.68, p < 0.01).
- Third-party evaluations to diffusion (Model 4A): negative media coverage (b = -0.23, p < 0.001) and stock market returns (b = 0.05, p < 0.001) were significantly related to the RM diffusion rate; the authors read this as "the more negative the third-party evaluations of RMs, the lower the subsequent diffusion of RMs."
- H1b supported: the total mediated effect of the past number of RMs on subsequent diffusion was -0.30 (p < 0.05), decomposed into a path via stock market valuations (b = -0.07, p < 0.05) and a path via negative media coverage (b = -0.22, p < 0.10, i.e., only marginally significant).
- H2b supported: the total mediated effect of the proportion of lower-status adopters on subsequent diffusion was -0.20 (p < 0.01), with negative mediated effects running through both stock market valuations and negative media coverage.
- Controls behaved as expected: IPO activity was positively associated with RM diffusion, and both the 2005 regulations and the SEC's 2011 warning letter were associated with a drop in the number of RMs. Model 4B also shows increases in negative media coverage exerting a downward effect on RM stock market valuations, and Model 4C shows more positive stock market evaluations reducing the negativity of media coverage.
- Supplementary analyses: adding an interaction to test whether third-party evaluations moderate the direct prevalence effect produced non-significant interaction terms ("We did not find support for this relationship"). Re-estimating the GSEM with the DV redefined as the weekly count of RMs divided by the weekly count of IPOs left all the hypotheses supported. Splitting new adopters into lower- and higher-status groups changed only Hypothesis 2a, showing that lower-status prospective adopters were less sensitive to the lower status of prior RM adopters. Separate regressions replicated the direct and mediating relationships, and estimating the GSEM models without control variables left the hypothesized relationships in place and in fact stronger.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to practice diffusion research by separating prevalence effects into two pathways that can diverge for controversial practices. It shows that prevalence can simultaneously produce a direct strength-in-numbers effect among potential adopters and an indirect weakness-in-numbers effect through third-party evaluators.

The paper also endogenizes third-party evaluations in diffusion theory. Rather than treating media and investor reactions as exogenous influences on adoption, it theorizes and tests how those reactions arise partly from the practice's own growing prevalence and from the declining status of past adopters.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practical-implications section. Its findings indicate that the prevalence of a controversial practice is not a single signal: reverse merger prevalence had a positive direct effect on subsequent diffusion, yet an increase in the number of reverse mergers was also followed by more negative stock market returns and more negative media coverage.

The study further reports that the composition of adopters matters alongside their number — a higher proportion of lower-status adopters had a negative direct effect on subsequent adoptions and was followed by more negative stock market reactions and heightened negative media coverage.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on reverse mergers in the United States, a visible and controversial financial-market practice where investor and media evaluations are consequential. Its theoretical scope is therefore strongest for practices that contravene established legitimacy pillars, are visible to third-party evaluators, and can plausibly threaten relevant actors in the organizational field.

The empirical design treats regulatory actions as controls rather than fully modeling them as mediators, and the authors note that the analyses did not allow them to adequately model regulatory actions' mediating role. The results also depend on a long enough observation window to see third-party contestation emerge after prevalence increases.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine contingencies that strengthen either the direct or mediated pathway, including differences in practice controversy, prospective adopters' vulnerability to third-party evaluations, and adopters' uncertainty about a practice's economic value. The framework could also be applied to noncontroversial practices, where the authors expect complementary rather than competitive mediation.

The paper points to research on multiwave diffusion, practice adaptation, abandonment, and symbolic abandonment of controversial practices. It also calls for work on how adopters shield controversial practices from opposing third-party evaluators through framing, persuasion, knowledge transfer, or adaptation.
