# Salient Expectations? Incongruence across Capability and Integrity Signals and Investor Reactions to Organizational Misconduct

**Abstract**
Research in signaling theory has recently begun to explore how audiences process signal sets and the incongruence across the signals within. However, prior studies have assumed homogenous compositions of signal sets, and thus unidimensional signal incongruence, although social evaluations tend to involve simultaneous processing of different dimensions. In this study, we examine audiences' responses to the interdimensional incongruence between capability and integrity signals, particularly by focusing on how the salience of positive capability signals aggravates investor reactions to organizational misconduct, a negative integrity signal. Using irregular financial restatements as the negative integrity signals and prior alliance announcements as the positive capability signals, we find that investors react more negatively to restatements by firms whose alliance announcements are more salient—that is, the firms that announce more frequently and firms that create more positive expectations from those announcements. We also find that firm size and level of diversification weaken these negative effects. We contribute to research on signaling theory, social evaluations, organizational misconduct, and alliances.

**Research Question**
How do investors respond when a firm has salient positive capability signals before revealing a negative integrity signal? The paper asks whether the salience of prior alliance announcements aggravates investor reactions to irregular financial restatements, and whether firm size and diversification weaken that reaction.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater the frequency of a restating firm's alliance announcements prior to an irregular financial restatement, the more negative the investor reaction toward the restating firm upon the financial restatement.
- H2: The greater the extremity of a restating firm's alliance announcements prior to an irregular financial restatement, the more negative the investor reaction toward the restating firm upon the financial restatement.
- H3: The relationship between the frequency of past alliance announcements and investor reaction to irregular financial restatements is less negative for larger firms than for smaller firms.
- H4: The relationship between the extremity of past alliance announcements and investor reaction to irregular financial restatements is less negative for larger firms than for smaller firms.
- H5: The relationship between the frequency of past alliance announcements and investor reaction to irregular financial restatements is less negative for more diversified firms than for less diversified firms.
- H6: The relationship between the extremity of past alliance announcements and investor reaction to irregular financial restatements is less negative for more diversified firms than for less diversified firms.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Salience of prior alliance announcements, measured as frequency of alliance announcements and extremity of investor reactions to those announcements.
- DV(s): Investor reaction to irregular financial restatements, measured as cumulative abnormal returns around the restatement.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Firm size weakens the negative effects of both frequency and extremity of alliance announcements; firm diversification weakens the frequency effect, while the extremity-by-diversification interaction is not statistically significant.

The mechanism is that irregular financial restatements act as negative integrity signals, while prior alliance announcements act as positive capability signals. When alliance announcements are salient, investors process them together with the restatement, producing interdimensional incongruence between capability and integrity. Because integrity judgments have primacy over capability judgments, investors reassess the prior capability signals through the negative integrity signal and impose larger valuation penalties. These penalties are weaker for larger firms. Diversification weakens the frequency-based salience effect, but the paper does not find a significant extremity-by-diversification interaction.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources and sample construction: 3,477 firms listed in Compustat under three-digit SIC codes 283 (chemical and pharmaceutical industry) and 737 (software industry). Over 2000-2014 firms in these industries issued 935 irregular financial restatements, identified from Audit Analytics; restatements caused by clerical and mathematical errors were excluded, and only irregular restatements that broke ethical, legal, or regulatory norms were retained. Other firm data came from Thompson's SDC Platinum, Compustat, and CRSP.
- DV, investor reaction to the restatement: cumulative abnormal return (CAR) of the restating firm in the five-day window [t-2, t+2], estimated with the market-adjusted model on an equally weighted index with dividends using the Eventus program (CRSP database), with normal-return parameters estimated over the [t-256, t-23] window; CAR values were multiplied by 100 to yield percentages.
- IV, frequency of alliance announcements: the number of alliance announcements a firm made, discounted by the number of months between each alliance announcement and the restatement, counting all alliances announced in the 36-month period before the restatement. Alliance data came from Thompson's SDC Platinum, complemented with the Recombinant alliance database.
- IV, extremity of alliance announcements: the sum of the CARs in the five-day period around a firm's alliance announcements, discounted by the same months-to-restatement term.
- Moderator, firm size: log-transformed total assets of the firm as reported in the quarter before the restatement (Compustat quarterly financial data).
- Moderator, firm diversification: the count of different industry segments in which a firm had a presence (Compustat Segment database).
- Controls: Fortune's Most Admired, CSR strengths, CSR concerns, KLD missing, firm age, firm recent performance (return on sales in the quarter before the restatement), firm liquidity, firm slack, price-to-earnings ratio, number of prior financial restatements, special announcement, restated size of financials in million USD (capturing restatement direction and magnitude), extraordinary items, auditor initiated, SEC investigation, board approved, after SOX Act, plus industry and year fixed effects.
- Estimation and identification: generalized linear regression on CAR with robust standard errors computed at the firm level, because the data contain multiple restatements by the same firm. Selection into restatement was handled with a Heckman two-stage model, using a first-stage probit on all 3,477 firms with employee stock options as the exogenous instrument (significant at p < .01) and the resulting inverse Mills ratio entered as an additional second-stage variable. The design is an observational event study; the paper does not claim experimental or quasi-experimental identification.

**Key Findings**
- H1 (frequency of alliance announcements): the main effect is negative and significant at the 10% level (b1 = -4.17, p < .10), and a one standard deviation increase in frequency corresponds to a CAR of -1.58%. The authors call this coefficient "partially significant" and state that they are cautious about asserting its main effect.
- H2 (extremity of alliance announcements): supported. The main effect is negative and significant (b2 = -0.58, p < .05), and a one standard deviation increase in extremity corresponds to a CAR of -1.19%.
- H3 (frequency x firm size): supported. The interaction is positive and significant (b3 = 2.53, p < .01); the association between CAR and frequency is much steeper for small firms than for large firms. The Johnson-Neyman analysis reported in the Results section finds the frequency effect significant only up to a firm size of 6.7 in logged total assets, approximately 800 million USD in assets.
- H4 (extremity x firm size): supported. The interaction is positive and significant (b3 = 0.26, p < .01); the decrease in CAR associated with extremity is steeper for smaller firms than for larger firms, and the Johnson-Neyman analysis reported in the Results section finds the interaction significant for firms with less than 150 million USD in assets.
- H5 (frequency x firm diversification): supported. The interaction is positive and significant (b3 = 0.70, p < .01); the association between CAR and frequency is much steeper for less diversified firms, and the Johnson-Neyman analysis reported in the Results section finds the interaction significant for all values of firm scope below 15 industry segments.
- H6 (extremity x firm diversification): not supported. The interaction term is positive but not significant.
- Supporting statistics: the mean CAR is -2.04%, and the inverse Mills ratio is not significant, which the authors read as indicating no sample selection bias. Model 9, which enters all four interaction terms together, has a highest VIF of 38, and the authors refrain from interpreting that specification.
- Robustness: results for event windows from [t-2, t+3] to [t-2, t+9] were consistent; at [t-2, t+10] the main effect of frequency of alliance announcements and the extremity-by-firm-size interaction became nonsignificant, and the [t-2, t+15] and [t-2, t+20] windows produced more unstable results. Using square and square-root discount factors, results were broadly consistent except that the frequency main effect becomes nonsignificant and the significance levels of the hypothesized interactions deteriorate slightly. A supplementary analysis separating alliances with competitors from alliances with other firms showed the pattern of results is stronger for alliances with noncompetitors.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling theory by shifting signal incongruence from intradimensional inconsistency among similar signals to interdimensional inconsistency between capability and integrity signals. It also contributes to social evaluations research by showing that integrity judgments can mar capability judgments, and to organizational misconduct and alliance research by showing when normally positive alliance announcements can intensify negative investor reactions.

**Practical Implication**
Managers cannot assume that making capabilities salient will protect firms when integrity failures emerge. The findings imply that firms with highly visible alliance activity may face sharper investor penalties after irregular financial restatements, especially when they are smaller or less diversified.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the empirical design cannot directly capture the microfoundational mechanism through which audiences resolve incongruence. They also focus on irregular financial restatements, investor reactions, frequency and extremity as forms of salience, and two alliance-intensive industries, which may limit generalizability to other forms of misconduct, other stakeholders, other salience mechanisms, or industries where alliances are less important.

**Future Research**
Future research could compare negative-integrity/positive-capability incongruence with other interdimensional combinations, including negative capability signals with positive integrity signals. The authors also call for experimental or policy-capturing work on audience processing, research on ambiguous misconduct, errors, accidents, non-investor stakeholders, alliance characteristics, other strategic initiatives, external shocks, and industries where alliances are less central.
