# Microfoundations of Framing: The Interactional Production of Collective Action Frames in the Occupy Movement

**Abstract**
Social movement scholars have typically focused either on how social movements strategically use collective action frames to confront targets and mobilize supporters or on how targets respond to social movements. Few have captured the interactional dynamics between the two. This oversight tends to obscure how an extant collective action frame may shift or how a new one may arise during such interactions. To address this issue, we focus on movement–target interactions and illuminate the microfoundations of framing that produce a new collective action frame. Drawing on real-time participant observations, we examine how an unintended collective action frame emerged and escalated during a year-long interaction between the Occupy London movement and St. Paul’s Cathedral, Church of England. Occupy protesters shifted from a “capitalism is crisis” frame targeting the U.K.’s financial establishment to a “what would Jesus do?” frame targeting the Church of England. We develop a process model based on the interplay of frame laminations and three situational mechanisms—emotional attachment to a frame, frame sacralization, and frame amplification—derived from an analysis of framing in movement–target interactions to explain the emergence and escalation of an unexpected collective action frame.

**Research Question**
How can the microprocesses of framing in movement-target interactions lead to the emergence and escalation of an unanticipated collective action frame? The paper asks this through the case of Occupy London, whose original "capitalism is crisis" frame targeting the financial establishment shifted into a "what would Jesus do?" frame targeting St. Paul's Cathedral and the Church of England.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: collective action frames, interactional frames, frame laminations, keying, frame breaks, misframing, grievance construction, and movement-target interaction.
- Process / phases: emergence, ascendance, and escalation of the WWJD collective action frame across eight interaction episodes from the initial encounter to the anniversary Evensong stunt.
- Situational mechanisms: emotional attachment to the frame, frame sacralization, and frame amplification through media and public audiences.
- Emergent outcome: an initially unintended religious-moral frame displaced the original anti-capitalist frame and turned a potential ally into a target.
- Boundary conditions: the model is grounded in a physically copresent, materially salient, highly public movement-target encounter.

The process begins with an accidental encounter in which St. Paul's became drawn into Occupy's activity frame after protesters were blocked from the London Stock Exchange. Misframing and frame breaks created a new grievance against St. Paul's; protesters then attached emotionally to the WWJD frame, sacralized it through Christian symbolism and the cathedral setting, and amplified it through media-facing performances. These mechanisms reinforced each other over time, making the new frame increasingly salient even though it diverted attention from Occupy's original target.

**Data & Measures**
Qualitative process study of the interlocking sequence of interactions between Occupy London and St. Paul's Cathedral, with interaction taken as the unit of analysis. The analytical approach is described as open-ended and inductive (Strauss & Corbin, 1997); there is no statistical estimation and no causal identification strategy. Real-time observation was chosen over retrospective analysis because outcomes often appear predictable or inevitable only in hindsight, and real-time work lets researchers capture interpretive processes as they occur rather than reconstruct them once the outcome is known. The case was selected as revealing for three reasons: their accidental encounter created a highly ambiguous situation for both sides, so what transpired was largely the unanticipated result of their interaction; the setting provided a public stage for observing encounters vital for interactional theorization; and studying a series of complex but observable interactions over a one-year period enabled the authors to track unfolding framing.

- Participant observation. One author spent one or two days per week over the 4.5 months of occupation, amounting to 280 hours, visiting the campsite, attending general assemblies (meetings of Occupy's governing body), working group meetings, and Tent City University debates, and observing the High Court case and meetings with St. Paul's officials. After the camp was evicted she attended events and meetings such as OccupyFaith and the anniversary Evensong service at St. Paul's. Field notes were taken on site and detailed accounts written up later.
- Interviews. 44 semi-structured, in-depth interviews with 28 Occupy activists and 16 respondents from St. Paul's and the Church of England, conducted in two phases: 30 interviews in early 2012 with respondents identified during fieldwork at the campsite, followed by 14 interviews after Occupy's eviction with respondents identified as critical in the interaction. Given the movement's "leaderless" nature, the authors targeted protesters in key roles such as court case defendants, the Economics Working Group, the Press Team, and the Church Liaison Group; on the Church of England side they identified vicars attending Occupy events (e.g., "flash prayers") and interviewed key staff from St. Paul's whose job titles are not disclosed at the respondents' request. Interviews lasted between 30 and 120 minutes and were recorded and transcribed. Alongside these, the authors engaged in multiple informal conversations with more than 40 occupiers, ranging from brief exchanges to multi-hour discussions.
- Documents and social media. Systematic content analysis of Occupy's and St. Paul's websites, press releases, articles in major British newspapers, The Church Times, and The Occupied Times (the camp's newspaper). Social media, Twitter, online discussion forums, blogs, "Radio Occupy," and livestream video recordings provided further accounts, as did minutes and recordings from general assemblies, Tent City University events, and internal meetings, all archived and transcribed selectively. Academic works on Occupy were read and visual symbols such as banners examined. Data from observations, interviews, videos, visuals, media, and documents were fed into NVivo to create a database.
- Operationalization of the focal unit. Following Goffman (1983), social interaction was defined as that which uniquely transpires in social situations when two or more individuals come into another's response presence, whether through physical copresence, telephonic connection, or letter exchange. Because there were relatively few face-to-face encounters, Goffman's typology of "basic substantive units" was consulted to identify a wider range of interactions. Table 1 presents over 47 types of interactions, classified as contacts, conversational encounters, platform performances, and social occasions, each tagged with an interaction episode number and date.
- Coding, phase 1 (process analysis, Langley, 1999). A detailed timeline of events was constructed and the focal interactions coded. The authors coded interaction frames (the definition of what is going on in a situation) and interactional framing (the process of collectively producing frames through interactions), and analyzed emerging keyings, frame breaks, and misframings. Temporal bracketing was then used to bundle sequences of interactions, defined not by their lengths but by shifts in interactional framing; several iterations yielded eight interactional episodes, from which three phases emerged (emergence, ascendance, escalation of the WWJD frame), with observations plotted on a visual map (Figure 2).
- Coding, phase 2 (thematic analysis, Gioia et al., 2013). The authors coded for underlying factors that could explain the attachment to a new collective action frame, going through multiple coding rounds and working up from data-led codes to clustering into themes, producing the first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions shown in the Figure 1 data structure and yielding the three situational mechanisms. Following Stinchcombe (1991), situations were treated as places to locate mechanisms, and mechanisms as analytical constructs providing hypothetical links between observable events. In a final phase the mechanisms from phase 2 were linked back to the phase 1 process analysis and assembled into the process model (Figure 4).
- Trustworthiness and reporting. The authors discussed their findings with an Occupy protester and a theologian to increase confidence in the analysis, and report quotes using generic descriptors, fictional names, and publicly available data wherever a quote could reveal a respondent's identity.

**Key Findings**
- Headline result. The year-long interaction produced an unanticipated collective action frame: Occupy protesters shifted from a "capitalism is crisis" frame targeting the U.K.'s financial establishment to a "what would Jesus do?" (WWJD) frame targeting the Church of England. Targeting St. Paul's was neither intended nor conducive to the original frame; a potential ally became the key target, and City of London bankers were let "off the hook." The frame's trajectory runs through eight interaction episodes grouped into three phases: emergence (IE 1-2), ascendance (IE 3-4), and escalation (IE 5-8).
- Phase 1, emergence. Canon Chancellor Giles Fraser's first-morning "welcome" (October 16, 2011) looked like frame alignment but proved to be misframing: protesters read it as an invitation to stay and set up a permanent tent city, while St. Paul's staff experienced a frame break, feeling that protesters were abusing the temporary sanctuary offered. The WWJD frame was born on October 21, 2011 at an emergency general assembly convened to debate the dean's open letter, which pleaded for protesters to leave and announced closure of the cathedral. A breakout group picked up a Guardian blog by a Christian social activist asking whether Jesus would kick the protesters off St. Paul's grounds, and the idea to provocatively ask "Where would Jesus be?" consolidated.
- Phase 2, ascendance. St. Paul's closed its doors for the first time since World War II, citing fire, health, and safety issues, and voted to join the City of London in legal action to clear the camp. Protesters treated this as a brutal frame break and rekeyed it as a "betrayal" of the initial welcome and as St. Paul's "closing the doors on the 99%." Fraser resigned on October 27, 2011 and the dean on October 31; "capitalism is crisis" banners were replaced by "what would Jesus do?" banners. The bishop of London's open meeting of October 30 attempted to align the conflicting frames, and St. Paul's suspended legal action a day after; but the collaborative keying entangled protesters further in the WWJD frame, because they believed it could induce the Church of England into doing what Jesus "would" have done. The camp became increasingly defined vis-a-vis St. Paul's rather than the financial establishment, and the conflict made front-page headlines in major British newspapers and ignited a national debate about the moral obligation of St. Paul's, and religion more broadly, to advocate for social justice.
- Phase 3, escalation. Two further frame breaks escalated the conflictual keying: St. Paul's registrar unexpectedly testified against Occupy in support of the City's court case in December 2011, and City of London police forcefully removed praying protesters from the cathedral steps on the night of the eviction (February 28, 2012), which protesters framed as the "third betrayal." At the anniversary Evensong on October 14, 2012, with some 50 occupiers inside the cathedral, four white-clad female protesters chained themselves to the base of the pulpit and unfurled an umbrella reading "Throw the moneylenders out of the temple," staging a "mic check" that compared their three "betrayals" to Jesus being denied three times. Stunned by this frame break, St. Paul's rescinded its offer to collaborate with Occupy; the parties parted ways acrimoniously, with both ruing an unintended escalation that transformed potential allies into adversaries.
- Mechanism 1, emotional attachment. Interactions produced emotional energy; hope raised by the "welcome" and violated through frame breaks generated anger and moral outrage, and the alternation between hope and anger reinforced attachment to a conflictual keying of the WWJD frame. Protesters could not extricate themselves from their fixation on St. Paul's even though it diverted the movement's attention from its original frame and target, an instance of a "feeling trap" in which emotions spiral on for long periods.
- Mechanism 2, sacralization. Potent but previously unattended sacred meanings were activated in interaction, imbuing the frame with a sense of higher moral purpose. Protesters reframed the chance encounter as "providential" and the frame shift as a "calling," symbolically linked the camp to the cathedral (protesters comparing themselves to St. Paul "the tentmaker," wearing rosaries, staging flash prayers), treated the campsite and cathedral steps as "holy ground," and compared their betrayal to Jesus's betrayal by religious authorities. The authors note that sacralization here coincided with religious meaning, but that in social movements more broadly strongly held values, beliefs, and ideals can imbue a frame with sacredness.
- Mechanism 3, frame amplification. Third-party audiences, above all the media, both reinforced and transformed the frame, lending it "situational resonance" and inducing protesters to stage dramatic performances aimed at "out-Christianing the Church." Amplification became a double-edged sword: protesters reached a much wider audience, but the more the WWJD frame was amplified the more they nourished it through dramatic performances, and the more it overshadowed the original anti-capitalism frame and detracted from the initial target.
- The mechanisms are mutually reinforcing rather than independent. Sacralization energizes participants emotionally and surges in emotional energy heighten and rivet attention to emerging frames (Figure 4, arrows 1b, 2b); amplification creates a positive feedback loop between collective action framing and audience adoption (arrows 3b, 3c); and amplification also reinforces emotional attachment and sacralization (arrow 4). Together they operate across three cycles that lead to the emergence, ascendance, and escalation of the collective action frame.
- Outcome contingency in the paper's own terms. Because the interaction was loaded with uncertainty and indeterminacy, multiple potentialities and alternative pathways inhered: the existing frame could have been altered instead, or both frames could have merged into a composite frame. Over time, however, reinforcing feedback loops made alternative outcomes less likely and increasingly bound movement actors to the new collective action frame. Attempts to revive the original frame did not succeed even though the archbishop of Canterbury prominently endorsed "capitalism is crisis," and after the eviction protesters refused to move their weekly general assemblies to a more potent symbol of financial wealth, which could have revived the original collective action frame.
- Retrospective assessment on both sides. Protesters regretted the unintended outcome, describing the conflict as a distraction that created a deviation from the actual target and saying they had been there for the London Stock Exchange rather than the Church; St. Paul's respondents likewise regretted becoming Occupy's target when the two could have made common cause against bankers and the City.
- Because the study is an inductive single-case process study, it tests no hypotheses and reports no effect estimates; the findings are the process model and its three situational mechanisms.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to social movement and organization theory by shifting attention from strategic deployment of ready-made collective action frames to the interactional production of frames in concrete situations. It theorizes collective action frames as emergent, contested, and endogenous to movement-target interactions rather than as context-free tools that movements simply choose and deploy.

The paper also identifies situational mechanisms that explain how interactional frames become collective action frames. Emotional attachment, sacralization, and amplification show how emotions, moral meaning, material setting, and third-party audiences can transform local interactional dynamics into a movement-level frame trajectory.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practitioner-facing implications section; its stated implications are addressed to social movement and framing scholarship. Its findings show that seemingly local interactional choices redirected the movement's grievance, target, and public identity: Occupy shifted from a "capitalism is crisis" frame targeting the U.K.'s financial establishment to a "what would Jesus do?" frame targeting the Church of England, an outcome that was neither intended nor conducive to the original frame.

The study reports that the target's own moves reshaped the conflict: the Canon Chancellor's first-morning welcome looked like frame alignment but proved to be a misframing, which St. Paul's staff then experienced as a frame break, while the cathedral's closure and its decision to join legal action were treated by protesters as a brutal frame break and rekeyed as a betrayal. The frame that emerged was thus the unanticipated product of the interaction order rather than of either side's strategy.

**Limitations**
The authors acknowledge that their model is built around a setting marked by physical copresence between a movement and its target. This may limit direct extension to virtual communities and social-media-based movements, where amplification and emotional dynamics may operate through different material and sensory conditions.

The authors also note that they studied a diverse movement with a loosely defined goal - fighting inequality - and that social movements with a higher degree of cohesion and focus might be less likely to sway from their core claims.

**Future Research**
Future research can examine how broader cultural meaning systems are reworked at the interactional level and how interactional meanings scale up to affect those systems. The authors specifically invite work on how collective meaning structures and micro-interactional meaning making recursively connect in other movements and contentious settings.

The paper also calls for more research on multimodal framing processes. Scholars could study how verbal, visual, material, sensory, and embodied modes combine in framing, including how building design, spatial arrangements, images, gestures, and immersive encounters shape collective action frames.
