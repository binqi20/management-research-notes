# Workplace Interpersonal Capitalization: Employee Reactions to Coworker Positive Event Disclosures

**Abstract**
“Capitalization”—the process of sharing a personal positive event with others— provides disclosers with many advantages such as increased self-esteem, relationship quality, and relationship trust. Existing theory on capitalization has been developed within the context of close relationships where individuals typically maintain aligned outcomes. However, when outcomes are opposed such as when individuals are competing, capitalization may not be an effective relationship behavior as currently conceptualized. In this work, I examine the consequences of capitalization within the workplace, a context that is characteristically competitive. I build a model of workplace interpersonal capitalization, defined as the process of sharing personal work-related positive events with coworkers. Drawing from social comparison theory, I posit that hearing a coworker disclose work-related positive events causes an employee to experience either inspiration or envy as a function of the degree to which the disclosing coworker is viewed as a competitor. Furthermore, I explain how these inspirational and envious reactions motivate interpersonal citizenship behavior and social undermining, respectively. Overall, I establish distinct responder-based reactions and postulate competition as a key boundary condition that alters the effects of capitalization.

**Research Question**
How do employees respond when coworkers disclose personal work-related positive events, and how does perceived competition with the disclosing coworker shape responders' emotional and behavioral reactions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The positive relationship of workplace interpersonal capitalization and inspiration is stronger when competition is low (vs. high).
- H2: The positive indirect effect of workplace interpersonal capitalization on interpersonal citizenship behavior (ICB), as mediated by inspiration, is stronger when competition is low (vs. high).
- H3: The positive relationship of workplace interpersonal capitalization and envy is stronger when competition is high (vs. low).
- H4: The positive indirect effect of workplace interpersonal capitalization on social undermining, as mediated by envy, is stronger when competition is high (vs. low).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Workplace interpersonal capitalization, defined as coworker disclosure of personal work-related positive events.
- DV(s): Interpersonal citizenship behavior toward the discloser and social undermining of the discloser.
- Mediators: Inspiration and envy.
- Moderators: Competition or rivalry with the disclosing coworker.

The mechanism is a social-comparison process. Positive event disclosures provide upward comparison information; when competition is low, the discloser's success appears more attainable or co-beneficial, eliciting inspiration and motivating interpersonal citizenship behavior. When competition is high, the same disclosure highlights opposed outcomes, eliciting envy and motivating social undermining as a way to reduce the comparison threat.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (dyadic field survey; the author states the study was correlational and does not speak to causality). A large sample of university employees from the United States was invited to take part in a study on coworker dynamics in exchange for a small gift card. Four hundred and fifty employees completed the initial "discloser" survey; 319 of the coworkers they nominated completed the "responder" survey; pairing the surveys produced 284 discloser–responder dyads after removing unmatchable cases and incomplete responses.
- Study 1 measures. Disclosers rated workplace interpersonal capitalization on the 5-item scale developed and validated in Appendix A (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree; α = .94). Responders rated competition as rivalry on Kilduff's (2014) 4-item rivalry scale (α = .94), inspiration on the 5-item Sweetman, Spears, Livingstone, and Manstead (2013) scale (α = .87), envy on the 2-item Cuddy, Fiske, and Glick (2007) scale (α = .88), discloser-directed ICB in the previous month on the 8-item person-focused scale of Settoon and Mossholder (2002) (α = .93), and social undermining of the discloser in the previous month on the 7-item Duffy, Ganster, Shaw, Johnson, and Pagon (2006) scale (α = .93). Controls were responder social comparison orientation (Gibbons & Buunk, 1999, ability scale; α = .91), perceived discloser extraversion (Saucier, 1994, 8-item scale; α = .89), and the reported frequency of the discloser's positive events (1 = never to 5 = always).
- Study 1 analysis. Path analysis in Mplus version 8.1 following Edwards and Lambert (2007), with indirect effects tested by the distribution-of-the-product method. Because of the low mean of competition, the moderator was probed at values of 1.00 (the scale floor) and 2.68 (+1 SD).
- Study 2 (2 × 2 between-individuals randomized experiment). A paid Prolific Academic online panel of 250 students from the United Kingdom and the United States, representing a diverse set of universities and disciplines; after removing incomplete responses and cases in which participants failed to follow directions, the final sample comprised 225 participants (119 female, 53%; mean age 25.23 years, SD = 7.40).
- Study 2 manipulations and measures. Competition was manipulated with a writing task (0 = competition low, 1 = competition high) in which participants wrote about a student with whom they currently compete (high) or cooperate (low). Capitalization was manipulated with a selfie video by a hired actor posing as a student who either disclosed that she aced the exam and was congratulated by friends (high) or discussed the exam in general with no reference to her score (low). Inspiration was self-rated on the Sweetman et al. (2013) inspiration, admiration, and awe items (1 = does not describe my feelings to 5 = clearly describes my feelings; α = .88) and envy on the Cuddy et al. (2007) envy and jealousy items with the same prompt and anchors (α = .93). To reduce social desirability concerns, ICB and social undermining were measured indirectly by asking how likely others would be to enact those behaviors toward the student in the video, using an adapted 8-item Settoon and Mossholder (2002) scale (α = .96) and a modified 7-item Duffy et al. (2006) scale (α = .95), both anchored 1 = extremely unlikely to 7 = extremely likely. Analysis used 2 × 2 ANOVA plus bias-corrected bootstrapping (1,000 samples) following Preacher and Hayes (2008).
- Supplementary study (reported alongside Study 2). Two hundred and twenty full-time workers were recruited from the United States via Mechanical Turk, and the 213 who passed attention checks (mean age 35.09; 48% female) read a scenario in which they imagined working at a restaurant with a coworker named Katie, crossing a competitive versus noncompetitive scenario with self-disclosure (told directly by Katie) versus other-disclosure (told by a third party) of Katie's perfect customer rating. Inspiration (α = .96) and envy (α = .90) were each measured with four items from Thrash and Elliot (2003) and Cohen-Charash (2009) (1 = very slightly or not at all to 5 = extremely), and conditions were compared with 2 × 2 ANOVA.
- Study 3 (experience-sampling diary study). A panel of 100 working adults located in the United Kingdom was recruited with the assistance of Prolific Academic for a two-week diary study. In an entry survey participants listed coworkers with whom they have daily face-to-face interaction; one was randomly selected as the referent for the whole study, and participants completed competition and control measures. One week later they received a survey at the end of each workday for 10 consecutive working days asking whether that coworker had shared a positive event that day, and were then instructed to describe their most recent capitalization conversation (if yes) or their last general conversation (if no). Five participants who provided no conversation descriptions and 13 who reported capitalization on either no days or all days were removed, leaving a final sample of 82 participants (mean age 35.89, SD = 10.19; 56% female). After excluding days on which participants had no interaction with their coworker, the data set provided 644 conversations, comprising 318 capitalization conversations and 326 general conversations; participants averaged 9.01 (SD = 1.53) survey completions and each completed at least four.
- Study 3 measures. Workplace interpersonal capitalization is a binary variable coded 1 on days when the coworker disclosed a positive event and 0 on days when the coworker did not. Competition was measured in the entry survey with the same 4-item Kilduff (2014) scale used in Study 1 (α = .95). Inspiration used the 4-item Thrash and Elliot (2003) scale (1 = very slightly or not at all to 5 = extremely; average daily α = .96), envy the 9-item episodic envy scale of Cohen-Charash (2009) (average daily α = .88), ICB the 3-item scale of Lam, Wan, and Roussin (2016) (average daily α = .91), and social undermining four items from Duffy et al. (2006) (average daily α = .89). Controls were social comparison orientation (α = .92), coworker positive event frequency, study day, and sine and cosine functions equal to one workweek.
- Study 3 analysis. Multilevel path analysis in Mplus version 8.1 with observed conversations nested within responders. Within-person effects were modeled with random slopes, whereas competition, social comparison orientation, and coworker positive events were modeled as between-person factors, with competition acting as a cross-level moderator on the capitalization–mediator relationships. The Level 1 and Level 2 predictor variables were respectively group-mean and grand-mean centered, and the indirect-effect hypotheses were tested at high and low values of competition (1 or −1 SD) via the distribution-of-products method. Within-person variance in the mediator and dependent variables ranged from 57% to 76%.
- Measure validation. Two supplementary Mechanical Turk studies supported the inspiration measure: 101 full-time employees rated each Sweetman et al. (2013) item as representing the definition of inspiration significantly above the scale midpoint (Ms ≥ 5.23; ts(100) ≥ 9.20, ps < .001), and 100 full-time employees who read an inspiring story rated it on the Sweetman et al. (2013) and Thrash and Elliot (2003) scales, with the two measures strongly correlated (r(100) = .90, p < .001). A separate study randomly assigned 54 participants to one of the two Study 2 videos and found no significant difference in the actor's displayed positive affect between the high (M = 3.68, SD = 0.54) and low (M = 3.62, SD = 0.60) capitalization videos, t(51) = −.42, p = .681.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (284 dyads) supported all four hypotheses. H1: the capitalization × competition interaction on inspiration was significant (b = −.12, p = .023), and capitalization was positively related to inspiration when competition was low (b = .14, p = .031) but not when it was high (b = −.08, p = .268). H2: the indirect effect on ICB via inspiration was positive when competition was low (ab = .05, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [.005, .105]) but not when it was high (ab = −.03, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [−.084, .022]), and the difference was significant (difference = −.08, 95% CI [−.156, −.013]). H3: the interaction on envy was significant (b = .12, p = .043), although capitalization was positive yet nonsignificantly related to envy when competition was high (b = .10, p = .244) and negative yet nonsignificant when competition was low (b = −.13, p = .082). H4: the conditional indirect effects on social undermining were nonsignificant at both high (ab = .01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [−.006, .031]) and low (ab = −.01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [−.034, .001]) competition, but the difference between them was significant (difference = .03, 95% CI [.001, .053]), which the author reports as supporting H4.
- Study 2 (225 participants) supported all four hypotheses. H1: capitalization and competition interacted on inspiration, F(1, 224) = 4.52, p = .035, ηp² = .02; under low competition, inspiration was higher in the high- than the low-capitalization condition (M = 3.18, SD = 1.06 vs. M = 2.49, SD = 0.95), F(1, 224) = 12.27, p = .001, whereas under high competition the conditions did not differ (M = 1.93, SD = 1.12 vs. M = 1.84, SD = 1.00), F(1, 224) = .26, p = .614. H2: capitalization indirectly predicted ICB when competition was low (ab = .51, SE = 0.16, 95% CI [.215, .841]) but not when it was high (ab = .07, SE = 0.15, 95% CI [−.213, .378]); the difference was significant (difference = −.44, 95% CI [−.931, −.056]). H3: the interaction on envy was significant, F(1, 224) = 5.37, p = .021, ηp² = .02; under high competition, envy was higher in the high- than the low-capitalization condition (M = 2.88, SD = 1.35 vs. M = 2.12, SD = 1.21), F(1, 224) = 15.09, p < .001, whereas under low competition the conditions did not differ (M = 1.46, SD = 0.74 vs. M = 1.35, SD = 0.62), F(1, 224) = .36, p = .551. H4: capitalization exhibited an indirect effect on social undermining when competition was high (ab = .48, SE = 0.18, 95% CI [.187, .886]) but not when it was low (ab = .07, SE = 0.08, 95% CI [−.080, .248]); the difference was significant (difference = .41, 95% CI [.065, .805]).
- Study 3 (644 conversations nested within 82 participants) supported H1, H3, and H4, but H2 was not supported. H1: the capitalization × competition interaction influenced inspiration (g = −.13, p = .045), and capitalization predicted inspiration both when competition was low (g = 1.08, p < .001) and when it was high (g = 0.68, p < .001). H2: capitalization had an indirect effect on ICB when competition was low (ab = .30, SE = 0.08, 95% CI [.164, .462]) and also when it was high (ab = .19, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [.087, .318]), and the difference in effects was not significant (difference = .11, 95% CI [−.079, .305]); the author states that Hypothesis 2 is not supported. H3: the interaction impacted envy (g = .13, p = .005), with capitalization predicting envy when competition was high (g = .37, p < .001) but not when it was low (g = −.01, p = .844). H4: capitalization showed an indirect effect on social undermining when competition was high (ab = .09, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [.028, .176]) but not when it was low (ab = −.01, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [−.041, .034]), and the difference was significant (difference = .09, 95% CI [.020, .184]).
- Across the three studies the support pattern is therefore partial: H1, H3, and H4 were supported in Studies 1, 2, and 3, whereas H2 was supported in Studies 1 and 2 and not supported in Study 3.
- The author also notes sign differences between the two field studies: when competition was high, the effect of capitalization on inspiration was trending downward in Study 1 but was positive in Study 3; and when competition was low, the effect of capitalization on envy was trending downward in Study 1 but was near zero in Study 3.
- Supplementary study (213 participants): disclosure type and competition interacted on inspiration, F(1, 212) = 7.09, p = .008, ηp² = .03, with a greater difference in inspiration across competition conditions when the positive event was delivered by self-disclosure than by other-disclosure. The corresponding interaction on envy was marginally significant, F(1, 212) = 3.17, p = .076, ηp² = .02, and was primarily attributable to a greater difference in envy across competition conditions for self-disclosures.
- Study 3 supplementary analyses: 60% of the time participants reported learning of their coworker's positive event from other sources "not at all." Disclosures primarily involved making meaningful work project progress (26%), attaining something such as a sale, new client, or managerial approval (19%), and receiving praise from a supervisor (17%) or customer (5%). For dyads above (vs. below) the mean in competition, 55% (vs. 41%) of events benefitted only the discloser, whereas 11% (vs. 36%) co-benefitted the responder; chi-squared tests of independence showed that competition impacted the likelihood of benefitting the discloser only (χ²(1) = 7.10, p = .008) and of co-benefitting the responder (χ²(1) = 29.50, p = .045).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts capitalization theory from a discloser-centered account to a responder-centered account. It shows that capitalization is not uniformly constructive in workplaces: under competition, disclosure of good fortune can backfire by producing envy and social undermining. The paper also extends workplace social comparison research by showing that privately disclosed, less visible positive events can trigger consequential comparisons.

**Practical Implication**
Employees should be attentive to competitive cues before sharing positive work events with coworkers, because the benefits of capitalization depend on whether outcomes feel aligned or opposed. Managers can reduce the risk of envy and undermining by deemphasizing competition and designing performance metrics or reward structures that support information and resource sharing.

**Limitations**
The author notes selection bias in Study 1 because participants chose which coworker to invite, and Study 1 was correlational rather than causal. Study 2 did not fully test whether personal sharing has unique effects, although the supplementary study addressed self-disclosure versus third-party disclosure. The field studies also reported relatively low levels of competition, limiting what can be inferred about highly competitive workplaces.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine how the content and type of capitalization shape responder reactions, including whether positive events reflect hard work, luck, first news, or information the responder already knew. The paper also calls for work on who does or does not capitalize at work, and what contexts encourage or discourage positive event disclosure.
