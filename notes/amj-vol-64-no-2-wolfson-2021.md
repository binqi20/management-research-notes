# Deploying Human Capital Resources: Accentuating Effects of Situational Alignment and Social Capital Resources

**Abstract**
Researchers and organizational leaders alike have long known that securing human capital (HC) is critical for organizational success. The bulk of research in this domain has addressed issues regarding the accrual of HC stocks. However, there has been a dearth of research exploring the underlying mechanisms through which accrued HC stocks translate to competitive advantage. We expand on Wolfson and Mathieu’s (2018) human capital resource complementarity (HCRC) theory by incorporating the deployment of human capital resources (HCRs) as well as the role of social capital resources (SCRs) in the form of members’ shared team task-specific experience in predicting performance. We empirically test HCRC theory-derived hypotheses using a sample of 448 cyclists from 17 world teams who competed in 26 races during the 2015 season of the Union Cycliste Internationale World Tour. Our results revealed that higher-level HCR stocks indirectly led to deployed HCR performance through deployed HCR alignment with situational characteristics. Furthermore, SCRs acted as a critical boundary condition for the value of aligning deployed HCRs with situational characteristics such that the alignment of deployed HCRs was only advantageous when paired with relatively high levels of SCRs. Implications for future theory building, research, and practice related to HCR deployment are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do accumulated human capital resource stocks translate into performance through deployment decisions across changing task situations? The paper asks whether aligning deployed HCRs with race characteristics improves start-list performance and whether social capital resources, operationalized as shared team task-specific experience, strengthen that alignment-performance relationship.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The paper first states three propositions and then derives six contextualized hypotheses from them. Footnote 1 specifies that the (a) portion of Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3 corresponds to mountains and the (b) portion corresponds to sprints.
- Proposition 1: HCR stocks and situational characteristics jointly influence deployed members' HCRs.
- Proposition 2: Deployed HCR alignment with situational characteristics influences the performance of deployed members.
- Proposition 3: SCRs accentuate the positive relationship between HCRs and performance and further bolster the positive effects of aligning HCRs with situational characteristics.
- Hypothesis 1a(b): Race-level mountain (sprint) characteristics moderate the positive relationship between team-level mountain (sprint) HCR stocks and deployed mountain (sprint) HCRs, such that the relationship is stronger in races with greater mountain (sprint) characteristics.
- Hypothesis 2a(b): Race-level mountain (sprint) characteristics moderate the positive relationship between deployed mountain (sprint) HCRs and mountain (sprint) performance, such that the relationship is stronger in races with greater mountain (sprint) characteristics.
- Hypothesis 3a(b): Deployed STTE interacts with the positive relationship between deployed mountain (sprint) HCRs and changing race-level mountain (sprint) characteristics as related to deployed HCR mountain (sprint) performance. Specifically, the positive relationship between deployed mountain (sprint) HCRs and mountain (sprint) race characteristics strengthens in the presence of higher deployed STTE.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team-level HCR stocks; deployed HCRs in mountain and sprint competencies; race-level mountain and sprint characteristics.
- DV(s): Deployed HCR performance, measured as mountain points and sprint points earned by team start lists.
- Mediators: Deployed HCR alignment with situational characteristics is the indirect pathway linking accumulated HCR stocks to performance.
- Moderators: Race characteristics moderate stock-to-deployment and deployment-to-performance relationships; deployed shared team task-specific experience moderates the value of HCR-situation alignment.

The mechanism is a cross-level deployment process. Teams with stronger HCR stocks have more options for composing start lists, but performance gains depend on whether those deployed competencies match the specific race demands. Social capital resources matter because members with greater shared task-specific experience can coordinate interdependent work more effectively, allowing aligned HCRs to become performance advantages rather than merely available talent.

**Data & Measures**
- Data and sample: three archival sources (Cycling Weekly, 2015; Pro Cycling Stats, 2015; Sportune, 2015) supplied race, individual, and team data for the 2015 Union Cycliste Internationale World Tour. The final sample was 448 professional male cyclists from 17 World Teams competing in 26 races, yielding 442 start-list performances. Teams carried rosters of 27 to 33 riders (mean = 30, SD = 1.93) and deployed seven to nine riders per race.
- DV, deployed HCR performance: the sum of points accrued by each team's deployed start list per race, indexed separately as mountain points (mean = 27.66, SD = 41.73) and sprint points (mean = 72.65, SD = 93.14). Of the 26 races, 12 offered only mountain points, 11 offered only sprint points, and 10 offered both.
- IV, team HCR stocks: team-level totals of general classification, mountain, and sprint points, averaging 6,971.59 general points (SD = 2,146.87), 637 mountain points (SD = 326.34), and 1,833.35 sprint points (SD = 618.46); team salary (13.06 million euros, SD = 3.34) was entered alongside them as a team-level predictor.
- Mediator, deployed HCRs: the sum of general classification, mountain, and sprint points from 2013 and 2014 accrued by the individuals deployed on each 2015 start list. Start lists averaged 1,158.75 general classification points (SD = 610.06), 116.76 mountain points (SD = 98.82), and 298.67 sprint points (SD = 168.81). Individual cyclist competencies were indexed the same way over the prior two seasons, averaging 148.79 general points (SD = 188.32), 36.22 mountain points (SD = 51.65), and 82.02 sprint points (SD = 113.28).
- Moderator, race characteristics: each race's mountain, sprinting, or general classification points expressed against the total points of that type across the 26 races, so that higher values represent a greater emphasis on that characteristic; computed from Pro Cycling Stats (2015).
- Moderator, deployed SCRs: shared team task-specific experience (STTE) indexed as the number of days the individuals in a deployed start list had ridden together in World Tour races over the prior two seasons, treated as a network density measure. Start-list STTE ranged from 0 to 1,144 days (mean = 329.26, SD = 190.42).
- Estimation: cross-classified mixed models in which Level 1 start-list deployments (n = 442) are cross-nested within 17 teams (Level 2A) and 26 races (Level 2B), fitted through a multistage model-building sequence (null model, linear trend, team characteristics, race characteristics, two-way interactions, three-way interactions). Because not all races awarded both point types, the mountain and sprint performance analyses used Level 1 sample sizes of 204 and 187, respectively. Variables were z-scored at their respective levels, p < .05 was used throughout, pseudo effect sizes are reported in place of conventional R-squared, and interactions were probed with regions-of-significance tests. The design is archival and associational, and the paper names no causal identification strategy; the implied cross-level indirect effect was examined with a 2-1-1 moderated mediation analysis using a Monte Carlo procedure with 20,000 bootstraps.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses (1a/b, 2a/b, 3a/b) were supported.
- Hypothesis 1a supported: race-level mountain characteristics accentuated the positive relationship between team-level mountain HCR stocks and deployed mountain HCRs (b = .10, SE = .05, p < .05), with the model explaining 28.73% of the variance in deployed mountain HCRs. Hypothesis 1b supported: the parallel sprint interaction was also positive (b = .15, SE = .05, p < .01), with 30.26% of criterion variance explained. Both interactions were significant across the entire observed range of the respective race characteristics.
- Hypothesis 2a supported: deployed mountain HCRs interacted positively with race mountain characteristics in predicting mountain points (b = 8.16, SE = 2.69, p < .01), significant in the top 42% of mountain races; the authors describe the magnitude as roughly the difference between the first-place and third-place team in their 17-team sample. Hypothesis 2b supported: the sprint counterpart was also positive (b = 23.82, SE = 3.54, p < .001), significant in the top 28% of sprint races and described as roughly the difference between the first-place and fifth-place team.
- Hypothesis 3a supported: the three-way interaction of deployed mountain HCRs, race mountain characteristics, and STTE predicted mountain points (b = 4.90, SE = 2.03, p < .05), bringing total variance explained to 44.08%. The alignment benefit was present only at high STTE, significant for the top 33% of mountain races, and absent at low STTE. Hypothesis 3b supported: the sprint three-way interaction was likewise positive (b = 8.33, SE = 3.84, p < .05), bringing total variance explained to 76.39%; the effect was present at high STTE for the top 27% of sprint races and yielded no significant benefit at low STTE.
- Unanticipated result: a negative interaction between race sprint characteristics and deployed sprint competencies as related to mountain performance (b = -6.28, SE = 2.18, p < .01), which the authors interpret as team performance suffering when competencies are misaligned with focal demands.
- Supplementary 2-1-1 moderated mediation: the moderated bootstrapped indirect effects excluded zero within the upper five of 12 mountain races (42%) and the upper five of 11 sprint races (45%).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends human capital resource complementarity theory by making deployment a central mechanism between HCR stocks and performance. It shifts attention from the accumulation of valuable human capital to the question of how organizations compose and recompose teams across changing situations.

It also bridges strategic human capital research and team composition research by treating start-list composition as the concrete vehicle through which HCRs are mobilized. The paper further identifies SCRs, especially shared team task-specific experience, as a boundary condition that helps explain when HCR-situation alignment yields superior performance.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should not assume that accumulating high-quality talent is sufficient for competitive advantage. They need deployment systems that match members' competencies to task demands and that preserve enough shared experience for team members to coordinate effectively under pressure.

For managers, the results imply that staffing decisions should consider both competence-task fit and prior working relationships among deployed members. The paper itself raises HR information systems only as a future-research resource, noting that many such systems maintain archives of members' KSAOs and educational backgrounds that could prove fruitful for research, rather than proposing them as a tool for tracking shared task experience in practice.

**Limitations**
The authors note that professional cycling offers objective measures and dynamic deployment situations, but it may limit generalizability because competencies and situational demands are unusually visible and well defined. Findings may not transfer directly to settings where task demands, individual competencies, or alignment requirements are harder to observe in advance.

The study indexes competencies using prior performance and measures SCRs through shared race experience, so these are proxies rather than direct measures of underlying ability or coordination processes. The models also include relatively few predictors, leaving room for other individual and team factors such as personality, leadership, and training interventions.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine the costs and benefits of HCR deployment over time, including underutilization, development costs, and whether superstar-focused or team-wide competence strategies perform better in different contexts. The authors also suggest studying multiple team memberships as a mechanism for flexible deployment and as a possible source of role stress.

Longer temporal designs could examine how collective turnover, leavers, replacements, and dynamic team membership reconfigure both HCRs and SCRs. Future studies should also investigate patterns and antecedents of shared team task-specific experience, including whether leaders sometimes need to sacrifice short-term performance to develop future deployment options.
