# Marriage of Unequals? Investment Quality Heterogeneity, Market Heat, and the Formation of Status-Asymmetric Ties in the Venture Capital Industry

**Abstract**
In this study, we investigate the emergence of status-asymmetric ties among venture capital firms. In particular, we highlight the venture's performance trajectory as a powerful antecedent of upward-status asymmetries (in which a lower-status actor brings a higher-status alter into a venture) as well as downward-status asymmetries (in which a higher-status actor brings in a lower-status alter). We hypothesize that lower-status firms tend to bring higher-status alters into ventures on a better performance trajectory, whereas higher-status firms tend to bring lower-status alters into poorly performing ventures. Furthermore, we argue that these effects will be moderated by market heat, which affects whether investors would focus on the upside or downside of deals. We test our hypotheses in a longitudinal analysis of venture capital syndication patterns in the United States between 1990 and 2017. We find support for most of our predictions and document that the ability of lower-status lead investors to bring higher-status followers into good ventures is particularly accentuated in hot markets, which can heighten market participants' concerns about missing good deals. We thus highlight the interplay between the internal and the external contexts in shaping the formation of status-asymmetric relationships.

**Research Question**
What drives the formation of upward- versus downward-status-asymmetric ties among venture capital firms? The paper asks how the performance trajectory of the venture and the heat of the external market jointly shape whether lower-status leads attract higher-status followers or higher-status leads bring in lower-status followers.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Baseline hypothesis: Upward-status asymmetric ties are less likely to form than downward-status asymmetric ties.
- H1: Upward-status asymmetric ties become more likely to form for ventures on a better performance trajectory.
- H2: Downward-status asymmetric ties become more likely to form for ventures on a worse performance trajectory.
- H3: A hotter market increases the tendency of upward-status asymmetric ties to form for ventures on a better performance trajectory.
- H4: A hotter market increases the tendency of downward-status asymmetric ties to form for ventures on a worse performance trajectory.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Directional status asymmetry between lead and follower VC, venture valuation trend, market heat, and their interactions.
- DV(s): Formation of a factual lead VC-follower VC syndication tie, compared with matched counterfactual follower alternatives.
- Mediators: No formal mediator is estimated; the theorized attentional and exchange mechanisms are status leakage, governance risk, opportunity quality, and investor focus on upside versus downside.
- Moderators: Market heat moderates the valuation-trend effect on upward-status asymmetry; the predicted analogous moderation for downward-status asymmetry is not supported.

The mechanism is a directed status-exchange process. A high-performing venture gives a lower-status lead a compelling opportunity that can compensate a higher-status follower for status-leakage and governance risks, making upward-status ties more likely. A poorer-performing venture makes higher-status followers less attractive or less attainable, while lower-status followers may accept a weaker opportunity for access to a high-status lead. Hot markets strengthen the upward-status pathway because investors attend more to upside opportunities, but the paper does not find the corresponding market-heat amplification for downward-status ties.

**Data & Measures**
Archival data from the VentureXpert database administered by Thompson Reuters, downloaded for the period between January 1985 and June 2017 (inclusive); deals from 1990 to 2017 were used for the main analyses and the 1985-1989 data were reserved to create the initial five-year rolling window. The authors focused on investments in U.S.-based portfolio companies with available industry classification and geographic information, excluded non-VC forms of financing (mezzanine, leveraged buyouts, and PIPEs), restricted prospective leads and followers to U.S.-based independent VC partnerships, and removed all VCs with no investments in the preceding five years. To qualify as the lead, a VC had to be either the sole investor from the preceding round or the investor with the largest cumulative investments in the portfolio company; where two or more firms tied, one plausible candidate was selected at random. A follower VC was one that had invested for the first time in an already-existing syndicate. This procedure identified 23,843 lead-follower combinations from among 12,878 discrete investment rounds; limiting attention to rounds with a known valuation trend reduced the number of factuals to 4,886 observations across 2,950 discrete investment rounds, of which 3,596 had an unambiguously identified lead and 1,290 had a lead selected at random from several plausible candidates. For each factual observation the authors held the preexisting syndicate, the other preexisting VCs, and the investment round fixed and replaced only the identity of the follower with an alternative VC that had invested in a new venture in the same investment stage, the same industry, and the same U.S. state during the same year, generating a pool of 370,822 counterfactual observations from which up to 10 counterfactuals were randomly selected per factual (the whole counterfactual set was retained where fewer than 10 were available); the final sample included 4,886 factuals and 40,491 counterfactuals, and the model tables report 45,377 observations. Status was measured with Bonacich power centrality computed from the preceding five years of syndication activity, with the scaling constant selected so that the maximum status for a given year equals 1 and the decay constant set at three-quarters of the inverse of the maximum eigenvalue. Status asymmetry was the difference between the status of the lead and that of the follower divided by their sum, split into two spline variables: downward-status asymmetry, equal to status asymmetry when the lead's status was greater than the follower's and zero otherwise, and upward-status asymmetry, equal to the absolute value of status asymmetry when the follower's status was greater than the lead's and zero otherwise. Valuation trend was the logged ratio between the portfolio company's pre-money valuation in the focal round and its post-money valuation after the conclusion of the preceding funding round; VentureXpert reports valuation data sparsely, and such data were available for around just 23% of all the deals in the sample. Market heat was the logged ratio between the number of distinct companies funded within the focal company's industry in the given year and the average number of companies funded within the preceding three years. Because the conditional logit holds constant all group-invariant characteristics, controls were confined to the follower VC and its relations: an assortative matching index (constructed like the status asymmetry index and likewise split into two splines), follower IPO percentage over the previous five years, logged investment count, logged fund count, logged age, industry and state specialization, logged ZIP-code distance to the portfolio company, industry and state overlap with the lead, logged ZIP-code distance between lead and follower, and logged counts of direct and indirect ties between lead and follower. Estimation used a conditional logit model with group fixed effects and robust standard errors clustered around factual-counterfactual groups (Table 3), replicated with fixed-effects linear probability models using the same fixed effects and clustered standard errors (Table 4). Models 1-5 and Model 8 use the full sample of 45,377 observations; the split-sample models use 25,384 observations for the cold-market subsample and 19,993 observations for the hot-market subsample. The design is associational rather than causally identified: the authors state that the study is based fundamentally on econometric modeling of archival data and that experimental designs would be needed to make truly causal claims and definitively isolate the operating mechanisms.

**Key Findings**
- Baseline hypothesis supported: in Model 2 of Table 3, downward-status asymmetry significantly increases the likelihood of matching (b = 0.991, p < .001) while upward-status asymmetry does not (b = 0.032, n.s.), and the difference between the two spline coefficients is highly significant (p < .001). Across the fuller specifications, upward-status asymmetry has either a null or a negative main effect on matching (for example, b = -0.571, p < .01 in Model 3 and b = -0.474, p < .01 in Model 5), whereas downward-status asymmetry has a strong positive effect in all models.
- H1 supported: the interaction between upward-status asymmetry and valuation trend is strongly positive (b = 0.981, p < .001 in Model 3; b = 0.754, p < .001 when both interactions enter together in Model 5), indicating that lower-status VCs are more likely to bring in higher-status followers when the venture is performing well.
- H2 supported: the interaction between downward-status asymmetry and valuation trend is strongly negative (b = -0.430, p < .001 in Model 4; b = -0.282, p < .001 in Model 5), indicating that higher-status VCs become increasingly unlikely to bring in lower-status followers as the portfolio company's performance trajectory strengthens, and more likely to do so as it struggles.
- H3 supported: in the split-sample analysis the upward-status asymmetry x valuation trend interaction is more than three times as large in the hot-market subsample (b = 1.096, p < .001, Model 7) as in the cold-market subsample (b = 0.338, n.s., Model 6), and the three-way upward-status asymmetry x valuation trend x market heat interaction is positive and significant in Model 8 (b = 0.678, p < .01). The authors caution that coefficients from different nonlinear models cannot be compared directly, so the split-sample contrast is read as a pattern suggesting greater sensitivity of upward-status asymmetry to performance in hot markets.
- H4 not supported: the negative downward-status asymmetry x valuation trend interaction is similar in magnitude in the cold-market (b = -0.205, p < .05, Model 6) and hot-market (b = -0.232, n.s., Model 7) subsamples, and the corresponding three-way interaction in Model 8 is insignificant (b = -0.091); the authors state that they find no support for Hypothesis 4, and describe the tendency to bring lower-status collaborators into poorer projects as consistent throughout the business cycle.
- Both two-way interactions between market heat and the status asymmetry splines are insignificant in Model 8 (upward b = -0.170; downward b = -0.135), suggesting that in this setting - contrary to Collet and Philippe (2014) - the incidence of status-asymmetric ties in either direction does not vary across hot and cold markets.
- The fixed-effects linear probability models in Table 4 reproduce the pattern of the conditional logit coefficients, which the authors read as evidence that the results are not sensitive to the chosen functional form.
- Predicted probabilities from Model 5 of Table 4: at zero status asymmetry the likelihood of tie formation is approximately 7%; at one standard deviation from zero of upward-status asymmetry (approximately 0.24) it is approximately 6% for an average portfolio company, falls to 4.5% for a company whose valuation trend is one standard deviation below the mean, and climbs to 7.4% for a company one standard deviation above. At one standard deviation from zero of downward-status asymmetry (approximately 0.33) the likelihood is 12% at the sample mean valuation trend and ranges from 11% to 13% for valuation trends one standard deviation above or below the mean.
- Three-way interaction illustrated in Figure 2 (Model 8, Table 4): in extremely cold markets (5th percentile of the market heat distribution) an upward-status asymmetric tie is relatively unlikely irrespective of company performance, at approximately 5.3% in both cases, whereas in very hot markets (95th percentile) it reaches approximately 9.9% when the performance trend is one standard deviation above the mean but just 3.6% when it is one standard deviation below.
- Robustness: results held across five, 15, and full counterfactual sets, counterfactual matching conditioned on prior ties, controls for nonlead investors, alternative lead definitions (33,504 observations across 3,596 factual cases using only unequivocal leads; 62,244 observations across 6,704 factual cases including every plausible lead), eigenvector centrality and raw or z-standardized status measures, alternative assortative-matching specifications, controls for the follower's raised capital and performance trends, and a modification of the Heckman selection model addressing nondisclosure of valuation data. Substituting valuation level measures (logged post-money valuation or an excess valuation residual) for valuation trend yields strong support for Hypotheses 1 and 2 but no statistically significant results regarding Hypothesis 3, while operationalizing market heat through industry IPO counts still supports Hypothesis 3.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to network dynamics by treating VC syndication as a directed network rather than an undirected alliance network, separating upward- and downward-status-asymmetric tie formation. It shows that the internal performance trajectory of the collaboration target matters alongside lead and follower characteristics and the external market context.

The paper also refines status theory by showing that status homophily is not the only default in interorganizational matching. High-status actors can use their position to bring lower-status partners into less promising ventures, while lower-status actors can sometimes reach higher-status partners by controlling access to high-performing ventures, especially in hot markets.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practitioner-facing implications section. Its findings indicate that the status composition of a syndication tie is not fixed by investor prestige alone: lower-status leads are more likely to bring in higher-status followers when the portfolio company's valuation trajectory is strong, while higher-status leads become increasingly unlikely to bring in lower-status followers as that trajectory strengthens.

The study also reports an asymmetry in how market conditions bear on this: the upward effect is more than three times as large in hot markets as in cold ones, whereas the parallel moderation of the downward effect was not supported.

**Limitations**
The authors emphasize that the study is based on econometric modeling of archival data, so it cannot make definitive causal claims about the operating mechanisms despite controls, fixed effects, and supplemental analyses. They also note that the data reveal accepted syndication ties, not the full set of invitations and rejections, so the first-stage invitation process and second-stage acceptance process cannot be separated.

The paper further acknowledges that focusing on the lead VC may understate the roles of nonlead investors and entrepreneurs in shaping follower selection. Valuation data are also sparse in VentureXpert, which restricts the analyzable sample and leaves possible selection issues that robustness tests can reduce but not fully eliminate.

**Future Research**
Future research could use experiments or venture-game designs to manipulate status asymmetry, venture performance trajectory, and market heat directly, allowing stronger causal tests of the mechanisms. Survey-based or multistage designs could distinguish which potential partners are approached from which invitations are accepted.

The authors also call for more work on directed network dynamics, including reciprocity, role specialization in VC syndication, and the different consequences of inbound versus outbound interorganizational ties. Additional research could examine how nonlead investors and entrepreneurs influence follower selection and how entrepreneurs trade off valuation and investor status across venture life-cycle stages.
