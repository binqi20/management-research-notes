# Lenient Reactions to Misconduct: Examining the Self-Conscious Process of Being Lenient to Others at Work

**Abstract**
How employees address others' misconduct is a common aspect of organizational life. Although some employees adhere to punishment standards when addressing misconduct, not all do. Instead, some are lenient. We propose that being lenient generates a self-conscious affective process that has countervailing effects for grantors. Drawing from the theory of self-conscious emotions and moral psychology, we propose that leniency elicits feelings of pride that enhance engagement and also elicits feelings of guilt that enhance exhaustion. Taken together, we propose that leniency indirectly influences energy states through self-conscious emotions. A multistudy approach was utilized to test these predictions. The results from Study 1 (a multiwave field study of police officers in the United States) and Study 2 (an experiment) largely supported our predictions. In Study 3 (an experiment), we extended our model to consider a theoretically relevant moderator. Grantors' forgiveness was predicted to impact the extent to which feelings of pride and guilt are experienced from leniency. As predicted, our results showed that the impact of leniency on pride was strengthened by forgiveness and the impact of leniency on guilt was weakened by forgiveness.

**Research Question**
How does being lenient toward others' workplace misconduct affect the leniency grantor's self-conscious emotions and subsequent energy states at work? The paper also asks whether forgiveness changes the extent to which leniency produces pride and guilt.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Leniency is positively related to pride.
H2. Leniency is positively related to guilt.
H3. Leniency has a positive indirect effect on engagement through pride.
H4. Leniency has a positive indirect effect on exhaustion through guilt.
H5. Forgiveness moderates the positive relationship between leniency and pride, such that the relationship is stronger when forgiveness is higher than lower.
H6. Forgiveness moderates the positive relationship between leniency and guilt, such that the relationship is weaker when forgiveness is higher than lower.

Hypotheses 1 through 4 are stated before the overview of studies; Hypotheses 5 and 6 are introduced immediately before Study 3, which extended the model to consider forgiveness as a theoretically relevant moderator.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leniency, defined as lessening or removing a negative consequence for misconduct.
- DV(s): Engagement and exhaustion; Study 1 also examines supervisor-rated task performance in supplemental analysis.
- Mediators: Pride and guilt as self-conscious emotions.
- Moderators: Forgiveness in Study 3.

The mechanism is an affective appraisal process. Leniency aligns with an ideal-self aspiration to be benevolent, which can evoke pride and increase engagement. At the same time, leniency can conflict with an ideal-self aspiration to be rule-abiding and a good organizational steward, which can evoke guilt and increase exhaustion. Forgiveness changes this appraisal by strengthening the pride pathway and weakening the guilt pathway.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 is a three-wave field survey of sworn police officers, recruited because the design required employees with the discretion to address misconduct committed by other individuals inside the organization. Data were collected from four different police organizations across the United States, and employees were offered a $5 gift card for each survey they completed. The three surveys were separated by approximately one month: 249 employees completed the Time 1 survey (leniency, demographics, control variables), 199 completed Time 2 (80% response rate from Time 1; self-conscious emotions), and 176 completed Time 3 (88% response rate from Time 2, a 71% response rate from Time 1; engagement and exhaustion). On average employees were 38 years old (SD = 9.05) and had been with their department for 12.69 years (SD = 9.14); 77% were men and 79% were Caucasian. All scale measures were rated using 5-point Likert scales. Leniency was a 3-item measure developed for this study, with participants rating their leniency over the past six months (alpha = .80). Pride used three items from Tracy and Robins's (2007) measure ("accomplished," "successful," "achieving"; alpha = .99) and guilt used Fredrickson, Tugade, Waugh, and Larkin's (2003) 3-item measure ("guilty," "repentant," "blameworthy"; alpha = .89), both rated for feelings about being lenient since completing the Time 1 survey. Engagement used three items covering physical, cognitive, and emotional aspects from Rich, LePine, and Crawford's (2010) measure (alpha = .78) and exhaustion used Maslach and Jackson's (1993) 5-item measure from the Maslach Burnout Inventory-General Survey (alpha = .89), both rated since the Time 2 survey. Age, gender, organizational tenure, race, and agreeableness (Donnellan, Oswald, Baird, and Lucas's 2006 4-item measure, alpha = .83) were examined as controls; only age and agreeableness significantly influenced the criterion variables and were retained. Hypotheses were tested with a fully latent structural equation model in Mplus 7.11, with indirect effects estimated by the product-of-coefficients approach using bias-corrected bootstrapping. Supplemental analyses added subjective ambivalence measured at Time 2 (Tourangeau, Rasinski, Bradburn, and D'Andrade's 1989 2-item measure plus a third added item, "unsettled"; alpha = .92) and supervisor-rated task performance (a 5-item measure from Rodell, 2013, adapted from MacKenzie, Podsakoff, and Fetter, 1991; alpha = .94) collected a month after Time 3, with 53 supervisors reporting on 150 of the 176 employees (84% response rate).

Study 2 is an online experiment run on Amazon's Mechanical Turk. Two hundred participants were recruited; the posting specified that participants be 18 years or older, native English speakers, and willing to complete a series of tasks. After removing participants who failed more than one of two conscientiousness checks, the final sample was 176 participants who were on average 34 years old (SD = 9.60), 77% Caucasian, and 54% men. Participants were randomly assigned to a high or low leniency condition and primed by transcribing a fabricated popular-press passage about leniency and then identifying the three largest words in a word cloud (high leniency: "leniency," "lenient," "lighter"; low leniency: "discipline," "stricter," "enforce"). Lenient behavior was operationalized as the mean of participants' recommendations on whether to reject or accept three different flagged MTurk worker responses, rated on a 5-point scale (1 = definitely reject, 5 = definitely accept; alpha = .85). Pride used Williams and DeSteno's (2009) 9-item measure of pride ("proud," "fulfilled," and "satisfied") on a 5-point scale (alpha = .97), and guilt (alpha = .94), engagement (alpha = .72), and exhaustion (alpha = .95) used the same measures as Study 1, with instructions referencing participants' experiences at that moment. The manipulation check used the 3-item leniency measure developed for Study 1 (alpha = .94). Participants completed both the Williams and DeSteno (2009) and the Tracy and Robins (2007) pride measures in Studies 2 and 3; results were consistent across the two, and the reported analyses use the Williams and DeSteno measure. A separate validation sample of 135 MTurk workers rated the fake worker responses as an example of misconduct on a 7-point scale, with a mean rating of 5.87 across the three erroneous responses. Hypotheses were tested with a factorial MANOVA on pride and guilt; a supplemental analysis modeled the leniency prime as a dichotomous predictor of lenient behavior in Mplus, using scale scores as indicators of the latent constructs and bias-corrected bootstrapping for indirect effects.

Study 3 is an experimental recall study with 200 participants from Amazon's MTurk. The posting specified that participants be at least 18 years old, native English speakers, working full-time, and able to recall incidents of being lenient and not being lenient over the past four months. After filtering participants who failed two conscientiousness checks the final sample was 185; participants who wrote unrelated and irrelevant open-ended responses were also excluded. On average participants were 39 years old (SD = 9.69); 86% were Caucasian and 52% were men. Following priming procedures used by Galinsky, Gruenfeld, and Magee (2003), participants were randomly assigned to recall the most recent incident in which they were particularly lenient (high leniency condition) or not lenient (low leniency condition) to another person at work. Pride (alpha = .96), guilt (alpha = .89), engagement (alpha = .66), and exhaustion (alpha = .96) used the same measures as Study 2 and were rated for participants' experiences at that moment. Forgiveness was assessed with McCullough, Worthington, and Rachal's (1997) 4-item measure on a 5-point scale (alpha = .87), with example items "I forgave them" and "I wished them well." Compassion was included as a control variable, coded 0/1 by two researchers reading the recalled incidents according to whether the narrative included concern or consideration of the wrongdoer's suffering (interrater reliability = 1.0). The manipulation check used the 3-item leniency measure (alpha = .96). The research team coded the recalled misconduct with Robinson and Bennett's (1995) classification of deviance: 83 incidents were minor and 100 were serious, and 57 incidents were interpersonal while 126 were organizationally directed. Hypotheses were tested with a multivariate analysis of covariance controlling for the main effect of forgiveness, followed by structural equation modeling in Mplus with all measured variables modeled with scale scores as indicators, the independent and moderator variables centered, and indirect effects analyzed with the product-of-coefficients approach using bias-corrected bootstrapping.

Studies 2 and 3 randomly assign participants to leniency conditions, and the paper states that these experiments provide evidence of causality. The paper states that Study 1's field design limits causal inferences and cannot fully control for alternative explanations.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (fully latent SEM, n = 176; standardized coefficients in Figure 1). Leniency was significantly related to pride (b = .27, p < .01) but not to guilt (b = .05, n.s.), so Hypothesis 1 was supported and Hypothesis 2 was not. Hypothesis 3 was supported: there was a significant indirect effect of leniency on engagement through pride (.06; 95% CI [.004, .107]). Hypothesis 4 was not supported: the indirect effect of leniency on exhaustion through guilt was not significant (.00; 95% CI [-.008, .015]). In a supplemental analysis, leniency was significantly related to subjective ambivalence (b = .19, p < .01) and the indirect effect of leniency on exhaustion through ambivalence was significant (.07; 95% CI [.012, .137]). In a further supplemental model, engagement was positively related to supervisor-rated task performance (b = .26, p < .05), exhaustion was negatively related to task performance (b = -.18, p < .05), and leniency had a significant positive indirect effect on task performance through pride and engagement (.02; 95% CI [.004, .067]).

Study 2 (n = 176). The manipulation was effective: participants in the high leniency condition reported being more lenient than those in the low leniency condition (F = 10.81, p < .001; M = 2.91 vs. 2.32) and were more lenient in their recommended actions (F = 10.72, p < .001; M = 2.40 vs. 1.89). The hypothesis test, a factorial MANOVA on pride and guilt, indicated nonsignificant main effects for the leniency prime (Wilks's lambda = .98; p = .16); because the MANOVA did not indicate significant main effects, the authors did not conduct follow-up ANOVAs on pride and guilt and did not test indirect effects that used pride and guilt as the direct outcomes of the prime. A supplemental analysis in which lenient behavior mediated the prime did find effects: the leniency prime predicted lenient behavior (b = .26, p < .01); lenient behavior was positively related to pride (b = .17, p < .05) and to guilt (b = .34, p < .01); and the indirect effects from lenient behavior to engagement through pride (b = .11; 95% CI [.008, .234]) and to exhaustion through guilt (b = .18; 95% CI [.078, .315]) were both significant. The paper describes this supplemental analysis as suggestive evidence that acting leniently influences engagement and exhaustion through self-conscious emotions.

Study 3 (n = 185). The manipulation was effective (F = 965.44, p < .001; M = 4.56 vs. 1.65). The MANCOVA indicated overall significant variance in the dependent variables for the leniency manipulation (Wilks's lambda = .96; p < .05), and the univariate main effects revealed that leniency was not significantly related to pride (F = .003, n.s.) but was significantly related to guilt (F = 6.46, p < .05), so Hypothesis 2 was supported and Hypothesis 1 was not. The SEM path coefficients were consistent with the ANOVA results (leniency on pride b = .02, n.s.; leniency on guilt b = .22, p < .01). The indirect effect of leniency on engagement through pride was not significant (b = .00; 95% CI [-.022, .044]) while the indirect effect of leniency on exhaustion through guilt was significant (b = .06; 95% CI [.014, .144]), supporting Hypothesis 4 but not Hypothesis 3. Hypotheses 5 and 6 were supported: the leniency by forgiveness interaction term was significantly related to pride (b = .25, p < .01) and to guilt (b = -.16, p < .01), and simple slopes analyses showed that the effect of leniency on pride was stronger when forgiveness was high (b = .62, SE = .22, p < .01) than low (b = -.50, SE = .28, p < .05), while the effects of leniency on guilt were weaker when forgiveness was high (b = .01, SE = .22, n.s.) than low (b = .76, SE = .29, p < .01). Study 3 also reported that leniency was significantly and positively related to guilt, whereas both forgiveness and compassion were either not significantly related to guilt or were negatively related to guilt.

The support pattern is heterogeneous rather than uniform. Hypotheses 1 and 3 were supported in Study 1 but not in Study 3; Hypotheses 2 and 4 were supported in Study 3 but not in Study 1; and Study 2's hypothesis test found no direct effect of the leniency prime on either emotion, with support appearing only in its supplemental behavioral analysis. The paper itself states that across studies general patterns of results emerged, but that some effects, notably the proposed main effects of leniency on self-conscious emotions, varied across studies.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reframes leniency as a double-edged experience for the person granting it, not simply as a benevolent alternative to punishment. It integrates moral psychology and punishment perspectives by showing that the same lenient act can be appraised as both self-confirming and self-sanctioning.

The paper also extends research on workplace misconduct by shifting attention from victims, wrongdoers, and observers to the grantor who decides how to respond. It highlights self-conscious emotions as a mechanism through which employees evaluate their own workplace behavior against competing ideal-self aspirations.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should recognize that employees who address misconduct with leniency may experience a complex emotional process rather than a simple relief from punishment decisions. Support systems after misconduct should therefore consider the grantor as well as victims and wrongdoers.

The findings also suggest that forgiveness may help grantors experience the positive side of leniency while reducing its guilt-related costs. The paper cautions, however, that efforts to promote forgiveness should not encourage disregard for organizational standards.

**Limitations**
The paper notes that Study 1's time-lagged field design makes it difficult to link emotions to specific leniency events, and the month-long separation may have allowed guilt to dissipate. Study 1 also did not collect detailed information on the particular misconduct incidents, so some incidents may fall near the boundaries of the paper's misconduct definition.

The results are not fully consistent across studies, especially for the main effects of leniency on pride and guilt. Study 3 may also include selection bias because participants had to recall recent lenient and nonlenient incidents, and recall designs may favor self-serving or emotionally accessible events.

**Future Research**
Future research should identify antecedents of leniency, such as holding close work relationships with wrongdoers or a context fraught with unethical conduct. The authors separately call for work on moderators beyond the forgiveness moderator they test, including whether self-interested versus other-interested motives for being lenient differentially influence outcomes, and note that certain personality traits, such as moral identity, may promote varied reactions to leniency. It should also compare leniency with punishment decisions to see whether punishers experience parallel self-conscious emotions.

The paper calls for work on how misconduct characteristics, such as severity, intent, and interpersonal versus organizational direction, shape reactions to leniency. It also suggests examining additional outcomes, including citizenship, voice, support, retaliation, avoidance, coordination, workgroup trust, team performance, and climate of hostility.
