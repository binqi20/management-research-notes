# Employer–Employee Matching and Complementary Assets: The Role of Cross-Organization Collaborations

**Abstract**
Building on human capital theory and social capital theory, we theorize that cross-organization collaborations generate a rich and distinct source of relational capital that enhances employer–employee matches when complementary assets are important in the production process. We test our theory in the context of academic scientists where collaborations within and across organizations are common channels to access complementary assets. We find that cross-organizational collaborations are positively related to an individual’s decision to move to a previous coauthor’s organization. Additionally, moving to an organization where an individual had a direct collaboration is positively related to postmobility performance. This suggests that prior collaboration facilitates better employer–employee matches. We unpack this finding and show that the postmobility performance increase is not only driven by increased productivity with prior coauthors, it is also driven by novel collaborations with new colleagues. Together, our findings suggest that cross-organization collaborations facilitate hiring employees who can integrate well with the complementary assets of the entire unit.

**Research Question**
How do cross-organization collaborations shape employer-employee matching when complementary assets are important to productivity? The paper asks whether prior collaborations increase mobility to collaborators' organizations and whether such moves improve postmobility productivity through better matching with prior and new colleagues.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Individuals with more cross-organization collaborations will be more likely to move to a previous collaborator's organization than individuals with fewer cross-organization collaborations.
- Hypothesis 2. Mobility to a previous collaborator is positively related to a subsequent increase in performance, compared to mobility to an organization with no previous collaborators.
- Hypothesis 3a. Mobility to a previous collaborator is positively related to the number of new collaborators in the new organization, relative to mobility to an organization with no previous collaborators.
- Hypothesis 3b. When moving to a previous collaborator, the number of new collaborators in the new organization is positively related to performance relative to mobility to an organization with no previous collaborators.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cross-organization collaborations; mobility to a previous coauthor's organization; current colleague-new coauthors.
- DV(s): Mobility choice, postmobility productivity, postmobility citation productivity, and current colleague-new coauthor counts.
- Mediators: No formal mediator is estimated; the theorized channels are reduced information asymmetry, lower search costs, better assessment of fit with complementary assets, and brokerage into new colleague relationships.
- Moderators: No formal moderator is central to the tested model.

The mechanism is a matching process in which cross-organization collaboration creates relational capital before hiring occurs. Prior collaboration gives both the employee and the receiving organization more direct information about general human capital and fit with complementary assets, reducing uncertainty in mobility decisions. After mobility, prior collaborators also help the mover integrate with new colleagues, so productivity gains are not limited to continued work with old coauthors.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: CV career histories of U.K. life-science academics combined with Scopus Elsevier publication records. Sample construction followed a cohort approach: seven U.K. research universities with a life science department were selected (Cambridge, Oxford, Edinburgh, University College London, Imperial College London, Glasgow, and Birmingham), and the public records of the 2001 Research Assessment Exercise yielded the surnames and initials of 1,026 biology researchers active in 2001; CVs were then collected manually from university or personal webpages, or by emailing the academics directly, giving 409 retrieved CVs (40%). Researchers with hybrid academia-industry careers and researchers whose careers span multiple countries were excluded, and individuals were observed only while employed in a permanent academic position (lecturer and above), leaving 349 individuals with available information from 1995 to 2009. Publication data were downloaded from Scopus Elsevier by a purpose-written crawler and then hand-checked researcher by researcher, yielding 14,457 peer-reviewed publications from 1995 to 2009; the 8,387 affiliations in the publication sample were manually checked and aggregated into 7,298 unique affiliations, and only papers with fewer than 20 coauthors (99% of all papers authored by the sampled researchers) were used. Dependent variables: Mobility (Hypothesis 1) codes each individual-year as 1 if the individual moves to an institution where no coauthors are employed, 2 if the individual moves to an institution where at least one coauthor is employed, and 0 if the individual did not move, where a coauthor is anyone appearing as a collaborator on a publication with the focal individual within the previous five years; Postmobility Productivity (Hypotheses 2 and 3b) is the cumulative count of journal articles published by each individual up to each point of time, adjusted by academic tenure, within a five-year rolling window; Current Colleague-New Coauthors (Hypothesis 3a) is the number of unique new coauthors who are also colleagues of the focal individual at the current employer in every period; Postmobility Citation Productivity, the citations-adjusted analogue of Postmobility Productivity, is used in supplementary analysis. Independent variables: Cross-Organization Collaborations (Hypothesis 1) is time variant and captures the number of unique coauthors working at organizations external to the focal individual's university of affiliation over the preceding five years; Mobility to Coauthors (Hypotheses 2, 3a, and 3b) is a dichotomous variable equal to 1 if a researcher moves to a university where one of their previous coauthors is affiliated within a five-year rolling window, and 0 otherwise; After Mobility equals 0 for the years previous to the individual's mobility event and 1 for the years after. Controls: Individual Grants (total research grants per researcher per year, hand-matched against the Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council grants database from 1997), Department Total Funds (research funds available on average per departmental employee, from RAE records for 2001 and 2008), Students Enrolled (students enrolled at time t at the university where the researcher works in t+1), Mobility pre-1995 (institution change during the career but before the 1995 sample window), Educational Mobility (number of different countries in which the individual was educated up to the first job placement, coded 0 to 3), Individual Productivity (cumulative scientific publications in the five-year window prior to the focal year), Academic Position (1 for assistant professor, 2 for associate professor, and 3 for full professor), Entrepreneurial Experience (dummy for founding or joining a new venture), shares of the five alternative coauthor categories, and year dummies. Estimation: a multinomial logit model of Mobility with no mobility as the baseline outcome tests Hypothesis 1 (349 individuals; 4,129 observations). The 156 movers are then split into 66 who move to a coauthor's institution and 90 who move to an institution where none of their coauthors are employed, and Postmobility Productivity (Hypotheses 2 and 3b) and Current Colleague-New Coauthors (Hypothesis 3a) are specified as difference-in-differences estimations using ordinary least squares, with individual fixed effects and robust standard errors (156 groups and 1,583 observations for Postmobility Productivity in Table 3; 156 groups and 1,428 observations for Current Colleague-New Coauthors in Table 4; Table 5 estimates the premove and postmove periods separately, with 530 and 1,053 observations for Postmobility Productivity). For the six individuals who moved to a coauthor's institution twice, only the first move is used. The authors acknowledge the potentially endogenous nature of the mobility treatment because of nonrandom job allocation and selection in mobility patterns, and state that the results are subject to limited causal interpretation. Robustness specifications include a probit random-effects model of a dichotomous Mobility to Collaborators variable, a share-based operationalization of Cross-Organization Collaborations (unique external coauthors out of total unique coauthors over the preceding five years, controlling for the total number of unique coauthors), a negative binomial alternative for Current Colleague-New Coauthors, and a triple interaction of Current Colleagues-New Coauthors, Mobility to Collaborators, and After Mobility.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 received moderate support: in Table 2 Panel B Model 2 the relative risk ratio of Cross-Organization Collaborations for moving to a coauthor's institution is positive and significant (relative risk ratio = 1.275; z = 1.94; p < 0.06), and an increase of 10% in the count of cross-organization collaborations out of total unique collaborations increases the likelihood of moving to a different institution where a coauthor is affiliated by 2.8% compared to the baseline outcome of no mobility. Cross-Organization Collaborations does not relate significantly to the likelihood of moving to an institution without a coauthor affiliation. The authors describe this as moderate support for Hypothesis 1.
- Hypothesis 2 supported: in the difference-in-differences OLS of Table 3 the coefficient on Mobility to Coauthors × After Mobility is positive (0.734), so an academic moving to a coauthor's institution reports a significant postmobility increase in yearly cumulated age-adjusted productivity of 0.73 publications (t = 3.32; p < 0.01) — a 35% increase compared to their own premove productivity and a 68% increase compared to the premove productivity of individuals moving to institutions without any coauthors.
- Hypothesis 3a supported: in Table 4 the coefficient on Mobility to Coauthors × After Mobility is a statistically significant positive increase in Current Colleague-New Coauthors of 2.06 (t = 2.42; p < 0.05), reflecting an increase of 1.1 times the count of new coauthors who are also colleagues in the new organization.
- Hypothesis 3b supported: in Table 5 the coefficient on Mobility to Coauthors × Current Colleague-New Coauthors is not significantly different between movers to coauthors and other movers in the premove period, but it is significantly different in the postmove period (coefficient = 0.08; p < 0.001), and the difference between the Model 1 and Model 2 coefficients is statistically significant (chi-square = 18.52; p = 0.000).
- Robustness and additional analyses: substituting Postmobility Citation Productivity replicates the results (Table 3 Model 2 and Table 5 Models 3 and 4), which the authors read as further support for Hypotheses 2 and 3b; a probit random-effects model of the dichotomous Mobility to Collaborators continues to support Hypothesis 1 (marginal effect = 0.004; p < 0.05, where a 10% increase in external collaborations raises the likelihood of moving to an institution where a collaborator is employed by 0.04 percentage points, a 2.5% increase over the 1.6% baseline probability); a triple interaction of Current Colleagues-New Coauthors, Mobility to Collaborators, and After Mobility is positive and significant (coefficient = 0.207; p < 0.001), again supporting Hypothesis 3b; and the largest postmove-to-coauthor productivity gain is associated with collaborating with Never Colleagues-New Coauthors (coefficient = 0.035; p < 0.05), which the authors say they cannot tie to an observed mechanism but read as consistent with cross-organization collaborations facilitating matches to other sources of complementary assets.
- The authors summarize that their empirical results are consistent with their hypothesized relationships.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to employer-employee matching theory by showing how collaboration across organizational boundaries can reveal fit with a receiving unit's complementary assets before a move occurs. It extends strategic human capital theory by treating complementary assets as central to both selection into mobility and postmove adaptation.

The paper also refines social capital theory by distinguishing cross-organization collaboration from general social ties. The authors argue that this hybrid form of relational capital combines features of internal and external relational capital, giving richer information about both employee quality and the employee's likely fit with organizational assets.

**Practical Implication**
For managers, the findings imply that cross-organization collaborations can operate as a hiring pipeline when productivity depends on fit with complementary assets. Such collaborations provide information about whether a potential employee can work productively with the receiving organization's people and resources, not just whether the person has high general ability.

The implication is not one-sided: collaborations may also increase outbound mobility by making employees visible to better-fitting employers. The authors therefore frame collaboration investment as potentially beneficial at the collective level, especially for organizations with idiosyncratic complementary assets that can attract better-fitting hires.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the evidence has limited causal interpretation because mobility events are not randomly assigned and omitted variable bias remains possible despite fixed effects, controls, and CV-based individual information. They also note that the academic life-sciences context has unusual transparency, autonomy, and persistent postmove collaboration opportunities, which may limit generalizability.

Additional limitations concern measurement and mechanism visibility. The study can classify within- and cross-organization coauthorships, but it cannot fully distinguish types of new collaborations, organizational onboarding ability, nonhuman complementary assets, or the dynamic evolution of matching after initial hiring.

**Future Research**
Future research should pursue stronger causal designs and richer data that can rule out alternative explanations for mobility and productivity changes. The authors also call for work that parses new collaborations by topic area, seniority, star status, and specialist versus generalist roles.

Further work could examine organizational heterogeneity in onboarding and integration, distinguish selection from treatment when hiring prior collaborators, and study how complementary assets shape matching after initial entry. The paper also suggests examining long-term postmobility publication trajectories, movement into administrative roles, involuntary turnover, and organization-level effects of investing in cross-organization collaborations.
