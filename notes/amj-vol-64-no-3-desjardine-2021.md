# Why Activist Hedge Funds Target Socially Responsible Firms: The Reaction Costs of Signaling Corporate Social Responsibility

**Abstract**
We develop and test a theory of how unintended audiences create reaction costs for firms that use corporate social responsibility (CSR) as a signal. We introduce and define reaction costs as costs that signal senders incur when unintended audiences react negatively to a true signal that was intended for another audience. We argue that activist hedge funds—an unintended audience—treat CSR as a signal that firms have wasteful intentions and capabilities, which prevent firms from maximizing shareholder value in the short term. On that basis, we hypothesize that activist hedge funds are more likely to target firms with higher levels of CSR, thus imposing reaction costs on these firms. We further argue that this relationship is weaker when firms operate in industries with high levels of CSR, and stronger when firms’ financial communication is vague. Using data on activist hedge fund campaigns in the U.S. between 2000 and 2016, we find supporting results. Our study shows that CSR signals may be costlier than previously assumed and contributes to research on CSR, signaling, and corporate governance.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why activist hedge funds target socially responsible firms and whether CSR can create reaction costs when unintended audiences interpret CSR signals negatively. It tests whether higher CSR increases the likelihood of activist hedge fund targeting, and whether this relationship depends on industry CSR norms and the vagueness of firms' financial communication.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. The more a firm engages in CSR activities, the higher the likelihood that this firm will be targeted by an activist hedge fund.
H2. A high level of CSR in an industry weakens the relationship between a firm's CSR activities and the likelihood that this firm will be targeted by an activist hedge fund.
H3. Vague financial communication strengthens the relationship between a firm's CSR activities and the likelihood that this firm will be targeted by an activist hedge fund.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm CSR score, measured from KLD strengths in environment, community, diversity, employee relations, and human rights.
- DV(s): Hedge fund targeting, measured as whether an activist hedge fund initiated a campaign against the firm in a given year.
- Mediators: Not modeled statistically; the theorized mechanism is activist hedge funds' inference that CSR signals wasteful intentions and capabilities from a short-term shareholder-value perspective.
- Moderators: Industry CSR weakens the CSR-targeting relationship; firm vagueness in earnings-call communication strengthens it.

CSR usually functions as a signal to intended stakeholders that a firm is willing and able to act with a long-term orientation and attend to stakeholder interests. DesJardine, Marti, and Durand argue that activist hedge funds are an unintended audience that can read the same CSR signal as evidence of wasteful intentions and capabilities. This makes socially responsible firms more attractive targets for campaigns that seek short-term shareholder-value reorientation. CSR is less salient when it blends into a high-CSR industry, but more informative when financial communication is vague.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and data sources: a single multi-year U.S. firm panel. The authors collected CSR data on all firms listed in the MSCI KLD index, accounting and financial data from Thomson Reuters Datastream, investor holdings from Thomson Reuters Institutional (13F) Holdings, investor classifications from Brian Bushee's website, executive data from ExecuComp, and transcripts of quarterly earnings conference calls from S&P Global and other sources. Targeted firms were identified from Activist Insight for 2011-2016 and from hand-collected Schedule 13D filings for 2000-2010, following the multistep process of DesJardine and Durand (2020) and Gantchev (2013). The final sample comprised (a) 506 targeted firms and 4,515 corresponding firm-year observations and (b) 1,106 non-targeted (control) firms and 10,447 corresponding firm-year observations; the reported models are estimated on 14,962 firm-year observations.
- DV: target, a dichotomous variable equal to 1 if a firm had been targeted by an activist hedge fund in a given year and 0 otherwise, used as a proxy for reaction costs; only the first instance in which a firm was targeted is included, to control for "wolf pack" activism.
- IV: CSR score, computed by summing a firm's KLD strengths in the environment, community, diversity, employee relations, and human rights subdomains. The corporate governance subdomain was excluded because the study's definition of CSR does not encompass corporate governance and because governance is a distinct reason activist hedge funds target firms; the product quality subdomain was also excluded.
- Moderator (H2): industry CSR, the average CSR score for all firms other than the focal firm in an industry group classified according to two-digit SIC codes.
- Moderator (H3): firm vagueness, the total number of vague words and phrases divided by the total number of words spoken by managers during quarterly earnings conference calls, expressed as a percentage, using Hiller's communication vagueness dictionary of 362 vague words and expressions classified along 10 dimensions of vagueness.
- Controls, all lagged by one year: firm size (log of total assets), ROA, market-to-book ratio, current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities), debt ratio (total debt divided by total assets), female CEO, corporate governance (sum of strengths in the KLD corporate governance domain), CEO duality, transient ownership, and dedicated ownership.
- Estimation: panel logistic regression specified to estimate probabilities rather than log odds, with firm-specific random effects in all specifications, year and industry fixed effects, mean-centered interaction terms, and standard errors clustered by firm; average marginal effects were checked using the margins command in Stata. Firm fixed effects were not used because a conditional logistic regression would force the authors to drop all control firms and would introduce endogenous selection bias.
- Identification: the main analysis is associational, and the authors describe the Model 2 estimate as an association between a firm's CSR and its probability of being targeted. Endogeneity is addressed in robustness analyses with two-stage probit models with continuous endogenous regressors, instrumenting CSR score with fatalities (the number of fatalities caused by natural disasters per capita in a state-year, from SHELDUS); the reported instrument diagnostics are a p value of 0.744 when fatalities is regressed on activist hedge fund targeting and a Cragg-Donald Wald F statistic of 21.667. Further robustness checks use propensity score matched samples (nearest neighbor without replacement, matching each targeted firm to a non-targeted control firm in the same year with the same two-digit SIC code, based on pre-treatment levels at t-1 and trends from t-3 to t-1), alternative CSR measures (a net score subtracting concerns from strengths, individual CSR subdimensions, and an industry-adjusted firm-CSR measure), and a multinomial logistic regression using the geographic distance between the activist hedge fund's and the target firm's headquarters (25-mile cut-off) as an alternative measure of information asymmetry.

**Key Findings**
- Univariate test of Hypothesis 1: the mean CSR score of targeted firms was 1.390 in the year they were targeted, compared with 1.168 for non-targeted firms; the difference of 0.222 is statistically significant (t = 8.566, p < .001).
- H1 supported. In Model 2 of Table 2 the coefficient on CSR score is positive, 0.112 (p < .001), and the coefficient is positive and statistically significant across all values of the variable; the average marginal effect for CSR score ranges from 0.03 to 0.04 (p < .001). For an average firm, whose CSR score takes the mean value of 1.737, the probability of being targeted is 3.04%, rising to 3.88% at one standard deviation above the mean (a 27.6% increase) and 5.11% at two standard deviations, so a firm two standard deviations above the mean is nearly twice as likely to be targeted as the average firm. The effect is nonlinear and becomes stronger at higher levels of CSR.
- H2 supported. In Model 3, industry CSR negatively moderates the relationship between a firm's CSR and its probability of being targeted (b = -0.073, p < .01); economically, the positive effect of CSR on the probability of being targeted weakens by 7.3% when industry CSR increases by one standard deviation.
- H3 supported. In Model 4, the positive effect of CSR on targeting becomes stronger as communication vagueness increases (b = 0.045, p < .01); when firm vagueness increases by one standard deviation, the positive effect of CSR becomes stronger by 4.5%. Model 5, which includes both interactions, further supports the prior results.
- Instrumental-variable replication (Table 3): Model 6 supports Hypothesis 1, with CSR score increasing the likelihood of a firm being targeted (b = 0.039, p < .05), and Model 7 supports Hypotheses 2 and 3, with the main effect weakened in high-CSR industries (b = -0.065, p < .05) and stronger when financial communication is more vague (b = 0.105, p < .05).
- CSR subdimensions: the authors report that the environment (b = 0.306, p < .001), community (b = 0.304, p < .05), employee relations (b = 0.145, p < .05), and human rights (b = 0.117, p < .10) subdimensions all have a positive and statistically significant effect on a firm's probability of being targeted, whereas diversity (b = 0.047, p > .10) has no significant effect.
- Geographic distance (Model 8, Table 4): the coefficient on CSR score is not statistically significant for firms targeted by an activist hedge fund located within 25 miles, but is positive and highly statistically significant for firms targeted by a hedge fund located more than 25 miles away (b = 0.172, p < .001); the authors read this as evidence that geographic distance strengthens the positive effect that CSR has on the probability of being targeted.
- Other robustness results: replicating each logistic regression model on the matched samples produced results similar to those reported in the main analyses; a net CSR score (strengths minus concerns) produced similar but economically stronger results; and using industry-adjusted firm-CSR produced slightly stronger results than Model 3.
- Control-variable patterns (Model 1): smaller, lower-value, and more liquid firms are more likely to be targeted; firms with a female CEO are more likely to be targeted, while firms whose CEO is also board chair are less likely; and greater institutional ownership, in the form of higher levels of transient and dedicated ownership, increases a firm's likelihood of being targeted.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends CSR research by showing that CSR signals need not only generate goodwill; they can also invite costly negative reactions from audiences that interpret stakeholder orientation as poor business character. It contributes to signaling theory by introducing reaction costs as a third signaling cost, distinct from production costs and penalty costs because it arises when unintended audiences react negatively to true signals. It also contributes to corporate governance research by showing that signaling can create, rather than merely reduce, governance conflicts.

**Practical Implication**
The paper warns that socially responsible firms can face activist pressure before activists later suppress CSR activities. For policy-makers, the authors suggest that protecting firms targeted by activist hedge funds could relieve pressures that undermine CSR. For socially responsible firms, the findings highlight the value of attracting socially minded and long-term shareholders who may moderate profit-centered activist interventions.

For sustainability-oriented investors, the paper suggests reassessing whether investments in activist hedge funds align with their values. More broadly, policy-makers and investors need to weigh the potential efficiency gains of activist hedge funds against their negative effects on CSR and the long-term prosperity of targeted firms.

**Limitations**
The authors note that they examine average reaction costs created by hedge fund activism but do not directly measure the reaction costs borne by each firm. They also assume substantial similarity among activist hedge funds, even though hedge funds differ in investment horizons, tactics, and campaign size. Finally, the analysis examines one context: hedge fund targeting based on CSR signals.

**Future Research**
Future research could directly measure the resources firms allocate to responding to activist campaigns and examine whether firms differ in their capacity to absorb reaction costs. It could also study how variation among activist hedge funds moderates the relationship between CSR signaling and targeting. More broadly, future work could examine reaction costs from other unintended audiences, such as social movements, customer watchdogs, or regulators, and explore how firms balance reaction costs against the benefits of true signals.
