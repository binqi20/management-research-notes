# The Use of Agency Workers Hurts Business Performance: An Integrated Indirect Model

**Abstract**
The use of agency temps in the workplace has been the subject of considerable research interest, much of it focused on the effects that using temps have on the job attitudes of regular employees. We advance this stream of research by examining the effects of using agency temps on business performance. We find first that, when otherwise identical workplaces make greater use of temporary help provided by agencies, the identification of the employee or "regular" workforce with their workplace declines because the perceived status of the workplace declines. This, in turn, leads to lower store-level service quality and sales. This effect is independent of the notion suggested in earlier studies that temp workers threaten the job security and opportunities for advancement of regular employees. Finally, we find that these negative effects can be mitigated by workplace-level strategies associated with shared instrumental values and social integration practices. We examine these relationships using a range of employeeand store-level data sources over time from a retail chain.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether greater use of agency temps in a blended workplace hurts store-level business performance by lowering the regular workforce's identification with the workplace. It also asks whether shared instrumental values and social integration practices buffer this indirect negative relationship.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. A higher proportion of agency workers (T1) has a negative effect on the workplace identification of regular workforce (T2) at the store level.
- Hypothesis 2. Sharing an instrumental value of using agency workforce (T2) moderates the negative effect that would otherwise be seen between the proportion of the agency workforce (T1) and the workplace identification of the regular workgroup (T2).
- Hypothesis 3. Social integration practices (T2) moderate the negative effect of being in a workplace with a higher proportion of agency workforce (T1) on the workplace identification of regular workgroup (T2).
- Hypothesis 4. Lower workplace identification among the regular workforce (T2) is negatively related to store-level business performance (T3).
- Hypothesis 5a. The indirect, negative effect of a greater use of agency workforce on store-level business performance (through the identification of the regular workforce with the workplace) is weakened when instrumental values are shared with regular workforce.
- Hypothesis 5b. The indirect, negative effect of a greater use of agency workforce on store-level business performance (through the identification of regular workforce with the workplace) is weakened when social integration practices are implemented.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Proportion of agency workers in the workplace.
- DV(s): Store-level service quality and private-label sales share.
- Mediators: Workplace identification of the regular workforce.
- Moderators: Shared instrumental values and social integration practices.

The proposed mechanism is identity-based. When regular employees work alongside lower-status agency temps, the workplace is perceived as lower status, which weakens regular employees' workplace identification. Lower workplace identification then reduces motivation to act on behalf of the store, harming service quality and private-label sales. Shared instrumental values and social integration practices are theorized to reduce this identity threat by explaining the business value of agency workers and blurring the "we-they" boundary between regular and agency workforces.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: 270 stores of a major retail service chain in Israel, with panel data collected four times a year over a four-year period from five sources — headquarters archives (store descriptives and headcounts), a survey of the agency workforce, a survey of the regular workforce, the company's accounting department, and outside "secret shoppers" contracted to rate stores against an objective standard of service. The unit of analysis is the store and the unit of observation is the store year; the reported fixed-effects models use 1,080 store-year observations, with standard errors clustered at the store level (n = 270). At the time of the study the average store had been in operation 9.80 years (SD = 5.40) with an average of 35.85 (SD = 7.87) total workers and an average of 9.5 (SD = 6.50) agency workers; average tenure was 2.93 years (SD = 0.85) for the regular workforce and 0.4 years (SD = 0.09) for the agency workforce. Survey timing: the agency workforce was surveyed in April–May and the regular workforce in June–July of each of the four years; the first survey went only to the agency workforce and carried the social integration practices items, the second survey went to the regular workforce two months later and carried the workplace identification items plus both buffering strategies (social integration practices and shared instrumental values); store performance results for each of the 270 stores were obtained at headquarters in December, four months after the regular-workforce survey. Age, tenure, and gender were not asked inside the survey and were matched later from human resource records. Independent variable: proportion of the agency workforce in the store, computed from headquarters' monthly headcount reports as the number of agency workers divided by the total workforce (agency plus regular), averaged over the three months of the first quarter of each year. Mediator: workplace identification of the regular workforce, measured with Mael and Ashforth's (1992) six-item scale and averaged over all employees in the store (ICC1 = .15; ICC2 = .67; rWG(J) = .80; Cronbach's alpha = .85). Moderators: shared instrumental value, measured with Pearce and Ensley's (2004) three-item shared-vision measure adjusted to refer to the instrumental value of using the agency workforce, with regular employees rating on a 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) scale the extent to which their store manager shared that value with them (ICC1 = .29; ICC2 = .70; rWG(J) = .86; alpha = .90); social integration practices, measured with Dumas, Phillips, and Rothbard's (2013) three-item scale, with both the regular and agency workforces rating the extent to which social integration activities were initiated in the workplace and ratings averaged over all workplace workers within each store (ICC1 = .19; ICC2 = .70; rWG(J) = .89; alpha = .82). Dependent variables: service quality, evaluated monthly by outside secret shoppers on (a) service provider appearance, (b) willingness to provide prompt service, (c) provision of professional information, (d) degree of kindness, and (e) degree of personal attention, each rated 1 (poor) to 10 (excellent) and averaged into a single monthly store score obtained after the four-month lag from completion of the regular-workforce survey; and private-label sales share, the percentage of total store sales accounted for by private-label products rather than name-brand versions of the same items, taken from the company's accounting data. Competing threat-based mechanisms: two items rated by the regular workforce from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) — "My job in this store is secure" and "I am satisfied with the opportunities for advancement in this store." Controls: store tenure (years in operation), store size (total number of workers), regular workforce tenure, agency workforce tenure, and regular workforce turnover rate. Estimation: a pooled store and year fixed effects regression analysis on the four-year panel, with manager fixed effects added because store managers changed over the four years of data collection and with controls for geographical region fixed effects; Tables 3 and 4 report year, store, manager, and geographical cluster fixed effects in every model. The authors describe fixed effects as holding constant time-invariant, unobserved store characteristics and as making causation clearer than the cross-sectional, correlational designs used in prior work, while stating in their limitations that they cannot rule out all confounding factors entirely and that designs in which the use of agency workers is more clearly exogenous would be useful. Conditional indirect effects (Hypothesis 5) were tested with a bootstrap approach using 10,000 sample draws and simulation-based 95% confidence intervals, evaluated at one standard deviation below and one standard deviation above the mean of each moderator.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported: in the store-level fixed effects model (Table 3, Model 2), the proportion of agency workers has a significant, negative effect on the workplace identification of the regular workforce (estimate = -0.323, p < .025), and Model 2 differs significantly from the controls-only Model 1.
- Hypothesis 2 supported: the interactive effect of the proportion of agency workers and shared instrumental values on the workplace identification of the regular workforce is significant (estimate = -0.351, p ≤ .05; Table 3, Model 3).
- Hypothesis 3 supported: the interactive effect of the proportion of agency workers and social integration practices on the regular workforce's identification is significant (estimate = -0.688, p ≤ .05; Table 3, Model 4).
- Hypothesis 4 supported: workplace identification of the regular workforce has a significant, positive effect on service quality (estimate = 0.240, p ≤ .05; Table 4, Model 6) and on private-label sales share (estimate = 0.003, p ≤ .01; Table 4, Model 8). The authors also report that the relationships between the proportion of agency workers and both performance measures are mediated through workplace identification (Models 6 and 8).
- Hypothesis 5a supported: the conditional indirect effect of the proportion of agency workers on service quality through workplace identification was -0.050 for low (-1 SD) and -0.030 for high (+1 SD) sharing of instrumental values (95% CIs [-0.188, -0.005] and [-0.117, -0.003]); on private-label sales share it was -0.0017 for low and -0.0013 for high sharing (95% CIs [-0.007, -0.0001] and [-0.004, -0.0001]). Because no confidence interval included zero, the conditional indirect effect is significant at both the low and the high level of the moderator.
- Hypothesis 5b supported: the conditional indirect effect on service quality was -0.097 for low and -0.059 for high manifestation of social integration practices (95% CIs [-0.307, -0.0005] and [-0.166, -0.0038]); on private-label sales share it was -0.004 for low and -0.002 for high manifestation (95% CIs [-0.011, -0.0001] and [-0.007, -0.0001]) — again significant at both levels because no confidence interval included zero.
- The competing threat-based explanations were not supported: the authors did not find support for job security or opportunities for advancement as explanatory mechanisms, with every conditional indirect effect on service quality and on private-label sales share insignificant because the 95% CI included zero (for example, job security with shared instrumental values as the moderator: -0.024, 95% CI [-0.140, 0.028] for service quality and -0.0004, 95% CI [-0.005, 0.0006] for private-label sales share).
- Supplementary checks: the agency-temp-to-identification relationship was strongest with no fixed effects (estimate = -0.58, p < .01), dropped with manager fixed effects (estimate = -0.49, p < .05), and was -0.42 (p < .05) with store fixed effects, which the authors read as showing that store fixed effects matter much more; the relationship was similar when identification was measured at the individual employee level; changes in the proportion of agency temps were negatively and significantly related to changes in employee identification (estimate = -0.118, p < .01); spline regressions showed that agency temp levels above the mean explain more of the variation in employee identification (estimate = -0.33, p < .05) than levels below the mean (estimate = -0.11, p < .05); and quantile regressions showed the negative coefficient on agency temp use growing with each quantile (0-20%: -0.08, p < .10; 20-40%: -0.10, p < .05; 40-60%: -0.11, p < .05; 60-80%: -0.14, p < .01; 80-100%: -0.17, p < .001). For the identification-to-performance link the quantile pattern was clear for private-label sales, where coefficients were greater at higher quantiles (0.12 to 0.18), but for service quality the coefficients were not necessarily greater for high quantiles (0.15 to 0.18).
- The relationships between the two buffering measures and employee identification appeared only in the within-store and within-manager analyses, which the authors interpret as showing that shared values and social integration practices are workplace-level practices that are not distributed randomly across stores and managers.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends research on agency temps from individual job attitudes to store-level business outcomes. It shows that the negative association between agency-worker use and performance operates through workplace identification, not only through prior explanations based on job security or advancement threats.

It also connects social identity theory, self-categorization theory, and diversity research to blended employment arrangements. The paper treats employment status differences as a contemporary workplace distinction that can create identity threat, while also showing how workplace-level practices may mitigate that threat.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not assume that agency temps improve performance simply by increasing flexibility or lowering labor costs. The findings suggest that extensive use of agency temps can carry hidden store-level costs through the regular workforce's reduced identification with the workplace.

The paper also implies that managers can partly offset these costs by communicating why agency workers are needed and by using social integration practices. The authors caution, however, that integration practices operate in a legal and policy context where treating agency workers too similarly to regular employees may create employment-law concerns.

**Limitations**
The authors state that they cannot rule out all confounding factors and that designs where agency-worker use is more clearly exogenous would be useful. The study is also limited to one form of nonstandard employment, agency workers, and different nonstandard arrangements may affect regular employees differently.

External validity is bounded by the empirical context: a retail service chain in Israel with low-wage frontline work. The authors also note that fixed-effects analysis limited the study to factors operating within stores and that they could not compare individual performance outcomes with business-level outcomes.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine other occupations, industries, and nonstandard work arrangements, including contractors and outsourced on-site operations. The authors call for a broader taxonomy of nonstandard employment by type, industry, cost effects, and business outcomes.

They also suggest examining whether regular employees accept buffering practices, whether agency temps affect other attitudes and behaviors such as commitment, citizenship, and prosocial behavior, and whether other organization-level outcomes such as innovation and productivity are affected. Further work should also study the process through which employment externalization and alternative work arrangements are introduced into workplaces.
