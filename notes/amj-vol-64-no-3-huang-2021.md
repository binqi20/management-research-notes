# Sizing Up Entrepreneurial Potential: Gender Differences in Communication and Investor Perceptions of Long-Term Growth and Scalability

**Abstract**
Female entrepreneurs have been found to face disadvantages when compared with male entrepreneurs, especially in acquiring the financial resources needed to sustain and grow their ventures. Across three studies, we examine how disparities in funding outcomes may be due to differences in how entrepreneurs communicate their ventures, whereby female entrepreneurs have a tendency to use more concrete language when describing their ventures than their male counterparts. We find that the use of abstract speech affects investors' perceptions of which ventures are oriented toward long-term growth and scalability, which in turn affects the likelihood that a venture will receive investment. We conclude with a discussion of the important role of communication style as a key mediating mechanism in influencing investor cognition.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether gender differences in entrepreneurs' communication style help explain gender disparities in entrepreneurial funding outcomes. Specifically, it examines whether female entrepreneurs use more concrete and less abstract language than male entrepreneurs, whether investors read communicative abstraction as a signal of long-term growth and scalability, and whether that perception affects investment likelihood.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: There is a negative relationship between female entrepreneurs and likelihood of investment, such that female-led ventures are less likely to receive financial capital for their ventures as compared with their male counterparts.
- H2: Female entrepreneurs are more likely to use concrete speech (less likely to use abstract speech) than male entrepreneurs.
- H3: Entrepreneurs communicating with abstract (concrete) speech are more (less) likely to be perceived as having ventures oriented toward long-term growth.
- H4: Growth orientation is positively related to the likelihood of funding, such that entrepreneurs perceived to have a predisposition for growth are more likely to receive funding for their ventures.
- H5: Speech abstraction and perceptions of long-term growth orientation mediate the negative relationship between female entrepreneurs and the likelihood of investment.
- H6: Perceptions of growth-orientation mediate the relationship between speech abstraction and likelihood of investment.
- Founder gender as a moderator is not hypothesized. The authors state that they had no a priori hypotheses for these effects and considered them in an exploratory manner (Study 2 post hoc tests; Study 3 additional exploratory analyses enabled by the orthogonal gender manipulation).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Entrepreneur gender and communicative abstraction versus concreteness in venture descriptions.
- DV(s): Investor perceptions of long-term growth orientation and scalability; investment likelihood, including advancement past VC screening and stated willingness to invest.
- Mediators: Communicative abstraction and perceived long-term growth orientation mediate the gender-investment relationship in Study 2; perceived growth orientation mediates the abstraction-investment relationship in Study 3.
- Moderators: Exploratory analyses test founder gender as a moderator of the abstraction to growth-perception link and the growth-perception to investment-likelihood link.

The mechanism links gendered communication to investor cognition. The authors argue that women entrepreneurs tend to communicate more concretely, while abstract communication is interpreted by investors as a sign that a venture is oriented toward long-term growth and scalability. In the field corpus, female founder gender is associated with lower abstraction, abstraction is associated with stronger growth perceptions, and growth perceptions are associated with greater investment likelihood. In the experiment, manipulating pitch abstraction provides causal evidence that abstract descriptions increase perceived long-term growth orientation and, through that perception, willingness to invest.

**Data & Measures**
Three studies with distinct populations: a field sample of pitch presenters (Study 1), an archival corpus of VC funding applications (Study 2), and a randomized experiment with accredited investors (Study 3).

- Study 1 sample and data: 129 early-stage entrepreneurs (Male = 93, Female = 36) who pitched at a technology entrepreneurship pitch workshop hosted by a nonprofit organization whose membership base included a consortium of accredited private investors and angel investor groups in the Mid-Atlantic region. Participants were full-time founders of technology ventures at a similarly early stage (prototype development or product market testing). Each pitch was limited to five minutes and had to cover six topic areas: opportunity, customer pain point, market and competitors, financial projections, key risks and solutions, and potential exit options. High-resolution videos of the presentations were obtained and transcribed into text. The event deliberately had no winners, so there were no funding outcomes to analyze.
- Study 1 measures: speech abstraction was measured two ways. (a) The Brysbaert Concreteness Index (BCI), built on concreteness norms created for 40,000 commonly used English word lemmas (Brysbaert, Warriner, & Kuperman, 2014) from 4,000 raters on a scale from 1 (abstract or language based) to 5 (concrete or experience based); scores were generated with the Recursive Inspection of Text (RIOT) scan, yielding both a mean concreteness estimate and an estimate of intraindividual variability in linguistic concreteness per pitch. (b) The linguistic category model (LCM), for which a manual coder blind to the study variables identified words in four categories running from least abstract to most abstract — descriptive action verbs, interpretive action verbs, state verbs, and adjectives — combined into a weighted overall abstraction score ranging from 1 to 4 for each participant. Robustness checks constructed additional measures from the pitches: speech tentativeness and certainty (LIWC dictionary), power and affiliation (Harvard General Inquirer), number of sentences, and total word count. Analysis: independent samples t-tests comparing men and women.
- Study 2 sample and data: 1,284 funding applications submitted by startup companies to a prominent VC firm based on the West Coast of the United States, comprising 243 female founders and 1,041 male founders and representing the entire corpus of funding requests received by that firm in 2018. Applications were filed through an online submission platform and included basic startup information plus an executive summary; the authors coded the communicative abstraction of each executive summary submitted at this initial stage.
- Study 2 measures: gender = a measure of founder gender from the application, used as a dummy variable (Female = 1, Male = 0). Level of abstraction = an overall BCI concreteness score computed for each entrepreneur's written proposal following the Study 1 procedure, plus a variability estimate, with the BCI concreteness measure then reverse-scored to provide a measure of abstraction. Long-term growth orientation = a three-item scale adapted from validated scales of entrepreneurial growth and scalability (Cardon, Mitteness, & Sudek, 2017; Walter, Auer, & Ritter, 2006), rated 1 (not at all) to 5 (a lot) on "large growth potential," "long-term revenue potential," and "highly scalable" (Cronbach's alpha = 0.89). Likelihood of investment = a binary measure of whether the startup was one of the approximately 10% (122 of the 1,284 startups) that made it through the initial screening stage. Controls: team size, industry, business target (B2B = 1, B2C = 2), and business offering (service = 1, product = 2).
- Study 2 estimation: binary logistic regression with regular nonstandardized coefficients (b) and standard errors for the dichotomous investment-likelihood models, and ordinary least squares with standardized estimates (b) and t-statistics for the continuous communicative-abstraction and long-term-growth models; tests are one-tailed and all six models in Table 3 use 1,284 observations. Mediation used PROCESS multiple mediation model 6 (Hayes, 2012) with 10,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals, reporting regular nonstandardized estimates. The authors describe the Study 2 design as correlational, which they identify as a key limitation; no causal identification strategy is claimed for this study.
- Study 3 sample and design: 238 active and experienced investors (143 male, 93 female; two investors chose not to indicate their gender), each an accredited investor, with an average of 15.1 years (SD = 11.5) of investment experience and 5.5 previously executed investments (SD = 4.1). Each was an attendee at a large entrepreneurial investment conference held annually in different locations across the United States, recruited through an end-of-workshop survey. The design is a two (concrete versus abstract style of pitch) by two (female versus male entrepreneur) experiment in which investors were randomly assigned to one of four versions of a short startup description; communicative abstraction was experimentally manipulated rather than measured, and founder gender was manipulated via the founder's first name (Jennifer Martin versus Jonathan Martin). Pretesting used nonoverlapping samples: 150 Amazon Mechanical Turk participants (82 male, 68 female) to confirm the venture industry and idea were gender neutral, and 190 further participants to confirm the concrete versus abstract manipulation; a post hoc test used a separate sample of 100 employed Mechanical Turk participants.
- Study 3 measures: perceptions of long-term growth = a two-item scale measured in similar fashion to Study 2 (Cardon et al., 2017; Walter et al., 2006) — "To what extent do you see this as a highly scalable, high-growth company?" and "To what extent do you agree that this company has long-term growth potential?" — rated on five-point Likert scales (alpha = .86). Likelihood of investment = a single five-point item, "This is a company that you would invest in: 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree." Manipulation-check items asked whether the pitch included many details and provided specifics about the venture (alpha = 0.84), and 100% of investors correctly recalled entrepreneur gender. Controls: investor gender, number of years of investment experience, and number of prior investments made. Estimation: ordinary least squares with standardized estimates (b) and t-statistics, one-tailed tests; ANOVA produced no differences in statistical conclusions. Simple slope and mediation analyses report regular unstandardized estimates, with mediation tested via a bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence interval.

**Key Findings**
- Scope: H1 and H5 were tested only in Study 2; H2 in Studies 1 and 2; H3 and H4 in Studies 2 and 3; H6 only in Study 3.
- Study 1 (H2 supported): using the BCI, men (M = 2.47, SD = .08) pitched their ideas less concretely than women (M = 2.51, SD = .10), t(128) = 2.15, p < .05, d = .38; using the LCM, men communicated their entrepreneurial ideas more abstractly (M = 2.66, SD = .17) than women (M = 2.56, SD = .16), t(128) = 3.10, p < .01, d = .60. Women (M = 1.00, SD = .07) also exhibited more intraindividual variability in speech abstraction than men (M = .97, SD = .07), t(128) = 2.02, p < .05. No significant gender differences emerged for tentative language, certain language, power words, affiliation words, word count, or number of sentences.
- Study 2 (H1 supported): the coefficient of female gender on investment likelihood was negative and significant in Model 1 (b = -.63, odds ratio = .53), so the odds of female-led ventures making it through the initial stage of consideration for funding were only 53% of that for male-led ventures.
- Study 2 (H2 supported): female entrepreneurs presented their ventures less abstractly, i.e., more concretely (b = -.19, t = -7.06, p < .001; Model 2). As in Study 1, there were no significant gender differences in tentative language, certain language, power words, affiliation words, word count, or speech length.
- Study 2 (H3 supported): abstract speech positively affected perceptions of growth orientation (b = .48, t = 19.29, p < .001; Model 3).
- Study 2 (H4 supported): perceptions of growth orientation were positively associated with investment likelihood (b = .37, odds ratio = 1.45; Model 4). The authors note that support for H4, and for each of their hypotheses, also held when control variables were omitted.
- Study 2 (H5 supported as partial mediation): the indirect effect of gender on investment likelihood through communicative abstraction and in turn long-term orientation was significant (indirect effect = -.03, SE = .02, CI95 [-.08, -.01]), but a significant direct effect of gender on investment likelihood remained (b = -.51, p = .05), which the authors interpret as partial rather than full mediation.
- Study 2 (supplemental serial mediation, Figure 1): gender exerted an effect on communicative abstraction (b = -.09, t = -7.06, p < .01), communicative abstraction impacted perceptions of long-term growth orientation (b = 2.11, t = 19.50, p < .01), and growth perceptions in turn influenced investment likelihood (b = .17, t = 12.45, p < .05). The indirect path running through communicative abstraction alone did not contain zero (indirect effect = -.12, SE = .03, CI95 [-.26, -.01]), whereas the path running through perceptions of long-term growth only did contain zero (indirect effect = -.01, SE = .01, CI95 [-.03, .01]).
- Study 2 (exploratory moderation, not hypothesized): gender did not significantly moderate the effect of communicative abstraction on perceptions of long-term growth orientation (Model 5). Gender did moderate the effect of growth perceptions on investment likelihood (b = .13, p < .05; Model 6); simple slopes showed perceptions of long-term growth were positively related to investment likelihood for female-led ventures (b = .05, p < .05) and male-led ventures (b = .18, p < .05), with the significant interaction indicating the effect was stronger for male-led ventures.
- Study 3 (H3 supported): experimentally manipulated communicative abstraction was positively related to perceptions of long-term growth (b = .12, t = 1.95, p < .05; Model 2).
- Study 3 (H4 supported): perceptions of long-term growth orientation were positively related to investment likelihood (b = .65, t = 11.76, p < .001; Model 4), which the authors describe as full support for the hypothesis.
- Study 3 (H6 supported): communicative abstraction had a significant indirect effect on investment likelihood through perceptions of long-term growth orientation (b = .29, SE = .12, CI95 [.16, .36]).
- Study 3 (exploratory moderation, not hypothesized): a significant gender-by-abstraction interaction on perceptions of long-term growth emerged (b = .64, p < .01; Model 3); simple slopes showed abstraction was positively related to growth perceptions for female-led ventures (b = .31, t = 3.44, p < .01) while the slope for male-led ventures was insignificant (p = .32). A significant gender-by-growth-perception interaction on investment likelihood also emerged (b = .46, p < .05; Model 5); simple slopes were positive for female-led ventures (b = .52, t = 5.46, p < .01) and male-led ventures (b = .77, t = 13.22, p < .01), with the interaction indicating the effect was stronger for male-led ventures.
- Study 3 (manipulation checks): an ANOVA showed concreteness was significantly affected by treatment condition, F(1,237) = 2.01, p < .05, with pitches in the concreteness condition rated higher (M = 3.42) than pitches in the abstractness condition (M = 3.11; p < .05).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to research on gender and entrepreneurship by identifying communicative style as a behavioral pathway that can partly explain gender disparities in investor funding decisions. It does not reduce the disparity only to investor bias or venture type; instead, it shows how apparently success-oriented investor criteria can still generate gendered outcomes when they reward a communication style more commonly used by men.

It also contributes to entrepreneurial resource acquisition and communication research by showing that the level of linguistic abstraction matters for investment evaluation. The paper extends construal-level arguments into entrepreneurial pitching by linking abstract communication to perceptions of long-term growth, scalability, and investment worthiness.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that entrepreneurs, particularly women entrepreneurs, should attend not only to the factual content of a pitch but also to the level of abstraction used to communicate venture potential. When investors are evaluating long-term growth and scalability, more abstract framing may help signal a broader, future-oriented venture trajectory.

The paper also cautions that investor reliance on communicative abstraction can appear economically reasonable while still producing gendered consequences. Because women entrepreneurs in the studies tended to communicate less abstractly, perceptions of long-term growth and scalability may partly reflect communication style rather than only the venture's underlying potential.

**Limitations**
The authors identify Study 2's correlational design as a key limitation because unobserved differences between ventures or founders could explain associations between abstraction, perceived growth, and funding outcomes. Study 3 addresses part of this problem experimentally, but the overall evidence remains focused on investor perceptions in venture-backed, growth-oriented settings.

The scope is also limited to entrepreneurial investment contexts where venture capital or equity investors seek financial returns from long-term scalable growth. The paper acknowledges that other investors, such as microfinance institutions or social-mission organizations, may value different communication styles and decision criteria.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether the effects of abstract and concrete communication differ for female entrepreneurs, social-mission ventures, or settings where concrete communication may be advantageous. Additional work could also study other resources that early-stage entrepreneurs seek, such as advice, introductions, human capital, and intellectual capital.

The authors also call for research on how entrepreneurs can modify speech abstraction and whether different ways of achieving abstraction have different consequences. Such work could clarify when impression management through abstract communication is beneficial and when concrete communication better supports entrepreneurial outcomes.
