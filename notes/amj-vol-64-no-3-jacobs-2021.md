# Political Dynamics in Organizational Identity Breach and Reconstruction: Findings from the Crisis in UNICEF Germany

**Abstract**
This paper elucidates the political dynamics of organizational identity breach and reconstruction. Drawing on the revealing case of UNICEF Germany, we develop a process model of four phases of identity breach and reconstruction: build-up of identity tensions, revelation of identity breach, identity reconstruction, and enactment of the reconstructed identity. Our analysis explains how each phase was characterized by specific political strategies employed by the managerialist and idealist organizational members, the effects of these strategies on the power balance and consensus or conflict over organizational identity, and how particular triggers explain shifts from one phase to another. By so doing, our analysis advances our understanding of organizational identity breach and reconstruction as political processes, and paves the way for new studies of political identity dynamics in other contexts.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how organizational politics shape processes of organizational identity breach and reconstruction. It also asks what political strategies organizational actors employ when wrestling with consensus and conflict over organizational identity.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / orientations: Organizational identity breach, organizational identity reconstruction, managerialist identity beliefs, and idealist identity beliefs.
- Process / phases: Build-up of identity tensions, revelation of identity breach, identity reconstruction, and enactment of the reconstructed identity.
- Political strategies: Normalizing identity beliefs and masking controversial practices; revealing identity violations and defending prevailing identity beliefs; arguing for alternative identity constructions and forging a compromise; enforcing and maintaining the compromise.
- Political effects: Distorted, reversed, restored, and effectuated power balance, paired respectively with disguised conflict, open conflict, orchestrated consensus, and sustained consensus.
- Triggers: Increasing tensions reaching a breaking point, crisis pressure for reconciliation, and the urge to conclude reconstruction and move on.

The process begins when managerialist actors normalize fundraising- and efficiency-oriented identity beliefs while masking practices that idealist actors may contest. Whistleblowing and media attention then reveal perceived identity violations, allowing idealists to reverse the power balance and force open conflict over what UNICEF Germany stands for. Reconstruction occurs through negotiation between activist-educator and professional-fundraiser identity constructions, producing a compromise that is later enforced publicly and maintained backstage.

**Data & Measures**
The design is a longitudinal, single-case study of UNICEF's German National Committee (UNICEF Germany) for 2007-2017, which the authors present as a revelatory setting for investigating organizational identity breach and reconstruction. The research team consisted of six individuals in total: the first author participated, together with another colleague, as a facilitator in the organization's mission review process; the second author and two research associates acted as nonparticipant observers documenting workshops, strategy retreats, and board or executive meetings, or conducting interviews with informants; the third author had no exposure to the organization and later acted as a critical peer reviewer of the case analysis. Real-time data were collected between May 2008 and May 2014, retrospective data were used to cover the initial phases of the analysis, and follow-up data covered 2015-2017.

Table 1 summarizes the empirical material in four blocks. Observations: 27 formal events in total, listed in Table 1 as mission development workshops (8) 2008-2009, board meetings (6) 2009-2017, strategy retreats (10) 2009-2012 and 2017, and other workshops and meetings (3) 2010-2013; the methods text describes eight mission review workshops observed and documented between January and June 2009, of which two comprised the entire board, the CEO, and the TMT, three were conducted with key constituencies (representatives of the volunteers, HQ staff, and representatives of the association's assembly), and another three were cross-constituency workshops with representatives of those three constituencies. The team also took part in strategy retreats (for instance, three in 2009, 2011, and 2012), numerous conference calls, and informal conversations, and took detailed field notes throughout. Interviews: 44 semistructured interviews conducted in four rounds, lasting 60-90 minutes, recorded and transcribed verbatim, listed in Table 1 as board members, new and old, including chairpersons (11), top managers, old and new, including CEOs (23), and middle managers (10), plus informal conversations with organizational members. The first round in 2008 covered the board and top management, the long-time Chairperson 1, interim CEO 2, his two successors (CEOs 3 and 4), and the five heads of divisions; the second round engaged CEO 4 and the five division heads after board approval of the new mission statement and endorsement of the new strategy in 2010; a third round followed in 2011; and a fourth round was conducted to shed light on the long-term consistency of UNICEF's identity construction among top and middle managers. Documents: agendas, minutes, and documents used in workshops and board and other high-level meetings (20) 2007-2019; internal presentations and reports on strategic planning, marketing, communication, and volunteer involvement (20) 2007-2017; strategic plans and their drafts (10) 2007-2017; and annual reports (10) 2007-2017. Media material: 50 articles selected out of a total of more than 250 concerning UNICEF Germany, tracked across eight German dailies and four weeklies for the period 2007-2014.

Constructs were coded interpretively rather than measured. The analysis was abductive, iterating between the authors' theoretical understanding and the emerging results, and proceeded in four steps: (1) analysis of identity-related themes and patterns, searching for what members considered core to the organization's identity, what they perceived as tensions, violations, or breaches, and how they made sense of reconstruction efforts; (2) analysis of the political dynamics at play, which produced the two distinct value systems labeled "idealist" and "managerialist" - treated as transpersonal perspectives available to any member rather than inherent characteristics of individuals; (3) analysis of the effects of political strategies on power balance and on consensus or conflict about organizational identity; and (4) development of a process model. All data were coded with MaxQDA in an open coding approach using "in vivo" labels wherever possible, yielding approximately 100 emergent codes created first independently and then through constant comparison; first-order concepts were inductively developed and aggregated into more abstract, theory-informed second-order themes and then into four aggregate dimensions, with visual maps and graphical displays used to frame, organize, and reduce the data. The study reports no statistical estimates and makes no causal-identification claim. The authors reflect critically on field access, acknowledging the involvement paradox created by the first and second authors' pro bono and paid process consultation, and describe four mitigations: assigning clear roles and responsibilities at each engagement, extending data gathering beyond the project assignment, attending to social desirability and other biases when interpreting observational and interview material, and using the uninvolved third author to question and challenge the coding, analysis, and conclusions.

**Key Findings**
The core finding is a process model of organizational identity breach and reconstruction consisting of four phases, each characterized by specific political strategies, phase-specific effects on the power balance and on consensus or conflict over identity, and a trigger that shifts the process to the next phase.

Phase 1, build-up of identity tensions. A managerialist coalition led by CEO 1, the head of fundraising, and other top management members used two political strategies: normalizing specific identity beliefs (focusing organizational attention on fundraising, marketing, and brand recognition; the organization quadrupled its income from 1989-2007) and masking controversial practices (compartmentalization around an "inner circle" of fundraisers, concealing arrangements such as commission fees on large donations from volunteers and idealist staff). The political effects were a distorted power balance and a disguised conflict: managerialists gained disproportionate influence over organizational identity, idealists felt sidelined and disenfranchised, and problematizing inherent tensions was described as "taboo" in a "culture of the implicit." The trigger to the next phase was increasing tensions reaching a breaking point.

Phase 2, revelation of identity breach. An idealist whistleblower approached first the board and then a media outlet, alleging that CEO 1 had committed fraud and misappropriated funds in connection with an HQ renovation and external consulting arrangements; national media diffused stories about "the scandal" for the following four months. Idealists employed revealing identity violations, framing the problems as violations of what UNICEF Germany should stand for; managerialists employed defending prevailing identity beliefs, calling the criticized practices "standard practice" and "a necessary form of organizational modernization," and also attacked key idealists by accusing them of "politicking" and "power plays." The political effects were a reversal of the power balance in favour of the idealists and an open, mediatized conflict, culminating in the resignation of CEO 1, Chairperson 2, and the entire board in early 2008, and in withdrawal of approval as a "trusted charity" (regained in November 2010 per Figure 1); the paper also reports a 24% decline in income in 2008 that it describes as likely related to the crisis. Neither the public prosecutor nor the consulting company KPMG, hired to investigate, later found any legal substance for the allegations, though neglect in formalizing business processes and decisions was noted. The trigger to the next phase was the crisis creating pressure for reconciliation.

Phase 3, identity reconstruction. Both sides engaged in arguing for alternative identity constructions - idealists advancing UNICEF as child-rights activist and civil-society educator and as the authority on children's rights, managerialists backing the professional-fundraiser construction grounded in cost ratios and organizational survival - with each side claiming the moral high ground. Because neither side had the ultimate power to force through its exclusive view, both then engaged in forging a compromise on identity, negotiating priorities and using behind-the-scenes tactics; the compromise was crystallized in a new mission statement in which the board took a rather idealist stance on purpose and the key role of volunteers while also including professionalism, decision-making, and innovation (Figure 1 dates board approval of the new mission to June 2009 and implementation to January 2010). The political effects were a restored power balance and an orchestrated consensus, with the key managerialists and idealists forming a new dominant coalition behind the compromise. The trigger to the next phase was the urge to conclude reconstruction and to move on.

Phase 4, enactment of the reconstructed identity. The new dominant coalition enforced the compromise through visible front-of-house activities - a polished version of the mission statement processed by communications and marketing, regular Friday staff gatherings applying the mission, integration of mission items into the annual staff appraisal, and distribution of an enlarged mission-statement artifact to HQ and regional volunteer offices, with a related staff survey reporting 80% approval - and maintained it through backstage deliberations at management retreats and workshops. The political effects were effectuation of the power balance and sustained consensus: the identity debate was largely settled and "hardly anyone talks about 'the crisis' anymore," yet at consecutive annual strategy retreats in 2010-2012 and in 2017 interviews both sides continued to point to underlying, unresolved identity tensions between the commercial logic and the social side of the organization.

The authors characterize the model as cyclical rather than linear, note that the four phases might involve recursive loops and overlap temporally in other contexts and may not be as salient elsewhere as in this revelatory case, and observe - while stating that this is not fully demonstrated empirically in their case - that the enforced consensus established in identity reconstruction may also hold the seeds and risk of developing a one-sided identity.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances organizational identity research by theorizing identity breach and reconstruction as political processes rather than merely cognitive or cultural disruptions. It contributes a four-phase process model linking political strategies to changing power balances and forms of consensus or conflict. It also extends research on hybrid identities by showing how idealist actors can use breach and reconstruction to restore a more favorable balance against managerialist dominance.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practitioner-facing implications section. Its findings show that hidden identity tensions and a distorted power balance built up at UNICEF Germany until they surfaced as a public identity breach, and that consensus over identity was restored through a reconstruction phase in which inclusive mission review, open argument over alternative identity constructions, and compromise formation shifted the power balance.

The study also reports that the reconstructed identity settlement required continued enactment and maintenance, because an enforced consensus can conceal unresolved tensions again.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that the model is based on a revealing but idiosyncratic single case in a German hybrid nonprofit organization. They note that the boundary conditions may differ in organizations with other goal sets, such as profit orientation or public value generation. The analysis also focuses mainly on intraorganizational dynamics, even though media and other external constituencies shaped the breach and reconstruction process.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine organizational identity breach and reconstruction in settings beyond hybrid organizations and beyond the German context. Comparative work could study how external stakeholders perceive and influence breaches, how different hierarchical or professional groups experience breach, and how similar processes unfold across sectors or strategic groups. The authors also call for deeper study of masking, silencing, framing, discursive microprocesses, and the darker side of organizational identity construction.
