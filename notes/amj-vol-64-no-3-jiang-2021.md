# Who Will Board a Sinking Ship? a Firm–Director Interdependence Perspective of Mutual Selection between Declining Firms and Director Candidates

**Abstract**
We develop a firm–director interdependence perspective to theorize director appointments as the outcome of a mutual selection process in which firms and director candidates select each other based on their organizational and personal dependencies. We apply this perspective to address a puzzle regarding director appointment in declining firms: Declining firms are often motivated to bring in director candidates with high resource endowments to help enact turnarounds, but these candidates may not join a declining firm’s board due to the potential uncertainties and reputational loss of doing so. Drawing on the firm–director interdependence perspective, we predict that director candidates’ social capital and human capital have inverted U-shaped effects on their likelihood of joining a declining firm. Moreover, the declining firm’s organizational social capital differently moderates the effects of candidates’ social capital and human capital. Evidence from a sample of public firms under special treatment (*ST) in China supports our theoretical predictions.

**Research Question**
Which director candidates will join declining firms, and why? The paper asks how declining firms' organizational dependence on candidates and candidates' personal dependence on declining firms jointly shape director appointments.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: All else being equal, there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between a director candidate's social capital and their likelihood of joining a declining firm, such that candidates who have moderate levels of social capital are more likely to become new directors in declining firms than are those with high or low levels of social capital.
- H2: All else being equal, there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between a director candidate's human capital and their likelihood of joining a declining firm, such that candidates who have moderate levels of human capital are more likely to become new directors in declining firms than are those with high or low levels of human capital.
- H3: A declining firm's organizational social capital flattens the inverted U-shaped effect of director candidates' social capital on their likelihood of joining the declining firm. For declining firms with higher levels of organizational social capital, the relationship between candidates' social capital and their likelihood of joining increases more slowly to reach the inflection point, and then declines more slowly.
- H4: A declining firm's organizational social capital steepens the inverted U-shaped effect of director candidates' human capital on their likelihood of joining the declining firm. For declining firms with higher levels of organizational social capital, the relationship between candidates' human capital and their likelihood of joining increases more quickly to reach the inflection point, and then declines more quickly.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Director candidates' social capital; director candidates' human capital; declining firms' organizational social capital.
- DV(s): Whether a director candidate joins a declining firm as a new outside director.
- Mediators: No formal mediator is estimated; matched organizational and personal dependencies are the theorized selection mechanism.
- Moderators: Declining firm organizational social capital flattens the inverted U-shaped effect of candidate social capital and steepens the inverted U-shaped effect of candidate human capital.

The mechanism is a mutual-selection process. Declining firms want resource-rich directors for turnaround, but director candidates also assess reputational risk, career development, positional returns, and experience-based returns. Candidates with moderate social or human capital are most likely to match with declining firms because they are attractive enough to be invited but still personally dependent enough to accept; candidates with very high endowments face lower personal dependence and greater downside risk.

**Data & Measures**
- Data: longitudinal data from two leading Chinese stock market databases, WIND Information and CSMAR Solution, on 396 firms that received the *ST designation due to organizational decline between 2004 and 2016. Following prior studies, 154 *ST firms that experienced reverse mergers were excluded, leaving a final sample of 242 *ST firms and 315 individuals who joined those firms as new outside directors during the decline observation window.
- Sampling: a choice-based sampling approach with propensity score matching (psmatch2 in Stata 15). Propensity scores came from a probit model regressing a dummy (1 if the candidate joined a public firm in the given year) on each candidate's age, gender, education, and the number of years since they first appeared in an upper echelon; each candidate who joined an *ST firm was matched with five candidates who had comparable propensity scores but did not join any *ST firm that year. The matched set is 1,890 candidate–*ST-firm dyads (315 in which the candidate joined and 1,575 matched dyads without an appointment), tracked into a final dataset of 6,782 year-candidate–*ST-firm observations.
- DV — joining a declining firm: a dichotomous variable taking a value of 1 if a candidate formally joined as a new director in a particular year and 0 otherwise.
- IV — director candidates' social capital: the candidate's eigenvector centrality within the board-interlock network among all directors in year t – 1, standardized, plus its squared term. Two alternative measures were used for sensitivity analysis: degree centrality in the board-interlock network, and the summed eigenvector centralities in a college alumni network and a province-level hometown network.
- IV — director candidates' human capital: the average of the standardized values of four indicators — accumulative experience as a top executive, accumulative experience as a director, accumulative experience in government, and accumulative experience in professional organizations — with government and professional-organization experience extracted from resumes disclosed in host firms' annual reports. Two alternative measures: the same experiences computed only for the previous 10 years (sum of standardized values), and the sum of two dichotomized measures, business expert and professional expert.
- Moderator — declining firms' organizational social capital: for each *ST firm, the average of its standardized eigenvector centralities in three interfirm networks among public firms — (a) the cross-shareholding network, (b) the common institutional investor network, and (c) the alliance or joint venture network.
- Controls: candidate age, gender, and prior directorate experience in specially treated firms other than the focal firm; firm age, assets (in billion RMB), state-owned enterprise status, ROA, board size, average tenure of sitting directors, board independence, CEO duality, director turnover, CEO turnover, turnover of other executives, strategic change (summed absolute log changes across advertising, R&D, overhead efficiency, capital intensity, plant and equipment newness, and financial leverage), declining firms' upper-echelon human capital, and outgoing directors' social capital and human capital. Models also include industry fixed effects (30 industry dummies based on China's official industry classifications) and fixed effects for years after the *ST filing.
- Estimation: Cox regression (proportional hazard regression), an event-history approach chosen because the finite observation window makes the data right-censored, with robust standard errors clustered on observation year. The proportionality assumption was tested with the estat phtest command in Stata 15 and no significant violation was found in any model; the full model's average VIF was 1.25 (maximum 2.31). The design is associational — propensity score matching serves to construct the choice-based matched sample, and the paper reports hazard/odds ratios without claiming an experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy.
- Sensitivity analyses: an alternative post-*ST-designation observation window (Cox models, Appendix A Table A1); observations in the year of *ST designation alone, estimated with logit regression (Appendix A Table A2); the alternative social and human capital measures; and an alternative sample in which non-appointment candidates are those who newly joined a nondeclining firm but not any declining firm in that year.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. In Model 2 the odds ratio is 1.49 for candidate social capital (z = 5.68, p < .001) and 0.87 for candidate social capital squared (z = -6.26, p < .001), the inverted U-shape predicted.
- Split-sample robustness check for H1 at the inflection point (candidate social capital = 0.71): in the low-end subsample social capital has a positive effect on the likelihood of joining (odds ratio = 1.50, z = 4.42, p < .001); in the high-end subsample the effect is negative (odds ratio = 0.73, z = -2.76, p < .01).
- H2 supported. In Model 2 the odds ratio is 1.35 for candidate human capital (z = 4.70, p < .001) and 0.96 for candidate human capital squared (z = -2.50, p < .05).
- Split-sample robustness check for H2 at the inflection point (candidate human capital = 0.92): the low-end subsample shows a positive effect (odds ratio = 1.69, z = 3.86, p < .001) and the high-end subsample a negative effect (odds ratio = 0.59, z = -1.99, p < .05).
- H3 supported. In Model 3 the interaction between candidate social capital and declining firms' organizational social capital has an odds ratio of 0.60 (z = -3.06, p < .01), and the interaction between candidate social capital squared and organizational social capital has an odds ratio of 1.15 (z = 5.23, p < .001) — the curve increases more slowly toward the inflection point and declines more slowly beyond it, that is, it is flattened.
- H4 supported. In Model 4 the interaction between candidate human capital and declining firms' organizational social capital has an odds ratio of 1.38 (z = 3.93, p < .001), and the interaction between candidate human capital squared and organizational social capital has an odds ratio of 0.96 (z = -3.15, p < .01) — the curve increases more rapidly toward the inflection point and declines more quickly beyond it, that is, it is steepened.
- Both interaction effects also hold in Model 5, the fully specified model; the authors conclude that the results provide strong support for Hypothesis 3 and Hypothesis 4.
- The authors flag one qualification: the effect of candidate human capital squared becomes insignificant in both Models 4 and 5, which they read as indicating that the inverted U-shaped effect of candidate human capital is highly dependent on, or accounted for by, its interaction with declining firms' organizational social capital.
- Declining firms' organizational social capital also has a positive main effect (Model 2 odds ratio = 2.75, z = 3.33, p < .001): increasing it by one standard deviation makes director candidates 1.75 times more likely to join the firm.
- The four sensitivity analyses (alternative observation window, year-of-designation logit models, alternative capital measures, and the alternative candidate sample) confirmed or largely confirmed the main findings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances board research by moving director appointment theory beyond a firm-centric selection model. It develops a firm–director interdependence perspective in which director appointments depend on the match or mismatch between firms' organizational dependence and director candidates' personal dependence.

It also extends resource dependence theory by theorizing dependencies across levels: firms depend on directors' personal resources, while individual directors depend on firms for positional and experience-based returns. This helps explain why firms may not obtain the directors they most desire.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practice-implications section. Its findings bear on the mutual-selection logic it develops: the likelihood that a candidate joins a declining firm's board is an inverted U-shaped function of the candidate's social capital and of the candidate's human capital, so the highest-resource candidates are not the most likely to be appointed.

The study also reports that a declining firm's organizational social capital moderates both curves in opposite ways: it flattens the inverted U-shaped effect of candidate social capital and steepens the inverted U-shaped effect of candidate human capital.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the RDT-based interdependence perspective is not the only possible explanation for mutual selection. Two-sided matching theory, social network theory, status-based matching, and person-organization fit could further explain how firm-director matches form.

The empirical design relies on secondary information from financial reports of public firms. This limits insight into the specific contents of candidates' social and human capital, the candidate pool firms considered, and the firms candidates targeted.

**Future Research**
Future research could compare the RDT-based account with two-sided matching theory, social network theory, and person-organization fit explanations of firm-director mutual selection. It could also examine other candidate-level and firm-level features, such as personal ties with incumbent or outgoing upper echelons and fit between firm culture and candidate values.

The authors also call for primary data collection and qualitative approaches that can more directly capture the interdependence and mutual selection process. Such work could clarify what candidate resources firms consider, what director opportunities candidates target, and how these judgments unfold before appointment.
