# Understanding When and Why Cover-Ups Are Punished Less Severely

**Abstract**
Cover-ups of unethical actions are undesirable and often costly. However, existing theory is unclear on when and why some cover-ups are punished less severely by in-group third parties compared to out-group third parties. Drawing on theories of attribution and social identity, we theorize that the punishment of cover-ups by inand out-group third parties depends on the type of cover-up: specifically, whether individuals conceal their own unethical transgressions (personal cover-ups) or the unethical transgressions of another individual (relational cover-ups). By highlighting this distinction, we hypothesize and find across three studies that in-group third parties punish relational—but not personal—cover-ups less severely than out-group third parties. Moreover, we theorize and find support for the mediating role of perceptions of group loyalty. Our theory and results reveal the ways in which different forms of cover-ups can escape severe punishment, and offer important theoretical contributions for research on unethical behavior, social identity, and loyalty.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when and why in-group third parties punish cover-ups less severely than out-group third parties. It specifically examines whether punishment depends on cover-up type: concealing one's own unethical act versus concealing another group member's unethical act.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. In-group third parties punish transgressors who engage in cover-ups less severely than out-group third parties.
- Hypothesis 2. Cover-up type (personal vs. relational) moderates the relationship between third-party group membership and punishment, such that in-group third parties punish transgressors who engage in relational cover-ups—but not personal cover-ups—less severely than out-group third parties.
- Hypothesis 3. The interaction between third-party group membership and cover-up type on punishment is mediated by perceptions of group loyalty.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Third-party group membership, distinguishing in-group from out-group third parties; cover-up type, distinguishing personal from relational cover-ups.
- DV(s): Punishment severity, measured through punishment recommendations, bonus withdrawal, and coded language severity.
- Mediators: Perceptions of group loyalty.
- Moderators: Cover-up type moderates the relationship between third-party group membership and punishment.

The mechanism is attributional and identity-based. In-group third parties interpret relational cover-ups as evidence that the transgressor risked personal sanction to protect the group, increasing perceived group loyalty and reducing punishment severity. Personal cover-ups do not receive the same credit because they appear self-serving and harmful to the group. Across the studies, in-group leniency emerges for relational cover-ups but not for personal cover-ups.

**Data & Measures**
All three studies randomly assign participants to between-subjects conditions; the paper describes Study 2 as strengthening inferences of causality in a laboratory experiment and Study 3 as an online experiment testing the full theoretical model, and reports in a footnote that two online supplemental studies (S1 and S2) used an experimental-causal-chain design to address the limitations of the measurement-of-mediation design in Study 3. Study 1 recruited 400 full-time working employees from Prolific (45.2% female; mean age 32.95 years, SD = 9.37; 82% White; mean work tenure 12.25 years, SD = 9.27) and combined the critical incident field study technique (Flanagan, 1954) with a 2 (third-party group membership: in-group vs. out-group) x 2 (cover-up type: personal vs. relational) between-subjects experimental design; both manipulations were delivered through the recall instructions, asking participants to recall a transgressor from the same organization (in-group) or a different organization (out-group), and an individual who covered up their own unethical or ethically questionable behavior (personal) or covered up such behavior that another individual committed (relational). Ten participants indicated they could not recall a matching event and five wrote no description of an event, resulting in a final sample of 385. The Study 1 DV is punishment, the three-item measure developed by Bauman et al. (2016) asking how (1) severely, (2) strongly, and (3) harshly the participant would punish the transgressor on a 7-point scale from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very) (α = .90); controls are the transgressor's legitimate power (one item from Wellman et al., 2016), the transgressor's gender, and the severity of the baseline unethical action (one item adapted from Wellman et al., 2016). Analysis is a between-subjects analysis of covariance plus regression (Table 3, n = 385). A preliminary content analysis by two undergraduate research assistants (coding agreement κ = .91) classified the 385 recalled events as lying (120), fabricating or manipulating records (82), failure to disclose (73), destruction of evidence (61), false accusation (18), bribe (16), and other (15). Study 2 is a laboratory experiment with 297 students recruited from the paid subject pool at the University of Pennsylvania in exchange for $10 (mean age 20.94 years, SD = 3.18; 71% female; 35% White, 32% Asian, 17% Black), using a 2 (third-party group membership) x 2 (cover-up type) between-subjects design built on a modified version of the virtual team task developed by Mayer et al. (2013) and framed around a Mid-Atlantic proposal task tied to the Amazon HQ2 search; group membership was manipulated by whether the previewed proposal came from a Penn group (in-group) or a Johns Hopkins group (out-group), and cover-up type was manipulated in the tracked-changes comments, where the focal participant (Participant 3) either concealed their own rule-breaking Google search (personal) or concealed the same behavior committed by Participant 2 (relational). The Study 2 DV is a binary behavioral measure of punishment: whether the participant rescinded the focal transgressor's bonus (coded 1) or not (coded 0); stimuli were pre-tested on TurkPrime (n = 94). Eight participants failed the pre-study attention check, seven failed the group-identification check, and 10 expressed suspicion during debriefing; since one participant failed the first two attention checks, this resulted in a final sample of 273, analyzed with logistic regression (Table 4, n = 273). Study 3 is an online experiment with 602 participants recruited from Mechanical Turk (mean age 36.04 years, SD = 10.21; 68% male; 75.40% White; mean work experience 15.55 years, SD = 10.26), using a 2 (third-party group membership: in-group vs. out-group) x 3 (cover-up type: personal cover-up, relational cover-up, or baseline unethical action) between-subjects design in which participants evaluated chat logs from an ostensible prior anagram study; group membership was manipulated by whether the chat log came from MTurk (in-group) or Clickworker (out-group), and the baseline condition showed the focal participant Googling answers without any subsequent cover-up. Study 3 measures punishment in two ways: punishment intentions, using the same three-item scale as Study 1 (α = .98), and language severity, coded from participants' written one-paragraph messages by two independent coders blind to hypotheses and condition on a 5-point Likert-type scale from 1 (very positive) to 5 (very negative) and averaged across raters (ICC2 = .81, p < .001). The mediator, perceptions of group loyalty, uses three items adapted from Burris (2012) (α = .88), and warmth perceptions, measured with three items from prior research (α = .90), are tested as an alternative mediator. Twelve participants failed the first attention check, 72 incorrectly identified the platform the transgressor was from (41 in the in-group MTurk condition and 31 in the out-group Clickworker condition), and 21 did not write a genuine message; five participants failed more than one attention check, leaving a final sample of 502. Study 3 analyses use between-subjects ANOVA and ordinary least squares regression (Tables 5-7, n = 502) with personal cover-up as the reference category, and moderated-mediation tests following Hayes's (2017) bootstrapping approach with 10,000 resamples. A separate TurkPrime sample (n = 298) was run to test whether third parties perceived differences in the number of unethical actions committed across conditions.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 (main effect of third-party group membership on punishment) received mixed support, in the authors' own summary. Study 1 supported it: a between-subjects analysis of covariance showed a significant effect of group membership on punishment, F(1, 379) = 14.83, p < .001, with in-group third parties punishing cover-ups less severely. Study 2 produced only a marginally significant effect in the predicted direction (M1: B = −0.43, SE = 0.25, p = .080), which the authors report as support only when the significance level was relaxed to p < .10. Study 3 supported it on both dependent variables: punishment intentions, F(1, 498) = 6.18, p = .013, and language severity, F(1, 498) = 9.52, p = .002, with in-group third parties punishing the transgressor less severely in both cases. In the General Discussion the authors state they found significant support for Hypothesis 1 in Studies 1 and 3, but not in Study 2, where it was marginally significant.
- Hypothesis 2 (cover-up type moderates the group-membership effect) was supported in all three studies. Study 1: significant group membership x cover-up type interaction, F(1, 378) = 5.27, p = .02; in-group third parties punished relational cover-ups less severely (M = 4.30, SE = .14) than out-group third parties (M = 5.18, SE = .14), F(1, 378) = 18.93, p < .001, with no significant difference for personal cover-ups (in-group M = 4.97, SE = .14; out-group M = 5.21, SE = .14), F(1, 378) = 1.43, p = .23. Study 2: significant interaction on withholding the bonus (M2: B = −1.37, SE = 0.50, p = .006); in-group third parties were less likely to withhold the bonus for a relational cover-up (34%) than out-group third parties (61%) (B = −1.11, SE = 0.36, p = .002), with no significant difference for personal cover-ups (in-group 61%, out-group 55%; B = 0.25, SE = 0.35, p = .47). Study 3: significant interaction on punishment intentions, F(1, 496) = 4.96, p = .026, with in-group third parties punishing relational cover-ups less severely (M = 4.52, SD = 1.69) than out-group third parties (M = 5.28, SD = 1.54), F(1, 496) = 9.30, p = .002, and no difference for personal cover-ups (in-group M = 5.04, SD = 1.63; out-group M = 5.04, SD = 1.50), F(1, 496) = 0.74, p = .39; and a significant interaction on language severity, F(1, 496) = 8.02, p = .005, with in-group third parties punishing relational cover-ups less severely (M = 2.79, SD = 1.09) than out-group third parties (M = 3.45, SD = 0.91), F(1, 496) = 13.58, p < .001, and no difference for personal cover-ups (in-group M = 3.31, SD = 1.05; out-group M = 3.37, SD = 1.13), F(1, 496) = 0.57, p = .45.
- Hypothesis 3 (moderated mediation through perceptions of group loyalty), tested only in Study 3, was supported for both punishment measures. Group membership x relational cover-up significantly predicted group loyalty (b = 0.72, SE = 0.30, p = .017): in-group third parties viewed relational cover-ups as significantly more loyal to the group than out-group third parties did (b = 0.55, SE = 0.21, 95% CI [0.13, 0.97]), but this was not the case for personal cover-ups (b = −0.17, SE = 0.21, 95% CI [−0.59, 0.24]). Group loyalty had a significant negative effect on punishment intentions (M2c: b = −0.37, SE = 0.05, p < .001) and on language severity (M3c: b = −0.15, SE = 0.031, p < .001). The conditional indirect effect of in-group membership through group loyalty was negative when the transgressor committed a relational cover-up (punishment intentions b = −0.20, SE = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.39, −0.03]; language severity b = −0.08, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.17, −0.01]) but not a personal cover-up (b = 0.06, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.08, 0.22]; b = 0.03, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [−0.04, 0.09]), and both indexes of moderated mediation excluded zero (Δ indirect effect = −.27, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [−0.50, −0.04]; Δ indirect effect = −.11, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.22, −0.02]).
- Alternative mechanisms tested in Study 3: warmth perceptions did not mediate the interactive effects on punishment intentions (Δ indirect effect = −.006, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.14]) or language severity (Δ indirect effect = 0.001, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.03]), and group loyalty remained a significant mediator with warmth entered as a parallel mediator (punishment recommendations Δ indirect effect = −.18, SE = 0.09, 95% CI [−0.38, −0.03]; language severity Δ indirect effect = −.12, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.22, −0.04]). In the separate TurkPrime sample (n = 298), there was no main effect of third-party group membership, F(1, 294) = 0.051, p = .82, no main effect of the relational cover-up variable, F(1, 294) = 0.16, p = .69, and no group membership x relational cover-up interaction, F(1, 292) = 0.13, p = .72, on the perceived number of unethical actions.
- Supplementary comparison against the baseline unethical action condition in Study 3: both personal cover-ups (b = 1.37, SE = 0.17, p < .001) and relational cover-ups (b = 1.25, SE = 0.17, p < .001) were punished more severely than the baseline unethical action when punishment intentions was the dependent variable, and the same pattern held for language severity (personal b = 0.74, SE = 0.11; relational b = 0.54, SE = 0.11; both p < .001).
- Integrating across studies, the authors report that in-group third parties, relative to out-group third parties, punished transgressors who engaged in relational cover-ups less severely, whereas there were no significant differences in punishment between in- and out-group third parties when a personal cover-up occurred.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to behavioral ethics by showing that cover-ups are not a unitary category of unethical behavior: personal and relational cover-ups elicit different punishment responses. It extends social identity research by identifying a boundary condition for in-group favoritism, showing that in-group third parties are more lenient only when the cover-up can be read as loyal to the group. It also advances work on prosocial unethical behavior by shifting attention from first-person motives to third-party punishment judgments.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should not assume that internal decision makers will punish all cover-ups consistently. Internal review boards or managers may punish relational cover-ups less severely when they view the actor as loyal, which can unintentionally signal that covering for peers is more permissible. The authors suggest involving out-group third-party input and balancing loyalty with fairness and care when evaluating cover-ups.

**Limitations**
The authors note that mechanisms beyond group loyalty may also explain punishment differences, including perceived incompetence or other judgments. They also acknowledge that the baseline unethical actions in Studies 2 and 3 were limited in severity compared with the wider range of incidents recalled in Study 1. The paper does not fully address how rank, leadership responsibility, or punishment of the original wrongdoer may alter responses to cover-ups.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine when in-group third parties punish personal cover-ups more severely, including situations where out-group observers are present. It could also compare cover-ups with other intentional unethical acts to clarify the role of intentionality and initial wrongdoing severity. Additional work could investigate high-ranking transgressors, leaders who oversee cover-ups, and whether the original wrongdoer in a relational cover-up is punished differently from the person who conceals the act.
