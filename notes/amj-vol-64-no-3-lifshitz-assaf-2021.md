# Minimal and Adaptive Coordination: How Hackathons’ Projects Accelerate Innovation without Killing it

**Abstract**
The innovation journey of new product development processes often spans weeks or months. Recently, though, hackathons have turned the journey into an ad hoc sprint of only a couple of days using new tools and technologies. Existing research predicts that such conditions will result in a failure to produce new working products, yet hackathons often lead to functioning innovative products. To investigate this puzzle, we closely studied the product development process of 13 comparable projects in assistive technology hackathons. We found that accelerating innovation created temporal ambiguity, as it was unclear how to coordinate the challenging work within such an extremely limited and ad hoc time frame. Multiple projects worked to reduce this ambiguity, importing temporal structures from organizational innovation processes and compressing them to fit the extremely limited and ad hoc time frame. They worked in full coordination to build a new product. They all failed. Only projects that sustained the temporal ambiguity—by working with a minimal basis for coordination and allowing new temporal structures to emerge—were able to produce functioning new products under the intense time pressure. This study contributes to theories on innovation processes, coordination, and temporality.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how accelerated new product development projects can produce functioning innovations under extreme time pressure when existing innovation and time-pressure research would predict failure. It examines how project teams in assistive technology hackathons deal with temporal ambiguity and how different coordination patterns shape whether they produce fully functioning, basic functioning, or nonfunctioning products.

**Mechanism Process**
- Context / triggering condition: Ad hoc 72-hour assistive technology hackathons create high time pressure and temporal ambiguity because only the beginning, end, and challenge are defined.
- Full coordination path: Seven projects import and compress familiar organizational innovation temporal structures, establish a full basis for coordination around methods, materials, and measurements, and continue rich realignment when problems emerge.
- Adaptive coordination path: Three projects let new temporal structures emerge, begin with only a minimal high-level product design, and gradually increase alignment through swift sensing, brief updates, nudges, and adjustment to one another's work.
- Minimal coordination path: Three projects also begin with a minimal basis for coordination but do not increase alignment enough to integrate separate development efforts.
- Outcomes: Full coordination projects fail to produce working products; adaptive coordination projects produce fully functioning products; minimal coordination projects produce only basic functioning products.

The process mechanism is not that less coordination is always better. Rather, under accelerated innovation conditions, early full coordination can lock teams into a detailed solution path and reduce flexibility when ambiguity re-emerges. A minimal basis for coordination preserves flexibility, but only adaptive coordination adds enough later alignment to integrate components into fully functioning products.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive comparative qualitative field study; the design is interpretive and processual, comparing the product development processes of 13 hackathon projects, and no statistical identification strategy is claimed. Comparability was achieved through case selection rather than statistical controls: two assistive technology hackathons held in two similar makerspaces in the United States in late 2015 and early 2016, both organized by the same nonprofit (TOM Global, sponsored by Google's charitable arm and other nonprofits working with individuals with disabilities), thereby controlling extraneous variation among cases. All projects began under similar conditions — the same 72-hour frame with only a beginning, an end, and a challenge defined; the same makerspace materials and machinery (3D printers, laser cutters, a woodshop, mechanical and electrical equipment); participants who had mostly just met and self-selected into projects; and no prescribed process, structure, or roles. The focal study followed a one-year (2014-2015) exploratory study of hackathons in general, in which the authors observed and interviewed at a dozen hackathons across different fields.

- Cases: 13 projects, seven followed at the first hackathon and six at the second; one project with only a single participant was excluded. Table 2 lists each project's challenge description and Table 1 summarizes participant demographics (54 participants; 30% female; 33% with software expertise; 54% with prior 3D-printing experience; 31% with prior "-thon" experience; 91% holding a bachelor's degree).
- Observation data: real-time, hour-by-hour observation notes covering each project for the duration of its hackathon (over 160 pages of field notes), plus more than 390 minutes of video recording of critical interactions and development activities, subsequently summarized or transcribed (over 60 pages in total). Data collection was carried out by multiple investigators — all three authors and two research assistants — with the first coauthor convening the team every four to six hours to exchange impressions and prominent themes, which in turn fed back into the interview protocols.
- Interview data: 90 formal and informal interviews in total, both onsite and post-hackathon, following a semi-structured protocol. All 54 participants were interviewed at least once within the 72 hours, and 14 participants were interviewed twice. In the two days immediately after each hackathon, the authors conducted an additional 22 in-depth interviews lasting between 45 and 90 minutes, in person or by video or phone. Interview transcripts ran to more than 155 pages.
- Documents and artifacts: over 260 photographs of project work artifacts (whiteboard and notebook sketches, prioritized task and product-feature lists, digital CAD files, models, and evolving components); over 25 postings of product documentation on participants' personal blogs or open-source sites; and secondary data including TOM Global's online material, participants' pre-hackathon applications, post-hackathon survey responses, and news coverage.
- Coding and analysis, in four stages. (1) Each project's development process was reconstructed after fieldwork in rich memos and detailed timelines, and a graphic new product development trajectory map was manually developed for each project using visual mapping strategies, recording the activities in detail, the number of hours devoted to each activity, the participants involved, the materials and equipment used, relevant interview quotes and photographs, and product milestones. Thematic analysis of the memos and trajectory maps with open coding produced first-order codes such as experiencing time pressure, experiencing sense of uncertainty, deliberating anticipated design, and allocating tasks clearly; these were then aggregated into groups and moved to more abstract analytical codes, including the clarity and degree of agreement around the product design and the strong ambiguity. (2) A comparative study of the 13 projects' processes compared the trajectories and timelines side by side, analyzing the nature, progression, and durations of activities and the related codes. (3) A search for alternative explanations examined participants' demographics — diversity of age, gender, professional training, previous experience in health care, previous experience in product development, and previous participation in hackathons (Appendix B) — and the complexity of the projects' challenges. (4) A confirmatory analysis of within-category differences validated the categorization and surfaced the split between adaptive and minimal coordination.
- Constructs coded for each project (Table 5): temporal structures (importing and compressing prior structures versus letting a new temporal structure emerge); basis for coordination (full versus minimal); nature of coordinating during development work (full, adaptive, or minimal); and new product development outcome.
- Outcome measure: outcome success was measured by whether or not the project delivered a functional product in 72 hours against the explicit goal of the hackathon (to provide solutions for users with disabilities). The authors further distinguished fully functioning products, defined as combinations of electronic and mechanical components, from basic functioning products, defined as those that were relatively less advanced or durable. Project-level outcomes are reported in Table 3 and Table 5, with detailed product descriptions in Appendix A (Table A1).

**Key Findings**
- Outcome distribution across the 13 projects: at the end of the hackathon process six projects were able to create functioning products while seven were not. Of the six, three created fully functional products (Mobility Now, Remote Control, Grabber) and three created products functional only at a basic level (Oxygen Tubes, Prosthetic Arm, Crutches).
- Two divergent responses to temporal ambiguity: seven projects worked to reduce it by importing temporal structures from prior organizational new product development processes and compressing them to fit the 72-hour frame; the other six sustained the ambiguity and let new temporal structures emerge.
- Full coordination (seven projects) yielded no working product. These projects created a full basis for coordination — a shared, clearly defined anticipated design specifying methods, materials, and measurements — and sustained rich, full feedback with tight and consistent alignment throughout development work. All seven failed to produce a working product within the extremely limited and ad hoc time frame.
- Adaptive coordination (three projects) yielded fully functioning products. These projects began with only a minimal basis for coordination — a preliminary, high-level product design without clarity on methods, materials, or measurements — and then gradually increased alignment through swift sensing and adjusting: quick exchanges, spontaneous updates, nudges, and impromptu check-ins that raised awareness without stopping work to re-plan.
- Minimal coordination (three projects) yielded basic functioning products. These projects also began with a minimal basis for coordination, but as time pressure increased participants felt too pressed to dedicate time to coordination, interaction became sporadic and alignment decreased; participants separated into pairs or worked as individuals, never integrating their separate development efforts, and their products were significantly simpler and more rudimentary than those of the adaptive coordination projects.
- Table 4 summarizes the comparative pattern: full coordination produced no working product, adaptive coordination produced a fully functioning product, and minimal coordination produced basic functioning products.
- Why full coordination backfired: establishing a full basis for coordination built shared understanding but limited flexibility. Participants came to perceive the agreed solution as strong and well-vetted and persevered with it rather than deviating when unexpected development hurdles emerged; their planned sequence of tasks became progressively less viable, significant time went into solving the agreed design's unanticipated problems, and time pressure rose further. The authors read this as the speed trap identified by Perlow, Okhuysen, and Repenning (2002).
- Why a minimal basis for coordination alone was insufficient: it preserved flexibility — for example, the Remote Control project changed its electrical approach quickly after a component burned out, because it was not committed to any specific electric design — but flexibility without increasing alignment left the minimal coordination projects unable to create alignment and integrate their development efforts. Only the combination of a minimal basis for coordination and adaptive coordination during development work enabled a real, fully functioning product.
- Alternative explanations did not account for the divergence. The demographic factors examined — diversity of age, gender, professional training, previous experience in health care, previous experience in product development, and previous participation in hackathons — were distributed rather evenly across projects and none of them systematically explained the projects' divergent nature of process; the projects' challenges were found to be comparably complex.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to innovation process theory by showing that acceleration need not mean compressing existing innovation stages into a shorter time frame. In this setting, projects succeeded when they allowed new temporal structures to emerge and sustained ambiguity long enough to adapt to unexpected development hurdles.

It contributes to coordination theory by identifying adaptive coordination as a distinct mode between full coordination and minimal coordination. Adaptive coordination combines an initially minimal basis for coordination with later swift sensing and adjustment, explaining how ad hoc teams can maintain flexibility without losing integration. It also extends temporality research by showing how actors' enactment of temporal structures affects coordination processes and innovation outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
For organizations using hackathons, innovation sprints, or crisis-driven accelerated development, the study cautions against simply compressing familiar agile routines into a shorter schedule. Teams may need visible workflows, rapid experimentation, and brief adjustment interactions rather than early closure around a fully specified design.

The findings are especially relevant for urgent health care or assistive technology innovation, where speed matters but premature alignment can prevent adaptation. Managers and organizers should create conditions that let participants see one another's work and adjust quickly without forcing extensive meetings or detailed upfront plans.

**Limitations**
The authors explicitly caveat that the study concerns acceleration under changed temporal conditions without clear temporal structures; the findings do not apply when a new innovation process has become routinized with a stable temporal structure. The setting is also bounded to two comparable U.S. assistive technology hackathons in visible makerspaces, where participants could directly observe one another's work.

The model therefore may travel less directly to settings that lack an equivalent visible workflow for sensing and adjustment. Because the study is qualitative and processual, it explains how the observed projects coordinated under temporal ambiguity rather than estimating population-level effects.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine coordination in other ambiguity-heavy contexts, including innovation and entrepreneurship settings beyond hackathons. The paper also calls for research on whether conceptual closure remains necessary in contemporary innovation processes as digital fabrication, open-source tools, and rapid prototyping reduce experimentation costs.

Further research can study other contemporary ways of organizing for innovation, including remote or virtual accelerated work where visible workflow must be produced through digital technologies. The paper also calls for work on new ways of leveraging human creative capabilities to innovate and solve scientific, technological, and social problems in combination with machines.
