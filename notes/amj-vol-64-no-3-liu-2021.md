# Institutional Intermediaries and Firm Choices in Response to Regulations

**Abstract**
This study develops a nuanced framework to unpack the heterogeneous mechanisms of institutional intermediaries’ efforts by aligning their bridging and buffering roles with the information and certification support they offer to clients. I further examine how these mechanisms explain firms’ intermediary choices in response to government regulations. Using a novel dataset of power plants across China, I find that the theorized intermediation mechanisms and the ways in which firms assess them are partially bounded by institutional complexities. Firms’ inexperience with regulations and their adoption of novel technologies are positively related to those firms’ selection of public intermediaries, with the former relationship strengthened when regulatory changes suggest a continuous need for policy updating. Meanwhile, firms’ noncompliance records and a lack of institutional linkage to the government are positively related to their reliance on private intermediaries’ buffering competency, with the latter relationship evident only in settings with an interventionist government. These findings contribute to the literature on the complex and dynamic value of intermediaries and firms’ intermediary choices at the intersection of management and government.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how firms choose between public and private institutional intermediaries when responding to government regulations. It examines how firms match organizational needs to intermediaries' bridging or buffering roles and to the information or certification support those intermediaries provide, and how regulatory and political conditions alter those choices.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Firms' inexperience with regulations is positively related to their selection of public intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 2. Records of noncompliance are positively related to firms' selection of private intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 3. Firms' adoption of novel technology that is consistent with social goals is positively related to their choice of public intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 4. Firms' inadequate institutional linkage to the government is positively related to their selection of private intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 5. Regulatory changes strengthen the positive association between firms' inexperience with regulations and their selection of public intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 6. Regulatory changes strengthen the positive association between firms' noncompliance records and their selection of private intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 7. Political commitment strengthens the positive association between firms' adoption of novel technologies and their selection of public intermediaries.
- Hypothesis 8. Institutional development negatively moderates the positive association between firms' inadequate institutional linkage to the government and their selection of private intermediaries.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm inexperience with the Cleaner Production program, noncompliance record, adoption of novel technology, and institutional linkage to the government.
- DV(s): Selection of a public intermediary rather than a private intermediary for Cleaner Production program auditing.
- Mediators: Not directly modeled in the quantitative analysis.
- Moderators: Regulatory changes, political commitment to environmental protection, and marketization as an indicator of government intervention versus institutional development.

The mechanism is an alignment process between firm needs and intermediary strengths. Public intermediaries are theorized to be more valuable when firms need bridging support: learning policy requirements or lending credibility to novel prosocial technologies. Private intermediaries are theorized to be more valuable when firms need buffering support: selective information disclosure or enough legitimacy to avoid heightened regulatory scrutiny. The results support public-intermediary selection for inexperienced firms and firms with novel technologies, private-intermediary selection for firms with noncompliance records, a stronger inexperience-public intermediary association during regulatory change, and private-intermediary selection by less-connected firms mainly where government intervention is stronger.

**Data & Measures**
Single-setting observational study of firms' intermediary choice. The dataset includes all 530 power plants that joined China's Cleaner Production (CP) program in 2004-2012; these plants were owned by 147 companies, including the 11 major electricity corporations and companies in energy-intensive industries such as steel and coal mining, and the compiled dataset covers 20 provinces, two autonomous regions, and four municipalities at the same administrative level. Of the 530 plants, 196 selected public intermediaries and 334 chose private ones. Data on firms' intermediary choices and noncompliance records were collected from the databases of the Ministry of Environmental Protection, local Environmental Protection Bureaus (EPBs), and a leading environmental NGO (the Beijing-based Institute of Public & Environmental Affairs); contextual factors came from editions of the Statistical Yearbook, the Environmental Yearbooks, and the Electric Power Yearbooks. The China Stock Market and Accounting Research database was searched to obtain information on public companies for an alternative, personal measure of political linkage (whether senior management had held government positions or served as delegates), which the author included as a robustness test rather than as the main linkage variable, relying on Internet searches for nonlisted firms and noting that data for this alternative measure are less precise. The quantitative analysis was informed by government policy archives and interviews with eight participating firms from various industries, eight intermediary entities, and five government officials at the central and local levels; interviews lasted between 30 and 70 minutes and were conducted between December 2016 and April 2017, with firms and intermediaries selected from a single city. Measures. Dependent variable: intermediary choice, a dummy coded 1 if a power plant hired a public intermediary (e.g., local EPB-affiliated service center, government-sponsored environmental association) and 0 if it hired a private intermediary (e.g., university lab, industrial association, or private consulting firm); this is a one-time measure for each plant. Independent variables: first certification, capturing inadequate experience with the CP program, coded 1 if the plant was the first to become certified within its company and 0 otherwise (99 firms in the dataset had more than one plant join during the study period, covering 319 sampled plants); noncompliance record, the number of environmental violations detected through online monitoring and regular on-site inspections in the three years before joining the CP program; novel technology, coded 1 for plants using relatively clean fuel sources such as natural gas, biomass, and biogas and 0 for plants using established technologies (coal-fired plants burning "brown" coal); and institutional linkage to the government, a continuous variable based on the proportion of majority shareholders that are state-owned enterprises or directly owned by the government. Institutional moderators: regulatory change, coded 1 if a power plant filed a CP application within the three years from early 2005 to 2007 when the State Environmental Protection Administration ran three unprecedented campaigns targeting major polluting industries, and 0 otherwise; political commitment to environmental protection, coded 1 if the plant was located in a National Environmental Model City (NEMC) or an NEMC-to-be within two years of joining the CP program, and 0 otherwise; and marketization, measured with the first subindex (government and market relations) of the National Economic Research Institute index, where a higher index means a more noninterventionist government. Controls: publicly listed status, firm age, firm size (electricity generation in the year before the CP audit, in 100 million kWh), peer influence (ratio of private to public intermediaries picked by prior power plants in the local city), private intermediary supply (proportion of private environmental impact assessment auditing entities), and intermediary's experience (coded 1 if the intermediary had worked for other local power plants before). Estimation: logit models of the likelihood that a firm would select a public rather than a private intermediary, run as robust regressions with standard errors clustered by company (n = 530); to avoid multicollinearity, time and area fixed effects were not included in the main analysis. The design is associational rather than causally identified - the paper states that Hypotheses 1-4 present mostly associations between firm features and selection decisions, and argues that the moderating effects indicate greater directionality in these correlational relationships. Robustness work includes a two-stage model using the number of local power plants enrolled before a firm's audit as an instrument (first-stage F-statistic 40.21, above the threshold of 10), probit-OLS and OLS-OLS specifications, coarsened exact matching with two matched datasets - one of 406 plants (295 first certified and 111 additionally certified) and one of 365 plants (154 non-SOEs and 211 SOEs), yielding 12% and 11% imbalance reductions respectively - an alternative personal political linkage measure, an alternative World Bank marketization measure, restricting private intermediaries to private consulting firms only (n = 271), lagging noncompliance, political commitment, and marketization by one, two, and three years, and a bivariate probit model addressing reverse causality.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported: in Model 1 of Table 3, firms' inexperience with regulations (first certified plant) was positively related to their selection of a public intermediary (coefficient = 0.62, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 2 supported: the negative coefficient indicated that firms with noncompliance records tended to favor private intermediaries (coefficient = -0.44, p < .001).
- Hypothesis 3 supported: plants with novel technologies, that is, those using relatively clean fuel sources, tended to prefer public over private intermediaries (coefficient = 0.61, p < .05).
- Hypothesis 4 not supported: the coefficient of institutional linkage was marginally negative (coefficient = -0.46, p < .10) but insignificant after controlling for political commitment in Model 2; the paper states that Hypothesis 4 on less-connected firms' choice of private intermediaries was not supported.
- Hypothesis 5 supported: in the full model (Model 7), the interaction of first certification and regulatory changes was positive and significant, indicating that regulatory changes strengthen the positive association between firm inexperience and the selection of public intermediaries (coefficient = 1.29, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 6 not supported: the noncompliance by regulatory changes interaction was nonsignificant.
- Hypothesis 7 not supported: the novel technology by political commitment interaction was nonsignificant.
- Hypothesis 8 supported: the negative moderating effect of marketization was significant (coefficient = -0.75, p < .05). Simulation-based interaction plots (Figure 2, based on Model 6) show that a positive relationship between inadequate linkage and private intermediary selection was mainly observed where the government was highly interventionist: in areas with very low marketization, fewer firm linkages resulted in greater choice of private intermediaries, and this difference was significant; as marketization increased the gap narrowed and became insignificant; and in areas with very high marketization the probability of selecting a private intermediary was significantly lower for less-connected firms.
- Among the controls, firms were less inclined to select a public intermediary if local competitors preferred private intermediaries, and the choice also reflected the supply of intermediary types in the market.
- The author concludes that although some of the hypotheses were not supported, the framework still provides a valid way to systematize how these processes are explored.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional intermediary research by integrating two dimensions that are often treated separately: support content, namely information versus certification, and intermediary role, namely bridging versus buffering. This 2 by 2 framework clarifies why intermediaries do not simply improve connectivity but can also preserve distance between firms and regulators.

It also contributes a dynamic account of firm-intermediary-regulator triads. The findings show that intermediary value depends not only on intermediary type but also on firm needs and institutional contingencies such as regulatory change and government intervention.

**Practical Implication**
For managers, the paper argues that choosing a suitable intermediary partner is no less important than hiring a compliance manager, since this decision allows the firm greater discretion and flexibility to mediate the meaning and effect of regulations. It adds that partner selection should be understood as a developmental process in which external institutional changes matter alongside internal organizational demands and experiences.

For policy-makers, the study implies that regulation is shaped by more than dyadic firm-regulator interaction. Intermediaries can translate, certify, filter, or buffer regulatory meanings and effects, so policy design and implementation need to account for the incentives and roles of third parties.

**Limitations**
The paper states that its empirical setting concerns situations in which firms must, or typically do, select an intermediary to obtain sociopolitical regulatory legitimacy. Generalizability is therefore bounded, and the author calls for evidence from other industries, societal contexts, and geographic settings.

The findings are also mixed across contingencies: regulatory changes did not strengthen the noncompliance-private intermediary relationship, and political commitment did not strengthen the novel technology-public intermediary relationship. Although the paper runs robustness and reverse-causality checks, the core evidence remains observational and centered on intermediary choice rather than downstream performance.

**Future Research**
Future research could test the framework in similar regulatory contexts such as food safety testing and financial accounting, and in more complex make-or-buy or keep-and-switch decisions where not using an intermediary is also possible. Additional work could examine policy-change sequences and whether responses to earlier reforms create residual structures that affect later responses.

The paper also calls for research on intermediaries' own private interests and evolution over time. Case studies or event-based designs could trace whether intermediaries become more firm allied, regulator allied, or neutral as political turbulence, public sector privatization, or anticorruption campaigns reshape the institutional environment.
