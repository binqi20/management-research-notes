# Performance Benefits of Reciprocal Vicarious Learning in Teams

**Abstract**
Team members' vicarious learning from other members' knowledge and experience is a critical component of learning and performance in interdependent team work contexts. Yet, our understanding of vicarious learning among individuals in teams is still quite limited, as this learning is often oversimplified (as one-way knowledge-sharing) or aggregated (as a collective, team-level property), resulting in incomplete and inconsistent findings. In this paper, I extend these views by exploring the underlying distribution of dyadic vicarious learning relationships in teams, specifically using a network approach to examine the consequences of reciprocity in team members' vicarious learning with one another (i.e., where both individuals in a given dyad learn vicariously from each other's knowledge and experience). Using a novel method for calculating weighted reciprocity in networks in a study of MBA consulting project teams, I demonstrate that greater team vicarious learning reciprocity is associated with greater team performance, and also moderates the performance consequences of teams' external learning efforts, offering a potential reconciliation of conflicting results in prior research. In doing so, this paper advances research on vicarious learning in teams, while also providing conceptual and empirical tools for studying learning and other interpersonal workplace interactions from a network perspective.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how reciprocity in dyadic vicarious learning relationships among team members affects team performance. It also asks whether team vicarious learning reciprocity changes whether external learning helps or harms team performance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Greater reciprocity of vicarious learning among team members is positively associated with team performance.
- H2: Teams' vicarious learning reciprocity moderates the relationship between their external learning and team performance. Specifically, when vicarious learning reciprocity is higher (lower), greater external learning will be more positively (negatively) associated with team performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team vicarious learning reciprocity; team external learning.
- DV(s): Sponsor-rated team performance, combining team output and team quality items.
- Mediators: Not modeled statistically; the theorized process is richer mutual knowledge exchange, stronger shared mental models, cross-understanding, and more efficient integration of internal and external learning.
- Moderators: Team vicarious learning reciprocity moderates the relationship between team external learning and team performance.

Myers argues that reciprocal vicarious learning gives team members a more robust map of one another's expertise and perspectives than one-way knowledge sharing. In reciprocal learning dyads, members can ask questions, compare experiences, and refine shared lessons, strengthening shared mental models and cross-understanding. These relational and cognitive resources are expected to improve performance directly and to help teams absorb external learning without overtaxing members' time and attention. Empirically, reciprocity positively predicts team performance, and external learning is more beneficial in high-reciprocity teams but more harmful in low-reciprocity teams.

**Data & Measures**
Single field study of MBA consulting project teams: MBA students from a large university in the midwestern United States worked full-time in 4-6-person teams for seven weeks on projects for client organizations around the world. Students completed surveys before the project (Time 0), about halfway through (Time 1), and near the end (Time 2); client organization sponsors completed a separate survey at the end of the project. A total of 454 first-year MBA students in 89 teams participated; one team that did not provide complete vicarious learning responses from all members was excluded, yielding an initial sample of 88 teams and 450 individuals and 1,926 unique dyadic vicarious learning assessments. Sponsor performance ratings were available for only 62 teams (a 70% sponsor response rate), so the final analysis sample is 62 teams.
- IV: team vicarious learning reciprocity, from a whole-network, within-team survey at Time 2 in which each member rated each other member on two items ("[This person] often shares his/her prior experiences, expertise, or knowledge with me to help my learning"; "I am able to draw meaningful lessons from the experiences and information [this person] shares with me"), alpha = .89, rescaled from 1-5 to 0-4 and averaged into a weighted directed tie. Following Squartini et al. (2013), each dyad's tie weight was decomposed into fully reciprocated and fully unreciprocated components, and team reciprocity was computed as the sum of reciprocated tie weight divided by total realized network tie weight (a 0-1 measure). Values in the final sample ranged .65-.98, mean .85 (SD = .08).
- IV / moderator: team external learning, rated by all team members at Time 2 on how much the team learned from five sources - faculty, industry experts, other teams, second-year MBA students, and personal network contacts - alpha = .66, aggregated to team level (median rwg(5) = .90; ICC(1) = .23, ICC(2) = .60). Values ranged 2.20-4.15, mean 3.04 (SD = .42).
- DV: team performance, rated by the company project sponsor on five team-outcome items and five team-quality items developed by the university MBA program office, averaged into a single composite; exploratory factor analysis showed all 10 items loading on one factor, alpha = .94. Mean 4.33 (SD = .66), range 1.60-5.00. Eight teams received ratings from multiple sponsors, and item scores were averaged across available ratings before constructing the measure.
- Controls: team vicarious learning density, team vicarious learning total degree centralization, team learning norms (Time 1, alpha = .83), feedback-seeking behavior (Time 1, alpha = .87), team size, average prior familiarity among members (Time 1), average team member age, and proportion of female team members.
- Estimation: tobit regression using maximum likelihood estimation with robust standard errors in Mplus Version 7, chosen because the sponsor-rated performance scores appeared right-censored at the scale maximum. Reported coefficients are unstandardized. The design is an associational survey study; the paper claims no causal identification strategy. Supplemental robustness models re-estimated the hypothesized effects with all control variables excluded.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: team vicarious learning reciprocity was a significant, positive predictor of team performance (b = 4.60, p = .004; Table 2, Model 2).
- Team external learning entered on its own was a nonsignificant predictor of team performance (b = -.01, p = .96; Model 3).
- H2 supported: the interaction of team vicarious learning reciprocity and team external learning significantly predicted team performance (b = 6.02, p < .001; Model 4).
- Simple slopes of external learning on team performance ran from negative to positive across levels of reciprocity: significant and negative at 1.5 SD below the mean (73.41% of tie weight reciprocated; b = -.62, p = .01), marginally significant and negative at 1 SD below the mean (77.24%; b = -.39, p = .06), nonsignificant at the mean (84.90%; b = .07, p = .74), marginally significant and positive at 1 SD above the mean (92.56%; b = .53, p = .06), and significant and positive at 1.5 SD above the mean (96.39%; b = .76, p = .03).
- Robustness models excluding all control variables supported both hypotheses: reciprocity remained a significant predictor of team performance (b = 3.75, p = .003; Model 5) and the reciprocity-by-external-learning interaction remained significant (b = 6.67, p = .001; Model 6).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances team learning research by shifting attention from aggregate team-level learning volume to the dyadic network distribution of vicarious learning ties. It challenges the common one-way sharer-recipient assumption in knowledge-sharing and advice-seeking research by theorizing learning as potentially bidirectional within dyads. It also contributes a weighted-network approach for measuring reciprocity in workplace relationships, preserving tie strength rather than reducing reciprocity to binary mutual ties.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not treat learning in teams as only a matter of increasing knowledge sharing or external information search. The paper suggests that managers might redirect efforts for promoting interpersonal learning relationships, reframing mentoring programs for example to emphasize bidirectional learning between mentors and proteges, and that organizational teams would likely benefit from investments in greater opportunities for vicarious learning where reciprocal learning interactions are more likely, such as face-to-face meetings, particularly when teams need to engage in external learning for a project's success.

**Limitations**
The study uses MBA student consulting teams, which offered strong response rates and realistic client work but may have inflated performance ratings and unusually high vicarious learning reciprocity because the project was embedded in an educational program. Team members also had little prior experience working together and no formal assigned hierarchy, limiting direct generalization to long-tenure or hierarchical organizational teams. The study focuses on dyadic reciprocity and does not examine generalized reciprocity across larger networks.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine reciprocity in other team environments where performance, reciprocity, density, and centralization vary more widely. It should study how prior shared experience, team tenure, formal power differences, and hierarchy shape reciprocal vicarious learning. Additional work should directly measure mediating processes, examine antecedents of reciprocity, and explore generalized forms of reciprocity beyond immediate dyadic reciprocation.
