# Social Network and Temporal Myopia

**Abstract**
This paper examines the link between the social networks surrounding business leaders and temporal myopia in strategic planning. Specifically, we hypothesize that processes characteristic of being embedded in a closed network are associated with a lack of foresight and a tendency to neglect long-run strategic planning. Using a probability sample of 700 CEOs in China, we show that network closure is associated with temporal myopia, which is evidenced in various measures of business planning. We show that managers embedded in closed networks are less experienced in long-run planning and are also less successful in implementing long-run business plans. Our contribution to the literature is twofold: we add a network perspective to the literature on temporal myopia in strategic management and, more significantly, by grounding temporal myopia in the network surrounding a person, we separate temporal myopia from the person. Myopia emerges from the social situations we create or in which we find ourselves.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how the social network surrounding a business leader is associated with temporal myopia in strategic planning. More specifically, it examines whether network closure around CEOs is linked to shorter planning horizons and to weaker performance benefits from long-run planning.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. The more closed the network around a business leader, the shorter the leader's planning horizon in business strategy.
- Hypothesis 2. Longer planning horizons are associated with greater success in business but with lesser levels of success for managers embedded in a closed network.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO network closure, measured primarily as network constraint around the CEO.
- DV(s): Planning horizon; long-term management contracts; long-term technical-staff contracts; employee training; perceived price elasticity; business success.
- Mediators: No formal mediator is estimated; the theorized process runs through information redundancy and social pressure within closed networks.
- Moderators: Network closure is theorized and tested as changing the performance implications of medium- and long-run planning horizons.

The mechanism is that closed networks expose managers to redundant information and stronger pressure to adopt majority views, making present-oriented, familiar, and incremental planning more likely. Open networks, by contrast, provide more heterogeneous information and weaker conformity pressure, which the authors argue supports foresight and longer-run strategic planning. The evidence is associational: the authors explicitly treat causality as unresolved because the design is cross-sectional.

**Data & Measures**
Data: a single cross-sectional survey. The 2012 survey drew a stratified probability sample based on city, industry, and company size, with the goal of sampling 100 companies in each of seven municipalities located in and on the margins of China's Yangtze Delta region (Shanghai, Hangzhou, Wenzhou, Ningbo, Nanjing, Changzhou, and Nantong), all firms being active in one of five manufacturing industries (textiles, machinery, automobile and vehicle parts, pharmaceutical products, and communication technologies); medium and large enterprises were oversampled and newly founded firms younger than three years were excluded from the sampling frame. The local survey organization invited 1,148 CEOs of private firms to face-to-face interviews, and 700 (60.97%) participated in and completed the survey; all interviews were conducted between August 2012 and January 2013. Independent variable: CEO network closure, measured as network constraint computed from each CEO's matrix of symmetric connections with and among cited contacts (connections scaled from zero to one, constraint values then multiplied by 100, so a score of 100 indicates contacts strongly connected with one another and no access to structural holes). Business contacts were identified with six name generators covering "event contacts" named for the founding and subsequent significant events plus current contacts (the three or four people most valuable to the business this year, the most difficult person this year, the most valuable employee this year, and any other particularly significant individual); the 700 respondents cited 4,464 different contacts, with an average network size of six. In the business-success models, "high constraint" is a dummy for CEOs whose constraint is above the sample median of 55.2. Dependent variables for Hypothesis 1, four measures: (a) self-reported planning horizon, from the question "In general, for how many years do you try to plan in advance in terms of the strategic or organizational development of your firm?", whose five response categories were collapsed into short-run (187 CEOs), mid-range (336), and long-run (177); (b) the percentage of management staff employed under three-to-five-year, "long-term" contracts (mean 37.65%); (c) the percentage of technical staff under such contracts (mean 34.56%); and (d) whether the company provided any in-house employee training during the last three years (67.7% did). Dependent variable for Hypothesis 2: business success, a principal component analysis z score aggregating the value of annual sales, number of full-time employees, and number of patents at the time of the survey, with the component describing 65% of the variance in the three indicators. A further outcome is perceived price elasticity, the percentage drop in sales a CEO expected if the firm raised the price of its main product by 10%. Controls: CEO age, gender, education in years of schooling, founder status, and a binary self-reported "unhappy" measure; firm age, log book value of 2011 assets, family firm, in-house research and development department, and initial business success (a principal component of full-time employees and sales revenue in the company's first registered year). Estimation: the Table 3 notes label M1 a multinomial logit over the three planning categories with mid-range as the reference, M2 and M3 ordinary least squares regressions of the two long-term contract percentages, and M4 a logit for employee training; the Table 4 and Table 5 notes do not name the estimator for M5-M7 (perceived price elasticity) or M8-M12 (business success). The business-success models are built stepwise: M8 includes only the continuous network-constraint measure, M9 adds measures of medium- and long-run planning using planning horizons up to four months as the benchmark category, M10 adds both interaction terms between high constraint and length of planning horizon, M11 adds a dummy for networks including one or more contacts known for less than two years, and M12 controls for fixed industry effects using the four-digit industrial classification (77 industry dummies) while retaining all other controls. Statistics in Tables 3, 4, and 5 are computed across the 700 respondents (M7 across 636 respondents, excluding observations where any network contact is known for less than two years); each model contains fixed effects for industry, city, and interviewer - five dummy variables for industries at the two-digit level of China's national industry classification, seven city effects, and 22 controls for interviewers - with robust standard errors clustered on city. The paper applies the more fine-grained four-digit classification (77 sectors) in M6 and M7, in M12, and in the appendix robustness exercises. Identification: the design is cross-sectional and the evidence associational rather than causally identified - the authors call the cross-sectional nature of their research design "problematic" and join "the chorus of researchers lamenting the deficiencies of cross-sectional studies, specifically when it comes to their inability to determine causality and rule out endogeneity," responding with covariate controls, network-history checks, and robustness tests rather than an identification strategy. Robustness: four-digit industry fixed effects (77 dummies), which reduced the sample size on average by 10% in the logistic regression models because not all responses varied in each of the 77 industry niches (Appendix A: M13 across 627 observations, M14 and M15 across 700, M16 across 647); exclusion of the 64 networks that included recent contacts (Appendix B: M17 across 566 observations, M18 and M19 across 636, M20 across 577); and network-composition controls for a mixed-gender network, the percentage of family members in the network, and a dummy for an above-median (15-year) spread in years known between the earliest and most recent entries into the network (Appendix C).

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 is supported for the three business-practice measures but only partly for the self-reported planning horizon. In the multinomial logit with mid-range planning as the reference category (Table 3, M1), CEOs in closed networks are significantly more likely than those in open networks to be short-run planners (b = .02, test statistic 2.89), but they are no less likely to be long-run planners (b = .01, test statistic 1.18, not significant). Network constraint is negatively associated with the percentage of managers on long-term contracts (M2, b = -.69, t = -4.51), the percentage of technical staff on long-term contracts (M3, b = -.65, t = -4.15), and the provision of employee training in the last three years (M4, b = -.04, logit test statistic -5.73).
- Zero-order associations plotted across five-point intervals of network constraint run in the same direction: -0.76 for the percentage of CEOs relying on long-term plans, -0.93 and -0.91 for long-term contracts for managers and technical staff respectively, and -0.87 for provision of employee training.
- The authors flag two qualifications. First, there is a difference between what respondents say they do and what they actually do: the negative associations are very strong for the three actual business practices (M2-M4) but the association is weaker for the self-reported planning horizon (M1). Second, most controls have negligible associations with the planning-horizon variables, with two exceptions - unhappy CEOs and family firms - and those associations exist only for the business-practice measures, not for self-declared short-run versus long-run planning.
- Perceived price elasticity was found to decrease as the network around a CEO became more closed (-0.87 correlation; M5, b = -0.09, t = -3.20). The estimate was slightly larger with four-digit industry fixed effects (M6, b = -0.10, t = -3.24) and when networks containing contacts known for only one year were excluded (M7, b = -0.11, t = -3.47).
- For Hypothesis 2, network constraint is negatively associated with business success in every model of Table 5 (b = -.005 in M8, -.004 in M9, -.005 in M10, M11, and M12; test statistics -3.255, -3.210, -3.499, -3.48, and -3.83). In M9, both planning measures carry the expected positive association with business success: medium-range planning b = 0.09 (t-test 4.53) and long-run planning b = 0.17 (t-test 3.41).
- The interaction evidence for Hypothesis 2 is directionally as predicted but statistically partial. In M10, high constraint x medium plan is positive and significant (b = 0.10, t-test 2.901) while high constraint x long-run plan is negative with only marginal statistical significance (b = -0.06, t-test -2.142); the medium-range main effect falls to b = 0.04 (t-test 1.971, marginally significant) and the long-run main effect rises to b = 0.20 (t-test 3.41). The authors read this as long-run plans exceeding one year carrying a coefficient about 30% smaller for managers embedded in closed networks. M11, which adds a dummy for networks containing contacts known for less than two years, is consistent with M10. In M12, with four-digit industry fixed effects, the coefficient for long-run plans conducted by managers surrounded by closed networks is no longer statistically significant, which the authors attribute to there being only 87 observations combining long-run plans with high network constraint; the positive interaction between medium plans and network closure remains statistically significant. The authors conclude that the results in Table 5 were consistent with their second hypothesis.
- Reverse-causality checks found no significant difference in firms' return on assets in the years preceding the survey (2009-2011) between firms relying on short-term plans and those planning for longer periods, no significant difference in the frequency of short-term plans between companies that had and had not experienced a sales drop in the three preceding years, and no significant difference in five-year survival between short-term planners and others.
- Robustness: with four-digit industry fixed effects the network association held for all four Hypothesis 1 outcomes (test statistics 2.27 for M13 short-run plans, -4.66 for M14 manager contracts, -3.81 for M15 technical-staff contracts, and -6.03 for M16 employee training). Excluding the 64 networks with recent contacts, the constraint estimates increased from -0.69 to -0.81 with a -4.20 test statistic (M18, managers) and from -0.65 to -0.73 with a -6.21 test statistic (M19, technical staff). Controlling for network composition, all four planning proxies retained statistically significant coefficient estimates for the network association, with no systematic association between the composition measures and planning horizons, leading the authors to conclude that structure rather than composition is the network condition inducing temporal myopia.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper adds a network perspective to research on temporal myopia in strategic management by shifting attention from individual cognitive traits to the social situation surrounding the decision-maker. It argues that myopia can emerge from network structure, especially the information redundancy and conformity pressures of closed networks.

It also extends social network theory by identifying temporal myopia and planning horizon as outcomes associated with network closure and structural holes. The paper reframes the familiar broker "vision advantage" as not only a creativity or opportunity advantage but also a planning advantage.

**Practical Implication**
For managers whose firms need long-run strategic plans, the findings imply value in maintaining relatively open business networks with diverse contacts not tightly connected to one another. For managers embedded in close-knit networks, the paper suggests actively disrupting established advice networks and adding diverse contacts to increase information heterogeneity.

The authors also caution that closed-network managers may be more successful with shorter planning horizons than with poorly designed long-run plans. Short-run planning is not always irrational, but relying on it as a long-run strategy is risky in competitive and changing environments.

**Limitations**
The central limitation is the cross-sectional research design, which cannot determine causality or fully rule out endogeneity. The authors use controls, network-history checks, industry fixed effects, and robustness tests, but they still call causal corroboration unresolved.

The sample is also concentrated in China's extended Yangtze Delta region and consists mainly of medium-sized, owner- or founder-led private manufacturing firms. The authors therefore caution that the association may differ in large public companies, professionally managed firms, and other institutional or cultural contexts.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine contingencies that protect people in closed networks from temporal myopia, including whether external knowledge sources increase diversity inside closed networks or are rejected as unfamiliar. The authors also call for longitudinal employee data, laboratory or field experiments, and comparative studies across cultural and institutional settings.

Additional work should test whether the network-closure association generalizes to larger firms and professional managers who answer to boards, shareholders, and external stakeholders. Such work could refine the boundary conditions before the findings are used to extend theory or guide practice.
