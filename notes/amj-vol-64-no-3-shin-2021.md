# When Putting Work Off Pays Off: The Curvilinear Relationship between Procrastination and Creativity

**Abstract**
Although it is widely assumed that procrastination is counterproductive, delaying task progress may have hidden benefits for creativity. Drawing on theories of incubation, we propose that moderate procrastination can foster creativity when employees have the intrinsic motivation and opportunity to generate new ideas. In two experiments in the United States, we tempted participants to engage in varying degrees of procrastination by making different numbers of funny YouTube videos easily accessible while they were supposed to be solving business problems. Participants generated more creative ideas in the moderate rather than low or high procrastination conditions. This curvilinear effect was partially mediated by problem restructuring and the activation of new knowledge. We constructively replicated and extended the curvilinear effect in a field study with Korean employees: procrastination predicted lower task efficiency but had an inverted-U-shaped relationship with creativity. Employees who procrastinated moderately received higher creativity ratings from their supervisors than employees who procrastinated more or less, provided that intrinsic motivation or creative requirement was high. We discuss theoretical and practical implications for time management, creativity, and motivation in organizations.

**Research Question**
Does procrastination have an inverted-U-shaped relationship with creativity, such that moderate procrastination can increase creative output relative to low or high procrastination? The paper also asks whether problem restructuring and activation of new knowledge explain this effect, and whether intrinsic motivation or creative requirement determines when moderate procrastination is beneficial.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Procrastination has an inverted-U-shaped effect on creativity, such that moderate procrastination leads to higher creativity than low or high procrastination.
- H2: The curvilinear effect of procrastination on creativity is mediated by (a) problem restructuring and (b) the activation of new knowledge.
- H3: The curvilinear effect of procrastination on creativity is jointly moderated by intrinsic motivation and creative requirement, such that moderate procrastination is beneficial to creativity as long as intrinsic motivation and/or creative requirement is high, but it is not beneficial to creativity when both intrinsic motivation and creative requirement are low.

The paper allocates the hypotheses across studies: Study 1 tests Hypothesis 1, Study 2 replicates the curvilinear effect and tests Hypotheses 2a and 2b, and Study 3 tests Hypotheses 1 and 3 in the field.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Procrastination level, operationalized experimentally as low, moderate, or high temptation to delay task work and, in the field study, as employee-reported procrastination across core tasks.
- DV(s): Creativity of business ideas or work-related ideas; Study 3 also examines task efficiency to test whether the pattern is specific to creativity.
- Mediators: Problem restructuring and activation of new knowledge in Study 2.
- Moderators: Intrinsic motivation and creative requirement in Study 3.

The proposed mechanism is an incubation process. Low procrastination can make people start too quickly and remain tied to obvious initial framings, while high procrastination leaves too little time and too much deadline pressure for creative restructuring. Moderate procrastination creates enough distance from the task introduction and deadline for people to reframe the problem and access remote knowledge. This process is expected to support creativity only when intrinsic motivation or creative requirement keeps the task active in the back of the mind rather than allowing full disengagement.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (laboratory experiment in the behavioral lab of a large U.S. university; 119 undergraduate students, 77% female, average age 20, SD = 2.21; n = 40 low, 40 moderate, 39 high; paid $12 for an hour): participants were randomly assigned to one of three temptation conditions and given 30 minutes to write a business proposal for a student entrepreneur who had won $10,000. IV: procrastination manipulated indirectly by the number of funny YouTube videos embedded on the task page (one, four, or eight), with total access held constant at nine videos across conditions; low-procrastination participants watched four videos before the task introduction. DV: creativity of the final proposal, rated by two independent raters (business-school graduate students told creativity means novel and useful ideas but not told the purpose, hypotheses, or conditions) on a 7-point scale from 1 (not at all creative) to 7 (very creative), ICC2 = .94, averaged. Two further coders scored the brainstormed ideas for originality (ICC2 = .79), flexibility (ICC2 = .92), and fluency (100% agreement). Manipulation checks: seconds spent watching videos plus four self-report items adapted from Mann's (1982) decisional procrastination scale (α = .85); the study also measured creative requirement (α = .78), intrinsic motivation (Grant, 2008; α = .90), and the Discrete Emotions Questionnaire subscales for happiness (α = .94), relaxation (α = .94), sadness (α = .84), and anxiety (α = .90).
- Study 2 (laboratory experiment in the behavioral lab of a large U.S. university; 126 graduate students, 58% female, average age 25.34, SD = 4.50; n = 42 per condition; paid $12 for an hour): each experimental session was randomly assigned to one of the same three conditions using the same video manipulation, except that the low-procrastination condition did not watch videos first; all participants had 30 minutes. The task, based on an assignment used in a technology ventures class (Seelig, 2009), was to generate as much money as possible in three hours from $10 in seed money and then email the chosen idea to a student named Mike. DV: creativity of the email, rated by two trained research assistants — business-school graduate students unaware of the purpose, hypotheses, and conditions — on the same 7-point scale, ICC2 = .85, averaged. Mediators coded by two separate coders from the brainstorming phase: problem restructuring = the number of times a participant restructured the problem as a percentage of the ideas explored (ICC2 = .94, AD = .02); activation of new knowledge = each explored idea rated 1 to 7 for new knowledge relative to the participant's earlier ideas, then summed (ICC2 = .95, AD = .49). Manipulation checks: seconds spent watching videos, procrastination items adapted from Mann's scale (α = .69), and the time at which the first idea was entered.
- Study 3 (field survey at a furniture company in South Korea; the questionnaire went to 250 employees from a representative cross section of office jobs spanning strategy, sales, design, marketing, quality management, production management, and customer service; 177 employees completed the surveys, a 71% response rate; supervisor responses were received for 170 employees; there were 27 supervisors, an average of just over six employees per supervisor; employees were 56% male, averaged 31 years of age, and averaged 3.63 years in their current job): IV procrastination measured with the personal-project methodology — employees wrote down up to five core tasks and indicated how much they procrastinated on each from 1 (not at all) to 7 (to a great extent), averaged into a global work procrastination score (α = .78). DV creativity = supervisor ratings on Tierney, Farmer, and Graen's (1999) nine-item scale (α = .96). Task efficiency = a per-task supervisor item adapted from Ashford and Black (1996) on quantity of work output (α = .83). Moderators: intrinsic motivation, four items from Grant (2008) (α = .94); creative requirement, four items from Morgeson and Humphrey (2006) (α = .86). Controls: extrinsic motivation (Grant & Berry, 2011; α = .84), age, gender, education, and job experience in months.
- Identification: Studies 1 and 2 are randomized laboratory experiments, a method the authors say facilitates causal inference and provides internal validity, while the field study provides external validity. Study 3 is a cross-sectional survey and its relationships are associational. In Study 3 all predictors were standardized, the nesting of employees within supervisors was handled with random coefficient modeling (80% of the variance in creativity was at the employee level and 20% at the supervisor level), curvilinearity was tested with a procrastination-squared term, and the inverted-U conditions were checked with the two-lines test (Simonsohn, 2018).

**Key Findings**
- H1 was supported in all three studies. Study 1: procrastination affected proposal creativity, F(2, 116) = 6.96, p < .01, partial η² = .11, with a significant curvilinear contrast, t(116) = 3.71, p < .001, d = .69; the moderate condition (M = 4.48, SD = 1.58) scored above both the low (M = 3.21, SD = 1.86) and high (M = 3.36, SD = 1.49) conditions, which did not differ significantly from each other. Study 2: F(2, 123) = 5.66, p < .01, partial η² = .08, curvilinear contrast t(123) = 3.21, p < .01, d = .58; moderate (M = 3.21, SD = 1.80) above low (M = 2.42, SD = 1.48) and high (M = 2.06, SD = 1.53), with low and high again not differing significantly. Study 3: the quadratic term was negative and significant (γ = -.12, p < .05), and the two-lines test produced a significant positive slope for low to moderate procrastination (γ = .42, p < .05) and a significant negative slope for moderate to high procrastination (γ = -.26, p < .05).
- H2a and H2b were tested only in Study 2 and were supported as partial mediation. The moderate condition showed significantly higher problem restructuring, F(2, 123) = 3.99, p < .05, contrast t(123) = 2.77, p < .01, d = .50, and significantly higher activation of new knowledge, F(2, 123) = 7.60, p < .01, partial η² = .11, contrast t(123) = 3.46, p < .01, d = .62. With both mediators entered, the curvilinear effect of procrastination on creativity decreased from b = -.98 (SE = .30, t = -3.21, p < .001) to b = -.64 (SE = .31, t = -2.05, p < .05), while creativity was significantly predicted by problem restructuring (b = 1.83, SE = .84, t = 2.18, p < .05) and by the activation of new knowledge (b = .03, SE = .02, t = 2.09, p < .05). The bootstrapped indirect curvilinear effects were significant through problem restructuring (indirect effect = -.16, 95% CI [-.38, -.01]) and through the activation of new knowledge (indirect effect = -.21, 95% CI [-.44, -.05]).
- H3 was tested only in Study 3 and was supported: the quadratic three-way interaction of procrastination squared with creative requirement and intrinsic motivation was significant (γ = .10, SE = .05, t = 2.16, p < .05). The high creative requirement–high intrinsic motivation curve was significantly curvilinear (γ = -.21, SE = .08, t = -2.64, p < .01), whereas the low–low slope was significantly linear (γ = -.20, SE = .08, t = -2.42, p < .05) but not significantly curvilinear (γ = -.04, SE = .07, t = -.62, p > .05). A footnote records that the linear two-way and three-way interactions of procrastination with intrinsic motivation and creative requirement were not significant.
- The pattern did not extend to efficiency: in Study 3 procrastination had a linear negative relationship with supervisor-rated task efficiency (γ = -.13, p < .01) and no curvilinear relationship with it, so the benefit of moderate procrastination applied to creativity but not task efficiency.
- Exploratory analyses in Study 1 found no condition differences in idea fluency, but significant differences in originality, F(2, 113) = 3.67, p < .05, and in flexibility, F(2, 113) = 3.06, p = .05, with the moderate condition higher on both contrasts (originality t(65.40) = 2.52, p = .01, d = .62; flexibility t(113) = 2.36, p < .05, d = .44). The indirect curvilinear effect through originality was significant (indirect effect = -.56, 95% CI [-1.04, -.13]), leading the authors to conclude that the creativity gain under moderate procrastination came from the quality rather than the quantity of ideas explored. They state explicitly that they do not treat originality, flexibility, and fluency as mediators, because these are behavioral outcomes rather than psychological processes.
- Manipulation checks behaved as intended in both experiments: conditions differed significantly in seconds spent watching videos (Study 1: F(2, 113) = 54.34, p < .001; Study 2: F(2, 121) = 77.62, p < .001) and in self-reported procrastination (Study 1: F(2, 116) = 7.33, p < .01; Study 2: F(2, 123) = 6.71, p < .01), and in Study 1 neither intrinsic motivation nor creative requirement varied significantly across conditions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the dominant treatment of procrastination as uniformly dysfunctional by showing that moderate delay can benefit creativity under bounded conditions. It extends creativity research by identifying procrastination as a behavioral antecedent of incubation, and by distinguishing the creative benefits of moderate delay from the efficiency costs of delay.

The study also contributes to motivation theory by showing that intrinsic motivation need not simply prevent procrastination. Instead, intrinsic motivation shapes how attention is allocated during procrastination, while creative requirement can substitute for intrinsic motivation by giving employees a reason to keep a creative problem mentally active.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should not treat all procrastination as equivalent. When novel and useful ideas are needed, a moderate delay after initial task exposure may help employees incubate ideas, especially when they are intrinsically interested in the work or when the job clearly requires creativity.

The findings do not justify open-ended delay. The authors emphasize that procrastination still reduces task efficiency and that useful creative procrastination requires actual work, sufficient task exposure, and limits that prevent last-minute rushing.

**Limitations**
The mediators and moderators were tested in separate studies, so the full moderated mediation model was not examined in one integrated design. The studies also do not directly compare different motives for procrastination or the content of alternative activities undertaken while procrastinating.

Additional limitations include partial mediation in Study 2, limited theory about what time intervals count as moderate versus high procrastination, a cross-sectional field study, and a focus on individual rather than group procrastination and creativity.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the full model in a single design and examine whether different motives for procrastination, such as dissatisfaction with initial ideas versus self-regulatory failure, change its creative consequences. Studies could also test how mindless, moderately interesting, or highly intrinsically motivating alternative activities affect incubation.

The authors call for richer timing theory, experience-sampling designs, research on group-level procrastination and creativity, and studies of organizational antecedents such as deadline design. They also suggest examining additional mechanisms, including selective forgetting, idea selection, and feedback from weak ties.
