# Amplifying Voice in Organizations

**Abstract**
We extend the field’s understanding of voice recognition by examining peer responses to voice. We investigate how employees can help peers get a status boost from voicing, while also raising their ownstatus, by introducing the conceptof amplification—public endorsement of another person’s contribution, with attribution to that person. In two experiments and one field study, we find that amplification enhances status both for voicers and for those who amplify voice. Being amplified was equally beneficial for voicers who framed their ideas promotively and prohibitively (improvement-focused and problem-focused, respectively; Study 1), and for men and women (Study 2). Furthermore, amplified ideas were rated as higher quality than nonamplified ideas. Amplification also helped amplifiers: participants reading experimentally manipulated meeting transcripts rated amplifiers as higher status than those who self-promoted, stayed quiet, or contributed additional ideas (Studies 1 and 2). Finally, in an intervention in a nonprofit organization, select employees trained to use amplification attained higher status in their work groups (Study 3). Overall, these results increase our understanding of how social actors can capitalize on instances of voice to give a status boost to voicers who might otherwise be overlooked, and help organizations realize the potential of employees’ diverse perspectives.

**Research Question**
How do peers' responses to employee voice shape observers' evaluations of the voiced idea, the voicer's status, and the responder's status? The paper asks whether amplification, defined as public endorsement of another person's contribution with attribution, can create status gains for both the person being amplified and the person doing the amplifying.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Observers will rate amplified ideas as higher quality compared to nonamplified ideas.
- H2: Being amplified by others increases a voicer's status within the group, compared to not being amplified by others.
- H3: Perceptions of idea quality will mediate the relationship between amplification and a voicer's status within the group.
- H4: Amplifying others increases the responder's status within the group more than does staying quiet, self-promoting, and voicing a new idea of equal merit.
- H5a (competing alternative to H5b): Amplifying, compared to staying quiet, self-promoting, and voicing a new idea of equal merit, increases the voicer's and responder's status when the voicer uses promotive, but not prohibitive, voice.
- H5b (competing alternative to H5a): Amplifying, compared to staying quiet, self-promoting, and voicing a new idea of equal merit, increases the voicer's and responder's status, whether the voicer uses prohibitive voice or promotive voice.
- H6a (competing alternative to H6b): Amplifying, compared to staying quiet, self-promoting, and voicing a new idea of equal merit, increases the voicer's (responder's) status more when the voicer (responder) is initially higher status (e.g., male) than when that person is initially lower status (e.g., female).
- H6b (competing alternative to H6a): Amplifying, compared to staying quiet, self-promoting, and voicing a new idea of equal merit, increases the voicer's (responder's) status whether the voicer (responder) is initially higher status (e.g., male) or lower status (e.g., female).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Peer response to voice, especially amplification, compared with staying quiet, self-promotion, or voicing an additional idea; Study 1 also varies promotive versus prohibitive voice, and Study 2 varies voicer and responder gender.
- DV(s): Perceived quality of the voiced idea, voicer status, responder status, and, in Study 3, team-member status after an amplification intervention.
- Mediators: Perceived idea quality mediates the relationship between amplification and voicer status.
- Boundary conditions / intervention target: Voice type, voicer gender, and responder gender are tested in the experiments; initially lower-status employees are the target of the Study 3 field intervention.

The proposed mechanism is social evaluative rather than purely individual: amplification signals that a peer regards the voiced contribution as worth considering, which can raise perceived idea quality and thereby increase the voicer's status. For amplifiers, the mechanism draws on competitive altruism: publicly crediting someone else's contribution appears prosocial and group-oriented, allowing the amplifier to gain status without taking attention away from the voicer. Voice type and gender were each tested in only part of the design: Study 1 found the amplification effect held for both promotive and prohibitive voicers, and Study 2 found it held for male and female voicers and responders alike, while Study 3 extended Study 2 by turning to employees whose low status was not necessarily due to their gender.

**Data & Measures**
Three studies: two online vignette experiments (Studies 1 and 2) and a pre-post field intervention in one organization (Study 3). The authors report that all data, materials, code, preanalysis plans, preregistrations, and supplemental analyses are posted on the Open Science Framework.

- Study 1 sample and design: targeting 200 working adults per cell, 1,197 participants were recruited from Prolific (excluding duplicate IP or worker IDs; $1.10 payment); 1,189 passed attention checks and one further participant was excluded for incoherent open-ended responses, giving a final sample of 1,188 (48.3% female, 0.8% nonbinary, 0.3% prefer not to say; mean age = 33.59, SD = 11.29; 73.4% white). Between-participants design: 2 (voice type: promotive vs. prohibitive) × 3 (response: amplification vs. additional voice vs. staying quiet), with participants reading a transcript of an insurance-company sales-team meeting. Cell sizes were amplification n = 398, staying quiet n = 389, additional voice n = 401 (promotive n = 588; prohibitive n = 600). The manipulation check was passed by 93.6%.
- Study 1 measures: perceptions of idea quality with six items as in Dooley and Fryxell (1999) (e.g., "The idea makes sense based on this sales team's current situation"; α = .90), on a seven-point scale from 1 = Definitely no to 7 = Definitely yes; each team member's status with five items as in Weiss and Morrison (2018) (respect, status, prestige, admiration, influence; α > .89), on 5-point scales from 1 = None or not at all to 5 = A lot or very. Participants rated the voicer's idea and the responder's first idea, and the status of the voicer, the responder, and an uninvolved third team member.
- Study 2 sample and design: targeting 100 per cell, 1,610 participants were recruited from MTurk (excluding duplicate IP or worker IDs; $0.70 payment); 1,585 passed attention checks and a sound capability check, 52 left before completing dependent measures, and 32 were excluded per the preregistration for incoherent open-ended responses, giving a final sample of 1,501 (59.8% female; 0.5% nonbinary; mean age = 34.07, SD = 10.86; 73.4% white). Between-participants design: 2 (voicer gender: female vs. male) × 2 (responder gender: female vs. male) × 4 (response: amplification vs. self-promotion vs. additional voice vs. staying quiet), delivered as audio clips of a meeting. Cell sizes were amplification n = 378, staying quiet n = 381, self-promoting n = 360, additional voice n = 382. Gender was operationalized via names, photographs, pronouns, and audio recorded voices (24 photos, 12 performance reviews adapted from Malmström, Johansson, and Wincent (2017), and six audio recordings). The manipulation check was passed by 87.3%.
- Study 2 measures: status and idea quality scales were the same as in Study 1 (all α > .87). Idea-evaluation analyses use n = 1,499 due to incomplete responses for that measure.
- Study 3 setting and sample: a nonprofit educational organization serving people with developmental disabilities, whose 10 classrooms each have a team of one or two teachers and 4-6 paraeducators, and sometimes behavior technicians. Seventy-seven employees (90% of current employees; 65% female; Mage = 30) completed the preintervention survey and 75 (77% of current employees; 69% female; Mage = 29) the postintervention survey, in exchange for $5 per survey plus a chance to win a $50 gift card. The school director identified 26 employees who in her view lacked due influence; 22 (85%) agreed to be trained as amplifiers for an additional $30 (59% female; Mage = 28; 20 paraeducators, one teacher, and one behavior technician; eight classrooms had two amplifiers and two had three). All amplifiers completed the preintervention survey and 19 (86%) the postintervention survey.
- Study 3 intervention and measures: amplifiers completed an interactive 17-minute online training; the amplification period was the two weeks after employees returned from a one-week school closure, with a daily reminder email, and postintervention surveys were sent five weeks after the first survey. Respondents rated the status of each other employee on their team with two items adapted from Kilduff and Galinsky (2013) — "How much influence does each person have during team meetings?" (1 = None, 5 = A lot) and a level-of-respect item (1 = Very low, 5 = Very high; Spearman-Brown r = .74). A two-item group-level manipulation check asked how much people promote and support each other's ideas (1 = Not at all to 5 = A lot; Spearman-Brown r = .86).

Identification: Studies 1 and 2 are randomized between-participants vignette experiments analyzed with two-way ANOVAs, Tukey's HSD planned comparisons, and indirect-effect tests based on 5,000 bootstrap samples. Study 3 is a pre-post field design that the authors state is not randomized — the manager selected which employees were trained, and the authors could train amplifiers but not control which voicers were amplified. Study 3 was analyzed with a mixed-model analysis in R (lme4 and lmerTest) using a fixed effect for amplification and random intercepts for individuals nested within teams, with adjusted intraclass correlation coefficients computed via sjstats; the reported analyses include only ratings from employees who did not receive training. Table 5 reports n = 952, with employees' status n = 924 due to missing data, and the Table 6 regression is based on 924 observations.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 — idea quality (H1 supported): a significant main effect of response on evaluation of the voicer's idea, F(2, 1182) = 8.78, p < .001, ηp2 = .015; Tukey's HSD showed the voicer's idea was rated higher quality in the amplification condition than in the additional voice or staying quiet conditions (p < .012). There was no significant main effect of promotive versus prohibitive voice type, F(1, 1182) = .63, p = .429, and no interaction, F(1, 1182) = .60, p = .324. Amplification did not affect the perceived quality of the responder's own initial idea, F(2, 1182) = 1.06, p = .348.
- Study 1 — voicer status (H2 supported): a significant main effect of response, F(2, 1182) = 27.20, p < .001, ηp2 = .044; voicer status was higher in the amplification than the additional voice and staying quiet conditions (p < .001). Voicer status was also significantly higher in the promotive than the prohibitive condition, F(1, 1182) = 4.66, p = .031, ηp2 = .004.
- Study 1 — mediation (H3 supported): the indirect effects of the evaluation of the voicer's idea on voicer status were significant when comparing amplification to both additional voice (b = -.11, 95% CI [-.17, -.05]) and staying quiet (b = -.07, 95% CI [-.11, -.02]).
- Study 1 — responder status (H4 supported): a significant main effect of response, F(2, 1182) = 13.65, p < .001, ηp2 = .023; responder status was higher in the amplification than the additional voice and staying quiet conditions (p < .001). Amplification did not affect the status of the uninvolved third group member, F(2, 1185) = .39, p = .677.
- Study 1 — voice type moderation (H5b supported over the competing H5a): the response × voice type interaction was not significant for voicer status, F(2, 1182) = .76, p = .466, and there was no significant voice-type main effect, F(1, 1182) = 2.33, p = .127, or interaction, F(2, 1182) = .31, p = .731, for responder status. H6a/H6b were not tested in Study 1.
- Study 2 — idea quality (H1 supported): a significant main effect of response, F(3, 1491) = 16.55, p < .001, ηp2 = .032; the voicer's idea appeared higher quality when amplified than when responders stayed quiet, voiced a different idea, or promoted their own idea (all p < .001). Ideas voiced by women were perceived as higher quality than those voiced by men, F(1, 1491) = 5.94, p = .015, ηp2 = .004, and the voicer gender × response interaction was not significant, F(3, 1491) = .42, p = .742 (supporting H6b). The main effect of response on the responder's own idea was only marginal, F(3, 1495) = 2.57, p = .053, ηp2 = .005, with a difference between amplification and self-promotion (p = .035) but not versus the other two conditions (p > .767).
- Study 2 — voicer status (H2 supported): response affected voicer status, F(3, 1493) = 80.00, p < .001, ηp2 = .141, with amplified voicers rated higher than voicers in any other condition (Tukey's HSD p < .001). Consistent with gender stereotypes, male voicers had higher status than female voicers, F(1, 1493) = 35.14, p < .001, ηp2 = .023; the response × voicer gender interaction was not significant, F(3, 1493) = .37, p = .774, supporting H6b.
- Study 2 — mediation (H3 supported): the indirect effects of the evaluation of the voicer's idea on voicer status were significant when comparing amplification to self-promotion (b = -.10, 95% CI [-.14, -.06]), additional voice (b = -.11, 95% CI [-.15, -.07]), and staying quiet (b = -.10, 95% CI [-.14, -.06]).
- Study 2 — responder status (H4 supported): a significant main effect of response, F(3, 1493) = 52.05, p < .001, ηp2 = .096, with responders who amplified rated higher status than responders in any other condition (Tukey's HSD p < .001). Male responders were seen as higher status than female responders, F(1, 1493) = 32.80, p < .001, ηp2 = .021; the response × responder gender interaction was not significant, F(3, 1493) = .88, p = .454, further supporting H6b. The status of the other group member was not affected by response, F(3, 1497) = 1.49, p = .215. H5a/H5b were not tested in Study 2.
- Study 3 — manipulation check: group members who had not been part of the training believed there was more amplification in the group after the intervention (M = 3.94, SD = .78) than before (M = 3.67, SD = .89), b = .25, t(49.65) = 2.39, p = .021.
- Study 3 — field intervention (consistent with H4): employees in the amplification group were rated as having higher status postintervention (M = 4.09, SD = .80) than preintervention (M = 3.78, SD = 1.00), b = .37, t(880.86) = 3.38, p < .001, whereas employees outside the amplification group did not experience a significant change in status over the same period (Mpre = 3.89, SDpre = .99; Mpost = 3.92, SDpost = 1.02), b = .09, t(904.43) = 1.27, p = .204; the interaction was significant, b = .28, p = .023, ICCadj = .32.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends voice research by shifting attention from manager recognition to peer responses during the voice process. It contributes to status theory by showing that voice episodes need not be zero-sum: voicers and amplifiers can both gain status when a peer publicly endorses and attributes a contribution. It also suggests that peer amplification can help ideas and contributors that might otherwise be overlooked, including prohibitive voice and voices from lower-status group members.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations can train employees to amplify peers' contributions in meetings, especially when some employees' ideas are not being heard. The field intervention suggests that low-status employees can learn to use amplification and may gain status in their work groups. The findings also reassure employees that amplifying a colleague need not lower their own standing relative to self-promotion or adding another idea.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 use experimental vignette methods, which provide control but may not capture the full information and social history present in real meetings. Study 3 improves realism but uses a pre-post design in one small nonprofit organization, with employees selected by a manager rather than randomly assigned. The authors also could train amplifiers but could not control which voicers were amplified.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine when amplification has stronger or weaker effects, including contexts with strong subgroup faultlines, competition, or only one idea under consideration. The authors call for research on whether status gains persist over time and whether explicit attribution helps voicers receive longer-term recognition. They also suggest studying group-level consequences, such as performance, decision quality, psychological safety, future voice, and gendered relationships between idea quality and status.
