# Getting the Picture Too Late: Handoffs and the Effectiveness of Idea Implementation in Creative Work

**Abstract**
Past research on idea implementation has focused on employees trying to win social support for their own ideas, yet employees are often handed ideas to implement that were developed by others. We propose and test hypotheses on such handoffs, focusing on how handing employees relatively mature ideas to implement may lead them to build less creative final products. We tested our hypotheses using two studies: an archival study of 5,676 movies in the U.S. film industry and a complementary experiment. Results suggest that late handoffs yielded less creative final products than no or early handoffs, meaning it was costly to creativity when employees implemented relatively mature ideas without driving at least some of their prior development. However, “serialized late handoffs”—wherein implementers were handed relatively mature ideas after an earlier handoff between two other individuals—were less costly to creativity than late handoffs from one other individual. Mediation results suggest that late handoffs reduced implementers’ creativity by restrictingtheirsenseofpsychologicalownershipandthecoherenceoftheirfinalproducts. This research advances theory on idea implementation, handoffs, and psychological ownership in creative work.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when and from whom creative ideas can be handed to implementers without reducing the creativity of the final product. It specifically tests whether late handoffs of relatively mature ideas reduce implementers' final-product creativity compared with no handoffs, early handoffs, and serialized late handoffs.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Late handoffs have a negative effect on creativity, such that implementers who receive late handoffs build less creative final products than implementers who receive (a) no handoffs, (b) early handoffs, and (c) serialized late handoffs.
- Hypothesis 2. The negative effect of late handoffs on creativity is mediated by psychological ownership, such that implementers who receive late handoffs have lower psychological ownership of the emerging product—leading them to build less creative final products—than implementers who receive (a) no handoffs, (b) early handoffs, and (c) serialized late handoffs.
- Hypothesis 3. The negative effect of late handoffs on creativity is mediated by product coherence, such that implementers who receive late handoffs build less coherent—and thus less creative—final products than implementers who receive (a) no handoffs, (b) early handoffs, and (c) serialized late handoffs.
- Hypothesis 1 was tested in Study 1 and Study 2; the two mediation hypotheses, Hypotheses 2 and 3, were tested only in Study 2, which the authors describe as the experimental test of the whole model.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Handoff type, comparing late handoffs with no handoffs, early handoffs, serialized late handoffs, and in Study 2 a separate no-handoff condition.
- DV(s): Creativity of the final product, measured with Rotten Tomatoes critic scores in Study 1 and independent consumer ratings of advertisements in Study 2.
- Mediators: Psychological ownership and product coherence in Study 2.
- Moderators: Not modeled statistically.

Berg and Yu argue that implementers receiving late handoffs enter only after an idea has become relatively mature, leaving less opportunity to shape the idea, invest the self in it, or develop a unified product vision. This weakens psychological ownership and product coherence, which in turn reduces the creativity of the finished product. Serialized late handoffs are theorized to be less costly because an earlier handoff makes ownership seem more open and may encourage the implementer to carry forward a coherent trajectory rather than overwrite a single predecessor's mature plan. Empirically, late handoffs predicted lower creativity in both studies; mediation in Study 2 supported psychological ownership for most late-versus-other comparisons and product coherence for all four late-versus-other comparisons.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data: an archival data set of the film industry in the United States, focusing on 5,676 films released from 1990 to 2016, assembled to test Hypothesis 1 in a real-life creative industry. The starting point was the movie list in OpusData, a preliminary list of 24,850 films released from 1915 to 2016. Data were available on IMDb for 82% of that list, narrowing the data set to 20,326 films, and this 20,326-film set was used to create the control variables. Films without Rotten Tomatoes critic scores (n = 6,088), films released prior to 1990 (n = 2,568), and films with more than one individual credited for any of the three stages (n = 5,957) were omitted.
- Study 1 independent variable (handoff type): coded from films' writer and director credits, relying on Writers Guild of America rules that distinguish writers who created the initial story and characters (generation) from those who wrote the screenplay (elaboration), with director credits listed separately (implementation). Films were late handoff (n = 1,604; 28.26%), no handoff (n = 2,821; 49.70%), early handoff (n = 483, 8.51%), and serial late handoff (n = 768; 13.53%).
- Study 1 dependent variable (creativity): the Rotten Tomatoes 0–10 mean critic score, which the authors argue aligns with the Consensual Assessment Technique because critics publish independently and the site aggregates their reviews into composite scores. The 0–100% Rotten Tomatoes score, MetaCritic critic scores, and IMDb user/audience scores were used as supplementary dependent variables. To examine validity, the authors collected independent ratings of 2,000 critic reviews on 200 films (10 reviews per film), recruiting 2,000 independent raters who each assessed 20 critic reviews.
- Study 1 controls and estimation: number of critics reviewing the film; cumulative prior film count for the director and for the writer(s); mean number of prior films on which the director had worked with each writer; cumulative prior mean Rotten Tomatoes critic score for the director and for the writer(s); a sequel dummy; dummies for each of 23 IMDb genres, for release year (1990–2016), and for director career start year (1957–2016); and the film's primary studio, identified for 98% of films. All continuous controls were standardized while the dependent variable was kept in its original 0–10 scale. Estimation used random-intercept models with films (level 1) cross-nested in directors (level 2) and studios (level 3), on 5,676 observations, 3,535 directors, and 996 studios. The authors state that because Study 1 was cross-sectional, Hypothesis 1 could not be tested causally there.
- Study 2 design and sample: an experiment designed to enable causal tests of Hypotheses 1 to 3, with 600 participants in the United States recruited via Amazon MTurk (53.33% female; ages 18 to 71; Mage = 35.36, SDage = 10.32), each compensated $6.00. The experiment had five conditions of 120 participants each: four yoked conditions (late, no, early, serialized late) plus a separate no-handoff condition. The creative task was developing an advertisement for a soft pretzel made with cricket flour, delivered as a three-slide presentation and divided into generation, elaboration, and implementation stages. Participants in the late, early, and serialized late conditions were handed initial ideas and outlines created by participants in the yoked no-handoff condition. Because the yoked no-handoff condition had to be run first to collect that content, it was not randomly assigned; the separate no-handoff condition was included as a randomly assigned counterpart.
- Study 2 creativity and product coherence: measured with the Consensual Assessment Technique using independent sets of raters, a total of 600 consumers in the United States recruited through MTurk (45.67% female; ages 19 to 74; Mage = 36.39, SDage = 10.76), each compensated $6.00. The 600 final ads were randomly divided into 10 groups of 60, and for each measure 30 raters were randomly assigned to each of the 10 groups, so every ad was rated by 30 raters for creativity and 30 separate raters for product coherence. Creativity was defined to raters as "Overall degree to which the ad is both novel and useful" and product coherence as the "Degree to which the different parts or elements of the ad work well together as a whole," both rated on a 7-point scale. For creativity, ICC(1,30) = .91 and ICC(2,30) ranged from .91 to .96; for product coherence, ICC(1,30) = .79 and ICC(2,30) ranged from .81 to .91.
- Study 2 psychological ownership: six items adapted from prior measures, for example "I feel a high degree of personal ownership over this presentation" and "This presentation belongs to me" (α = .93), rated on a 7-point Likert-type scale directly after participants submitted their final ads.
- Study 2 estimation: random-intercept models with a random intercept for yoked group, in which the 480 participants in the four yoked conditions were clustered into 120 yoked groups and the 120 participants in the separate no-handoff condition were each assigned to their own group, allowing all 600 participants to be included in the same models. The 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects in Hypotheses 2 and 3 were computed with the Monte Carlo method recommended for multilevel mediation.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (Hypothesis 1): across all models, films with directors who received late handoffs scored significantly lower in creativity than films with directors who received no (Hypothesis 1a), early (Hypothesis 1b), and serialized late (Hypothesis 1c) handoffs. Planned contrasts using Model 1 estimates placed late handoffs at M = 5.46, SE = 0.05, significantly below no handoffs (M = 5.98, SE = 0.04), p < .001, d = .38; early handoffs (M = 6.01, SE = 0.07), p < .001, d = .40; and serialized late handoffs (M = 5.71, SE = 0.06), p < .001, d = .18. In absolute differences, late handoffs scored 0.52 points below no handoffs, 0.55 points below early handoffs, and 0.25 points below serialized late handoffs. Although the effects were generally larger with fewer controls (Models 1 and 2), the results remained significant with all controls included (Model 3).
- Study 1 benchmark and caveat: the 87 films out of 5,676 nominated for the Academy Award for Best Picture scored 2.00 points higher on average than the rest of the sample (SE = 0.13, p < .001). Using that 2.00-point gap as a benchmark for a very large practical difference, the authors judge the point differences between late and the other three handoff types large enough to carry practical significance, but note that given the effect sizes the disadvantage of late handoffs is likely more meaningful relative to no and early handoffs than relative to serialized late handoffs.
- Study 2 (Hypothesis 1): the late handoff condition finished significantly lower in creativity than each of the other four conditions: no (yoked), b = .32, p = .002, d = .37; early, b = .28, p = .006, d = .32; serialized late, b = .30, p = .003, d = .34; and no (separate), b = .24, p = .02, d = .27. No other comparisons between conditions were significant. Supplementary independent ratings of participants' initial ideas and outlines suggest the conditions did not differ in creativity or coherence heading into the implementation stage.
- Study 2 (Hypothesis 2, psychological ownership): late handoff was significantly lower in psychological ownership than the other conditions, with one exception — it was numerically but not significantly lower than serialized late handoff (p = .10). The Monte Carlo indirect effects were significant for three of four conditions versus late: no (yoked), 95% CI [.04, .13]; early, 95% CI [.02, .10]; and no (separate), 95% CI [.03, .13]. The indirect effect was not significant for serialized late, 95% CI [-.007, .08]. Hypothesis 2 is therefore supported except for the serialized late versus late handoff comparison.
- Study 2 (Hypothesis 3, product coherence): late handoff was significantly lower in product coherence than each of the other conditions, and the indirect effects were significant for all four conditions versus late: no (yoked), 95% CI [.17, .42]; early, 95% CI [.14, .40]; serialized late, 95% CI [.16, .42]; and no (separate), 95% CI [.12, .42]. Hypothesis 3 is supported.
- Study 2 supplementary analysis of implementation-stage changes: while a moderate degree of change was optimal, late handoff participants tended to change the content of their emerging products too much, making more drastic changes that strayed from the core concept they were handed, whereas participants in the other four conditions tended to make refinements and enhancements to the core concept they started with. Those relatively drastic changes in turn reduced the coherence and creativity of their final products.
- Support pattern: Hypothesis 1 was tested in both studies and supported in both. Hypotheses 2 and 3 were tested only in Study 2, where Hypothesis 3 held for all four comparisons with late handoff and Hypothesis 2 held for three of the four, failing for the serialized late versus late comparison; the authors read this as a hint that product coherence may be a better explanation than psychological ownership for the advantage of serialized late over late handoffs, while noting more research is needed.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts idea implementation research from a primarily social-political focus on winning support for one's own ideas toward the production side of building handed-off ideas into tangible creative products. It extends handoff theory in creative work by distinguishing no, early, late, and serialized late handoffs according to idea maturity and prior handoff structure. It also contributes to psychological ownership research by showing how the timing and structure of creative-process handoffs can shape implementers' ownership, product coherence, and downstream creativity.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should avoid giving implementers mature creative ideas only at the implementation stage when those implementers have had no role in earlier development. When possible, implementers should enter before ideas become detailed plans, so they can develop ownership and a coherent vision. If late handoffs are necessary to use specialized skills, making implementers aware of an earlier handoff may reduce some creativity costs by framing their role as the next step in a sequence rather than as taking over a single creator's mature plan.

**Limitations**
The authors note that generalizability may be limited by the film-industry setting in Study 1 and the advertising task in Study 2, both of which involve aesthetic final products. Their framework assumes one principal individual drives each creative stage, while many real projects involve co-led stages, changing groups, repeated entries and exits, or shared ownership. Study 2 measured psychological ownership after the final ads were completed and measured product coherence from the final products, limiting direct evidence on the cognitive and motivational processes during implementation.

**Future Research**
Future research should test whether the handoff effects generalize to other creative and organizational contexts, especially settings where final products are less aesthetic. Researchers could examine shared ownership, co-led stages, varying group size and stability, repeated handoffs, and relationships between handoff givers and receivers. Additional work should directly measure ownership, coherence, and related cognitive processes during implementation and investigate when late handoffs may help because they bring in unique specialized skills.
