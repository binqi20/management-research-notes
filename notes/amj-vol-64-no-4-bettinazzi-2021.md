# Stakeholder Orientation and Divestiture Activity

**Abstract**
We conceptualize divestitures as a costly alternative to the internal resolution of conflicts among stakeholders, albeit one that avoids the more costly liquidation of the firm. In firms that have lower stakeholder orientation (defined as the extent to which management focuses attention on and integrates the interests of multiple stakeholders in its decisionmaking), divestitures will be less costly than the internal resolution of stakeholder conflicts, while the opposite will be true in firms that have higher stakeholder orientation. Consistent with this argument, we document a negative relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity, using a unique dataset of 909 U.S.-based, publicly listed firms from 2002 to 2015. This negative relationship is more pronounced for selloffs than for spinoffs, for selloffs of businesses that are unrelated to or located far from the divesting firm, and for selloffs to acquirers that are not alliance partners of divesting firms. The core contribution of this paper is to treat different types of divestitures as increasingly costly responses to conflicts among stakeholders, thereby populating the theoretical middle ground between negotiated adaptations of firms’ governance structures and total firm failure. In so doing, this paper contributes to research at the intersection of stakeholder theory and corporate strategy.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether stakeholder orientation explains firms' divestiture activity and whether this relationship varies across modes and types of divestiture. It examines whether lower stakeholder orientation makes divestitures, especially selloffs, a less costly alternative to internal governance adaptation when stakeholder conflicts arise.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. There is a negative relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity.
- Hypothesis 2. The negative relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity is stronger (more negative) for selloffs than for spinoffs.
- Hypothesis 3. The negative relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity is stronger (more negative) when the divested business is unrelated to or geographically distant from the divesting firm.
- Hypothesis 4. The negative relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity is stronger (more negative) when the divesting and acquiring firms are not alliance partners.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Stakeholder orientation, measured from Asset4 indicators of attention to employees, customers, suppliers, local communities, and shareholders.
- DV(s): Number of divestitures, with additional outcomes for spinoffs, selloffs, unrelated versus related selloffs, distant versus proximate selloffs, and selloffs to alliance partners versus nonpartners.
- Mediators: Not modeled statistically; the theorized mechanism is the relative cost of resolving stakeholder conflicts internally versus divesting businesses or liquidating the firm.
- Moderators: Not estimated as interaction terms. Divestiture mode and selloff characteristics — selloff versus spinoff, business relatedness, geographic distance, and whether the acquirer is an alliance partner — enter instead as separate count outcomes in simultaneously estimated regressions, with Wald tests comparing the stakeholder-orientation coefficients across each pair.

The mechanism is a governance-cost comparison. Firms with higher stakeholder orientation have routines, relationships, and reputations that make internal conflict resolution less costly, so they undertake fewer divestitures. Firms with lower stakeholder orientation have weaker capabilities for stakeholder engagement and compromise, making divestiture a more attractive response to unresolved stakeholder conflicts. Selloffs are theorized as much more costly than spinoffs for stakeholders of the divested business, and a selloff to a current or former alliance partner as less costly for those stakeholders than one to a nonpartner, so both patterns should concentrate among lower-stakeholder-orientation firms. For unrelated or distant businesses the argument runs through the other side of the comparison: those stakeholders' idiosyncratic, context-dependent needs complicate renegotiation, and firms with higher stakeholder orientation are better placed to resolve such conflicts internally.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: the baseline sample is the universe of firms mapped by the Thomson Reuters Asset4 database in the United States in all years in which it operated as an autonomous entity (2002-2015), giving 909 publicly traded U.S. companies and 7,143 firm-year observations; the SDC Platinum M&A database supplied all divestitures (selloffs and spinoffs) announced and completed between January 1, 2002 and December 31, 2015 by these firms (592 companies undertook 3,720 divestitures), together with the headquarters locations and industries of divesting firms, divested businesses, and acquiring firms, and data on alliances between divesting and acquiring firms; Compustat supplied firm- and segment-level data on the divesting firms. Independent variable: stakeholder orientation is built from Asset4 dummy items measuring the firm's orientation toward five stakeholder categories - employees, customers, suppliers, local communities, and shareholders (10, 6, 5, 9, and 4 items respectively, listed in Appendix A) - with each category score normalized on a 0-1 scale and the five normalized scores then averaged. Dependent variables: the number of divestitures a firm undertakes each year (Hypothesis 1); the number of spinoffs and the number of selloffs, separated using the "spinoff" flag in SDC Platinum (Hypothesis 2); the number of selloffs of unrelated versus related businesses, where related means the divested business's primary SIC code overlaps the divesting firm's by at least three digits, and the number of selloffs of distant versus proximate businesses, where proximate means the zip-code distance between the divested business and the divesting firm's headquarters is below the sample median of 562 kilometers (Hypothesis 3); and the number of selloffs to acquirers that are or were alliance partners versus acquirers that are not or were not alliance partners, measured within 15 years of any given selloff (Hypothesis 4). Controls (all independent and control variables lagged one year): firm performance (EPS), financial slack (current ratio), firm size (log of total assets), interest coverage (EBIT over interest expense), capital intensity (capital expenditures over sales), degree of diversification (number of segments), past acquisitions and past divestitures in the previous three years, number of institutional investors, percentage of independent board members, average industry profitability (average ROA in the firm's 2-digit SIC code), and average industry sales growth (2-digit SIC codes, previous three years). Estimation: Hypothesis 1 is tested with a negative binomial model with robust standard errors and both firm and year fixed effects, implemented with individual firm dummies because xtnbreg, fe has been shown to suffer from severe bias; Hypotheses 2, 3, and 4 use simultaneously estimated OLS regressions (Stata sureg) with firm fixed effects, with Wald tests of whether the stakeholder-orientation coefficients in each pair of regressions differ significantly. The design is associational rather than causally identified: the authors state that because their theoretical framework is explicitly premised on divestitures arising endogenously in firms that exhibit certain levels of stakeholder orientation, they do not seek to account for the effects of nonrandom selection in their empirical models. Robustness: results are consistent using Poisson firm fixed-effects models, OLS regressions, and logit regressions of whether a firm undertakes at least one divestiture in a given year; similar results obtain with a NAICS-based relatedness classification and with alliance windows of 20 and 10 years.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 supported: in the negative binomial model (Table 2, Model 2) the coefficient on stakeholder orientation is negative (-1.135, p = 0.002), indicating that the higher a firm's stakeholder orientation, the fewer divestitures it undertakes. Standardizing the variable, a one standard deviation increase in stakeholder orientation is associated with a 14.4% decrease in the annual intensity of divestiture.
- Hypothesis 2 supported: the relationship between stakeholder orientation and divestiture activity is significantly more negative for selloffs (-0.418, p = 0.001) than for spinoffs (0.012, p = 0.524) in Models 3 and 4 (Wald test difference = -0.426, p = 0.000).
- Hypothesis 3 supported: the relationship is more negative for selloffs of businesses that are unrelated (-0.325, p = 0.003) rather than related (-0.081, p = 0.242) to the divesting firm in Models 5 and 6 (difference = -0.244, p = 0.066), and more negative for selloffs of businesses that are distant from (-0.386, p = 0.000) rather than proximate to (-0.184, p = 0.008) the divesting firm in Models 7 and 8 (difference = -0.202, p = 0.060); the paper marks both Wald-test differences at p < 0.1.
- Hypothesis 4 supported: the relationship is significantly more negative for selloffs to acquirers that are not or were not alliance partners of the divesting firms (-0.407, p = 0.001) than to acquirers that are or were alliance partners (-0.011, p = 0.527) in Models 9 and 10 (difference = -0.396, p = 0.002).
- The authors state that they test and find support for all of their predictions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes by framing divestitures as an intermediate governance response between internal adaptation and liquidation. It extends stakeholder theory into corporate strategy by showing how stakeholder orientation can shape firm-boundary decisions, not only stakeholder treatment or performance. It also differentiates divestiture types as progressively costly responses to governance failure.

**Practical Implication**
The analysis implies that managers should treat divestiture decisions as stakeholder-governance decisions, not only portfolio or financial decisions. Firms with stronger stakeholder orientation may be better positioned to resolve conflicts internally or use less disruptive divestiture paths, such as spinoffs or sales to alliance partners. For firms considering selloffs, relatedness, geographic distance, and acquirer relationships matter because they shape the stakeholder costs of separating a business.

**Limitations**
Not reported in paper

**Future Research**
The authors suggest future studies could examine whether divestitures resolve misalignments among stakeholders in divesting firms by creating better alignment within acquiring firms. They also call for work on the interplay between governance and scope, including managerial agency, motivation, and perceptions of stakeholders in divestiture and other scope-changing transactions. They note that in-depth qualitative methods could help study these managerial perceptions and motivations.
