# Unpacking Political Ideology: CEO Social and Economic Ideologies, Strategic Decision-Making Processes, and Corporate Entrepreneurship

**Abstract**
We integrate political psychology and upper echelons research to introduce an alternative conceptualization of executive political ideology by separating the two distinct ideologies: social and economic. We theorize and test how the two ideologies exert distinct effects on a critical strategic outcome: corporate entrepreneurship. We examine this contention in Iran, a political context that sharply deviates from the exclusively studied U.S. context. We find that social and economic conservatism exert opposing effects on corporate entrepreneurship through distinct strategic decision-making processes; CEO social conservatism positively affects corporate entrepreneurship by promoting intuitive strategic decision-making, whereas CEO economic conservatism negatively affects corporate entrepreneurship by impairing cooperative strategic decisionmaking. These results highlight the need to separate social and economic ideologies, especially in non-U.S. contexts, and inform the underlying strategic decision-making processes through which executive ideology shapes strategic behaviors. The promising results also underscore the importance of examiningthestrategicimplicationsofexecutivepoliticalideologyindiversepoliticalcontexts that differ from the U.S. context.

**Research Question**
How do CEO social and economic ideologies independently shape strategic decision-making processes and corporate entrepreneurship? The paper asks whether separating these two ideological dimensions explains firm-level strategic outcomes better than the unitary liberal-conservative view developed mainly in the U.S. context.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. CEO social conservatism positively impacts CE (corporate entrepreneurship).
H2. The impact of CEO social conservatism on CE is mediated by intuitive strategic decision-making processes within the TMT.
H3. CEO economic conservatism negatively impacts CE.
H4. The impact of CEO economic conservatism on CE is mediated by cooperative strategic decision-making processes within the TMT.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO social conservatism; CEO economic conservatism.
- DV(s): Corporate entrepreneurship, measured as innovation, corporate venturing, and strategic renewal.
- Mediators: Intuitive strategic decision-making mediates the CEO social conservatism path; cooperative strategic decision-making mediates the CEO economic conservatism path.
- Moderators: No formal moderator is estimated in the main model; the theoretical context is Iran as a low ideology-constraint political system.

The mechanism is cross-level. CEO social conservatism promotes reliance on experience-based intuition in the TMT, which helps identify and implement corporate entrepreneurship opportunities under uncertainty and time pressure. CEO economic conservatism reduces cooperative strategic decision-making by emphasizing competition and individualized goals, which impairs the TMT's ability to coordinate and implement corporate entrepreneurship. The results show opposing indirect effects: social conservatism increases corporate entrepreneurship through intuition, while economic conservatism decreases it through reduced cooperation.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from three-wave, multi-informant surveys of SMEs in six Iranian provinces that varied in economic development: Bushehr, Khuzestan, Yazd, Hormozgan, Fars, and Kerman. The list of SMEs came from the government database of the Iranian Small Industries & Industrial Parks Organization and contained 566 SMEs in these provinces; the authors contacted these firms and 278 CEOs agreed to participate. CEOs identified the executives who directly report to them and with whom they work closely and regularly in making strategic decisions. Surveys were administered in three time periods: at T1 the authors collected CEO social and economic ideologies (independent variables) and controls; three months later (T2) they conducted the strategic decision-making surveys (mediators); and another six months later (T3) they collected corporate entrepreneurship data from CEOs and TMT members (dependent variable). Only SMEs returning completed responses from the CEO and at least three TMT members in all three waves were retained, giving a final sample of 192 firms from 10 industries and a 69% response rate, with an average of 4.12 TMT members per firm, representing an average of 91.1% of the total TMTs, responding. Unpaired t-tests comparing the final sample with nonresponding firms on firm age and size and on TMT age, gender, and tenure indicated no selection bias. The survey was developed through a back-translation procedure between English and Persian using two independent pairs of non-author raters, and was pilot-tested with 15 executives from Iran who were not part of the main sample. Separately, the authors conducted post-survey interviews with 15 CEOs drawn from the sample, using open-ended questions with probes, asking them to explain their strategic decision-making processes.

CEO social conservatism was measured with five items capturing beliefs and preferences for relevant traditional norms in Iran (collectivism, environmental protection, and concerns about diversity), and CEO economic conservatism with six items capturing beliefs about cooperation in society and economic inequality; both used a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). Items were compiled from previous studies and reviewed by five Iranian scholars for appropriateness to the Iranian context, avoiding issues that may be sensitive or offensive there (for example homosexuality, abortion, and military policy). A sorting exercise by 20 senior SME executives not in the main sample and five Iranian academics distinct from those who generated the items produced a substantive-validity coefficient Csv of 0.985. A separate scale-validation pilot study surveyed 178 students (75 graduates and 103 undergraduates) from a major Iranian university, spanning business (51), engineering (42), sciences (40), law (19), economics (18), and accounting (8); confirmatory factor analysis showed the 11 items loading on the two theorized factors (chi-square = 244.26, CFI = .93, RMSEA = .10, TLI = .92, SRMR = .05), with a chi-square difference test confirming superiority over the one-factor model, and reliability alphas of .92 for social conservatism and .93 for economic conservatism. Criterion-validity checks in that pilot found need for cognitive closure positively related to social conservatism (r = .51, p < .001) but not to economic conservatism, and economic individualism positively related to economic conservatism (r = .42, p < .001) but not to social conservatism. In the main sample, the CEO-reported two-factor model fit well (chi-square = 243.08, CFI = .95, RMSEA = .08, TLI = .94, SRMR = .04), with alphas of .88 for social conservatism and .91 for economic conservatism.

Intuitive strategic decision-making was measured at T2 with a four-item validated scale (Covin, Slevin, & Heeley, 2001) aggregated to the TMT level (ICC(1) = .20, ICC(2) = .49, rWG(J) = .62; alpha = .91). Cooperative strategic decision-making used a validated five-item scale (Alper, Tjosvold, & Law, 1998; Zhang, Hempel, Han, & Tjosvold, 2007) capturing the extent to which members perceived rewards or goals in their team as interdependent, also aggregated to the TMT level (ICC(1) = .23, ICC(2) = .55, rWG(J) = .69; alpha = .94). At T3, CEOs and TMTs reported corporate entrepreneurship through a widely used and validated 15-item formative scale with three underlying dimensions (innovation, corporate venturing, and strategic renewal; Ling et al., 2008), modeled as a second-order factor subsuming the three first-order factors (chi-square = 102.58, CFI = .995, TLI = .994, RMSEA = .028), with within-TMT agreement of ICC(1) = .20, ICC(2) = .51, rWG(J) = .87; the z-scores of the three factors were averaged to derive corporate entrepreneurship (alpha = .91). Controls collected at T1 were firm age, firm size (number of employees), R&D expenses as a percentage of total firm expenses (coded 1 = 0-20% to 5 = 80-100%), sales compared with the prior year (1 = lower, 2 = equal, 3 = higher), slack resources (four-item measure of Ling et al., 2008, averaged across TMT members; alpha = .83; ICC(1) = .30, ICC(2) = .63, rWG(J) = .84), dummy variables for service, manufacturing, and regulated industries, TMT tenure diversity (coefficient of variation), TMT functional background and ethnicity diversity (Blau's index), TMT size, and dummy variables for CEO education (1 for graduate degrees) and CEO tenure (1 if longer than five years).

Hypotheses 1 and 3 were tested with ordinary least squares (OLS), and Hypotheses 2 and 4 with bootstrapping procedures using Hayes's (2013) PROCESS macro (model 4), which estimates indirect effects with 95% confidence intervals; the authors ran 5,000, 10,000, and 20,000 bootstrap samples with consistent results and report the results based on 20,000 samples. All six OLS models in Table 3 are estimated on 192 observations, and variance inflation factors ranged from 1.79 to 1.86. The design is associational, with all constructs measured by survey rather than manipulated, although the authors state that the temporal separations and multiple key informants help alleviate common method bias and strengthen the inference of directionality. Robustness to endogeneity was assessed with an impact threshold for a confounding variable (ITCV) analysis and with two-stage least squares regressions instrumenting four key variables: CEO social and economic conservatism (instruments based on CEO ethnic background and affiliations) and intuitive and cooperative strategic decision-making (instruments based on conflicts within the TMT, proactiveness, and team identification).

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 was supported: CEO social conservatism was positively and significantly related to corporate entrepreneurship in the OLS models (Model 1: b = 0.12, p < .001; Model 3, which enters both ideologies: b = 0.08, p = .04). For Hypothesis 3, the CEO economic conservatism coefficient was negative and significant in Model 2 (b = -0.10, p < .001) and negative but only marginally significant in Model 3 (b = -0.06, p = .09), which the authors report as consistent with Hypothesis 3. Overall, the two ideologies show opposing associations with corporate entrepreneurship.

The mediator paths were construct-specific in the theorized direction. CEO social conservatism was positively related to intuitive strategic decision-making (Model 4: b = 0.59, p < .001) but not to cooperative strategic decision-making (Model 5: b = 0.10, p = .27), whereas CEO economic conservatism was negatively related to cooperative strategic decision-making (Model 5: b = -0.55, p < .001) but not to intuitive strategic decision-making (Model 4: b = -0.08, p = .40). Statistical comparisons showed that the social and economic conservatism coefficients in Models 4 and 5 differ significantly on intuitive decision-making (p < .001) and on cooperative decision-making (p < .001). With both mediators added (Model 6), intuitive strategic decision-making (b = 0.07, p = .01) and cooperative strategic decision-making (b = 0.20, p < .001) were significant, while the direct effects of CEO social conservatism (b = 0.02, p = .62) and CEO economic conservatism (b = 0.05, p = .16) were not.

The bootstrapping tests reported in Table 4 supported both mediation hypotheses. Hypothesis 2 was supported: CEO social conservatism exerted a significant positive indirect effect on corporate entrepreneurship via intuitive strategic decision-making (b = 0.04, 95% CI [0.01, 0.09]), with a nonsignificant direct effect (b = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.10]), which the authors read as full mediation. Hypothesis 4 was supported: CEO economic conservatism exerted a significant negative indirect effect via cooperative strategic decision-making (b = -0.11, 95% CI [-0.16, -0.07]), again with a nonsignificant direct effect (b = 0.05, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.12]) suggesting full mediation. The crossed indirect paths were not significant: social conservatism via cooperative decision-making (b = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.06]) and economic conservatism via intuitive decision-making (b = -0.01, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.01]). Total indirect effects were 0.06 (95% CI [0.02, 0.12]) for social conservatism and -0.11 (95% CI [-0.17, -0.07]) for economic conservatism. The authors also tested whether CEO social and economic conservatism interacted on intuitive strategic decision-making, cooperative decision-making, and corporate entrepreneurship, and did not find any significant results.

The robustness checks did not overturn these results. In Table 5, the impact values of the control variables were substantially lower than the ITCV across Models 1-4; in Model 1, for example, the largest control impact was CEO tenure at 0.0259 against an ITCV of 0.3794, leading the authors to conclude that the significant OLS results are unlikely to be overturned by an omitted confounding variable. The two-stage least squares analyses generated consistent support for the hypothesized relationships.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances executive political ideology research by replacing a unitary conservatism-liberalism spectrum with a two-dimensional account of social and economic ideology. This matters especially outside high ideology-constraint contexts such as the United States, because social and economic conservatism may be distinct or negatively correlated.

It also contributes to upper echelons theory by specifying strategic decision-making processes as mechanisms linking CEO ideology to firm outcomes. For corporate entrepreneurship research, it highlights the potential value of intuitive decision-making under uncertainty and connects corporate entrepreneurship to behavioral strategy.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that CEOs should recognize how their ideological tendencies shape TMT decision processes. Socially liberal CEOs may need to deliberately create room for experience-based intuition when speed and uncertainty are central to corporate entrepreneurship.

Economically conservative CEOs may need to compensate for competitive and individualistic tendencies by designing shared goals and rewards that support cooperative strategic decision-making. This is especially relevant when corporate entrepreneurship requires coordination across units.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the study is conducted in Iran to address the prior U.S. focus, but it does not directly compare countries with different degrees of ideology constraint. Therefore, the cross-national scope of the two-dimensional ideology argument remains to be tested more directly.

The paper also does not explain why TMT members follow CEOs' ideological tendencies in strategic decision-making. In addition, the ideology scales were adapted to avoid controversial issues that are illegal or socially unacceptable in some contexts, which improves contextual fit but may limit comparability.

**Future Research**
Future research could compare the strategic implications of social and economic ideology across countries with different ideology constraints, including single-party contexts such as China. It could also examine whether the two ideology dimensions explain other outcomes such as organizational culture, citizenship behavior, turnover intentions, strategic flexibility, diversification, and mergers and acquisitions.

The authors call for more work on the black box of upper echelons theory, including the motivations that lead TMT members to behave consistently with CEO tendencies. Future studies could also examine how combinations of social and economic ideologies within TMTs create different group dynamics and strategic outcomes.
