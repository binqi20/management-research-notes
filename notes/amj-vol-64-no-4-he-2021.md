# Covering in Cover Letters: Gender and Self-Presentation in Job Applications

**Abstract**
Despite decades of research and intervention efforts, gender-based occupational segregation remains a significant problem. An emerging body of research has suggested that one way women overcome gender discrimination when applying for male-dominated jobs is by deliberately managing gender impressions. However, social role theory and research on prescriptive stereotypes have suggestedthat these attemptsto manage gender may backfire. In this research, we theorize that, while women actively respond to anticipated sexism using social identity–based impression-management (SIM) strategies (e.g., attempting to appearlessfeminineincoverletters),theseactionscanactuallybackfirebecause theyclash with prescriptive gender stereotypes. Across three studies, we investigate the motivations, techniques, andoutcomesofmanaging genderinjob applicationsfordifferentkindsofjobs. We find that women, but not men, manage gender when applying for gender-incongruent (i.e., male-dominated) jobs by using less feminine language, and that, paradoxically, they are less likely to be hired when they do so. The current research contributes to our understanding of the consequences of SIM strategies and shows that women’s coping behaviorinresponsetoexisting genderinequalitiesinthe labormarket isa novel and ironic mechanism through which occupational gender-segregation is perpetuated.

**Research Question**
How do job seekers manage gender impressions when applying for gender-incongruent jobs, and what consequences do these strategies have for hiring outcomes? The paper specifically asks whether women applying for male-dominated jobs cover by reducing feminine language, whether anticipated gender discrimination motivates this behavior, and whether evaluators penalize it through lower liking and hireability.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Gender moderates the effect of job context on covering: when applying for gender-incongruent jobs, women (but not men) will engage in covering, that is, they will reduce feminine cues in their applications.
H2. The interaction between job context and gender has an indirect effect on covering through anticipated gender discrimination: when applying for gender-incongruent jobs, women (but not men) will anticipate more gender discrimination, and consequently women (but not men) will cover by reducing feminine cues in their applications.
H3. Applicant gender moderates the effect of covering on job outcomes: female (but not male) applicants who cover by downplaying femininity will experience less favorable hiring outcomes for a job of any type.
H4. The interaction between covering and applicant gender has an indirect effect on job outcomes via likeability: female (but not male) applicants who cover by downplaying femininity will be viewed as less likeable, and in turn all applicants who are viewed as less likeable will experience less favorable hiring outcomes for a job of any type.
H5. The interaction between job context and gender has an indirect effect on hiring outcomes through covering: when applying for gender-incongruent jobs, women (but not men) cover, which leads to less favorable hiring outcomes.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Applicant gender, job gender context, and applicant covering through reduced feminine language in cover letters or self-descriptions.
- DV(s): Job attainment, MBA callback, evaluator-rated hireability, and related evaluations of likeability and competence.
- Mediators: Anticipated gender discrimination links gender-incongruent job context to covering; likeability links reduced feminine language to lower hireability.
- Moderators: Applicant gender and job gender congruence; the theorized backlash is strongest for women who reduce feminine language.

The mechanism begins with devaluation threat: women applying to male-dominated jobs anticipate gender discrimination and attempt to signal fit by downplaying feminine cues. This social identity-based impression-management strategy conflicts with prescriptive gender stereotypes, so evaluators interpret women who use less feminine language as less likeable and less hireable. The result is an ironic reinforcing cycle in which women respond to anticipated discrimination in a way that can reduce their chances of entering male-dominated jobs.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (real job applications; cross-sectional and correlational): 306 participants were recruited on Amazon's Mechanical Turk, and after excluding incomplete responses the final sample was 257 (54% women, n = 139; average age 34, SD = 9). Participants pasted a deidentified resume and cover letter they had used in the past year, identified the job title, expected salary, and educational requirements, and reported whether they obtained the job. Covering was measured with Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) using an uploaded dictionary of published agentic and communal word lists plus masculine and feminine trait words, producing the percentage of cover-letter text that was feminine or masculine. Job type was the percentage of women in the position from U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2017) data, subtracted from 100 so that higher values indicate higher male representation. The outcome, job attainment, was self-reported and coded 0 = unsuccessful, 1 = successful. Controls were age, education, self-reported gender identity (four-item scale, Schmader 2002), expected salary, required education, cover-letter word count, and words per sentence. Estimation used hierarchical moderated regression, binary logistic regression, and bootstrapped moderated mediation (PROCESS v3.1 Model 58, 5,000 bootstraps). Table 1 reports n = 257 except for percentage of men in positions (n = 250) and education (n = 255); the Table 2 regressions report n = 250 with seven missing cases.
- Study 2 (MBA admissions records; correlational): admissions data came from an MBA program at a large public university in Canada and comprised 1,215 applications, 30% of them from women, with no other demographic data available. Applicants submitted 3-5 words describing themselves, and admissions officers collected 3-5 descriptive words about each applicant from the single referee the applicant nominated. LIWC generated counts of feminine and masculine words in both the self-descriptions and the referee descriptions, with total word counts controlled; referee descriptions were treated as the more objective "true" impression of the applicant, so a lower degree of applicant-referee overlap was interpreted as gender management. The outcome, callback, indicated whether the applicant was invited back for an in-person interview and was coded 0 = no callback, 1 = callback. Controls were GPA and GMAT or GRE scores, with GRE scores converted to GMAT equivalents through the ETS website; because some GPA and test-score data were missing, complete-case models with controls are reported alongside a robustness check on the full sample without controls. Estimation used binary logistic regression. A footnote reports n = 1,173 for all analyses using referee description words, and the regression tables report N = 1,173 for the feminine-words models, N = 1,213 for the callback models, and N = 607 and N = 621 for the models that add GPA and GMAT.
- Study 3A (randomized experiment, applicant side): 463 undergraduates at a large Canadian university participated for course credit in a study titled "Job Application Workshop"; 61 who did not submit a cover letter or resume or did not perform the central revision task were excluded, leaving a final sample of 402 (52% women, n = 208; average age 20, SD = 1.96; 40.6% East and South East Asian, 17% South Asian, 16% Caucasian). Participants pasted their current resume and cover letter (Time 1), were randomly shown one of three job postings, and then spent 20 minutes revising their materials in response (Time 2). The postings were an administrative assistant position (female-dominated condition; BLS 2016 statistics report 95% of administrative assistants were women), an information technology assistant position (male-dominated condition; 22% of technology support assistants were women), and a sales assistant position (gender-neutral condition; 49% of such positions were held by women). Job condition was coded relative to participant gender as gender-congruent, gender-incongruent, or gender-neutral, with the gender-congruent job as the referent. Covering was LIWC-measured feminine and masculine language at Time 1 and Time 2. The mediator, anticipated gender discrimination, was a newly developed and validated five-item measure ("I would be overlooked for the job", "I would be seen as unqualified for the job", and three similar items) rated from 1 (very unlikely) to 7 (very likely). Estimation used ANCOVA on Time 2 language controlling for Time 1 language, age, ethnicity, gender identity, and interest in the job, plus bootstrapped moderated mediation with multicategorical predictors (PROCESS v3.1 Model 58, 5,000 bootstraps). The manipulation check indicated that the female-dominated and male-dominated conditions worked well but that the sales assistant position was viewed as female-dominated.
- Study 3B (yoked randomized experiment, evaluator side): 427 participants were recruited through Prolific, an online survey panel located in the United Kingdom, and selected for hiring experience; 67 were excluded (64 with no hiring experience, two who completed no measures, one who did not finish), leaving a final sample of 360 (51% male, n = 184; 48% female, n = 174; 1% other, n = 2; 85% Caucasian; average age 40, SD = 12; an average of seven years of hiring experience, range six months to 40 years). Evaluators imagined being the hiring manager for a fictional corporation, were randomly shown one of the same three job postings, and then rated four real cover letters written by Study 3A participants in a fully crossed 2 (applicant gender: male vs. female) by 2 (feminine language: more vs. less) within-subject design. Cover letters one standard deviation above and below the average for feminine language usage were selected for each applicant gender and job condition, yielding 12 cover-letter manipulations with two to five letters each; letters were proofread, stripped of identifiable information, and randomly assigned one of four gendered names ("Emily", "Jessica", "Christopher", or "Michael"). Likeability (three items), competence (two items), and hireability (three items) were rated on 7-point Likert scales. Estimation used within-person ANOVA and within-person multilevel (1-1-1) moderated mediation in MLMed, with standard errors clustered at the person level.
- Identification: Studies 1 and 2 are described by the authors as cross-sectional and correlational, so their estimates are associational; Studies 3A and 3B are randomized experiments in a yoked design, in which the cover letters produced by Study 3A applicants become the stimuli rated by the Study 3B evaluators.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 257; regressions n = 250): H1 supported. The gender by job type interaction was significant for feminine language (b = -0.015, SE = 0.006, p = 0.008) but not for masculine language (b = 0.007, SE = 0.004, p = 0.15); the simple slope was negative and significant for women (b = -0.009, SE = 0.004, p = 0.03) and nonsignificant for men (b = 0.006, SE = 0.004, p = 0.13), indicating that women use less feminine language when applying for male-dominated positions. H3 supported. The three-way gender by feminine language by job type interaction on job attainment was not significant (b = -0.01, SE = 0.01, p = 0.31), but the gender by feminine language interaction was (b = 0.69, SE = 0.27, p = 0.01, Exp(B) = 2.0): feminine language was positively related to job attainment for women (b = 0.38, SE = 0.17, p = 0.03, Exp(B) = 1.45) and negatively but nonsignificantly related for men (b = -0.32, SE = 0.21, p = 0.13), so minimizing feminine language produced backlash for women regardless of job context. H5 supported. The indirect effect of job context on job attainment via covering was significant and negative for women (indirect effect = -0.004, bootstrapped SE = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.01, -0.0001]) and nonsignificant for men (indirect effect = -0.002, bootstrapped SE = 0.002, 95% CI [-0.0059, 0.0013]); a footnote reports that the overall index of moderated mediation was not significant (index = -0.002, SE = 0.003, 95% CI [-0.009, 0.003]).
- Study 2 (1,215 applications): results consistent with H1 and supporting H3. Applicant gender significantly moderated the relationship between referee and applicant use of feminine language (b = -0.11, SE = 0.05, p = 0.03); the referee-applicant association was positive and significant for male applicants (b = 0.11, SE = 0.03, p < 0.001) but nonsignificant for female applicants (b = 0.001, SE = 0.04, p = 0.99). At lower levels of referee-rated femininity, female applicants used more feminine language than male applicants (b = 0.13, SE = 0.05, p = 0.02); at higher levels the paper reports the pattern reversed, with women using fewer feminine self-descriptors than men rated equally feminine, but only when the significance level was relaxed to 0.10 (b = 0.29, SE = 0.16, p = 0.06). Applicant and referee use of masculine language did not significantly differ by applicant gender (b = -0.08, SE = 0.05, p = 0.10), while referee masculine language was significantly associated with applicant masculine language (b = 0.09, SE = 0.03, p = 0.001). For callbacks, the applicant gender by feminine words interaction was significant (b = 0.92, SE = 0.32, p = 0.004, Exp(B) = 2.52): feminine word use had a significant positive effect on callback likelihood for women (b = 0.54, SE = 0.25, p = 0.034, Exp(B) = 1.69) and a negative effect for men (b = -0.39, SE = 0.19, p = 0.040, Exp(B) = 0.67), so women who used fewer feminine words were less likely to be invited to interview. The masculine words by gender interaction on callback was not significant (b = 0.30, SE = 0.24, p = 0.24).
- Study 3A (n = 402): the covering effect held within women, but the omnibus interaction was weak. There was no main effect of gender (F(1, 391) = 0.20, p = 0.66) or job condition (F(2, 391) = 1.90, p = 0.15) on feminine language, and the interaction between gender and the gender-incongruent job condition was significant for feminine language only when the significance level was relaxed to 0.10 (F(2, 391) = 2.84, p = 0.06, ηp² = 0.014). The effect of job condition on feminine language was significant for women (F(2, 391) = 4.63, p = 0.010, ηp² = 0.023) but not men (F(2, 391) = 0.048, p = 0.95): women in the gender-incongruent condition used less feminine language (M = 1.31, SE = 0.07) than women in the gender-congruent (M = 1.56, SE = 0.08, p = 0.02) and gender-neutral (M = 1.58, SE = 0.07, p = 0.005) conditions. The results section labels this passage as supporting Hypothesis 3, while the Study 3A discussion attributes the same result to Hypothesis 1. Masculine language showed no main effects and no interaction (interaction F(2, 391) = 0.51, p = 0.60). H2 supported: women anticipated more gender discrimination than men (F(1, 391) = 10.52, p = 0.001; M = 3.61 vs. M = 3.18), the gender by job condition interaction on anticipated discrimination was significant (F(2, 391) = 3.87, p = 0.02), and the condition effect held for women (F(2, 391) = 9.93, p < 0.001) but not men (F(2, 391) = 0.25, p = 0.78), with women in the gender-incongruent condition anticipating more discrimination (M = 4.13, SE = 0.15) than women in the gender-congruent (M = 3.47, SE = 0.16, p = 0.003) or gender-neutral (M = 3.22, SE = 0.14, p < 0.001) conditions. The indirect effect of the gender-incongruent condition on women's use of feminine language via anticipated gender discrimination was significant and negative (indirect effect = -0.05, bootstrapped SE = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.11, -0.01]) and nonsignificant for men (indirect effect = 0.002, bootstrapped SE = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.03]); the overall moderated mediation index was significant (b = -0.05, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [-0.12, -0.003]).
- Study 3B (n = 360): H3 and H4 supported, with a qualification for male applicants. Cover letters with more feminine language were rated more hireable (M = 4.49, SE = 0.07) than those with less feminine language (M = 4.19, SE = 0.08; F(1, 359) = 12.81, p < 0.001), with no main effect of applicant gender (F(1, 359) = 1.48, p = 0.22), and the applicant gender by feminine language interaction was significant (F(1, 359) = 4.63, p = 0.03): women with less feminine language were rated significantly less hireable (M = 4.15, SE = 0.09) than women with more feminine language (M = 4.63, SE = 0.09; F(1, 359) = 17.24, p < 0.001), whereas male applicants did not differ (F(1, 359) = 1.03, p = 0.31). The three-way interaction adding job condition was not significant (F(2, 357) = 2.09, p = 0.12) and the two-way interaction remained significant controlling for it (F(1, 357) = 5.00, p = 0.03), which the authors read as support for Hypothesis 5 regarding the persistence of prescriptive gender norms across job contexts. For likeability, female applicants were rated more likable overall (M = 4.72, SE = 0.06) than male applicants (M = 4.29, SE = 0.06; F(1, 359) = 41.40, p < 0.001), letters with more feminine language were rated more likable than those with less (M = 4.70, SE = 0.06 vs. M = 4.31, SE = 0.06; F(1, 359) = 36.0, p < 0.001), and the gender by covering interaction was significant (F(1, 359) = 6.41, p = 0.01): female applicants with less feminine language were rated significantly less likable (M = 4.45, SE = 0.07) than those with more (M = 5.00, SE = 0.07; F(1, 359) = 41.15, p < 0.001), and male applicants with less feminine language were also rated significantly less likable (M = 4.18, SE = 0.08) than those with more (M = 4.41, SE = 0.08; F(1, 359) = 5.45, p = 0.02). For competence, letters with more feminine language were rated more competent (M = 4.80, SE = 0.06) than those with less (M = 4.64, SE = 0.06; F(1, 359) = 4.66, p = 0.03), and the gender by covering interaction was significant (F(1, 359) = 9.49, p = 0.002): female applicants who used less feminine language were rated less competent (M = 4.52, SE = 0.08) than those who used more (M = 4.90, SE = 0.08; F(1, 359) = 13.53, p < 0.001), with no difference for male applicants (F(1, 359) = 0.34, p = 0.56); the authors note these findings contradict the alternative hypothesis that women who cover are perceived as more competent and subsequently more hireable. Mediation supported H4 for women: likeability significantly mediated the relationship between feminine language use and hireability for female applicants (indirect effect = -0.54, SE = 0.09, p < 0.001, 95% CI [-0.70, -0.37]), with a significant moderated mediation index (-0.29, 95% CI [-0.53, -0.06]). Against H4's female-but-not-male prediction, likeability also significantly mediated this relationship for men, though to a lesser extent (indirect effect = -0.24, SE = 0.08, p = 0.004, 95% CI [-0.41, -0.08]).
- Overall pattern and its limits: the authors conclude that gender-management strategies were predominantly employed by women applying for male-dominated jobs, that these women most commonly minimized feminine language in their cover letters (Studies 1, 2, and 3) in response to anticipated gender discrimination (Study 3A), and that women who used such tactics were ultimately less likely to be hired (Studies 1, 2, and 3), a backlash effect driven by perceptions of likeability (Study 3B). The support pattern is not uniform: the covering test reached conventional significance in Study 1 (gender by job type on feminine language, p = 0.008) and Study 2 (gender moderation of the referee-applicant feminine language link, p = 0.03), whereas Study 3A's omnibus gender by job condition interaction and Study 2's high-femininity contrast each reached significance only at the relaxed 0.10 threshold (both p = 0.06).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends Roberts's professional image construction model by showing that social identity-based impression-management can backfire when the identity being managed is governed by strong prescriptive stereotypes. It contributes to social role theory by specifying a context in which women adopt counterstereotypic self-presentation to signal job fit but are penalized for violating gender prescriptions. It also offers a supply-side and demand-side mechanism for occupational gender segregation: women's coping behavior and evaluators' prescriptive expectations interact to perpetuate underrepresentation.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue against placing responsibility on women to navigate biased hiring systems through careful self-presentation. Organizations should change hiring structures and signals, including anonymized recruitment, changes in how applicants are presented to hiring managers, group-based hiring processes, and job postings that genuinely value historically devalued traits. Employers also need to follow through on such signals by valuing candidates who possess more feminine traits rather than treating inclusive language as symbolic.

**Limitations**
The paper reports that it cannot determine whether the observed gender-management strategies apply across job levels or mainly to relatively entry-level jobs. It also did not test how gendered wording in job advertisements shapes applicant self-presentation, because the experimental postings were tightly controlled. The authors further note that they measured devaluation threat through anticipated gender discrimination but did not run competing tests against legitimacy threat.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine whether women use similar strategies when applying for more senior jobs and whether the same backlash persists at later career stages. The authors call for work on how gendered job-advertisement language affects applicants' gendered language in application materials, and on the relative roles of devaluation and legitimacy threat. They also suggest studying men's gender-management strategies, noncisgender applicants' experiences, and intersections of gender with age, social class, and race.
