# Seeing the Glass as Half Full or Half Empty: The Role of Affect-Induced Optimistic and Pessimistic States on Justice Perceptions and Outcomes

**Abstract**
In this paper, we develop and test theory to explain how employees’ perceptions of supervisor justice behavior are subjectively influenced by optimistic and pessimistic states. We propose that state affect gives rise to optimistic and pessimistic states, which color justice perceptions and impact performance behaviors (i.e., task performance, citizenship behavior, counterproductive behavior). Results from an experience sampling study and a set of experimental studies showed that state positive affect fosters an optimistic state that promotes perceptions of justice rule adherence, which influences task performance and citizenship behavior, whereas state negative affect promotes a pessimistic state that promotes perceptions of justice rule violation, which influences counterproductive behavior. Interestingly, state affect does not have a direct relationship with justice perceptions, which points to a new perspective on affect and justice.

**Research Question**
How do employees' affect-induced optimistic and pessimistic states shape their perceptions that supervisors adhere to or violate justice rules, and how do those perceptions influence performance behaviors? The paper asks what cognitive lenses explain the subjective "eye of the beholder" in justice perceptions beyond supervisor behavior itself.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: State positive affect is positively related to an optimistic state.
- H2: State negative affect is positively related to a pessimistic state.
- H3: An optimistic state is positively related to justice rule adherence perceptions.
- H4: A pessimistic state is positively related to justice rule violation perceptions.
- H5: Perceptions of justice rule adherence are positively related to (a) task performance and (b) citizenship behavior, and negatively related to (c) counterproductive behavior.
- H6: Perceptions of justice rule violation are negatively related to (a) task performance and (b) citizenship behavior, and positively related to (c) counterproductive behavior.
- H7: State positive affect has a positive indirect effect on (a) task performance and (b) citizenship behavior and a negative indirect effect on (c) counterproductive behavior, partially mediated by an optimistic state and perceptions of justice rule adherence.
- H8: State negative affect has a negative indirect effect on (a) task performance and (b) citizenship behavior and a positive indirect effect on (c) counterproductive behavior, partially mediated by a pessimistic state and perceptions of justice rule violation.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): State positive affect and state negative affect; in the experiments, manipulated state affect, optimistic/pessimistic states, and justice rule adherence or violation.
- DV(s): Justice rule adherence perceptions, justice rule violation perceptions, task performance, citizenship behavior, and counterproductive behavior.
- Mediators: Optimistic state mediates the effect of state positive affect on justice rule adherence; pessimistic state mediates the effect of state negative affect on justice rule violation; justice perceptions then mediate performance outcomes.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

The mechanism is that momentary affect produces valence-congruent optimistic or pessimistic states, which act as perceptual lenses for interpreting supervisor behavior. Optimistic states make employees more likely to see supervisor behavior as adhering to justice rules, supporting task performance and citizenship behavior. Pessimistic states make employees more likely to see supervisor behavior as violating justice rules, supporting counterproductive behavior. The ESM results and causal-chain experiments indicate that affect operates through these states rather than directly overriding justice perceptions.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 was a 10-workday experience sampling (ESM) study. Participants came from a university alumni panel and online ads and had to be employed full-time, report to a supervisor, and supply a coworker who reported to the same supervisor. Of 197 eligible focal employees and their coworkers, 126 pairs completed the registration survey; after dropping non-participants, dyads missing independent or mediating variable data, and dyads whose focal employee did not complete all daily surveys on at least three days, the final sample was 101 focal employee-coworker dyads with 873 usable observations (86.4% retention; 8.64 observations per focal employee on average; Level 1 n = 873, Level 2 n = 101). Employees completed three surveys each workday - state affect at 7:30 a.m., optimistic and pessimistic states at 11:30 a.m., and justice rule adherence and violation at 4:00 p.m. - while coworkers rated the focal employee's job performance in a 4:00 p.m. survey. Measures: state positive affect and state negative affect used the 10 positive and 10 negative PANAS items (Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988) rated at that moment on 5-point scales (α = .97 and .91); optimistic state and pessimistic state used new 3-item measures developed and validity-tested for this study ("I have seen the good side of things"; "I have seen the bad side of things"; α = .94 each); justice rule adherence used 12 items from Colquitt (2001), three per justice dimension (α = .93); justice rule violation used 12 injustice items from Colquitt et al. (2015), three per dimension (α = .95); task performance used Griffin, Neal, and Parker's (2007) 3-item coworker-rated measure (α = .91); citizenship behavior and counterproductive behavior used shortened four-item coworker-rated versions of Dalal et al.'s (2009) measures (α = .90 and .96). Controls were hours slept, anticipated hindrance stressors (Rodell & Judge, 2009; α = .92), coworker-rated supervisor justice rule adherence (α = .95) and violation (α = .97), previous-day values of each endogenous construct, and day of the week plus its sine and cosine. Estimation used multilevel path analysis with maximum likelihood in Mplus 8, group-mean-centered Level 1 predictors, and parametric bootstrapping with 20,000 resamples for 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals around the indirect and serial indirect effects; the ESM design is associational, with lagged prior-day controls included to better establish the presumed causal ordering.

Studies 2A-2C were separate MTurk experiments with full-time employed adults based in the United States, using a causal chain design (Spencer, Zanna, & Fong, 2005) in which each link of the model was manipulated in its own non-overlapping sample. Study 2A: 500 participants completed a Time 1 personality and demographics survey and 351 completed the Time 2 experimental survey three days later (70.2% response rate); 78 were removed for failing attention checks, leaving 273. Participants were randomly assigned to a state positive, negative, or neutral affect condition induced by a recall writing task about the most positive, negative, or middle-of-the-road morning experienced in the past month; optimistic and pessimistic states were measured with the Study 1 3-item scales (α = .97 and .95), and manipulation checks used the Study 1 PANAS scales (positive α = .94, negative α = .92) plus a 6-item ad hoc neutral affect measure (α = .93). Study 2B: 201 participants completed the study and 2 were removed for failing attention checks, leaving 199. Participants were randomly assigned to an optimistic or pessimistic state condition through a recall writing task about a time in the past month when they were very optimistic or pessimistic, then read a common vignette describing a hypothetical supervisor's behavior and rated justice rule adherence (α = .88) and violation (α = .96) with the Study 1 measures; scenario realism was checked with Chen et al.'s (2011) 3-item measure (α = .83; overall scale mean 4.26 out of 5.00). Study 2C: 186 participants completed the study and 2 were removed, leaving 184. Participants were randomly assigned to a justice rule adherence or justice rule violation vignette and then rated their intentions to engage in task performance (α = .94), citizenship behavior (α = .96), and counterproductive behavior (α = .91) using the Study 1 measures, so the experimental outcome is behavioral intention rather than observed behavior. All three experiments were analyzed with ANOVA.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (873 observations from 101 dyads): H1 supported, state positive affect was positively related to optimistic state (γ = .18, p < .01); H2 supported, state negative affect was positively related to pessimistic state (γ = .32, p < .01); H3 supported, optimistic state was positively related to justice rule adherence perceptions (γ = .12, p < .01); H4 supported, pessimistic state was positively related to justice rule violation perceptions (γ = .09, p < .01). State positive affect explained 12.4% of within-individual variance in optimistic state and state negative affect explained 5.4% in pessimistic state; optimistic state explained 11.4% of within-individual variance in justice rule adherence perceptions and pessimistic state explained 12.3% in justice rule violation perceptions.
- Study 1, H5 partially supported: justice rule adherence perceptions were positively related to (a) task performance (γ = .12, p < .05) and (b) citizenship behavior (γ = .11, p < .05), but were not significantly related to (c) counterproductive behavior (γ = -.05, n.s.), so H5c was not supported.
- Study 1, H6 partially supported: justice rule violation perceptions were positively related to (c) counterproductive behavior (γ = .33, p < .01), but were not significantly related to (a) task performance (γ = -.03, n.s.) or (b) citizenship behavior (γ = .01, n.s.), so H6a and H6b were not supported. The authors read this asymmetry as justice rule adherence predicting only productive, functional behaviors and justice rule violation predicting only dysfunctional behavior.
- Study 1, H7: the indirect effect of state positive affect on justice rule adherence perceptions through optimistic state was positive and significant (.022, 95% CI = .010, .043), and the serial indirect effects on task performance (.003, 95% CI = .001, .007) and citizenship behavior (.002, 95% CI = .001, .007) were also positive and significant; because the total effects were not significant (.005 and .051, respectively), the paper reports partial support for H7a and H7b. The serial indirect effect on counterproductive behavior was not significant (-.001, 95% CI = -.005, .002), so H7c was not supported.
- Study 1, H8: the indirect effect of state negative affect on justice rule violation perceptions through pessimistic state was positive and significant (.029, 95% CI = .006, .065). The serial indirect effects on task performance (-.001, 95% CI = -.005, .001) and citizenship behavior (.000, 95% CI = -.002, .004) were not significant, so H8a and H8b were not supported. The serial indirect effect on counterproductive behavior was positive and significant (.010, 95% CI = .002, .026), but because the total effect was not significant (.045) the paper reports partial support for H8c.
- Study 1, total effects: the total effect of state positive affect on justice rule adherence was -.02 and the total effect of state negative affect on justice rule violation was .05; the paper describes these total effects of state affect on justice perceptions as nonsignificant, which is the basis for its claim that affect does not directly influence justice perceptions.
- Study 2A (n = 273) supports H1 and H2: optimistic state ratings were higher in the state positive affect condition than in the negative and neutral conditions (F = 12.85, p < .001), and pessimistic state ratings were higher in the state negative affect condition than in the positive and neutral conditions (F = 10.89, p < .001).
- Study 2B (n = 199) supports H3 and H4: justice rule adherence perceptions were higher in the optimistic state condition (F = 4.25, M = 4.33 vs. 4.16, p < .05) and justice rule violation perceptions were higher in the pessimistic state condition (F = 4.65, M = 1.86 vs. 1.56, p < .01).
- Study 2C (n = 184) supports H5a-c and H6a-c: task performance intentions (F = 17.94, M = 4.58 vs. 4.20, p < .001) and citizenship behavior intentions (F = 237.26, M = 4.13 vs. 2.32, p < .001) were higher in the justice rule adherence condition than in the justice rule violation condition, and counterproductive behavior intentions were higher in the justice rule violation condition than in the justice rule adherence condition (F = 216.79, p < .001). The support pattern therefore differs from Study 1, where H5c, H6a, and H6b were not supported.
- The paper concludes that, taken together, the results of Studies 2A-C support the predictions in H7a-c and H8a-c that state affect influences performance behaviors through optimistic and pessimistic states and justice perceptions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a more precise account of the "eye of the beholder" in organizational justice by specifying optimistic and pessimistic states as the cognitive lenses through which employees interpret supervisor behavior. It extends Carver and Scheier's optimism-pessimism framework into workplace justice research and conceptualizes optimism and pessimism as dynamic states rather than only stable traits. It also shows that justice rule adherence and violation have differentiated behavioral consequences: adherence predicts productive behaviors, whereas violation predicts counterproductive behavior.

**Practical Implication**
Supervisors should not assume that adhering to justice rules is sufficient for employees to perceive them as just, because employees' current affective states can color their interpretations. The findings imply that managers should attend to work conditions that foster positive affect and reduce negative affect, since these states can indirectly shape justice perceptions and performance behaviors. Supervisors may also need to monitor whether their own view of justice-relevant behavior diverges from employees' perceptions.

**Limitations**
The ESM study relies on self-rated independent and mediating variables, so common-method variance remains a concern despite temporal separation and group-mean centering. Study 1 uses coworker-rated performance rather than supervisor-rated performance, and the experiments measure behavioral intentions rather than actual workplace behavior. The paper also focuses on overall justice perceptions, leaving open whether the same affect-induced lens operates similarly across specific justice dimensions and longer-term entity justice perceptions.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether discrete emotions, nonwork recovery, or other perceiver factors produce optimistic and pessimistic states that shape justice perceptions in specific events such as layoffs, pay cuts, or policy changes. Studies could also test whether these states influence other interpersonal processes, including perceived support, ostracism, incivility, leader affect, and team performance. Additional research could compare event-level and entity-level justice perceptions and investigate how employees react when supervisor behavior contradicts their optimistic or pessimistic expectations.
