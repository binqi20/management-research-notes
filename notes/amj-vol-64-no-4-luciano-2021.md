# The Double-Edged Sword of Leadership Task Transitions in Emergency Response Multiteam Systems

**Abstract**
Multiteam systems (MTSs) operating in complex and dynamic environments often have a formal hierarchical leadership structure. However, it is unclear whether individuals should stick exclusively to performing their designated tasks within the hierarchical leadership structure, or if, instead, they should switch between different types of tasks to align efforts with changes in the environment. We refer to such task switching—an individual shifting to or from tasks designated for a particular leader position—as leadership task transitions. Our qualitative study of six MTSs responding to live-actor mass-casualty incidents revealed that leadership task transitionsare a double-edged sword as they can simultaneously help manage the MTS–environment interface and harm MTS internal functioning. More specifically, leadership task transitions benefit the MTS by rapidly reallocating effort to alleviate the dominant environmental pressure at that time. However, they also harm the MTS by disrupting its internal task-based cycles. Rapidly restoring the disrupted cycles mitigates this harmful effect, but such cycle restoration is not successful when there is a high level of cycle activity or when multiple areas of the MTS are disrupted. Our findings generate new knowledge on how and why leadership task transitions impact MTSs. Implications and future directions are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do leadership task transitions influence multiteam system effectiveness in complex and dynamic environments? The paper further asks how MTSs function in such environments, and how leadership task transitions influence that functioning and, in turn, effectiveness.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / setting: Emergency response multiteam systems with system- and sector-level task-based cycles under a formal hierarchical leadership structure.
- Environmental pressures: Dynamic complexity creates pressure to gain awareness; dynamic challenge creates pressure to make progress.
- Process trigger: Shifts in the dominant environmental pressure prompt leadership task transitions, defined as temporary movement to or from tasks designated for a leader position.
- Beneficial pathway: Leadership task transitions rapidly reallocate effort toward awareness or progress, helping the MTS alleviate the current dominant pressure.
- Harmful pathway: The same transitions disrupt one or more task-based cycles by interrupting updating, awareness, leader tasks, enabling, worker tasks, or progress.
- Boundary conditions: Rapid restoration of the disrupted cycle mitigates harm; restoration is less successful when cycle activity is high or multiple cycles are disrupted.

Leadership task transitions operate as a double-edged process. They help the MTS manage the environment by moving effort to the pressure that most urgently needs attention, but they also pull effort away from the baseline task-based cycles that keep the system internally coordinated. The net effect depends on whether the disrupted cycle can be restored quickly and completely.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from one full-day capstone exercise at an emergency medical response academy comprising six live-actor mass-casualty incident scenarios (three motor vehicle accidents and three mass shootings), each averaging 26:30 minutes:seconds with a range of 22:09 to 31:35 and staffed with 15 to 16 live-actor "patients." Thirteen paramedic students, all licensed EMTs, formed a different MTS for each scenario under a two-tier formal hierarchy: one system commander overseeing three sectors (Triage, Treatment, Transport), each with one sector captain and three members. All paramedics wore personal audio recorders and 12 video cameras were positioned across roughly 7,000 square feet of space, with all devices left on throughout the day, generating more than 180 hours of raw video and personal audio. Following Knoblauch and Schnettler's focused ethnography, the authors reconstructed the exercise into 78 time-synchronized videos, each following a single paramedic (13 total) through each scenario (six total).

Coding proceeded in two initial teams. Two of the authors categorized the types of tasks each paramedic performed, identified all task transitions, and marked those involving an individual shifting to or from tasks designated for a particular leader position; what counted as a leader task for each position was taken from the local city mass-casualty incident protocol (City of Phoenix, 2000) rather than from a leadership style. Across all six scenarios they captured 1,107 task transitions, 501 of which were leadership task transitions. A second team of two medical professionals independent of the research team coded paramedic-patient interactions (798 total) for effectiveness on three indicators: quality of care, order of care, and speed of care. For the environment, six medical professionals and the authors agreed criteria for four factors — uncertainty, volatility, workload, and urgency — and three coders (two authors and one medical professional) coded the intensity (low or high) of each pressure during and immediately prior to every leadership task transition episode; uncertainty and volatility were then collapsed into dynamic complexity and workload and urgency into dynamic challenge. Temporal bracketing was used to compare the tasks each paramedic performed before, during, and after a transition. In the final phase the authors used Langley's "following forward" technique from each of the 501 leadership task transitions and retained the 209 chains of events in which at least two authors agreed there was a clear link to impact on MTS effectiveness, defined as the proximal influence of an action on quality, speed, and order of care; these chains are the "episodes." A disruption was defined as any disturbance in the baseline flow of a task-based cycle, and cycle restoration as repairing all elements of the cycle such that its flow is reestablished. The design is inductive and interpretive process theorizing; there is no statistical estimation or causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
The study yields two complementary process models. First, MTS internal functioning is composed of system- and sector-level task-based cycles in which updating produces awareness, awareness grounds leader tasks, some leader tasks enable lower-level tasks, those lower-level tasks contribute to progress, and progress changes status so that updating must occur again. The environment interfaces with these cycles through opposing pressures: dynamic complexity (uncertainty and volatility) was exerted on gaining awareness, and dynamic challenge (workload and urgency) was exerted on making progress. Tasks aimed at gaining awareness and at making progress were mutually exclusive, so effort had to be prioritized toward one or the other, and the dominant pressure shifted both within an area of the MTS over time and across sectors as patients flowed through the system.

Second, shifts in environmental pressures triggered leadership task transitions, and every transition created two processes with opposite signs — the double-edged sword. Rapid effort reallocation alleviated the new dominant pressure and positively impacted MTS effectiveness, while the same transition disrupted one or more task-based cycles and negatively impacted it. The net impact depended on cycle restoration. In 139 of the 209 episodes all elements of the disrupted cycle (updating, awareness, leader tasks, enabling, worker tasks, and progress) were restored, restoration successfully mitigated the negative impact, and the net impact was positive. In the other 70 episodes only some elements were restored and the net impact was negative; of these, 34 involved the individual simply returning to the previous tasks and short-circuiting the process by not first engaging in updating, while in the remaining 36 individuals could not restore the entire cycle either because the level of cycle activity was high or because multiple task-based cycles were disrupted. Leadership task transitions were not rare occurrences even in this strongly protocol-governed setting: at least one person was engaged in a task not designated for their position 80.1% of the time somewhere in the MTS, and 58.4% of the time within each particular sector.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper builds process theory on how leadership task transitions affect MTS effectiveness, explaining why fluid leadership can be simultaneously beneficial and harmful inside a formal hierarchy. It reframes the MTS-environment interface from a single external influence into shifting, opposing pressures of dynamic complexity and dynamic challenge. It also advances MTS theory by foregrounding task-based cycles, enabling scholars to examine taskwork alongside the more established focus on teamwork and system-level processes.

**Practical Implication**
Emergency-response MTS members should not treat "stay in your lane" as an absolute rule; brief transitions can be useful when environmental pressures shift. However, members should avoid transitions during high cycle activity, avoid leading outside their zone when they lack awareness, and restore the disrupted cycle quickly by reestablishing updating, awareness, direction, enabling, work, and progress.

**Limitations**
The study is based on one emergency-response training exercise with paramedic students in simulated mass-casualty incidents, so transferability is strongest for similar emergency response MTSs. The MTS had clear formal hierarchy, shared training, low competency separation, and low structural dynamism; systems with flatter structures, high information transparency, different competencies, or more structural change may show different dynamics.

**Future Research**
Future research should test the boundary conditions of the theory in other emergency settings and in non-emergency organizational crises where dynamic complexity and dynamic challenge shift over time. Scholars could examine MTSs with different leadership structures, information transparency, competency separation, and structural dynamism, and could study when repeated task transitions become leader role transitions. The paper also calls for work on individual differences, learning, adaptability, and transferability to teams, matrix organizations, and other organizational forms.
