# The Hidden Cost of Prayer: Religiosity and the Gender Wage Gap

**Abstract**
Religion is a preeminent social institution that meaningfully shapes cultures. Prevailing theory suggests that it is primarily a benevolent force in business, and differences across world religions preclude examining effects that thread across religions. We develop a theoretical account that fundamentally challenges these assumptions by explaining how and why religiosity—regardless of which religion is prominent—differentiates based on gender, widening the gender wage gap. Guided by an integrated review of the religion literature, we specify three dimensions of gender differentiation—social domains, sexuality, and agency—that explain why religiosity widens the gender wage gap. A series of studies tested our theoretical model. Two studies showcased the predictive power of religiosity on the gender wage gap across 140 countries worldwide and the 50 United States via gender-differentiated social domains, sexuality, and agency, explaining 37% of the variance in the wage gap. U.S. longitudinal data indicated that the gender wage gap is narrowing significantly faster in secular states. Moreover, experiments allowed for causal inference, revealing that gender-egalitarian interventions blocked the effect of religiosity on the gender wage gap. Finally, theoretical and empirical accounts converge to suggest that religiosity's effect on the gender wage gap applies across the major world religions.

**Research Question**
How and why does cultural religiosity, regardless of which religion is predominant, relate to the gender wage gap? The paper asks whether religiosity widens wage inequality through three forms of gender differentiation: social domains, sexuality, and agency.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Religiosity has a positive effect on the gender wage gap.
- H2: Religiosity has a positive indirect effect on the gender wage gap through gender differentiation in social domains, prescribing that men should contribute publicly and professionally while women should contribute privately and domestically.
- H3: Religiosity has a positive indirect effect on the gender wage gap through differentiation in sovereignty over one's sexuality, prescribing that women's sexuality is subject to external regulation.
- H4: Religiosity has a positive indirect effect on the gender wage gap through gender differentiation in agency, prescribing that men should pursue power and decision-making authority while women should heed men's directive.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cultural or organizational religiosity; in Study 3b, gender-egalitarian organizational policy interventions that target the theorized mechanisms.
- DV(s): Gender wage gap or gender-differentiated wage allocation.
- Mediators: Gender differentiation in social domains, sexuality, and agency.
- Moderators: Religious affiliation was tested and did not carry or significantly moderate the religiosity effect; Study 3b policy interventions blocked the effect of religiosity on wage allocation.

The paper argues that religiosity works as a broad cultural value that legitimizes differentiated expectations for men and women. Religious cultures are theorized to assign men to public and professional domains, regulate women's sexuality more than men's, and grant men greater agency and decision-making authority. These forms of differentiation can shape organizational wage allocation by devaluing women's workplace contributions, reducing women's accumulated human capital, and limiting women's ability to change unequal systems. Field studies support indirect associations through these mechanisms, while the experiments show that explicitly egalitarian policies can interrupt the wage-allocation effect.

**Data & Measures**
The integrative review searched EBSCO Academic Search Premier and JSTOR for the terms religio and sex or gender together with the names of the six world religions; the first author highlighted gender-focused passages, both authors worked iteratively to identify themes and build a coding scheme, and the first author continued coding until theoretical saturation, yielding 133 articles and 1,041 passages, at least 34 articles per world religion, with 44% of articles discussing multiple religions. Study 1 covered 140 countries: the gender wage gap was computed as 1 minus women's income as a percentage of men's, using estimated earned income for men and women from the Human Development Report (2014); religiosity was the percentage of respondents per country who answered yes to the Gallup item "Is religion important in your daily life?", administered to approximately 1,000 adults per country across 143 countries between 2006 and 2008. Differentiated social domains combined four indicators (average birth rate per woman, the gender gap in labor force participation, restrictiveness of abortion law, and family-friendly policies indexed by mandatory paid maternity leave length and whether the government mandates paternity leave); sexuality differentiation used averaged Google Trends search volume for the words rape and porn translated into the six United Nations languages; female agency combined four indicators (women's share of national parliamentary seats, the female-to-male ratio of professional and technical workers, the female-to-male ratio of tertiary enrollment, and equal capacity plus equal inheritance rights under the law). Median age and logged GDP per capita were covariates; indicators were standardized, and a confirmatory factor analysis and path analysis were estimated with full information maximum likelihood in Mplus 8.0. Study 2a covered the 50 U.S. states, computing the wage gap from median annual earnings of men and women employed full-time, year-round (Status of Women in the States, 2013) and measuring religiosity from Gallup's 348,306 adult interviews in 2012, with respondents classified as nonreligious (1), moderately religious (2), or highly religious (3) and averaged per state; mechanism indicators paralleled Study 1 apart from the U.S. substitutions listed in Table 2. Study 2b nested 500 annual wage-gap measurements (2009-2018, from U.S. Census Bureau median annual income for full-time workers) within 50 states, paired with Gallup religiosity polls (2008-2016), and estimated a cross-classified multilevel model in Mplus 8.0 with religiosity grand-mean centered and evaluated at Time 0 (2008). Studies 3a (n = 91) and 3b (n = 143) were preregistered online experiments with English-fluent, full-time employees holding supervisory responsibilities recruited through Prolific Academic; religiosity was manipulated through the core company values of a fictitious employer, ServiceOne (religious vs. nonreligious control), and the wage gap was computed from allocated annual salary (range $30,000 to $120,000) and annual raise (range $0 to $10,000) for a female and a male internal consultant who each received a performance rating of 4 out of 5. Study 3a also measured differentiated social domains (seven items, alpha = .87), differentiated sexuality (five items, alpha = .86), and differentiated agency (six items, alpha = .78); Study 3b exposed all participants to the religiosity condition and randomly assigned them to a control condition or one of three policy-blocking conditions, with a three-item gender-equality manipulation check (alpha = .92). The country- and state-level studies are associational; causal inference rests on the Study 3 double-randomization (experimental-causal-chain) and moderation-by-process designs.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (140 countries) supported all four hypotheses: religiosity related positively to the gender wage gap (b = 8.47, SE = 1.99, p < .001), explaining 10% of the variability over and above covariates, with positive indirect effects through differentiated social domains (indirect effect = 3.37, p = .002), sexuality (1.20, p = .04), and agency (1.59, p = .03), the last running through reduced female agency (religiosity to agency b = -.39; agency to wage gap b = -4.09). The direct effect was no longer significant once the mechanisms were included (b = 2.31, SE = 1.77, p = .19), and the total effect model explained 37% of the variability in the global wage gap; women earned 46% as much as men in countries where 95% or more of the population reported religion was important versus 75% where fewer than 20% did, a 29-percentage-point difference. Study 2a (50 U.S. states) reproduced the pattern: religiosity related positively to the wage gap (b = 2.28, SE = .70, p < .001), explaining 17% of the variability, with positive indirect effects via social domains (.80, p = .067), sexuality (.70, p = .07), and agency (.81, p = .04, again via reduced female agency); the authors read all three as supporting H2-H4 while reporting the two borderline p values, the direct effect became nonsignificant (b = -.03, SE = .80, p = .97), and the model explained 37% of the variability. The five most religious states showed a 26% wage gap versus 18% in the five least religious states. In supplemental analyses, the percentage of the population affiliated with each religion neither related significantly to the wage gap nor moderated the religiosity effect (Table 6, n = 139 countries; Table 7, n = 48 states, with data missing for Hawaii and Alaska), and adding Hofstede's cultural dimensions (available for 89 countries) left the indirect effects via social domains (2.22, p = .03) and agency (2.73, p = .01) significant but rendered the sexuality indirect effect nonsignificant (.63, p = .31). Study 2b found the U.S. wage gap narrowed overall from 2009 to 2018 (b = -.045, SE = .003, p < .001) and that 2008 religiosity interacted with time (b = .036, SE = .009, p < .001), so the gap narrowed faster in less religious states (very low religiosity b = -.067; very high b = -.023). In Study 3a the religiosity manipulation shifted allocation by gender: the male employee received 3% higher pay under religious values whereas the female employee received 6% higher pay under nonreligious values (t89 = 2.11, p = .037), and the religiosity condition raised differentiated social domains (M = 4.56 vs. 4.07, t89 = 3.97, p < .001), acceptability of sexualized behavior toward female colleagues (M = 2.04 vs. 1.61, t89 = 2.10, p = .039), and endorsement of men as more suitable for power and leadership (M = 3.58 vs. 2.95, t89 = 2.66, p = .009). In Study 3b the wage gap differed significantly from zero in the control condition (M = .08, SE = .03, 95% CI [.009, .141]), amounting to 8% higher recommended wages for the male employee, but not under the equitable parental leave (M = -.03), sexual harassment prohibition (M = -.04), or inclusive leadership development (M = -.07) policy conditions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges management scholarship's prevailing view of religion as mainly benevolent for organizations by theorizing and testing a systematic inequality-producing effect. It contributes to religion-and-organization research by treating religiosity as a cross-religion cultural value rather than comparing discrete faith traditions. It also extends work on patriarchy, social role theory, and biosocial explanations of gender roles by identifying social domains, sexuality, and agency as cross-cultural mechanisms linking religiosity to wage inequality.

**Practical Implication**
Managers in religious cultures should not assume that religious inclusion is automatically neutral for wage equality. The experiments suggest that policies supporting equitable parental leave, prohibiting sexual harassment, and promoting inclusive leadership development can block gender-differentiated wage allocation. Organizations can also audit pay equity, increase transparency, and strengthen accountability where cultural norms may make unequal valuation of men's and women's work seem natural.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the global sample lacks complete data for every country, and missingness may reflect development stage, censorship, or other external conditions. The theory contrasts religious and secular cultures, but religiosity is measured on a continuum, and some mediator indicators were collected after wage-gap data. The sexuality indicators rely on online searches for rape and pornography, which may miss non-digital behavior, censorship, or differences in whether people seek information online.

**Future Research**
Future research should examine whether separation between church and state reduces gender inequality and whether religiosity affects other organizational phenomena beyond the gender wage gap. Additional work could study how pornography consumption, rape, and broader sexualization relate to wage inequality. The authors also call for integrating cultural tightness-looseness to test whether religious cultures impose tighter behavioral expectations on women than on men.
