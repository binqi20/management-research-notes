# Networks, Creativity, and Time: Staying Creative through Brokerage and Network Rejuvenation

**Abstract**
In this paper, we adopt a dynamic perspective on networks and creativity to propose that the oft-theorized creative benefits of open networks and heterogeneous content are less likely to be accrued over time if the network is stable. Specifically, we hypothesize that open networks and content heterogeneity will have a more positive effect on creativity when network stability is low. We base our prediction on the fact that, over time, network stability begets cognitive rigidity and social rigidity, thus limiting individuals' ability to make use of the creative advantages provided by open networks and heterogeneous content. On the contrary, new ties bring a positive "shock" that pushes individuals in the network to change the way they organize and process knowledge, as well as the way they interact and collaborate—a shock that enables creators to accrue the creative advantages provided by open network structures and heterogeneous content. We test and find support for our theory in a study on the core artists who worked on the TV series Doctor Who between 1963 and 2014.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when open networks and heterogeneous network content continue to support individual creativity over time. It specifically examines whether network stability weakens the creative benefits of brokerage and content heterogeneity, and whether adding new ties rejuvenates those benefits.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Network stability moderates the relationship between open networks and creativity. The positive association between open networks and creativity is weaker in more stable networks, and stronger in less stable ones.
Hypothesis 2. Network stability moderates the relationship between content heterogeneity and creativity. The positive association between the exposure to heterogeneous content and creativity is weaker in more stable networks, and stronger in less stable ones.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Network openness and content heterogeneity.
- DV(s): Individual creativity in artists' contributions to Doctor Who episodes.
- Mediators: Not directly tested; the theorized process is cognitive rigidity and social rigidity in stable networks versus cognitive and collaborative disruption from new ties.
- Moderators: Network stability, operationalized as the degree to which artists maintain existing ties rather than add new collaborators.

The mechanism is that open networks and heterogeneous content provide nonredundant perspectives and raw material for creative recombination, but stable network membership can make creators cognitively and socially rigid. When artists add new ties, newcomers create a positive shock that disrupts established mental models and collaboration routines, making creators more able to use the advantages of brokerage and heterogeneous content. The panel results show that openness and heterogeneity foster creativity mainly when they are coupled with low network stability.

**Data & Measures**
Archival data on the Doctor Who production world. The sample is the entire population of core crewmembers who worked on at least one of the 273 episodes produced between 1963 and 2014, where "core" artists are the three creative roles of one producer (sometimes called a showrunner), one or more directors, and one or more writers; core teams range in size from two to five, with 81% containing three people. Credits were taken from the BBC website and cross-checked against specialized Doctor Who publications and Doctor Who-dedicated wikis, then cleaned for duplicates and inconsistencies, yielding a final sample of 866 observations for 200 individual artists. Two data sets were constructed: a cross-sectional one aggregating all network and creativity data with the 200 artists as the unit of analysis, built to verify that the well-known positive network-creativity relationships hold in this setting and to describe the collaboration network; and an unbalanced panel of 866 artist-episode pairs, with episodes per artist ranging from 1 to 50 and an average of 4, used for the main hypothesis tests.
- DV: Creativity, measured with the consensual assessment technique. Two expert judges (British television critics who had written on the history of Doctor Who) were given a shared definition of creativity as the generation of novel and appropriate outcomes and independently rated the creativity of the producer, of the director, of the writer, and overall episode creativity on a 5-point scale (1 = not creative, 5 = very creative). Cohen's weighted kappa for the three roles and the episodes varied between .79 and .83. Ratings were provided two years after the last episode was broadcast, which the authors argue minimizes reverse causality; judges were asked to re-watch episodes they had not seen in more than three years. In the panel data set, an artist's creativity is the creativity of their role in the focal episode, averaged across roles if the artist covered more than one. In the cross-sectional data set, four measures were computed: maximum individual creativity, maximum episode creativity, number of highly creative individual contributions, and number of highly creative episodes, with a contribution counted as "highly creative" when the judges' score was equal to or higher than 4.5.
- IV: Network openness, computed from Burt's (1992) network constraint on the artist-episode affiliation network and operationalized as 1 - constraint, so that high scores reflect openness and low scores closure. Constraint was computed within four-episode time windows in the panel data set (an episode takes about a month to produce, so a four-episode window is roughly four months) and over the artist's whole time on Doctor Who in the cross-sectional data set, where network size ranged between two and 47, with a mean of 5.93 and a median of four.
- IV: Content heterogeneity, measured as how dissimilar an episode's content was from the other episodes the artist had worked on. Four content categories identified from domain sources and validated by the expert judges were split into 41 binary variables: three distinguished story type, three story setting, 12 incarnations of the Doctor, and 23 the kind of alien opposing the Doctor. The second author and a research assistant blind to the hypotheses coded each episode independently, disagreeing on about 2% of cases and resolving those through discussion. Jaccard coefficients measured dissimilarity between pairs of the artist's M content profiles, and the averaged coefficients gave the heterogeneity score; single-episode artists' scores were shifted from 0 to .33 to avoid outlier problems. Content heterogeneity correlated with network openness at r = .92.
- Moderator: Network stability, measured in the panel data set as 1 - (n new ties / max new ties), where new ties are the new faces on the creative team the artist was working with on a given episode and a collaborator counts as "new" if the artist had not worked with them before the current episode. New faces were typically one or two per episode, with 13.39% of observations having none and 12.89% having three or four. In the cross-sectional data set, stability is the average of the panel measure across the artist's episodes.
- Controls (panel): expertise (number of episodes worked on up to the focal one), input nonredundancy (content elements the artist's alters had experience in but the artist did not, over the total alters had experience in), outside experience (TV shows outside Doctor Who worked on during the focal year), previous creativity (average judged creativity of prior contributions), previous outside collaborations, outside ties, and dummies for the artist's role and for the cohort of the focal episode. Controls (cross-sectional): expertise, outside experience, and role and cohort dummies.

Estimation is associational rather than causal: the authors state that their cross-sectional design and variable aggregation do not allow them to claim causality. The cross-sectional analyses use OLS regression for the maximum-creativity measures and Poisson regression for the counts of highly creative work, with a negative binomial specification rejected because the dispersion parameter alpha was not significantly different from zero. The main panel analysis uses a conditional fixed-effects linear model, chosen because repeated observations violate the OLS independence assumption and error variance may be heteroscedastic across units, and because a Hausman test indicated the random-effects estimator was not consistent. Huber-White robust standard errors, equivalent to clustering on the creator, are reported; predictors were not centered, so interaction coefficients are read as the effect of the independent variable when the moderator equals zero. Variables were entered in four hierarchical steps (controls, predictors, each interaction separately, both interactions together), and the collinearity condition number for the full model was 13.11.

**Key Findings**
Both hypotheses were tested in the panel data set and both were supported.
- H1 supported (Table 5, Model 3): the network openness x network stability interaction is negative and significant (-1.899, p < .01), indicating that the effect of open networks or structural holes becomes less positive as network stability increases.
- H2 supported (Table 5, Model 4): the content heterogeneity x network stability interaction is negative and significant (-2.465, p < .01), indicating that the effect of content heterogeneity becomes less positive as network stability increases.
- Model 5, entering both interactions together, leaves both coefficients negative and significant (-1.567 and -2.241, p < .01 for both), and an F test shows Model 5 improves significantly on Model 2 (Pr > F < .001).
- Main effects in the panel (Model 2): neither network openness nor network stability has a significant effect on creativity, while content heterogeneity has a positive and significant effect (0.558, p < .05). Among controls, previous creativity has a negative effect on creativity (p < .01).
- Marginal effects: the effect of network openness is positive when network stability is low (p < .05), becomes increasingly less positive as stability increases, and turns negative when the network is characterized by zero change (p < .01). The effect of content heterogeneity is positive and significant when network stability is low (p < .01) and becomes increasingly less positive as stability increases. Descriptively, creativity is highest for artists in open networks working with multiple new teammates, is lower when half the team is unchanged, and reverses to negative when the team is unchanged or contains only one new member; a similar pattern holds for content heterogeneity.
- Preliminary cross-sectional analysis (a replication check on aggregated data, not a hypothesis test): correlations of network openness and content heterogeneity with the four creativity measures are positive and significant, ranging between .540 and .572 and between .519 and .593 respectively (p < .001). In the cross-sectional regressions with all predictors entered together (Models 2, 4, 6, 8), network openness is positive and significant for every operationalization of creativity except the number of highly creative episodes (p < .01 for maximum role creativity, p < .05 for maximum episode creativity, p < .01 for number of highly creative individual contributions), content heterogeneity is not significant for any operationalization, and average network stability is positive (p < .05 for both maximum-creativity measures, p < .01 for both creative-contribution measures), so over this long aggregated horizon artists working in stable networks generated more creative work. Entered in isolation, brokerage and stability behaved almost identically to the main models, and content heterogeneity became significant for all operationalizations (p < .001), which the authors attribute to brokerage "washing out" its effect in the joint models.
- Robustness: using Burt's effective-size measure of structural holes instead of constraint left results identical, with the effective size x network stability interaction negative and significant (p < .01 entered alone, p < .05 entered alongside the other interaction). A Lee-modified Heckman selection model using audience ratings as the selection condition produced an inverse Mills ratio with no significant effect on creativity and left the main results robust. Adding a squared prior-experience term, itself positive and significant (p < .01), did not affect the main results, with marginal effects virtually identical.
- Boundary test of the theorized mechanism: three-way interactions testing whether the benefit of new ties depends on new ties' human capital were all non-significant. New ties' expertise x stability was not significant for network openness (p = .110) or for content heterogeneity (p = .366), and new ties' knowledge nonredundancy x stability was not significant for network openness (p = .188) or for content heterogeneity (p = .938), supporting the assumption that adding new ties fosters the creative returns of openness and content heterogeneity regardless of the new ties' human capital.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper adds a dynamic view to network-creativity theory by showing that the benefits of brokerage and content heterogeneity depend on how network composition changes over time. It also separates network structure from network content, arguing that open networks offer a vision advantage beyond access to heterogeneous knowledge. The study further suggests that short-term network rejuvenation can explain how individuals sustain creative performance across longer creative careers.

**Practical Implication**
Managers and creative professionals should not treat a nonredundant network as a static asset. Sustained creativity may require keeping collaboration structures open to newcomers, even when existing teams or products are successful. The implication is to balance brokerage and continuity with periodic renewal that introduces fresh perspectives and disrupts rigid routines.

**Limitations**
The study is set in the Doctor Who production world, a creative, project-based context with fixed roles, so the findings may travel most directly to settings such as consulting, scientific research, and new product design. The analysis focuses on one product category, which helps control product- and company-level confounds but may miss collaboration networks spanning other product domains. Because the data are archival, the study cannot directly measure the cognitive and social mechanisms through which network stability and structural holes shape creativity.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how much network change is too much across different time horizons, since short-term rejuvenation and long-term instability may have different effects. Scholars could also study alter-centric network dynamics, including how changes in collaborators' ties affect a focal creator's creativity. Additional work using laboratory studies or ad hoc surveys could directly test the cognitive rigidity, social rigidity, and newcomer-shock mechanisms proposed here.
