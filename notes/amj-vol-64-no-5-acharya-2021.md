# Too Many Peas in a Pod? How Overlaps in Directors’ Local and Global Status Characteristics Influence Board Turnover in Newly Public Firms

**Abstract**
Drawing on status characteristics theory, we explore how boards’ social structures influence board turnover. We theorize that (a) understanding directors’ relative standing and spheres of influence in the local status hierarchy creates deference structures that reduce conflict and enhance stability, thereby reducing board turnover; and (b) shared performance expectations and attraction based on homophily in the global status hierarchy can also reduce conflict and enhance stability and thus serve as another means of reducing board turnover. Using five years of post-initial public offering data on 218 firms that went public between 2001 and 2005, we find that overlaps in directors’ local status characteristics captured by their shared tenure and expertise and directors’ global status homogeneity increase the likelihood of board turnover. However, the directors’ global status characteristics shape the relative salience of the positive or negative effects of boards’ local status characteristics, leading to different effects on board turnover.

**Research Question**
How do overlaps in directors’ locally valued status characteristics (board tenure and functional expertise) and global status characteristics shape board turnover in newly public firms? The paper asks whether board-level status structures stabilize boards by clarifying deference relationships or destabilize them when too many directors share the same status characteristics.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Higher board tenure overlap is positively associated with board turnover.
- H2: Higher board expertise overlap is positively associated with board turnover.
- H3: Higher board global status homogeneity is negatively associated with board turnover.
- H4a: The greater the board’s global status homogeneity, the weaker the positive relationship between the board’s tenure overlap and board turnover.
- H4b: The greater the board’s global status homogeneity, the weaker the positive relationship between the board’s expertise overlap and board turnover.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Board tenure overlap, board expertise overlap, board global status homogeneity, and interactions between global status homogeneity and the two local-status overlap measures.
- DV(s): Board turnover, measured as the count of outside directors exiting the board in a given year.
- Mediators: Not directly estimated; the theorized mechanisms are local deference structures, conflict/process losses, shared performance expectations, and status homophily.
- Moderators: Global status homogeneity moderates the tenure-overlap and expertise-overlap relationships; the tenure interaction is supported, while the expertise interaction is not supported.

The mechanism is conditional rather than uniformly stabilizing. When directors differ in tenure or expertise, those characteristics can create distinct spheres of influence and deference patterns; when overlap is high, those characteristics lose differentiating power and can increase board turnover. Global status homogeneity has mixed effects: it initially appears to reduce turnover, but in the fuller models it increases turnover while attenuating the positive tenure-overlap effect at higher levels of tenure overlap.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: the list of start-ups that conducted IPOs in the United States between 2001 and 2005 came from SDC Platinum’s equity new issues database and company filings accessed via Edgar Online; director and CEO data were hand-collected and coded from SEC filings (prospectuses, proxy statements, annual reports) and supplemented by searches of corporate websites, Bloomberg, Crunchbase, and the Financial Times; venture capital data came from VentureXpert; media data from Factiva and Bloomberg; financial and accounting data from Compustat and the Center for Research in Security Prices. Unit offerings, closed-end mutual funds, demutualizations of savings banks and insurance companies, corporate spin-offs, REITs, and leveraged buyouts were excluded, and only firms 10 years old or younger at IPO were retained; each firm was observed for the five years following its IPO or until it was delisted, whichever was earlier. The final sample consisted of 218 firms and 828 firm-year observations, and Table 2 reports N = 828 for all models.
- DV — board turnover: the count of the total number of outside directors who exited the board in a given year (following Garg et al., 2018). The measure does not distinguish voluntary from involuntary exits because information on reasons for exit is limited and scarcer still for newly public firms; the authors argue involuntary exits make the test more conservative.
- IV — tenure overlap (TLAP): the average of the pair-wise overlaps in board service among directors, TLAP = 1/N Σ min(ui, uj) for i ≠ j, where ui and uj are the ith and jth directors’ board tenures and N is the total number of pair-wise comparisons. Individual director tenures ranged from 0 to 15 years, and TLAP ranged from 0 to nine years.
- IV — expertise overlap (ELAP): directors were assigned, from job titles and career descriptions, to one of five occupational categories adapted from Hillman et al.’s (2000) taxonomy — business experts, support specialists, community influentials, private equity managers, and venture capitalists. ELAP applies the TLAP formula to binary same-expertise comparisons and ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (complete overlap). Inter-rater reliability was high (ICC1 = .94), and 27 directors placed in an "unsure" category were resolved through discussion between the first author and the research assistants.
- IV — global status homogeneity: a director was treated as globally high status if he or she possessed at least one of educational prestige (a degree from an elite institution on Finkelstein’s (1992) list), employment prestige (executive at the level of vice president or above at an S&P 500 company), or directorial prestige (outside director at an S&P 500 firm), assessed against a lagged annual S&P 500 list. Homogeneity was measured with the Blau (1977) index over the two director categories (high status, non-high status), which ranges from 0 to .5; the authors reversed it by deducting the values from .5 so that 0 represents complete status heterogeneity and .5 complete status homogeneity.
- Controls: VC backing, offer size (logged), firm age at IPO, firm size (logged annual sales), founder-CEO, board size, staggered board, firm performance (total stockholder return), media coverage (logged count of media articles), cumulative hires, five industry dummies (business services, chemical and allied products, instruments and related products, electronic and other electric products, and retail), and year-of-issue and years-since-IPO dummies.
- Estimation and identification: mixed effects negative binomial regression (the menbreg command in Stata 16.0), chosen because the dependent variable is a count and observations repeat within firms; mixed rather than fixed effects because the authors’ main interest was inter-firm differences and several predictors are time invariant. The design is archival and associational rather than causally identified — endogeneity is addressed by directly controlling for VC backing and firm performance and by an impact threshold of a confounding variable analysis (Frank, 2000) run in KonFound-it!, not by an identification strategy. Robustness checks used generalized estimating equations (the xtgee command with a Poisson family, log link, and exchangeable correlation structure), random effects negative binomial regression, alternative expertise-category collapses, a weighted status measure, and SOX checks that both dropped the 33 firms filing IPOs in 2001 and added a dummy coded 1 for years beyond 2005. Collinearity diagnostics gave condition index values of 13.41 to 19.32 and VIFs of 1.02 to 1.91 (mean 1.27).

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: in Model 2, tenure overlap has a positive and significant (p < .001) relationship with the likelihood of board turnover, and the result remained positive and significant at p < .01 or better in all models in Table 2. If the board’s tenure overlap increased by 1, board turnover would increase by a factor of 1.35, holding all other variables in the model constant.
- H2 supported: in Model 2, expertise overlap has a positive and significant (p < .001) relationship with board turnover, and the result remains positive and significant at p < .01 or better in all models. If the board’s expertise overlap increased by 1, board turnover would increase by a factor of 1.50, holding all other variables in the model constant.
- H3 not supported: in the main-effects Model 2 the coefficient of global status homogeneity is negative and significant (p < .05), consistent with the prediction, but it becomes positive and significant at p < .001 in Model 3, which adds the interaction with tenure overlap, and in the fully specified Model 5, which includes both interactions. The authors read the negative main-effect result as likely picking up the negative moderating effect of global status homogeneity on tenure overlap and conclude that, considered in conjunction with the other local status characteristics, higher levels of global status homogeneity increase the rate of board turnover.
- H4a supported: in Model 3 the coefficient for the tenure overlap x status homogeneity interaction is negative and significant at p < .001 while the main effect of tenure overlap remains positive and significant. Figure 1 shows tenure overlap is positively and significantly associated with board turnover at both high and low status homogeneity (both slopes differ significantly from zero), and that high status homogeneity increases the effect of tenure overlap when tenure overlap is low but weakens it once tenure overlap exceeds its mean value of approximately three. The authors note the theoretical mechanism at work may be different from the one they hypothesized.
- H4b not supported: in Model 4 the coefficient for the interaction between expertise overlap and status homogeneity is negative and not significant.
- Robustness: the GEE results were identical to the reported models, random effects estimates were identical, and results were unchanged when the 33 firms filing IPOs in 2001 were dropped and when a post-2005 SOX dummy was added (the dummy was not significant in any model). The impact threshold of a confounding variable analysis indicates the inferences for tenure overlap, status homogeneity, and their interaction are robust, but the inference for expertise overlap is less robust to potential omitted variable bias. In analyses not reported in the paper, the authors found no curvilinear effects and no significant moderation of tenure overlap by expertise overlap.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends status characteristics theory by shifting attention from whether an individual possesses a valued status characteristic to what happens when multiple group members share the same characteristic. It also theorizes nested local and global status hierarchies, showing that status characteristics can have different implications depending on whether their value is local to the board or recognized in the broader director labor market. For corporate governance research, it moves beyond formal board structure to explain board turnover through informal board social structures.

**Practical Implication**
For newly public firms, board composition should not be treated as a simple exercise in adding high-status or similarly credentialed directors. The findings suggest that maintaining board stability may require enough heterogeneity in tenure, expertise, and status characteristics for deference structures and distinct spheres of influence to form. Firms refreshing boards during transitions should consider how new appointments affect the board’s informal social order, not only whether appointees look prestigious on paper.

**Limitations**
The study uses archival data, so it cannot directly observe boardroom interaction, directors’ perceptions of the local status hierarchy, or the actual decision processes behind director exits. The dependent variable does not distinguish voluntary from involuntary exits because such information is limited for newly public firms. The sample also has limited variance in diffuse status characteristics such as race, gender, and nationality, and it focuses on young U.S. firms during a period that included SOX and the 2008 financial crisis.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine individual director-level responses to board status hierarchies, including whether committee chairs, board chairs, or directors with specialized skills react differently to hierarchy and overlap. Scholars could also study the consequences of board turnover and refreshment, the interaction of diffuse and specific status characteristics, and how board fault lines are created and activated. Additional work using experiments, ethnography, surveys, policy capturing, or qualitative comparative analysis could directly observe board dynamics and test whether these mechanisms travel to older firms, other time periods, and outcomes beyond board turnover.
