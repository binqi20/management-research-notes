# Zoning Out or Breaking Through? Linking Daydreaming to Creativity in the Workplace

**Abstract**
Much has been written about the liabilities of mind wandering in the workplace. Given its prevalence, however, mind wandering may carry underappreciated benefits—especially with respect to creativity. Examining this possibility, we hypothesize that mind wandering involving imaginative thoughts, also known as “daydreams,” has the potential to spur creativity. We develop a theoretical model in which we examine two facets of daydreams based on their content: problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams. In addition, we specifyanantecedentconditionthat produces such daydreams(cognitively demanding work; Studies 1 & 2) as well as a boundary condition of the effects of daydreaming on creativity (professional identification; Study 2). Taken together, the studies reported here largely support our theoretical model. Cognitively demanding work reliably elicits both facets of daydreams. However, only problem-oriented daydreams relate to creativity directly; the relationship between bizarre daydreams and creativity is entirely dependent onprofessionalidentification. Inaddition, weobserve negative relationshipsbetween both facets of daydreams and performance more generally when employees lack professional identification (Study 2). Our results indicate that among professionally identified individuals, daydreaming carries noteworthy benefits for creativity but can also impair performance in the absence of identification.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether daydreaming, a form of imaginative mind wandering, can produce creative benefits in workplace settings rather than only performance costs. It examines whether cognitively demanding work elicits problem-oriented and bizarre daydreams, and whether professional identification conditions their effects on creativity and performance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Cognitively demanding work is positively related to both problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams.
H2. Problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams are positively related to creativity.
H3. Cognitively demanding work exerts indirect effects on creativity via both problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams.
H4. Professional identification serves as a second-stage moderator of the indirect effects of cognitively demanding work on creativity via problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams; the indirect effects are stronger when professional identification is high as opposed to when it is low.

Study 1 was designed to test Hypotheses 1-3 and Study 2 was designed to test the full model (Hypotheses 1-4). The authors state that they advance no formal hypotheses involving performance, but evaluate performance-related effects in order to offer evidence about the discriminant validity of the model.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Cognitively demanding work.
- DV(s): Creativity, with performance examined for discriminant validity.
- Mediators: Problem-oriented daydreams and bizarre daydreams.
- Moderators: Professional identification moderates the daydreaming-to-creativity links in Study 2.

The proposed mechanism is that cognitively demanding work stimulates or overloads employees' cognition, drawing the mind toward imaginative daydreams. Problem-oriented daydreams can directly generate novel and useful ideas for existing work problems, while bizarre daydreams may become creatively useful when highly professionally identified individuals treat them as material for problem finding or idea generation. Without professional identification, the same daydreaming can undermine broader performance.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 is a weeklong daily-life diary study conducted in South America. Participants were recruited via LinkedIn; an initial pool of 192 individuals expressed interest, and registered individuals were emailed a survey link every day from Monday to Friday at 4:00 pm. The authors retained 169 individuals for analysis (attrition rate 22%) who had provided complete information for at least two days; 98.2% had a college degree, 59.7% described themselves as professional employees, average age was 33.85 years (SD = 6.31) and average tenure 3.9 years (SD = 1.67), 55.6% were men, and respondents came from service (26.4%), production (3.1%), consulting (8.2%), commerce (10.1%), banking (12.6%), and other (39.6%) industries. All Study 1 constructs were self-reported daily on a 1 (not at all) to 5 (a great deal) scale. Daily cognitively demanding work used three items developed by Jackson et al. (1993) and used by Wall et al. (1995, 1996) (Cronbach's alpha = .78). The two daydream facets used six items selected and adapted from Singer and Antrobus (1972), with three items averaged into problem-oriented daydreams (alpha = .93) and three into bizarre daydreams (alpha = .92). Daily creativity used three items derived from Amabile et al. (2005) (alpha = .91) and daily performance efficiency used three items adapted from Parke et al. (2018) and developed by Williams and Anderson (1991) (alpha = .91). Daily attention residue, three items based on Leroy (2009) (alpha = .86), was included as a control to rule out multitasking and task-switching explanations. Analysis used confirmatory factor analysis in Mplus, intraclass correlation coefficients to estimate within-subjects variability, and multilevel structural equation modeling estimated with PROCESS in Mplus. Table 1 reports descriptive statistics and correlations for n = 676-685; Table 2 reports the multilevel structural equation model for n = 462, which the note to that table attributes to the inclusion of lagged daily creativity and daily performance, while the note to Figure 1 reports n = 466.

Study 2 is a multisource survey study of three technology consulting companies, whose respondents worked across domains including data management (48.4%), information systems (22%), and education technology (29.6%). The initial sample comprised all 186 employees of the three companies; 117 respondents completed the employee survey (response rate 62.9%), of whom 85.4% had a college degree and 82.9% described themselves as professional employees, with average age 35.87 years (SD = 8.64), average tenure 3.17 years (SD = 3.00), and 62.9% women. Fifty-six supervisors were asked to rate their employees' creativity and 46 took up the invitation (response rate 82.1%), each rating 2.6 employees on average (SD = 1.54); the matched supervisor-employee sample was 117, with missing values reducing usable observations to 114 for some variables. Employees reported cognitively demanding work with items based on Jackson et al. (1993) and used by Wall et al. (1995, 1996), including three items beyond those used in Study 1 (alpha = .80, 1 to 5 scale). The two daydream facets used 12 items selected from Singer and Antrobus (1972) on a 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) scale, with six items averaged into problem-oriented daydreams (alpha = .89) and six into bizarre daydreams (alpha = .89); all problem-oriented items were worded in terms of "at work", whereas no "at work" qualifier was attached to the bizarre daydream items. Professional identification used five items developed by Mael and Ashforth (1992) for organizational identification, with the organization placeholder replaced by "my profession" and one item omitted from the original six-item scale (alpha = .76, 1 to 7 scale). Supervisors rated creativity (alpha = .93) on a 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely) scale and rated effort, quality, and quantity of work as performance (alpha = .81), using items developed by Oldham and Cummings (1996). Organizational membership (dummy coded), education, tenure, and age were treated as controls; models run with and without them showed no substantive differences, and the reported results exclude them for parsimony. Analysis used confirmatory factor analyses in Mplus and multilevel structural equation modeling (path analysis) following Preacher, Zyphur, and Zhang (2010), supplemented with PROCESS models to evaluate unconditional and conditional indirect effects. Table 3 reports descriptive statistics for n = 114-117 and Table 4 reports the path analysis for n = 114.

The design is associational in both studies: all variables were measured rather than manipulated, and the authors state that their two studies did not allow them to determine the causal ordering of the hypothesized relations.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (multilevel structural equation modeling; unstandardized coefficients, Table 2 and Figure 1) supported Hypothesis 1: daily cognitively demanding work was positively related to both daily problem-oriented daydreams (b = .30, p < .01) and daily bizarre daydreams (b = .10, p < .05). Hypothesis 2 was partially supported: daily problem-oriented daydreams were positively related to daily creativity (b = .23, p < .01), but the link between daily bizarre daydreams and daily creativity was not statistically significant (b = .02, p > .05). Hypothesis 3 was also partially supported: daily cognitively demanding work exerted a significant indirect effect on daily creativity via problem-oriented daydreams (b = .07, p < .01) but not via bizarre daydreams (b = .01, p > .05). For performance, on which no formal hypotheses were advanced, neither daydream facet related significantly to daily performance (ps > .05) and neither indirect effect of daily cognitively demanding work on daily performance was significant (ps > .05); the authors note that, contrary to previous work suggesting daydreaming can undermine performance, they find no evidence of such an effect in this sample.

Study 2 (path analysis, Table 4 and Figure 2) reproduced this pattern and added the moderation test. Hypothesis 1 was supported: cognitively demanding work related positively to both problem-oriented (b = .48, p < .01) and bizarre (b = .32, p < .05) daydreams. Hypothesis 2 was partially supported: problem-oriented daydreams were positively related to creativity (b = .18, p < .01) while the bizarre daydreams-creativity link was nonsignificant (b = .07, p > .05). Hypothesis 3 was partially supported: the indirect effect of cognitively demanding work on creativity via problem-oriented daydreams was significant (b = .09, p < .05) but the indirect effect via bizarre daydreams was not (b = .02, p > .05). Hypothesis 4 was partially supported. The interaction between problem-oriented daydreams and professional identification was positively related to creativity (b = .12, p < .05), with a significant positive simple slope when identification was high (b = .31, p < .01) but not when it was low (b = .00, p > .05), and the conditional indirect effect was positive and significant at high identification (b = .14, p < .05) but not at low identification (b = .01, p > .05). The interaction between bizarre daydreams and professional identification was positively related to creativity only marginally (b = .09, p = .06), with a significant positive slope at high identification (b = .21, p < .01) and a nonsignificant negative slope at low identification (b = -.06, p > .05), and its conditional indirect effect was positive and marginally significant at high identification (b = .06, p = .10) but not at low identification (b = .01, p > .05).

On performance in Study 2, neither daydream facet related significantly to performance overall (ps > .05), but both interactions with professional identification were statistically significant. The interaction between problem-oriented daydreams and professional identification was related to performance (b = .13, p < .01), such that there was no significant relation between problem-oriented daydreams and performance when identification was high (b = .10, p > .05) but a negative relation when it was low (b = -.17, p < .05). The interaction between bizarre daydreams and professional identification was related to performance (b = .12, p = .01), such that the relation between bizarre daydreams and performance was significantly positive when identification was high (b = .13, p < .05) and significantly negative when it was low (b = -.08, p < .05). The authors summarize the joint pattern as daydreams spurring creativity while also jeopardizing performance for employees who lack professional identification.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reframes workplace mind wandering by showing that daydreaming can be a source of creativity rather than only a liability. It differentiates problem-oriented and bizarre daydreams and identifies professional identification as a boundary condition shaping whether employees convert daydreaming into creative value.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations that depend on creativity should be cautious about treating daydreaming only as distraction or deviance. The findings suggest that professionally identified employees may benefit from more license to daydream and from reduced stigma around discussing ideas that emerge from such episodes.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the two studies do not establish the causal ordering of cognitively demanding work, daydreaming, and creativity. They also note that the designs measured quotidian and generalized tendencies rather than specific daydreaming episodes.

**Future Research**
Future research could use experimental designs and finer-grained experience sampling to test causal ordering and episode-level dynamics. The authors also call for direct tests of the mechanisms linking cognitively demanding work to different daydream types and of whether problem-oriented and bizarre daydreams map onto different stages or forms of creativity.
