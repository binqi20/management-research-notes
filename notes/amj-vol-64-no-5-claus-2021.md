# Institutional Translation Gone Wrong: The Case of Villages for Africa in Rural Tanzania

**Abstract**
Why do ideas that have been successfully moved across highly different contexts subsequently disappear? To answer this question, we use longitudinal data on the organization Villages for Africa that introduced “macrocredit” loans to rural Tanzanians that would enable them to establish their own village enterprises. Several years after the implementation of the idea, it collapsed. Our findings allow us to make two key contributions. First, we provide a process model of high-distance translation that shows how proponents can strategically introduce an idea across highly different contexts by “culturally detaching” it from its institutional origins, leading to the idea being “culturally assimilated” into the recipient context. However, although cultural detachment and cultural assimilation indicate the successful translation of an idea, the means of doing so can later prompt its rejection. We call this the “reactance effect” of translations across highly different contexts. Second, we showcase the role of history for translation theory more generally. History—particularly the historical relationship between the sociocultural categories of the mzungu (Swahili for “foreigner”) and the villagers— influenced the way in which the macrocredit idea could be introduced to villagers and played a key role in its subsequent rejection.

**Research Question**
The paper asks why ideas that appear to have been successfully moved across high institutional distance may later disappear. It examines this question through Villages for Africa's macrocredit initiative in rural Tanzania, focusing on how an initially accepted development idea became rejected after adoption.

**Mechanism Process**
- Contextual conditions: Historical suspicion of the mzungu coexisted with hope and admiration for foreign-backed development initiatives.
- Preadoption translation: VFA culturally detached the macrocredit idea from its foreign origins through local surrogates, political cooptation of elders, localized customization, and ceremonial affirmation.
- Postadoption translation: Villagers culturally assimilated the idea through inclusive-exclusivity, materialization of social norms, and emotive celebration, creating a locally owned interpretation of the village enterprises.
- Collision and aftermath: Requests for repayment and renewed VFA involvement triggered in-group shaming, historically anchored vilification, anger, and reluctance to reengage with foreigners.

The process is not a simple failure of initial adoption. The paper argues that cultural detachment helped VFA gain acceptance, while cultural assimilation made the idea meaningful and locally owned for villagers. The same dual success later produced a reactance effect: when VFA reappeared to advise the enterprises and request repayment, villagers interpreted this as a violation of local ownership and as another instance of mzungu exploitation.

**Data & Measures**
Longitudinal qualitative case study of VFA's macrocredit project in four Tanzanian villages (A, B, C, and D); constructs were coded interpretively rather than measured, and no causal identification is claimed. Data collection ran in three rounds designed to "follow events forward" and "trace them backward" (Langley & Tsoukas, 2010). The first round (late-2013 until mid-2014) built familiarity with the empirical context through texts on Tanzania's history, internal VFA documents, and five pilot interviews with Willem Mark (a pseudonym), who conceived VFA in 2008, and his European team. The second round centred on 18 days of fieldwork in Manyara by the first author between July and September of 2014, during which she shared offices with VFA and VEI employees for roughly 10 hours per working day, alongside the third author's ongoing engagement as an early local employee of the Village Enterprise Incubator (VEI) and, from 2016, as a coauthor. The third round, from January 2016 until January 2020, traced the aftermath of the collapse through follow-up interviews with VFA, VEI, villagers, and regional politicians.

Three sources of data were combined and are summarized in the paper's Table 1: observations, interviews, and documentary material. Observation totalled 141 formal observation hours by the first author across VFA headquarters in France, VEI, the village enterprises, and group meetings, a figure the table's note says includes only formal observation hours by the first author and excludes hours spent in the context by the third author as a local resident; the first author also kept a personal diary and a field diary, and videoconferences between VFA and VEI were recorded on Zoom and QuickTime from as early as 2013. Interviews numbered 109: five pilot interviews by the first author before fieldwork and 32 during fieldwork, 38 conducted by the third author after the first author left the field in 2014, and a further 34 follow-up interviews (mainly with former village enterprise members) conducted later to refine the analysis. Interviewees comprised 17 VFA members, 20 VEI members, 47 village enterprise members, 20 village residents, three investors in the macrocredit project, and two regional politicians. Interviews with villagers were conducted in Swahili and later translated into English; where the Internet connection was poor, written chat or email was used; 61 interviews were recorded and transcribed and the remainder were written up from real-time notes within 24 hours; interviews lasted between 12 minutes and 3 hours and averaged 41 minutes. The authors also participated in 10 meetings involving VFA and village enterprise members, and complemented formal interviews with daily informal conversations conducted mainly by the third author. Written material amounted to 1,186 pages (VFA internal documents including emails 138 pp., a VFA director's personal diary and blog entries 87 pp., VEI strategy documents 13 pp., village enterprise internal documents including vignettes 39 pp., and books and academic articles on Tanzania's (pre-)colonial history 234 pp. and on postcolonial politics, legal system, and economy 675 pp.), supplemented by 67 minutes of video material — VFA presentations to public audiences and investors, and local TV reports on the village enterprises. Five self-written "vignettes" narrating personal experiences of relevant events were written by Tanzanian informants themselves, following Jarzabkowski and Lê (2017), in order to preserve their original voices, feelings, and modes of expression.

Analysis was iterative, moving between data, emerging analysis, and existing literature, in three steps. Step 1 developed a rich case description that synthesized the sources into an empirical narrative and divided the data into contextual conditions plus three analytically distinct phases (preadoption translation, postadoption translation, and collision and its aftermath); because the four village enterprises progressed through the phases in different years, the authors refrained from imposing time stamps. Step 2 used NVivo to code the raw data into core constructs: two for the contextual conditions ("historical suspicions" and "hope and admiration toward wazungu"), four for the preadoption phase ("using surrogates," "political cooptation," "localized customization," and "ceremonial affirmation"), three for the postadoption phase ("inclusive-exclusivity," "emotive celebration," and "materialization of social norms"), and two for the collision and its aftermath ("in-group shaming" and "historically anchored vilification"); these were then aggregated into the overarching theoretical dimensions of concealing foreignness, adaptive conveyance, cultural assimilation, and collision, with illustrative data reported in Tables 2a-2d. Step 3 connected the constructs and dimensions into the process model of high-distance translation failure shown in Figure 1. Four steps were taken to strengthen robustness: the composition of authorship (two authors familiar with Tanzanian culture, one of them from the local context, and a third at greater distance acting as "outsider" in the analysis), triangulation across multiple data sources and a comprehensive set of actors, the combination of real-time and secondary data to reduce retrospective rationalization, and sharing the findings with one VFA member and two local informants during data collection and analysis.

**Key Findings**
The study induces a three-phase process model of high-distance translation failure, preceded by ambivalent contextual conditions in which historical suspicion of the mzungu — villagers reminding one another of foreign organizations that ran trainings or installed water pumps and then left — coexisted with hope and admiration for VFA's proposition.

In Phase 1 (preadoption translation, cultural detachment), VFA concealed the idea's foreignness by using surrogates (the four-member VEI, each member from a different ethnic group and all seen as locals rather than foreigners) and by politically coopting village elders, and adaptively conveyed the idea through localized customization of training in Swahili and local languages and through ceremonial affirmation, including plaques of achievement and the transfer of symbolic ownership shares with no economic value. The success of concealing foreignness "was reflected in the fact that 20 villages applied to be included in the VFA project." Outcome 1 was acritical adoption: each village quickly accepted the loan terms, formal contracts crafted in Swahili were signed committing villages to invest one third of profits in a chosen social need, one third to repayment, and the remainder to reinvestment, and there was "neither conflict nor negotiations over the terms of the loan." VFA knew the contracts were legally weak — "it was much more symbolic to have them sign these papers" — and imposed no repayment schedule, allowing up to 18 months at no interest before an average effective rate of 2-3% applied.

In Phase 2 (postadoption translation, cultural assimilation), the villages showed entrepreneurial initiative and three of the four — B, C, and D — reported profits in their first year; the fourth, Village A, did not, and was the only village that chose not to go into agricultural farming and, by siting its enterprise outside the village, found it harder to garner community support. Villagers assimilated the idea through three mutually reinforcing mechanisms: a sense of inclusive-exclusivity (management-team offices in the village centre, admiring visitors from neighbouring villages, local TV coverage, and even politicians' bribe demands read as signs of being special), materialization of social norms, and emotive celebration. Materialization and celebration were most pronounced in Villages B and C, whose visibly renovated classrooms and teacher's house produced joyous, lengthy public festivities; they were muted in Village D, whose health insurance for 490 households (ca. 1,470 villagers) was less visually tangible and drew few attendees to its card-distribution events. Outcome 2 was local ownership — but the phrase carried different meanings on each side, which neither side noticed: for VFA it meant villagers could profitably and independently operate the enterprises, while for villagers it meant widespread community acceptance of the enterprises as serving social needs ("if there is no social need, there is no need for business").

In Phase 3 (collision and its aftermath), VFA judged the moment right to scale up, which meant advising the villages on further business development and requesting repayment so funds could be reinvested elsewhere; "the response was contrary to what was expected." VEI members were rebuffed when offering advice ("it's their thing now") and were shamed in-group when requesting repayment, because villagers expected VEI to respect the ujamaa norm that "family" members do not ask for the return of something borrowed and accused them of serving the mzungu company over their own community; VFA members entering the villages met historically anchored vilification invoking colonialism and exploitation ("There is no colonialism here anymore. Why are you harassing me?"; "You made us laboratory animals!"). Relations deteriorated from "we" to "us versus them," with both sides feeling cheated, and the anger extended to VEI to the point of an incident of physical violence between a VEI member and a villager. Outcome 3 was anger and reluctance to reengage: overall VFA recovered less than 50% of its loans — Village D repaid its $10,000 loan in full, Villages B and C each repaid around 40%, and Village A paid less than 30%. VFA then pivoted toward macrofinanced village housing in a new cluster, ran into immigration corruption issues shortly afterward, and, unwilling to be coerced into paying, was forced to leave the country; the project and the VFA and VEI offices in Tanzania were shut down in 2016. Even Village D, which had repaid in full, refused further connection with VEI.

The central emergent finding is the "reactance effect" of high-distance translation: the very combination that made translation succeed — cultural detachment before adoption and cultural assimilation after it — invokes a sense of local ownership and hubris, so that attempts by outsiders to revoke or change the assimilated idea are resisted, and "Double success, in other words, paradoxically risks subsequent failure." The reactance remains latent until triggered; in this case the trigger was the physical reappearance of the mzungu, which recalled past failures and elicited anger. A second finding concerns history: because cultural detachment left the translation "thin," villagers never learned the implicit Western meanings of "local ownership," "entrepreneurship," and "contracts," and the recollections that surfaced at the collision carried "emotional registers" that drowned reflective reconstruction of the past — leading the authors to conclude that history attached to strong emotions "is not a resource but a possible liability." The paper also records that the failure was not universal in villagers' own accounts: some still hoped VFA might return, and one recalled the model as "the best alternative to an NGO."

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a process model of high-distance translation failure that distinguishes preadoption cultural detachment from postadoption cultural assimilation. It shows that the conditions enabling successful translation across very different sociocultural contexts can also heighten later rejection when recipients' locally owned interpretation collides with the proponent's assumptions. The paper also extends institutional translation theory by foregrounding history, sociocultural categories, and emotional registers as forces that shape both translation strategies and translation breakdown.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations moving social or business ideas across high institutional distance should not assume that local acceptance means shared understanding. The study suggests that proponents need to communicate their own sociocultural assumptions, including meanings attached to contracts, ownership, repayment, entrepreneurship, and accountability, while still translating those assumptions into recipient-language and recipient-context terms. It also implies that historically charged relationships can remain latent until a triggering event makes them central.

**Limitations**
The study is a single qualitative case of failure in rural Tanzania, and the authors explicitly note that generalizing from one failed case is difficult. The data predominantly capture local informants' perspectives, and the authors acknowledge that their longitudinal narrative was influenced by the third author's interpretations and field relationships. The empirical setting is also a high-distance development context, so the model should be tested against other forms and degrees of institutional distance.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine whether the strategies and processes identified here characterize all high-distance translation contexts, and whether they are unique to such contexts compared with low-distance translations. Scholars could also study high sociocultural distance within countries, including racial, class, religious, ideological, or migration-related divides. The paper further calls for longer-term studies of translation, including how adapted ideas may be translated back into their context of origin as a form of reverse innovation.
