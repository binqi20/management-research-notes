# Who Should Become a Business School Associate Dean? Individual Performance and Taking on Firm-Specific Roles

**Abstract**
The "firm-specific human capital dilemma" suggests that firms generally want employees to make firm-specific investments but that employees prefer not to make them. We suggest that individual performance may moderate this dilemma such that the dilemma increases as individual performance increases—that is, firms may prefer high performers in firm-specific roles while high performers may resist these roles more than their lowerperforming counterparts. We examine our extended firm-specific human capital theory in a context in which the classic firm-specific human capital dilemma likely exists: business academia. Using a unique data set of 4,164 business school professors from 39 of the top 100 U.S. business schools, we examine how research performance affects propensity to become an associate dean and their compensation increases when taking on these roles. Even though associate dean roles come with a significant pay increase, we find that higherperformingindividualsare lesslikelytobecomeassociatedeans. Surprisingly,wefind that lower performers receive higher pay increases when taking on these roles. We conduct exploratory interviews to understand this surprising finding, and discuss implications and opportunities for future research.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how individual research performance affects a business school professor's likelihood of taking on a firm-specific administrative role as associate dean and the compensation increase attached to that role. More broadly, it asks whether individual performance intensifies the firm-specific human capital dilemma by increasing both firm desire for high performers and high performers' resistance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. A faculty member's probability of taking on a firm-specific administrative role is negatively related to that individual's research productivity.
Hypothesis 2. High-productive faculty receive higher total compensation increases when taking on firm-specific administrative roles than lower research-productive faculty.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Research performance/general human capital performance, measured primarily as cumulative Financial Times 50 publications.
- DV(s): Serving as an associate dean in a given year; log annual salary/compensation change when serving as associate dean.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Individual performance is theorized as the condition that changes the magnitude of the firm-specific human capital dilemma; performance also interacts with associate dean status in the salary models.

The mechanism is that universities often prefer research-productive faculty for firm-specific administrative roles because performance signals ability, credibility, and capacity to help others. At the same time, high performers face higher opportunity costs and mobility risks because research productivity is portable and externally valued. The authors therefore predict a lower probability of becoming an associate dean as research productivity increases, while also expecting stronger inducements for high performers. The first prediction is supported; the compensation prediction is reversed, with lower-performing associate deans receiving larger salary increases.

**Data & Measures**
Individual-level data on salaries, tenure, and administrative positions were obtained from records departments, HR departments, and/or online public databases for 4,162 professors from 39 of the top 100 U.S. business schools (as ranked by U.S. News and World Report in 2016), giving 37,206 professor-year observations, roughly nine years of salary observations for each professor on average, over a sample period that roughly covers 1990-2017. Assistant professors and professors with no salary data or with inaccurate salary data were removed; the main results use a $30K total-compensation cutoff, with robustness to $50K, $75K, and $100K cutoffs. After data cleaning the final sample is 1,639 professors for 14,651 professor-years. The salary data were supplemented by additional harvesting efforts using research assistants and redundant Mechanical Turk assignments to recover details such as PhD completion year and department, and professors were classified into accounting (14%), economics (9%), finance (14%), information systems (8%), management (31%), marketing (17%), and operations (7%). Publication data were collected from SCOPUS and Web of Science, retaining only peer-reviewed publications.

The proxy for a firm-specific administrative role is whether the individual was an associate dean (AD) in a given year, coded 1 in any year the individual served as an AD and 0 otherwise, hand-collected from ADs' CVs across all schools in the sample. Compensation is the log of annual salary, the natural log of the professor's annual financial compensation; across all years the mean salary was $176K with a minimum of $32K and a maximum of $631K, and salaries were logged to adjust for skew. Most universities reported a nine-month base salary while a few reported total annual compensation including summer support, which the authors addressed through a school fixed effect. Performance on general human capital (GHC) tasks is proxied by a cumulative count of a professor's Financial Times 50 (FT50) publications. Controls are Leaving in Next Year (whether this was the individual's last year at that institution), Experience (years since PhD completion), Full Professor, Administrator (coded 1 for a professor serving in a department- or program-level role such as program director or area chair in the prior year, with higher-level administrators such as provosts and chancellors excluded), and Department, alongside individual, school, and year fixed effects that differ by model.

Hypothesis 1 was tested with a logit model predicting whether an individual was an AD in a given year using an interacted school-year fixed effect, so that the comparisons are between persons within school-year combinations; the logit models use 6,215 observations across 219 school-year groups and the parallel OLS linear probability models use 13,012 observations across 635 school-year groups, with robust standard errors clustered at the school-year level. Hypothesis 2 was tested with OLS on 14,651 observations using fixed effects for individual and year, capturing the within-individual effect of becoming an AD on salary, with robust standard errors clustered at the individual level. The design is observational panel data with fixed effects rather than an explicit causal identification strategy, and the paper states the relationships associationally, describing higher research productivity as correlating with higher compensation and noting that actual resistance cannot be observed in the secondary data. Robustness checks include nearest-neighbor matching of ADs to non-ADs, a composite performance measure incorporating both publication counts and citation impact, person-school and school-year interacted fixed effects, and both removing and controlling for stars coded at the top 90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles of research productivity.

The exploratory qualitative component comprises semi-structured, in-person interviews with a convenience sample of 10 individuals who were serving or had previously served as ADs, analyzed with a grounded theory methodology using the constant comparative method, recorded and transcribed, and discontinued when saturation was reached; a separate set of interviews with 14 former or current deans from top 100 U.S. business schools about the university's perspective; an email to 36 current deans and ADs that shared the unanticipated compensation finding and asked them to select and rank-order the explanations they felt best explained it or choose an "other" option, which yielded 20 responses; and a follow-up email to those 20 asking whether AD packages such as reduced teaching load or increased research funds were standard or customized to each AD, which yielded 10 responses.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. The coefficient on GHC performance is negative in the logit models, and the paper interprets Model 2 as meaning that a one-unit increase in performance, that is one additional FT50 publication, decreases the odds of becoming an AD by 6.2%. A stripped-down logit specification and the OLS linear probability models point the same way, and the paper reports that both the logit and the linear probability models are consistent with the predictions of Hypothesis 1.

Associate dean roles do carry a pay premium: the coefficient on the AD indicator in the log-salary models is positive and significant, which the paper interprets in its fully specified model as a 21.7% higher salary when serving as an AD than when not, corresponding to approximately $38.1K in annual salary on average and making the AD role roughly 22 times more financially beneficial than publishing one more FT50 article. The main effect of research performance on salary is also positive and significant, translating to approximately $1.7K in compensation increase per FT50 publication per year at the margin.

Hypothesis 2 is not supported, and the result runs in the opposite direction to the prediction. The interaction between professor performance and serving as an AD is negative and statistically significant, whereas the hypothesis predicted a positive and significant interaction, so as performance increases the salary bump for being an AD decreases. The gap between AD and non-AD is greatest at the lowest end of the performance range and smallest, and no longer statistically different, at the highest end. The average salary increase for becoming an AD is $34.4K at the 25th percentile of performance and $31.4K at the 75th percentile, so the pay increase associated with becoming an AD at the 25th percentile is 9.6% higher than at the 75th percentile.

Supplemental archival analyses are consistent with resistance as the underlying mechanism: ADs have significantly lower forward research productivity, top performers sacrificed significantly more research productivity than low performers when serving as ADs, and individuals who had served as ADs were significantly less likely to move schools, so AD investments do not increase mobility. Only nine of the 111 ADs in the sample ever became full deans and only 13% overall stayed in administrative roles after serving as ADs. The main results are substantively unchanged under nearest-neighbor matching, the citation-based composite performance measure, alternative fixed-effects specifications, and both removing and controlling for stars, and there is no significant change in total compensation when individuals leave AD roles and return to the faculty.

The exploratory qualitative evidence converges with the resistance interpretation and supplies candidate explanations for the reversed compensation result. Of the 14 deans interviewed, nine (64%) rejected the notion that they excluded faculty from AD consideration because of their research productivity, and 13 (93%) indicated resistance and/or getting turned down when inviting faculty to take on AD roles. The interviewees suggested three explanations for the surprising compensation result: compensation salary bands, equity theory, and opportunity costs. Among the 20 AD/dean survey respondents, the most frequently mentioned explanation (16 of 20) was that universities have a set salary band with an upper limit for the AD position that high-performing faculty are already in or near, so low performers' salaries must be raised by a greater amount to reach that band; an equity-theory logic of minimizing salary variance across a school's ADs was supported by 12 of 20. In the follow-up survey, nine of the 10 responding deans reported that non-monetary compensation such as teaching loads was largely standard for ADs, so differentiated non-salary compensation does not appear to be a prevalent practice.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends firm-specific human capital theory by making individual performance a moderator of the classic firm-specific human capital dilemma rather than treating employee resistance as uniform. It shows that the dilemma can intensify for high performers because firm demand and employee resistance move in opposite directions as performance rises. The exploratory evidence further refines the theory by showing how salary bands and equity concerns can constrain the financial inducements offered to high performers. From the extended theory the authors additionally derive a zone of misaligned incentives, which they present as a logical conclusion they did not explicitly test, along with possible boundary conditions around star performers.

**Practical Implication**
For business schools, associate dean selection involves a trade-off: high-performing faculty may bring credibility and capability but sacrifice more research productivity and may resist the role more strongly. Lower-performing faculty may gain more financially from the role and may be easier to attract, but schools need to assess whether they can perform the administrative work effectively. Compensation systems based on salary bands or internal equity may unintentionally make high performers less willing to accept firm-specific administrative roles.

**Limitations**
The setting is business academia, where tenure, visible publication metrics, nonprofit missions, and public salary information may make the human capital dilemma different from other professional service firms. The authors also cannot directly observe resistance in the archival data and therefore use exploratory interviews and supplemental analyses to support that mechanism. Generalizability depends on whether other contexts have similar job security, performance transparency, relative value of general versus firm-specific investments, and organizational mission.

**Future Research**
Future research could test whether the proposed performance-based dilemma appears in consulting, financial services, legal services, health care, and other professional service settings. The authors also call for work on star performers, where firms may stop wanting the highest performers to take firm-specific roles because their continued general human capital production is too valuable. Additional research should examine other factors that moderate individual resistance to firm-specific investments and the conditions under which firms compensate, undercompensate, or avoid assigning such roles.
