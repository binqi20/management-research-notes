# Navigating Space for Personal Agency: Auxiliary Routines as Adaptations in Toxic Organizations

**Abstract**
Many workers experience organization dysfunction stemming from leaders. Yet organization members have limited responses; they can directly or indirectly confront senior leaders, engage individual stress coping strategies, or leave the organization. We offer another response by theorizing auxiliary routines as behavioral sequences through which multiple actors coordinate responses to complex and enduring socioemotional dynamics that threaten to undermine the enactment of standard operating task routines. Through a qualitative, inductive study of a consulting firm, we delimit three auxiliary routines—absorption, dissemination, and differentiation—through which people navigate between the destructiveness of organizational toxicity and the need to perform given roles and tasks. We illustrate how these routines emerged in response to role and psychological diminishment originating from senior leaders, how the routines helped manage and sometimes perpetuate diminishment, and the consequences for individuals’ personal agency and the organization as a whole. In doing so, we contribute to knowledge about coping with toxic organizational conditions and on routines as a facet of emotional capability in organizations.

**Research Question**
How do organization members work with and around toxic conditions that narrow personal agency while still accomplishing necessary work? The paper asks how recurring patterns of coordinated behavior allow managers and subordinates to navigate role and psychological diminishment originating from senior leadership.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: organizational toxicity, CEO centrality, CEO aggression, role diminishment, psychological diminishment, personal agency, focal routines, and auxiliary routines.
- Process / routines: absorption, dissemination, and differentiation.
- Triggering conditions: persistent diminishment by senior leaders, collusion by other Alpha members, and hierarchical boundaries that varied in permeability.
- Emergent outcomes: held, narrowed, or expanded space for personal agency; maintenance, amplification, or separation from diminishment; and reduced organizational growth.
- Boundary conditions: the routines depended on middle leaders' positions between senior leaders and group members, and differentiation depended on a distinct client base and external network.

The process begins with toxic organizational conditions that reduce members' role authority and psychological space. Middle leaders respond by initiating auxiliary routines: absorption buffers subordinates and holds limited space for agency, dissemination aligns middle leaders upward and amplifies diminishment downward, and differentiation creates more separate group space that expands agency. These routines help work continue, but they also stabilize the toxic system and limit firm-wide integration and growth.

**Data & Measures**
The site is AMP, a pseudonym for a consulting firm based in the New England area of the United States that serves clients in New England and New York. At the time of data collection the firm had been in business for approximately 15 years and had served more than 300 clients, and members were organized around four key business areas labeled Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Epsilon plus a senior leadership group labeled Alpha. Over the course of a year the authors set out to engage everyone at the firm, and the sample included all 34 people employed by the organization (10 men, 24 women) during the data collection period; average tenure was approximately four years and average age 34.4 years. The primary data were 57 interviews with 34 participants: Wave 1 consisted of 32 interviews (two members had yet to join the firm), and Wave 2, conducted 6 months after the conclusion of Wave 1, consisted of 25 interviews (four members had left, three members were unavailable, and two new members joined); all members currently employed at the firm were asked to participate in Wave 2. During the first four months of data collection the authors also participated in or observed 14.5 hours of meetings, comprising two meetings with the senior leadership team, full staff meetings, business area meetings, and meetings to prepare pitches to new clients; the paper treats this observational material as providing a supplemental understanding to the interview data. At the conclusion of data collection the authors met with senior leadership to share initial findings and hear their reactions; the CEO was unable to attend, which left the COO and CFO as her representatives, and observations from that meeting provided additional evidence for the emergent theory.

Interview questions were semi-structured and evolved over the course of data collection in the spirit of grounded theory (Locke, 2001; Charmaz, 2006; Corbin & Strauss, 2008). Given an initial interest in creativity, early interviews asked how people understood creativity and what inhibited or enabled it within the organization. Noticing that participants were sometimes reluctant to share their perspectives and were much more focused on the challenges of getting their work done, the authors shifted the protocol toward members' experiences of being overwhelmed and destabilized, and conducted the second wave to delve further into those emergent topics. Data reduction used contact summary forms (Miles & Huberman, 1994), regular meetings to discuss emerging themes, research memos (Charmaz, 2006), and constant comparison, iteratively moving between data and emergent themes.

Formal coding proceeded in three steps. Step 1 mapped the contributions of individuals: interviews were coded for the different task and socioemotional contributions people made, with work tasks including activities like "managing clients," "developing proposals," "responding to emails from superiors," and "attending new business meetings," and socioemotional tasks including "supporting Jane," "protecting subordinates," "providing a shoulder to cry on," and "explaining Jane's behavior," and each person's primary tasks were then mapped onto the organizational chart. Step 2 analyzed diminishment dynamics by coding descriptions of behaviors and experiences of when and how people were made to feel small, then grouping them to describe how members' roles were reduced in scope, authority, or autonomy in favor of the CEO's centrality and importance (sample codes: not being authorized to work with clients, not being backed up when clients complained, work being taken away) and how members were treated as insignificant, incompetent, unworthy, unimportant, and in other ways "less than" the CEO (sample codes: "yelled at for making a mistake," "yelled at for asking a question," "received snarky emails"). Step 3 mapped auxiliary routines and their outcomes: the authors analyzed how each middle leader responded to diminishments of themselves and their group members and the interactions that unfolded as a consequence, focused on how people managed hierarchical boundaries and engaged their tasks and roles, triangulated from multiple people's interviews and their own observations, mapped out the key steps in the observed patterns, and linked the routines to outcomes. Constructs were operationalized interpretively rather than measured. The design is inductive and qualitative, it reports no statistical estimates, and the paper explicitly pursues theoretical rather than statistical generalization, offering principles that explain action rather than predictions, so it makes no causal-identification claim.

**Key Findings**
Recurring diminishment at AMP took two forms with distinct supports. Role diminishment, defined as recurring behaviors that reduced the size, scope, and authority of people's roles, was tied to the centrality of Jane, the firm's founder and CEO, which three organizational factors maintained: the business model, which featured the promise of the CEO's personal attention to clients and left members in the background of their own client work; the decision-making process, in which Jane insisted on seeing written work and presentations multiple times before they were released to clients and became a bottleneck; and a vacuum of organizational structures, including a lack of structured interdependence across groups, staff meetings that were repeatedly moved or cancelled, and the absence of a formal human resources function. Psychological diminishment, being treated personally as "less than," was maintained by Jane's aggression, which the authors read as anxiety acted out and projected onto others rather than contained, and by the collusion of key Alpha members, principally Hannah, the COO, who chose to excuse rather than confront Jane's behavior, and Judy, the marketing director, in what the paper describes as an abuser-abused pairing. The authors locate both types of diminishment in the system rather than in simply a destructive leader.

Middle leaders responded by initiating three auxiliary routines, each a four-part sequence with a different consequence for diminishment and for the space available for personal agency. Absorption was the primary, default routine, enacted by Belle (Beta), Naomi and Rita (Gamma), Jessica (Delta), and Blaine (Epsilon): middle leaders received and absorbed diminishment, provided emotional support, sense-giving, and perspective to group members, distanced themselves from senior executives, and implicitly reinforced the status quo of diminishment. Its purpose was to create protective spaces enabling others' work; it held the space for personal agency, though only bounded agency within circumscribed limits, while maintaining recurring diminishment rather than dispelling it. Dissemination, enacted by Taylor (Gamma) and Jessica (Delta), ran the other way: middle leaders embraced Alpha expectations about client service and growth, dismissed group members' struggles, blamed group members and contributed to their diminishment, and justified the CEO's centrality and aggression. Its purpose was to protect the self in moments of overwhelm and struggle; it amplified recurring diminishment and sharply narrowed the space for personal agency. Differentiation, enacted by Blaine and Sean (Epsilon), arose under three conditions the paper names: focal routines distinct from those under the purview of Alpha leaders, a distinct base of power in the group's own clients, and success in growing that segment of the business. In it, middle leaders cultivated distinct external networks, used those external relationships to physically separate themselves and their members from organizational dynamics, group members created significant intragroup work attachments that minimized their need for connections to senior leaders, and group members emotionally disconnected from senior leaders. Its purpose was to establish ownership of client work by leveraging a distinct base of power; it separated members from diminishment and expanded the space for personal agency.

Which routine was enacted at any particular moment was a function of how well and for how long middle leaders were able to protect themselves or others from diminishment, and the three routines differed in boundary permeability: under absorption the boundaries between middle and senior leaders were impermeable enough to withstand diminishment, under dissemination they were overly permeable, and under differentiation they were impermeable to the point that group members attached to and identified with local rather than senior leaders. Collectively the routines foreclosed organizational growth. Because all three emphasized members' relationships with their middle leaders rather than with senior leaders or the firm, they left middle leaders and members unwilling or unable to attend to growth activities driven by integration across groups and centered in the firm itself. Each of the three senior-leader interventions failed: the formal brainstorming sessions any member was free to invoke were never actually invoked; the reconfigured office space created more common areas but did little to instigate collaboration on behalf of strategic growth; and the strategic plan for Beta to develop digital products complementing the other groups' analog work floundered, with members largely avoiding training and treating digital content as Beta's responsibility while Alpha leaders sold against Beta in client pitches and took no responsibility for the lack of digital integration. The explanation the paper gives is the paradoxical reality of the CEO occupying so much of the center of the firm that there was little space for executive leadership to occur.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends routine theory by theorizing auxiliary routines as emergent, coordinated adaptations to socioemotional complexity, not only as supports for focal task routines. It also contributes to research on toxic organizations by shifting attention from individual coping choices to system-level patterns through which members collectively manage and perpetuate toxicity. Finally, it links auxiliary routines to emotional capability by showing how routines can regulate collective emotional energy, either supporting or handicapping members' availability for work.

**Practical Implication**
The study suggests that organization members facing toxic conditions may develop recurring pathways for protecting themselves and others while still doing their work, but these pathways have costs. Middle leaders need to attend to whether their boundary-management routines buffer others, transmit toxicity, or create healthier independent bases of agency. Leaders and consultants should examine how structures, decision processes, and collusion authorize some people while deauthorizing others, because those patterns shape both personal agency and actual work.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the theorizing is limited by one specific organization with idiosyncratic personality, political, hierarchical, structural, and cultural dynamics. AMP involved a particular form of toxicity centered on CEO centrality and aggression, so other organizations with peer-, partner-, or customer-generated toxicity may develop different auxiliary routines. The small case setting also leaves open whether a larger cohort of middle leaders would produce variations on these routines or different mechanisms for managing collective emotional energy.

**Future Research**
Future research could chart the range, frequency, triggers, and implications of other auxiliary routines across different toxic organizational contexts. Studies could examine whether routines focus on boundary negotiation, coalition building, dissent, or other responses when toxicity has different sources. Additional empirical work could also investigate how auxiliary routines relate to emotional capability in settings marked by emotional exhaustion, emotional labor, dysfunctional decision-making, or unacknowledged organizational disturbance.
