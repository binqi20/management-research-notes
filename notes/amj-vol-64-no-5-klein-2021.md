# The Dynamics of Framing: Image, Emotion, and the European Migration Crisis

**Abstract**
an inherently dynamic process.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how and why the framing of a contentious societal issue changes over time, with particular attention to emotions in media organizations. Empirically, it examines how the publication of the Alan Kurdi photograph altered U.K. newspaper framing of the European migration crisis.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / frames: migrant frame, refugee frame, frame prominence, framing bricolage, emotional array, iconic photograph, receptive ideology, non-receptive ideology, emotional fatigue.
- Process / phases: pre-image framing of displaced people, publication and circulation of the Alan Kurdi photograph, rapid frame-emphasis shift, emotional-array amplification/degradation, newsroom behavior change, and temporal diminishing of the image effect.
- Practices / analytic moves: historical contextualization, frame identification, frame-prominence coding, emotional-intensity coding, visual analysis, and interview-based reconstruction of newsroom mechanisms.
- Emergent outcomes: the refugee frame gained prominence, the migrant frame lost dehumanizing emotional force, resource allocation and campaigning changed in some newspapers, and the duration of change varied by ideology and emotional fatigue.
- Boundary conditions: the process depends on an emotionally powerful image, media organizations as framing agents, audience expectations, organizational ideology, and the continuing salience of the issue.

The process begins with two historically available frames: migrants as economic or social threat and refugees as vulnerable people needing protection. The Alan Kurdi photograph carried emotional cues that aligned with the refugee frame, intensifying compassion, solidarity, anger, and shame while weakening disgust, fear, anger, and dehumanizing language in the migrant frame. In receptive newspapers, emotional energy and moral justification supported longer-lasting reframing and action; in non-receptive newspapers, perceived reader expectations produced a more instrumental and shorter-lived shift. Emotional fatigue and competing news issues then reduced attention, although the emotional arrays remained altered.

**Data & Measures**
Two sources of data were drawn on to capture changes in the U.K. media's framing of the European migration crisis: newspaper articles and semi-structured interviews. Constructs were coded interpretively rather than measured, and no causal identification strategy is claimed. Articles came from the 10 U.K. national newspapers with sales of more than 100,000 daily copies — the right-favoring The Sun, Daily Mail, The Daily Telegraph, Daily Star, Daily Express, The Times, The Financial Times; the left-leaning The Guardian and Daily Mirror; and the centerist The Independent. Following protocols established elsewhere, a LexisNexis keyword search was conducted in each newspaper using "migration OR immigration OR migrant OR refugee OR asylum," and only articles in which the keywords were present in the headline were included. The pre-image window ran from May 8 to September 2, 2015 — data collection began one day after the U.K. General Election of May 7, 2015, to avoid articles distorted by election campaign activities — and the post-image window from September 3, 2015, to September 2, 2016, representing 118 days before and 366 days after publication; this generated 1,032 articles prior to the release of the image and 3,826 afterward. A subset from the leftist The Guardian and the right-favoring The Daily Telegraph, selected for ideological balance and for a tradition of serious and considered engagement with major societal issues, consisted of 382 articles before the image and 1,573 afterward; identical articles appearing in more than one issue of a newspaper were included only once. The authors also conducted 17 semi-structured interviews with editors and journalists who were involved with newspapers during the period of the crisis, drawn from The Guardian, The Daily Telegraph, The Independent, The Times, The Sun, Daily Mail, and Daily Mirror; interviews lasted between 30 and 60 minutes and were fully transcribed, with the transcript returned to the interviewee for checking, and were continued until no new insights into newspaper practices were emerging.

Analysis spanned five phases. Step 1 identified the frames: building on the historical contextualization, the subset articles were coded to uncover the idea elements forming the "building blocks" of frames in use, using a constant comparative analysis approach, yielding two frames in which displaced people were framed either as "migrants," pursuing a better way of life, or "refugees," fleeing a perilous situation in their home countries; robustness was checked by laying out the findings and method to a "disinterested peer" (a highly experienced qualitative scholar not involved in the study) and by describing the two frames to an industry insider (an experienced former editor of a quality newspaper). Step 2 determined frame prominence in two ways: applying a pattern-deducing approach and the Step 1 frame descriptions, the first author coded each of the 1,955 subset articles as representative of the refugee frame, the migrant frame, neutral, or irrelevant, and the second author coded 10% selected at random as a coding check, with agreement of 95.1%; articles coded as irrelevant were excluded from all subsequent analyses. Separately, occurrences of the keywords "migrant(s)" and "refugee(s)" were counted in each of the 4,858 articles and collated by ideological position, with The Sun, Daily Mail, The Daily Telegraph, Daily Star, Daily Express, The Times, and The Financial Times constituting the right-leaning group, The Guardian and Daily Mirror the left-favoring group, and The Independent excluded because of its centrist positioning.

Step 3 uncovered emotions by returning to the subset articles classified as constituting the migrant and refugee frames — a total of 709 articles: 199 before and 510 after publication of the image. Text was inductively coded in context for three types of emotional expression: specific emotional utterances, descriptions of emotional encounters, and accounts of activities expressing emotions; in total 3,006 passages of text in which emotions were present were isolated, from which seven prevalent emotions emerged, with "anger," "fear," and "disgust" associated with the migrant frame and "compassion," "solidarity," "anger," and "shame" aligned with the refugee frame. Intensity was then captured through a novel form of emotional "magnitude coding" against an emotions coding book with descriptions for different levels of intensity of each emotion, created following a review of the emotions literature and operationalized for the study's context; a 4-point scale proved difficult to apply consistently, so the authors reverted to a 3-point scale, with scores assigned to each emotion of 1 (low), 2 (medium), and 3 (high), and passages could carry multiple emotions at varying levels (for example, "Fear2, Anger3"). The second author analyzed 336 passages, over 10% for each emotion, with coding agreement of 95.8%. Step 4 was a visual analysis of the Kurdi photograph, coded through Barthes's (1981) distinction between the "studium" — "rational, coded, and cultural" — and the "punctum" — "emotional, uncoded, and personal" — focusing on the objects and relationships apparent within the photograph and informed by the surrounding discursive elements. Step 5 qualitatively uncovered the mechanisms of framing change across newspaper organizations, leaning heavily on the interview data from editors and reporters, supplemented with articles published in newspapers that related to the impact and outcomes of the photograph; the data were reviewed to identify initial concepts noted using in vivo or first-order codes, which were systematically grouped through successive rounds of axial coding into higher-order themes, with the second author reviewing the process and results, independently coding 10% of the sample, and examining the identification and amalgamation of emergent themes, from which no new themes emerged. The emerging ideas were further tested in discussions with others involved in framing research.

**Key Findings**
Frame prominence shifted rapidly after the photograph. In the Guardian and Daily Telegraph subset, of the 382 collated articles published prior to the death of Alan Kurdi, 138 (36.13%) were coded as constitutive of the migrant frame, 61 (15.97%) of the refugee frame, 105 (27.49%) as neutral, and 78 (20.42%) as irrelevant. In the first three days after the image, 35 of the 49 articles published (71.43%) were coded as featuring the refugee frame, while only three (6.12%) exemplified the migrant frame, 10 (20.41%) were neutral, and one (2.04%) was irrelevant. Across the whole post-image period of 1,573 articles, 133 (8.46%) were coded as characteristic of the migrant frame, 377 (23.97%) as featuring the refugee frame, 541 (34.39%) as neutral, and 522 (33.19%) as irrelevant. The dramatic swing took place in September 2015, and from that point on the refugee frame remained dominant, only dropping below the migrant frame in August 2016.

The same shift appeared in vocabulary across the entire 10-newspaper data set. Article volume rose from 1,032 in the 118 days prior to the image to 3,826 for the 366 days afterward, a rise from 8.74 articles on average per day to 10.45. Before the picture was published, the word "migrant(s)" was used 4,393 times (average of 4.26 times per article) compared to 9,867 (average of 2.58 times per article) times afterward; the word "refugee(s)" had been used 1,409 times (average of 1.37 times per article) before publication and 12,376 times (average of 3.23 times per article) afterward. "Migrant(s)" was used more often than "refugee(s)" in all 10 of the U.K.'s major newspapers before the image was published; after publication this trend reversed, with all newspapers initially using "refugee" more often than "migrant," something sustained over the entire study period in four of the 10 newspapers (Daily Mirror, The Independent, The Guardian, and Financial Times), and the use of "refugee(s)" increased and the use of "migrant(s)" decreased after the release of the image in every newspaper. The photograph cut through the ideological leanings of newspapers, but the increase was markedly more extreme among the left-leaning newspapers than those to the right: right-oriented newspapers rapidly returned to preferencing "migrant(s)," though in a way less polarized, with "migrant(s)" used less and "refugee(s)" more than before publication, whereas among left-oriented newspapers "refugee(s)" retained an almost entirely unbroken dominant position over the course of the year while "migrant(s)" never reached the levels prior to publication. No other incident in the study period had a similar impact to the Kurdi photograph.

Emotional arrays changed in both frames. Before the image the migrant frame was constituted by anger — the dominant emotion — fear, and disgust, expressed at often extreme levels with regular use of dehumanizing language that generally avoided identifying individuals. After publication, no expressions of disgust were uncovered during the entire year, the levels of intensity at which both anger and fear were expressed significantly reduced, dehumanizing language was notably absent, and there was more emphasis on factual reporting rather than the use of emotive comments. In the refugee frame, analysis revealed four constituent emotions — compassion, solidarity, shame, and anger — expressed at low levels prior to Alan Kurdi's death; after publication all four remained but the levels of intensity became much more extreme. Only two passages were coded "Compassion3" before publication, both in the same article, compared with 46 afterward, and all passages coded "Shame3" occurred after the photograph. In sum, the most extreme levels of solidarity and shame were only expressed after the Kurdi image was published, and while there were some expressions of extreme compassion and anger beforehand, these were much more frequent afterward.

Interviews located the drivers of change inside newspaper organizations. Editors and journalists reported a powerful emotional impact — a picture editor recalled that when the photograph was shown in the midday news conference "the room fell very silent," and another editor reported that people in the newsroom cried — alongside an instrumental impact, in that interviewees perceived that the expectations of their readership had been altered. These translated into behavioral change: more resources and space were devoted to reporting the crisis, including more reporters and local freelance journalists on the ground, and several newspaper organizations set up petitions and campaigns. Left-leaning newspapers engaged more in activities to initiate change than right-leaning ones, although the authors state that their data do not allow them to derive ultimate conclusions on this point and instead point to more symbolic changes in behavior by right-leaning organizations. The duration of the effect was influenced by organizational ideology and by emotional fatigue: in organizations with a receptive ideology a powerful emotional impact and a weak instrumental impact produced a gradual temporal diminishing of the image effect, whereas in organizations with a non-receptive ideology a weak emotional impact and a powerful instrumental impact produced a rapid temporal diminishing and a return to an ideologically sustainable position. Contrary to the general expectation that the emotional effects of a photograph are short-lived, the photograph continued to have an effect one year after publication even among media firms that returned to favoring the migrant frame, and the emotional arrays of both frames remained significantly altered until the end of the study period.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends framing theory by showing that macro-level frames are more plastic and temporally dynamic than static accounts imply. It introduces emotional arrays as the combination of emotions, emotional intensity, and associated language through which frames gain meaning, legitimacy, and behavioral force. It also theorizes distinct mechanisms of framing change in media organizations with receptive versus non-receptive ideologies, showing how iconic images can reshape frame emphasis even when ideology would otherwise resist change.

**Practical Implication**
For actors trying to influence public policy or social debate, the study suggests that iconic photographs can create a window for reframing, but the mechanism and duration of that window vary across media organizations. Advocacy and policy actors should attend not only to message content, but also to the emotional array attached to competing frames and to whether target media organizations are ideologically receptive. The findings also caution that emotional fatigue and new events can quickly redirect media resources even after a powerful reframing moment.

**Limitations**
The authors identify scope conditions around the focal organizations, the medium of the photograph, and the extreme nature of the case. The empirical setting is U.K. newspapers, so the theorizing needs testing in other framing organizations such as social media platforms, television companies, governments, and nongovernmental organizations. The Alan Kurdi photograph was unusually powerful, so less extreme images or other forms of text may not generate the same cross-ideological effect.

**Future Research**
Future research could test the theory across other media, organizational, and policy settings, including social media and multimodal technologies. The authors call for work on how frame plasticity varies over time, whether hybrid frames emerge from conflicting frames, and how different emotional arrays produce different social outcomes. They also highlight emotional fatigue as a promising area for studying why reframing effects decay or persist.
