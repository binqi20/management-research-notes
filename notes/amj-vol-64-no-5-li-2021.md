# High Risk, Low Return (and Vice Versa): The Effect of Product Innovation on Firm Performance in a Transition Economy

**Abstract**
Common wisdom suggests that high-risk strategies will be associated with high expected returns and vice versa. Focusing on the effect of new product development on firm performance, in this paper we argue that this relationship may reverse in a market undergoing substantial institutional transition. We examine domestic pharmaceutical firms in China during the 1990s and find that in this context, introducing new products was associated with lower average firm profitability but higher variance. In conformity with our predictions, these relationships were stronger in areas where the rate of institutional change was higher and for product types that took longer to develop. Thus, we explain why, for particular strategic actions, high risk may be associated with low returns. A key conceptual corollary of these findings—and for strategic management research in general—is that firms may sometimes be more focused on the potential upside of their actions than on the expected value of those actions.

**Research Question**
The paper asks when a specific strategic action can generate a Bowman-type effect by increasing risk while decreasing expected return. It studies whether product innovation in China's transitioning pharmaceutical industry lowered average firm profitability while increasing variance in firm performance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: In the Chinese pharmaceutical industry during the period 1991-2000, product innovation is negatively related to firm performance.
- H1b: In the Chinese pharmaceutical industry during the period 1991-2000, product innovation is positively related to variance in firm performance.
- H2: The relationship between product innovation and firm performance is moderated by the length of the product development process, so that the effects in Hypotheses 1a and 1b are stronger when the product development process is longer.
- H3: The relationship between product innovation and firm performance is moderated by the rate of institutional change in the firm's focal market, so that the effects in Hypotheses 1a and 1b are stronger where the rate of institutional change is higher.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Product innovation, measured as the number of self-developed new drugs introduced by a firm.
- DV(s): Firm performance, measured as annual return on sales, and variance in firm performance.
- Mediators: Not modeled as mediators; the theorized process is value-appropriability uncertainty created by institutional transition and lengthy product development.
- Moderators: Length of the product development process and rate of institutional change in the focal provincial market; post hoc moderators include IPR protection and bribery prosecution.

The mechanism is that long product-development lead times expose innovating firms to unpredictable shifts in institutional rules, drug approval, pricing, and appropriability conditions. In this setting, new products could occasionally create large upside gains, but more often the targeted performance peak had diminished by commercialization, producing lower average returns and higher variance. The authors argue that firms may still innovate because they focus on upside potential and visible top innovators rather than expected value.

**Data & Measures**
Quantitative data come from the Firm Registration Yearbooks for 1991-2000, compiled by the Chinese State Economic and Trade Commission, which record firm-level product, sales, profit, and output data for all domestic pharmaceutical manufacturers, and from the SFDA's new drug registration database of approved new drugs developed by domestic firms between 1986 and 2000. The authors started from the entire population of 3,235 firms that had ever existed in the chemical drug industry from 1991 to 2000, dropped 892 mainly because of incomplete yearbook data, and retained 2,343 firms; the main models use 10,841 firm-year observations covering 10 years, while models using FDA approval duration use 9,549 observations from 2,171 firms because data for the year 2000 are missing. The dependent variables are firm performance, measured as annual return on sales (RoS, the ratio of the firm's profit to its product sales that year), and the variance of RoS; return on assets could not be computed because asset data were unavailable for all but one year, and in 2000 RoA correlated 0.78 (p = 0.000) with RoS. Product innovation is the count of self-developed new drugs approved for a firm in a given year, lagged one year: of the 1,516 new drugs the SFDA approved with a sample firm listed as inventor, 861 (from 339 firms) had active ingredients already registered in the USFDA Drugs@FDA database and were classified as imitations, leaving 655 (from 281 firms) as innovations, and 486 of the 2,343 firms (20.7%) introduced at least one self-developed new drug. Product development time has two proxies, both lagged one year: the log of the series of scientific and clinical experiments the SFDA requires for the drug's application type (25 series for a type-1 drug, seven for a type-5), and SFDA approval duration (mean 406 days, or 1.1 years; longest 2,410 days, or 6.6 years). Rate of institutional change is the yearly change in the percentage of state-owned firms in the pharmaceutical industry of each of 30 provinces and autonomous regions, with an alternative measure counting the new SFDA policies on price cuts and new drug approval issued each year. Controls are foreign imitations, licensed products, firm size (production value), a new-firm dummy, a state-owned dummy, and the number of research alliance partners. Estimation uses multiplicative heteroskedasticity models that simultaneously estimate effects on the level and on the variance of RoS, supplemented by 2SLS instrumental-variable models (instruments: the logged number of firms innovating in provinces other than the focal firm's in the prior year, first stage 0.043, p = 0.000, F(1, 10833) = 17.16; and medical schools per capita in the firm's province in the prior year, first stage -6.480, p < .001, F(1, 10831) = 10.71), a coarsened exact matching (k2k) design that reduced the sample from 10,841 to 588 observations (294 innovator-year plus 294 non-innovator-year), and an ex ante risk-preference control measured as the standard deviation of firm profitability over the preceding five years. The authors state that the 2SLS procedure addresses endogeneity in the mean regression but not necessarily in the variance regression. Post hoc moderators are provincial IPR protection (a 0-10 index from the China Reform Foundation at the National Economic Research Institute, available only from 1997 onward) and bribery prosecution (the annual provincial count of bribery and corruption cases in the Procuratorial Yearbook of China, divided by 1,000). Qualitative material comprises 33 retrospective interviews with industry insiders, quoted in Tables 1a, 1b, and 4a; post hoc archival material comprises 22 articles published between 1991 and 2000 in authoritative pharmaceutical news outlets and magazines, used qualitatively, and a count of 156 People's Daily articles acknowledging drug discoveries by domestic firms (seven to 24 per year), used as a press-on-innovators variable in logistic regressions with firm random effects on 10,194 observations from 2,228 firms.

**Key Findings**
Hypotheses 1a and 1b were supported. In the baseline multiplicative heteroskedastic model (Model 1), product innovation had a negative and highly significant effect on mean RoS (-0.040, SE 0.012), so that introducing one new drug depressed a firm's RoS by 4.0%, and a positive effect on the variance of RoS (0.129, SE 0.041), increasing performance variance by 13.8% (e^0.129 - 1 = 0.138). The 2SLS second stage fully replicated this pattern (Model 2: -1.412 on the mean, 19.160 on the variance), and the alternative medical-school instrument produced a mean effect of -2.47 and a variance effect of 65.3 (both p < 0.001). The coarsened-exact-matching sample of 588 observations reproduced it as well (-0.096, p < .01 on the mean; 1.88, p < .001 on the variance), as did models adding ex ante risk preference as a control. The four-year cumulative effect on profitability was -8.2% and significant, which the authors read as evidence that positive effects of innovation did not simply take longer to materialize; the six-year cumulative effect was -2.02% and did not reach significance (p = 0.690). Hypothesis 2 was supported: the interaction of product innovation with experiments required by the FDA was -0.169 on the mean and 2.585 on the variance (Model 5), and the interaction with FDA approval duration was -0.111 and 1.779 (Model 6), with the pattern replicated in the subsample of innovating firms only. Hypothesis 3 was supported: the interaction with provincial institutional change was -0.356 on the mean and 2.270 on the variance (Model 7), and highly similar results followed from the alternative measure based on the annual count of new SFDA policies. Post hoc, the effects were weaker where appropriability was legally protected: the interaction with provincial IPR protection was positive and significant on the performance level (0.033) and negative and significant on the variance (-0.323), and the interaction with bribery prosecution was likewise positive on the level (0.026) and negative on the variance (-0.362), though the authors call this indirect evidence and caution against assuming causality. Both the number of provincial top-10 firms that were innovators (0.165) and People's Daily press coverage of innovators (0.024) positively predicted a firm's likelihood of innovating the following year. The control variables ran in the opposite direction: foreign imitation and licensing were on average positively associated with performance (1.5% and 2.3% respectively) while reducing the variance in RoS by 63.7% and 66.3% respectively, a combination of low risk with high return. The qualitative evidence diverged from the quantitative results: many interviewees believed product innovation had generally enhanced firm performance, and the archival articles and interviews without exception highlighted success stories, although three interviewees independently expressed doubts about that common belief. The authors interpret the gap as a variance-induced perception bias, because the higher variance associated with innovation makes innovators disproportionately visible among the industry's top performers.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts Bowman-paradox research from population-level correlations between firm risk and return to the level of a particular strategic action. It shows that product innovation can be high risk and low return when lengthy lead times meet institutional transition and uncertain value appropriability. It also argues that variance in performance should be studied as a dependent variable in its own right, not only as noise around mean performance.

**Practical Implication**
Managers in turbulent transition economies should not infer that a strategy is generally profitable merely because it is common among top performers. For long-cycle innovation projects, they need to evaluate both expected value and performance variance, especially where pricing, approval, IPR, and corruption risks affect appropriability. Policymakers seeking indigenous innovation should recognize that unstable institutions may make innovation privately unattractive even when the upside cases are visible.

**Limitations**
The authors state that they do not observe the pre-transition period, so they cannot directly test whether product innovation would avoid a Bowman effect without institutional change. They also observe new products that emerge from the invention pipeline and seek production permission, but not abandoned development processes, products withheld from permission requests, R&D expenditures, or each product's inherent promise. The sample consists mostly of small and medium-sized firms, making the influence of a single new product especially salient.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how other strategic actions, such as mergers and acquisitions, international expansion, top management turnover, or adoption of management techniques, affect both mean performance and variance. Studies could also test whether similar mechanisms arise from market or technological uncertainty rather than institutional transition. Additional work observing aborted product-development processes and complementary-resource arrangements could clarify how firms generate and trade off risk and return.
