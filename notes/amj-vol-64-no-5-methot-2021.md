# Office Chitchat as a Social Ritual: The Uplifting Yet Distracting Effects of Daily Small Talk at Work

**Abstract**
Small talk—trivial communication not core to task completion—is normative and ubiquitous in organizations. Although small talk comprises up to one-third of adults’ speech, its effects in the workplace have been largely discounted. Yet, research has suggested that small talk may have important consequences for employees. Integrating theories of interaction rituals and microrole transitions, we explore how and why seemingly inconsequential workday conversations meaningfully impact employees’ experiences. In a sample of employed adults, we used an experience sampling method to capture within-individual variationinsmalltalkoverathree-weekperiod.Giventhatweare thefirsttoexaminesmall talk as an episodic phenomenon, we also conducted a validation of our daily small talk measure with master’s students and two samples of employed adults. Using multilevel path analysis, results show that small talk enhanced employees’ daily positive social emotions atwork,whichheightened organizational citizenshipbehaviors(OCB) and enhanced well-being at the end of the workday; furthermore, small talk disrupted employees’ ability to cognitively engage in their work, which compromised their OCB. Additionally, higher levels of trait-level self-monitoring mitigated negative effects of small talk on work engagement. Combined, results suggest that the polite, ritualistic, and formulaic nature of small talk is uplifting yet also distracting.

**Research Question**
The paper asks how and why daily small talk at work affects employees' end-of-day well-being and organizational citizenship behavior. It examines whether these effects operate through positive social emotions and cognitive work engagement, and whether self-monitoring changes the strength of these pathways.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Daily small talk at work is positively associated with daily positive social emotions.
- H2a: Daily small talk has a positive indirect relationship with feelings of end-of-day well-being through increased daily positive social emotions.
- H2b: Daily small talk has a positive indirect relationship with daily OCB through increased daily positive social emotions.
- H3: Daily small talk at work is negatively associated with daily cognitive work engagement.
- H4a: Daily small talk has a positive indirect relationship with end-of-day well-being through decreased cognitive work engagement.
- H4b: Daily small talk has a negative indirect relationship with daily OCB through decreased cognitive engagement.
- H5: The relationship between small talk and (a) positive social emotions and (b) cognitive work engagement is moderated by self-monitoring; specifically, higher (vs. lower) self-monitoring will (a) strengthen the positive association between small talk and positive social emotions, and (b) mitigate the negative association between small talk and cognitive work engagement.
- H6: The positive indirect relationship of daily small talk with end of day well-being through increased daily (a) positive social emotions and (b) cognitive work engagement is stronger when self-monitoring is higher (vs. lower).
- H7: The negative indirect relationship of daily small talk with OCB through increased daily (a) positive social emotions and (b) cognitive work engagement is weaker when self-monitoring is higher (vs. lower).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily small talk at work.
- DV(s): End-of-day well-being and organizational citizenship behavior.
- Mediators: Positive social emotions and cognitive work engagement.
- Moderators: Trait-level self-monitoring.

The mechanism is dual-sided. Drawing on interaction ritual theory and microrole transitions, small talk works as a polite social ritual that generates positive social emotions, which support well-being and OCB. At the same time, small talk can distract employees from cognitively engaging in work, reducing OCB through a resource and attention pathway. Higher self-monitoring weakens the negative small-talk-to-engagement pathway, suggesting that employees who navigate social cues more fluidly experience less disruption.

**Data & Measures**
The primary study is an experience sampling study of full-time employees recruited by email from alumni of undergraduate and graduate human resource management degree programs at a large public university in the Northeastern United States and through LinkedIn advertisements, with recipients encouraged to forward the announcement. Eligibility required employment of at least 32 hours per week, working outside the home, and a traditional schedule (for example, arrive by 9 a.m., leave by 5 p.m.) based on Eastern Standard Time; participants were told they could receive up to $70. Phase 1 was a sign-up survey containing informed consent, the Level 2 moderator (self-monitoring), and demographics, completed by 151 qualified employees. Approximately two weeks later, Phase 2 administered three daily email surveys for 15 consecutive workdays: a morning survey (completed on average at 9:41 a.m.) measuring small talk, positive social emotions, sleep quality, and cognitive engagement as control variables; an afternoon survey (1:43 p.m.) measuring small talk, modeled as the independent variable, plus positive social emotions and cognitive work engagement, the mediators; and an evening survey (7:42 p.m.) measuring end-of-day well-being and OCB, the dependent variables. Twenty-six employees did not participate in the daily part of the study; of the remaining 125, 25 did not complete at least three days and were excluded, leaving 978 Level 1 data points out of a possible 1,500 (65.2% response rate) from 100 employees (9.78 days per employee). Because the one existing small talk scale focuses narrowly on conversational content and does not capture the broader global definition, the authors developed their own measure: in Stage 1 they generated 15 items and retained the six that 10 subject-matter experts judged best; in Stages 2 and 3 they collected three independent samples (n = 455) and used principal component analysis and confirmatory factor analyses in Mplus 8 to retain the four items loading highest on the small talk factor and to distinguish them from related constructs such as coworker support, gossip, and daily interruptions. With the exception of daily sleep quality, daily measures were rated 1 (not at all) to 5 (a great deal). Small talk used the new four-item scale (coefficient alpha averaged across days = .94 for the morning survey and .93 for the afternoon survey); positive social emotions used seven items from Kitayama, Mesquita, and Karasawa's (2006) social emotion scale (alpha = .87 in both the morning and afternoon surveys); cognitive work engagement used three items adapted from Rich et al.'s (2010) scale (alpha = .97 morning, .95 afternoon); end-of-day well-being used Sonnentag's (2001) three items (alpha = .77); and OCB used six items from Dalal, Lam, Weiss, Welch, and Hulin's (2009) scale (alpha = .89). The Level 2 moderator, self-monitoring, used the eight positively worded items from Snyder and Gangestad's (1986) scale rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (alpha = .78). Controls were negative social emotions (six items from Kitayama et al., 2006; alpha = .89 morning, .88 afternoon), ego depletion (five items from Lanaj, Johnson, and Barnes, 2014; alpha = .94 morning, .95 afternoon), single-item daily sleep quality (Buysse et al., 1989), survey day (1-15), and the morning assessments of small talk, positive social emotions, and cognitive work engagement. Estimation used multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8 with a 1-1-1 mediation model, exogenous Level 1 variables within-person centered and self-monitoring grand-mean centered, and 95% confidence intervals from a Monte Carlo bootstrap with 20,000 simulated bias-corrected parameter estimates; conditional indirect effects were modeled at 1 SD above and below the self-monitoring mean. The design is an observational within-person ESM study, not an experiment: the authors temporally separated variables across surveys to better infer causality, but small talk, positive social emotions, and cognitive work engagement were measured at the same point in time, and a supplemental reverse-causal model was estimated to address the resulting concern.

**Key Findings**
Controlling for morning levels of the mediators, H1 was supported: daily small talk was positively related to positive social emotions (γ = .10, p < .01). H2a was supported: positive social emotions were positively related to end-of-day well-being (γ = .24, p < .01), and the indirect effect of daily small talk on well-being via positive social emotions was significant (estimate = .024, 95% CI [.0097, .0453]). H2b was also supported: positive social emotions were positively related to OCB (γ = .22, p < .01), and the indirect effect of daily small talk on OCB was significant (estimate = .022, 95% CI [.0103, .0391]). H3 was supported: daily small talk was negatively related to cognitive work engagement (γ = -.12, p < .05). H4a was not supported: cognitive work engagement did not relate to end-of-day well-being (γ = .00, n.s.), and the confidence interval for the indirect effect included 0 (estimate = .000, 95% CI [-.0109, .0102]). H4b was supported: cognitive work engagement was positively related to OCB (γ = .08, p < .01), and the indirect effect of small talk on OCB via cognitive work engagement was negative and significant (estimate = -.010, 95% CI [-.0221, -.0031]). For the cross-level moderation, H5a was not supported (γ = .05, n.s.), while H5b was supported (γ = .16, p < .01): the relationship between daily small talk and cognitive work engagement was negative and significant at low self-monitoring (simple slope = -.24, p < .01) and nonsignificant at high self-monitoring (simple slope = -.01, n.s.). Because H5a was not supported, the authors were precluded from examining H6a and H7a, and because cognitive work engagement did not relate significantly to end-of-workday well-being they were also precluded from examining H6b. H7b was supported: the indirect effect of daily small talk on OCB via cognitive work engagement was not significant at higher self-monitoring (estimate = -.001, 95% CI [-.0090, .0083]) and was significant and negative at lower self-monitoring (estimate = -.020, 95% CI [-.0406, -.0064]). The model accounted for 6% of the within-person variance in positive social emotions, 11% in cognitive work engagement, 31% in end-of-day well-being, and 28% in OCB. In a supplemental reverse-causal model, positive social emotions were positively related to small talk (γ = .35, p < .01) and cognitive work engagement was negatively related to small talk (γ = -.16, p < .01), but small talk did not significantly relate to well-being (γ = .05, n.s.) or OCB (γ = .03, n.s.), which the authors read as lending support to their hypothesized ordering.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces small talk as a distinct management construct rather than treating it as trivial background communication. It extends interaction ritual theory and microrole transition theory by showing that brief, scripted, seemingly inconsequential exchanges can shape daily emotions, engagement, well-being, and citizenship behavior. It also adds an individual-difference boundary condition by showing that self-monitoring matters for the distracting side of small talk.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should recognize that casual workday interaction can support well-being and citizenship by creating social connection, especially in settings where remote, gig, or flexible work reduces informal contact. At the same time, office and workday design should preserve both opportunities for spontaneous small talk and conditions for focused work, because small talk can also interrupt cognitive engagement. Managers can use small talk carefully in onboarding and cross-cultural integration because it may help create belonging without requiring deep personal disclosure.

**Limitations**
The primary study did not measure alternative daily communication episodes such as gossip, incivility, or task-focused exchanges, so the paper cannot fully position small talk among all daily interpersonal interactions. The ESM design used global self-reports of small talk and did not capture the qualitative content, partner, initiator, or recipient of each interaction. Small talk, positive social emotions, and cognitive work engagement were measured at the same time point, which leaves some concern about reverse causality despite supplemental analyses.

**Future Research**
Future studies could compare different forms and motives of small talk, including weather talk, personal-hobby talk, networking, affiliative talk, and talk used simply to pass time. Research could examine whether morning and afternoon small talk have different effects, and whether small talk initiated by the focal employee differs from small talk received as an interruption. Dyad-level studies could clarify how relationship closeness and partner identity shape the effects found here, since the authors presume that the functions of small talk differ based on the nature of the relationship between interaction partners.
