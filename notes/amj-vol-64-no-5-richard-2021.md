# The Effects of Racial Diversity Congruence between Upper Management and Lower Management on Firm Productivity

**Abstract**
We examine whether matching levels of racial diversity in upper management and lower management (i.e., racial diversity congruence) impact firm productivity. In a sample of high-tech firms, we found that congruence between upper-management racial diversity and lower-management racial diversity positively impacts firm productivity, supporting knowledge-based view perspectives. Furthermore, organizations with high levels of racial diversity in both upper and lower management (i.e., high–high racial diversity congruence) realized superior productivity compared to organizations with low levels of racial diversity in both upper and lower management (i.e., low–low racial diversity congruence). Results also revealed differences across levels of racial diversity incongruence between upper management and lower management (i.e., asymmetry effects) whereby firmswith amore racially diverse uppermanagement thanlowermanagement outproduced firms with a more racially diverse lower management than upper management. A supplemental sample of Fortune 500 firms enhanced generalizability, as the pattern of findings was very similar to that of the high-tech sample. We discuss the study’s implications and make a call for future research that simultaneously considers the upper echelons and the lower echelons.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether congruence between racial diversity in upper management and lower management is associated with higher firm productivity. It further asks whether high–high racial diversity congruence outperforms low–low congruence, and whether high upper-management/low lower-management incongruence differs from the reverse pattern.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Racial diversity congruence between upper management and lower management will be associated with higher firm productivity compared to racial diversity incongruence.
- H2: High upper-management racial diversity–high lower-management racial diversity congruence will be associated with higher firm productivity compared to low upper-management racial diversity–low lower-management racial diversity congruence.
- H3: High upper-management–low lower-management racial diversity incongruence will be associated with higher firm productivity compared to low upper-management–high lower-management racial diversity incongruence.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Upper-management racial diversity, lower-management racial diversity, and their congruence or incongruence.
- DV(s): Firm productivity, measured as revenue divided by number of employees and log-transformed.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper; the study models congruence level and direction of asymmetry between upper-management and lower-management racial diversity as focal response-surface configurations rather than as conventional moderators.

The mechanism is that racial diversity functions as a knowledge-based resource, but its productivity value depends on how diversity-based knowledge and absorptive capacity are distributed across managerial tiers. Congruence between upper and lower management supports knowledge symmetry and strategy implementation, while incongruence creates knowledge asymmetry. High–high congruence is theorized to generate the strongest productivity because both strategy formulation and strategy implementation benefit from racial diversity-based knowledge.

**Data & Measures**
Race data for upper-level and lower-level managers came from the EEOC's EEO-1 Report, aggregated from the subsidiary level to the organizational level and combined with Compustat financial information; racial demography was gathered between 2007 and 2011, beginning in 2007 because that was when the EEOC first categorized management into upper management and lower management. The primary sample covers the U.S. computer hardware (SIC 3571, 3572, 3575, 3577) and computer software (SIC 7371, 7372, 7373, 7374) segments; the authors identified 201 firms from the EEOC database for 795 firm years and, due to missing predictors and outcome variables, were able to include 168 firms for 594 firm-year observations. The dependent variable, firm productivity at time t + 1, was measured as revenue divided by number of employees (thousands of dollars per employee) and log-transformed, and was lagged one year behind the racial-diversity predictors. Upper-management and lower-management racial diversity were both calculated using Blau's (1977) index of heterogeneity at time t across five race categories (White, African American, Hispanic, Asian, and Native American), and were mean-centered for the polynomial analyses; following Shanock et al. (2010), congruence was defined as scores within 0.5 SD of each other. Controls measured at time t were firm size (log number of employees), firm age, California, New York, and Texas headquarters dummies, upper- and lower-management gender diversity (Blau's index), upper- and lower-management team size (log), TMT average age, TMT average tenure, TMT average international work experience, TMT average education level (0 = no college to 4 = PhD or law degree), and TMT average work experience (number of firms worked at), plus year and industry dummies. Hypotheses were tested with time-series multilevel mixed-effects linear regression (-mixed in Stata) using random effects, and with polynomial regression adding UMRD², UMRD × LMRD, and LMRD² plus response-surface analysis of the congruence and incongruence lines, using 10,000 bootstrapped iterations to obtain 95% confidence intervals for the first principal axis slope (p11) and intercept (p10); the design is a lagged longitudinal panel and the authors claim no causal identification strategy. A supplemental Fortune 500 replication covered the same period (2007 to 2011 for the independent variables, with the dependent variable lagged by one year): 434 Fortune 500 firms were identified from the EEOC database for 2,094 firm years, and 417 firms for 1,992 firm-year observations were included due to missing predictors and outcome variables; 24 overlapping high-tech firms for 102 firm years were present, and results remained unchanged when they were excluded.

**Key Findings**
All three hypotheses were supported in the high-tech sample and again in the Fortune 500 replication. In the high-tech sample, initial quadrant comparisons showed that firms with racial diversity congruence outperformed firms without congruence by 0.518 (t = 2.01, p < 0.05), Quadrant 4 (high upper–high lower) outperformed Quadrant 1 (low upper–low lower) by 1.32 (t = 2.57, p < 0.05), and Quadrant 3 (high upper–low lower) outperformed Quadrant 2 (low upper–high lower) by 2.03 (t = 2.48, p < 0.05). H1 was supported: the surface along the incongruence line was an inverted U-shape ((b3 – b4 + b5) = −34.80, curvature test statistic = −4.93, p < .01), and the first principal axis had a slope (p11) not significantly different from 1 (95% bias-corrected bootstrap CI −1.030, 2.333) and an intercept (p10) not significantly different from 0 (CI −2.668, 6.918). H2 was supported: the slope of the congruence line was positive and significant (slope (b1 + b2) = 2.36, test statistic = 2.17, p < .05), so high–high congruence brought about significantly higher firm productivity than low–low congruence. H3 was supported: the slope along the incongruence line was significant (slope (b1 – b2) = 2.79, test statistic = 2.46, p < 0.05), indicating that high upper-management–low lower-management incongruence is more positively related to productivity than low upper-management–high lower-management incongruence. The Fortune 500 replication reproduced the same pattern: an inverted U-shape along the incongruence line ((b3 – b4 + b5) = −40.21, curvature test statistics = −5.39, p < .01) with p11 CI (−.029, 2.017) including 1 and p10 CI (−7.647, .860) including 0 supporting H1; a congruence-line slope of 3.91 (test statistics = 4.25, p < .01) supporting H2; and an incongruence-line slope of 5.46 (test statistics = 4.50, p < .01) supporting H3. Median-centered polynomial regressions produced the same results as the mean-centered analyses in both samples. Using the untransformed productivity measure, the authors report that a 1% increase in racial diversity congruence between upper management and lower management increased firm productivity by $729 per employee in the high-tech study and by $1,590 per employee in the Fortune 500 study; in the discussion they note that the highest firm productivity is observed when racial diversity is high in both upper and lower management, whereas the lowest firm productivity is observed when racial diversity is high in lower management but low in upper management.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends upper echelons theory by arguing that lower management should be incorporated into explanations of firm outcomes, because lower managers implement strategy and transmit knowledge upward. It also contributes to knowledge-based view and diversity research by showing that racial diversity effects depend on cross-tier alignment rather than on diversity within a single managerial group alone.

**Practical Implication**
The findings suggest that firms should avoid large racial-diversity disparities between lower and upper management if they want to convert diversity-based knowledge into productivity gains. Organizations with racially diverse lower management may especially need to improve racial diversity in upper management so that ideas, knowledge, and absorptive capacity are not concentrated in lower tiers with less strategic influence.

**Limitations**
The authors state that the study does not directly measure the mechanisms explaining the relationship between racial diversity congruence and firm productivity. They also do not measure the knowledge of every upper- and lower-level manager, and the EEOC data do not allow them to distinguish subgroups within broad racial categories or identify whether diversity came from internal promotion or external hiring.

**Future Research**
Future research could directly measure the mechanisms linking managerial racial diversity to productivity, including promotion versus external hiring, leadership, empowerment culture, and knowledge exchange across tiers. The authors also call for studies of racial diversity congruence in other industries, nationality congruence across management tiers, and faultline approaches that consider multiple demographic characteristics simultaneously.
