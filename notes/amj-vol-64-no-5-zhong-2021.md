# Customer Concentration, Executive Attention, and Firm Search Behavior

**Abstract**
It is unclear why, despite the increasing emphasis on innovation in modern business, firms are still myopic in their search efforts. Although prior studies have examined the role of supply-side factors such as technologies, capabilities, and managerial cognition, they rarely explore how demand-side factors such as customers can shape managerial cognition and firm search behavior. Extending the attention-based view to the demand side, we argue that major customers channel the attention of executives to the existing competitive environment, resources and capabilities, and management practices (i.e., existing-oriented attention), resulting in firms’ increased emphasis on deep search within familiar domains and limiting their search in new fields. Through an empirical examination of the patenting behavior of U.S. publicly listed firms, we show that customer concentration increases search depth and decreases search breadth via executives’ existing-oriented orientation. We also find that both performance feedback above social aspiration levels and product market competition amplify the effects of customer concentration. Our study contributes to the organizational search literature by adding a demand-side explanation. It also extends the cognition view by foregrounding the role of customers in shaping executive attention. Moreover, it explores the interaction between supplyand demandside factors in driving firm search behavior.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether and how a demand-side structural factor, customer concentration, affects firm search behavior. It specifically examines whether major customers shape executives' existing-oriented attention and thereby increase search depth while reducing search breadth.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Firms with high customer concentration are (a) more likely to perform a deep search, and (b) less likely to perform a broad search.
- H2: Executives' existing-oriented attention mediates the relationship between customer concentration and firms' (a) search depth and (b) search breadth.
- H3: Performance exceeding aspirations strengthens (a) the positive relationship between customer concentration and search depth, and (b) the negative relationship between customer concentration and search breadth.
- H4: Product market competition strengthens (a) the positive relationship between customer concentration and search depth, and (b) the negative relationship between customer concentration and search breadth.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Customer concentration, measured with an HHI-based measure using sales to major customers.
- DV(s): Search depth and search breadth, measured from firms' patent citation patterns.
- Mediators: Existing-oriented executive attention, derived from textual analysis of top managers' shareholder letters and earnings conference calls.
- Moderators: Performance feedback relative to social and historical aspirations, and product market competition measured primarily with TNIC.

The mechanism is that dependence on a few major customers directs executives' attention toward the existing competitive environment, existing resources and capabilities, and established management practices. This existing-oriented attention encourages firms to deepen search within familiar knowledge domains and to limit broader exploration into new fields. The paper finds partial mediation through existing-oriented attention, and it shows that social performance feedback above aspirations and high product market competition strengthen the customer-concentration relationship with deeper and narrower search.

**Data & Measures**
Longitudinal panel of U.S. publicly listed manufacturing firms (SIC 2000-3999) over 1976 to 2005; the intersection of the source databases produced 19,101 supplier firm-year observations. Patent data came from the NBER patent database, the USPTO's Electronic Information Products Division, and the Harvard Dataverse (patent subclass information); customer-supplier data, industry segment data, and financial statement data came from Compustat. Dependent variables were measured in year t+1: Search depth = the average frequency with which previously referenced patents cited by a focal firm in a given year were repeatedly cited during the previous five years (repeat citation counts divided by previously referenced citations); Search breadth = the share of the total citations of a focal firm's patents in a given year that cannot be found in the firm's patent citations from the previous five years (new citation counts divided by total citations). Independent variable (year t): Customer concentration = a Herfindahl-Hirschman Index-based measure, the sum of squared ratios of the supplier's sales to each major customer (a customer accounting for over 10% of sales) to the supplier's total sales, ranging from 0 to 1. Mediator: Existing-oriented attention = computer-aided text analysis of letters to shareholders (Mergent Online, LexisNexis) and earnings conference calls (Factiva) using a thesaurus-derived dictionary of existing- and future-oriented words validated by three content experts, with word counts normalized by document length and the measure computed as the ratio of existing-oriented attention to the sum of existing- and future-oriented attention; full LTS and ECC coverage was available for about 14.6% of the full sample (2,783 observations). Dictionary validation used manual coding of 50 firm-year observations (86% inter-rater agreement for existing-oriented and 93% for future-oriented attention) and a separate study of 36 EMBA-enrolled CEOs whose GLOBE survey-based future-orientation score correlated with the dictionary measures of existing-oriented attention (r = .49, p < .05) and future-oriented attention (r = -.56, p < .05). Moderators: Social performance feedback = patent citations received by a focal firm in year t minus the median patent citations of its three-digit SIC peers in the same year; Historical performance feedback = the firm's patent citations in year t minus the average of its own previous two years' patent citations, which uses a three-year window and thus a shorter panel (N = 18,996); Product market competition = minus one times the TNIC HHI from Hoberg and Phillips's (2010) database, whose data collection began in 1996, so the period for testing this moderating effect spanned 1996 to 2005 (N = 7,084). Controls: firm size, diversification, book to market, leverage, profitability, tangibility, R&D, firm age, customer heterogeneity, and alliance, with continuous variables winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Estimation: OLS regressions on the panel with industry and year fixed effects and heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors; firm fixed effects were not used because the within-firm variance of customer concentration was low (mean within-firm variance 0.03 versus sample mean variance 0.14). Mediation was tested with Monte Carlo simulations in R and bootstrapping analyses in Stata. For identification the authors used an instrumental-variable approach with downstream M&A activity in customers' industries (Customer M&A) as the instrument (N = 4,952), plus a difference-in-differences design leveraging the deregulation of Acts and Orders by the federal government as a shock to downstream M&A activity.

**Key Findings**
H1(a) and H1(b) were supported: customer concentration had a positive and significant effect on search depth (b = 0.25, p < .001) and a negative and significant effect on search breadth (b = -0.06, p < .001) in Table 2 Models 2A and 2B (N = 19,101). H2 was supported as partial mediation in the 2,783-observation attention subsample: customer concentration had a significant positive effect on existing-oriented attention (b = 0.01, p < .05); existing-oriented attention had a significant positive effect on search depth (b = 1.29, p < .001) and a significant negative effect on search breadth (b = -0.32, p < .01); and when the mediator was added the magnitude of the customer-concentration effects dropped, from b = 0.33 (p < .01) to b = 0.32 (p < .01) for search depth and from b = -0.08 (p < .05) to b = -0.07 (p < .05) for search breadth. Monte Carlo simulations produced 95% CIs excluding zero for the indirect path to search depth [0.002, 0.038] and for the indirect path to search breadth [-0.010, -0.000], and bias-corrected bootstrap 95% CIs for the indirect effects on search depth and search breadth were [0.009, 0.038] and [-0.015, -0.001] respectively. H3 was only partially supported: the customer concentration x social performance feedback interaction was positive and significant on search depth (b = 0.26, p < .001) and negative and significant on search breadth (b = -0.16, p < .001), supporting H3(a) and H3(b), but the interaction with historical performance feedback was insignificant in both models, so only the social-aspiration results support H3. Economically, when social performance feedback was one standard deviation above the mean a one-unit increase in customer concentration was related to a 0.23-unit increase in search depth and a 0.07-unit decrease in search breadth, whereas one standard deviation below the mean it was related to a 0.03-unit decrease in search depth and a 0.09-unit increase in search breadth. H4(a) and H4(b) were supported: the customer concentration x product market competition interaction was positive and significant on search depth (b = 0.70, p < .05) and negative and significant on search breadth (b = -0.25, p < .05); at high competition a one-unit increase in customer concentration was related to a 0.57-unit increase in search depth and a 0.19-unit decrease in search breadth, versus a 0.17-unit increase in search depth and a 0.05-unit decrease in search breadth at low competition. In the instrumental-variable estimation (N = 4,952) predicted customer concentration was positive and significant for search depth (b = 2.21, p < .05) and negative and significant for search breadth (b = -0.78, p < .01), and the difference-in-differences analyses produced consistent results. Robustness checks held under seemingly unrelated regressions, lagged dependent variables, alternative customer-concentration measures and controls, additional fixed-effect and clustering specifications, and the traditional sales-based HHI competition measure; customer concentration also had a significant negative effect on conglomerate M&As (b = -0.03, p < .001), an alternative measure of search breadth. When the moderation hypotheses were re-tested in the instrumental-variable regressions the main results continued to hold, except that the product market competition interaction on the customer concentration-depth relationship became insignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper adds a demand-side explanation to organizational search research by showing that customer structure can help explain why firms persist in myopic search. It extends the attention-based and cognition literatures by positioning customers, not only technologies, capabilities, or competitors, as sources of executive attention. It also integrates demand-side and supply-side accounts by showing that social performance feedback and product market competition condition the effect of customer concentration.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that firms highly dependent on a few major customers should monitor whether customer demands are narrowing executive attention and search behavior. The results also suggest that positive performance relative to peers and high product market competition can reinforce customer-driven search myopia rather than automatically encouraging broader exploration.

**Limitations**
The authors note that patent data may not capture all forms of search because patenting propensity varies across industries and some firms may avoid patenting critical knowledge. They also state that executive attention measures based on public statements may be affected by social desirability bias and emotional elements. The paper further acknowledges that it does not explain the determinants of customer concentration and focuses on customer structure rather than the content of heterogeneous customer demands.

**Future Research**
Future research could use alternative indicators of search behavior beyond patents and improve executive-attention measurement through field experiments, video coding, or other designs. Scholars could examine why customer concentration emerges, especially in smaller, younger, less profitable, and more R&D-intensive firms. Additional work could also study how different customer-demand characteristics, not just the concentration structure of customers, affect firm search behavior.
