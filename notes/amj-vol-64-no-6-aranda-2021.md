# Distinct but Not Apart? Stigma Reduction and Cross-Industry Evaluative Spillovers: The Case of Medical Marijuana Legalization

**Abstract**
Previous research on stigma reduction has been primarily confined to single industries. However, industry boundaries are porous in the eyes of audience members and can hardly act as artificial confines to moral reevaluations. We contend that evaluative spillovers induced by stigma reduction in a focal industry diffuse to other industries that share the same vilifying label when (a) they possess the morally approved attribute that underlies the reduction of stigma, and (b) the morally approved attribute is embedded in formal rules and procedures that ensure the desired purposes. Empirically, we focus our tests on the legalization of marijuana as a medicine in several U.S. states during the period 2000–2015, which has reduced the “vice” stigma around marijuana by highlighting the “health safety” attribute of marijuana, namely its safe consumption and potential health benefits. We show that legalization generated evaluative spillovers to other “vice” industries sharing the same health safety attribute as marijuana (i.e., alcohol), but not to those that did not share it (i.e., tobacco). Those spillovers were primarily observed when the law was accompanied by a patient registration system that highlighted the health safety attribute of marijuana by limiting its use to medical purposes.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether stigma reduction in one industry can generate positive evaluative spillovers across industry boundaries. It examines when medical marijuana legalization changes customer behavior toward other vice industries, especially alcohol and tobacco, and whether formal patient registration systems strengthen those spillovers.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Proposition 1. Incongruent information about a stigmatized industry generates positive evaluative spillovers to other industries covered by the same vilifying label if they possess the same morally approved attribute.
Proposition 2. The positive evaluative spillovers that cross industries covered by the same vilifying label are stronger when the morally approved attribute central to stigma reduction is embedded in formal rules and procedures.
Hypothesis 1. The legalization of marijuana for medical purposes increases the sales of alcohol but not of tobacco.
Hypothesis 2. The positive evaluative spillovers of the legalization of marijuana for medical purposes on alcohol sales are stronger when a patient registration system limits the supply and consumption of marijuana to medical reasons.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Medical marijuana legalization and medical marijuana legalization accompanied by a mandatory patient registration system.
- DV(s): State-level alcohol sales and tobacco sales, with supplementary analyses of marijuana consumption, media health-safety discourse, binge drinking, and gambling sales.
- Mediators: The paper theorizes evaluative reassessment of vice industries through the morally approved health safety attribute, but does not estimate a formal mediation model.
- Moderators: Mandatory patient registration systems that limit marijuana use to medical purposes and embed the health safety attribute in formal rules and procedures.

The mechanism begins when legitimate but incongruent information from medical marijuana legalization challenges taken-for-granted vice stigma and shifts audiences from label-based judgment to attribute-based evaluation. Alcohol, unlike tobacco, is theorized to share the health safety attribute with medical marijuana when consumed responsibly, so evaluative spillovers should increase alcohol sales more than tobacco sales. The paper argues that patient registration systems make the health safety claim more procedurally legitimate, which is why the positive spillover to alcohol appears mainly when legalization is paired with such systems.

**Data & Measures**
Single quantitative study on a hand-assembled state-level panel: all 50 U.S. states plus Washington, DC, over 2000-2015, yielding a balanced panel of 816 state-year observations. The analysis is set at the state level because states are the loci of enactment of medical marijuana laws, and the 2000-2015 window is chosen because most medical marijuana laws were implemented within it and several dependent variables are unavailable before 2000 or after 2015.
- DVs: Alcohol sales and Tobacco sales, measured in millions of U.S. dollars, with alcohol sales obtained from the Barnes Reports and tobacco sales from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention; all sales variables are transformed to 2010 U.S. dollars. The authors prefer dollar sales to survey self-reports (which understate consumption of substances treated as vices) and to unit counts (because dollar sales capture willingness to pay); gallons of alcohol and packs of cigarettes are used as robustness alternatives.
- IV: Medical marijuana, a treatment dummy set to 1 beginning in the year a medical marijuana law became effective in a given state and 0 for years prior to legalization, using the implementation rather than the enactment date; law coding builds on Pacula, Boustead, and Hunt (2014) and Pacula et al. (2015), supplemented by the authors' own coding and by NORML and the Marijuana Policy Project.
- IV / moderator: Medical marijuana registry, set to 1 only for states that mandated a patient registration system upon legalization and to 0 for states with a voluntary system or none, so it is de facto the interaction of Medical marijuana with a patient-registration dummy.
- Controls: Recreational marijuana (set to 1 once recreational legalization came into effect), Republican governor, GDP per capita, state population, percentage of males, median age, and social capital measured as the number of nonprofit "civic and social" organizations per 1,000 inhabitants.
- Supplementary outcomes: the proportion of state residents aged 12 or older consuming marijuana, from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health via the CDC (available only from 2003; 663 observations); the salience of the "health safety" attribute in media discourse, built from LexisNexis newspaper articles referencing marijuana (251,924 articles, of which 51,655 also involve alcohol and 12,594 tobacco) and measured as the percentage of health-safety dictionary words per article averaged by state and year; Soft liquor, Hard liquor, Wine and Beer sales from the Barnes Reports; Binge drinking from the CDC, computed as the percentage of males over 12 having five or more drinks on one occasion and of females over 12 having four or more (255 observations); and Gambling sales, measured as yearly lottery ticket sales minus prizes from the Urban-Brookings Tax Policy Center (557 observations).

The paper claims causal identification through a difference-in-differences design that treats staggered state medical marijuana laws as policy shocks, following Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan's (2004) application to laws enacted in different states at different times. The unit of analysis is the state-year; all models include state and year fixed effects, and standard errors are clustered at the state level. Skewed sales variables are estimated with a Poisson pseudo-maximum-likelihood (PPML) estimator rather than log-linear OLS, while marijuana consumption, media salience, and binge drinking are estimated with OLS. The authors argue the laws can plausibly be treated as an exogenous shock to alcohol and tobacco because influencing consumption of products other than marijuana was outside the scope of the law, and they support the design with lead-and-lag pretrend tests, controls for other marijuana, alcohol, and tobacco policies, neighbor-state and legalizing-state-only subsamples, and finer controls for state political orientation.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is not supported; Hypothesis 2 is supported.
- H1 not supported: medical marijuana legalization on its own has a positive but statistically insignificant effect on alcohol sales (0.013, Table 3a, column 2) and a significant negative effect on tobacco sales (-0.073, Table 3b, column 2). Because H1 predicted increased alcohol sales but no change in tobacco sales, the authors state that the hypothesis is not supported. They do report that the difference between the two medical marijuana coefficients is statistically significant (p = 0.01), so legalization raised alcohol sales more than tobacco sales, and they treat this as a possible qualification of Hypothesis 1 rather than as support for it.
- H2 supported: the Medical marijuana registry coefficient is positive and statistically significant for alcohol sales (0.045, Table 3a, column 3) and negative but insignificant for tobacco sales (-0.002, Table 3b, column 3), so alcohol and tobacco sales diverged significantly only where a medical registration system was implemented. Legalization accompanied by a patient registration system augmented alcohol sales by about 2.3%, the sum of the Medical marijuana and Medical marijuana registry coefficients, and the result is robust to controlling for recreational marijuana legalization, which has no significant effect on either alcohol or tobacco sales.
- The alcohol effect is not consumption complementarity: medical marijuana raised the proportion of residents consuming marijuana by only 0.5 percentage points (Table 2, column 2), an effect that disappears once the registry and recreational laws are controlled, whereas recreational legalization raised marijuana consumption by 2.6 percentage points without raising alcohol sales.
- Mechanism and counterfactual evidence: the registry increased the salience of the "health safety" attribute in media discourse overall and in articles associating marijuana and alcohol, but not in those connecting marijuana and tobacco (Table 4). The registry coefficient is positive and significant for soft liquors, and separately for wine (p = 0.05) and beer, but fails to reach significance for hard liquors, which are deemed less safe. Legalization with a patient registration system is also associated with roughly a 1.5-percentage-point decrease in binge drinking (p = 0.06), which the authors read as evidence that the sales increase is not driven by heavier drinking. Gambling sales, a vice industry more distant on the health safety attribute, were affected neither by legalization nor by the registration system (Table 6).
- Identification checks: there was no significant difference in alcohol sales patterns between registry and non-registry states before the registry was enacted (Table 8), which the authors also read as alleviating reverse-causality concerns, and the main estimates survive controls for marijuana decriminalization, cannabidiol and edibles laws, dry-county and smoking-ban laws, restriction to legalizing states and their neighbors or to legalizing states only, and controls for presidential and congressional voting patterns.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to stigma research by showing that stigma reduction need not remain confined to the focal industry where reevaluation begins. It reconciles stigma as a vilifying label with stigma as moral evaluation by arguing that legitimate incongruent information can move audiences into an attribute-centered reassessment mode. It also adds counterfactual quantitative evidence that formal rules and procedures shape the credibility and cross-industry reach of stigma reduction.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that policymakers should consider how regulations in one stigmatized industry can alter evaluations and behavior in adjacent industries. Medical marijuana rules accompanied by patient registration systems changed alcohol sales patterns, suggesting that formal procedures can make a stigma-reducing health-safety claim more credible. The paper cautions against assuming that customer preferences remain fixed when new laws redefine the moral meaning of stigmatized products.

**Limitations**
The authors note that their theory accounts for multiple audiences but their empirical analysis focuses mainly on customers and, secondarily, media discourse. They also acknowledge that they infer micro-level reassessment processes from aggregate behavior rather than testing individual-level mechanisms directly. The boundary conditions are limited to settings where industries share a vilifying label and differ in whether they possess the morally approved attribute made salient by a legitimate source.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine other audiences such as social movements, watchdog groups, religious organizations, social media, banks, insurers, and corporations. Studies could test the individual-level mechanisms directly, for example through laboratory experiments. Further work could also generalize the argument beyond vice industries to other contexts where a legitimate source makes a morally approved attribute salient across stigmatized categories.
