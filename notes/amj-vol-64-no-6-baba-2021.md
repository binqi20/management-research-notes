# Increasing Dispositional Legitimacy: Progressive Legitimation Dynamics in a Trajectory of Settlements

**Abstract**
By focusing on individual settlements rather than viewing them as part of a trajectory of change, prior research on institutional settlements has not fully captured the dynamics of longer-term institutional change; yet this would be important to better understand progressive legitimation dynamics—in other words, how successive settlements may increase legitimacy in a long-term trajectory of change. This is a crucial issue, especially for peripheral actors lacking the resources, status, or power of more central ones. Our historical analysis focuses on the long-term struggle of the Cree First Nation in Canada and explains how they succeeded in increasing their dispositional legitimacy in and through the settlements. This required work in three interrelated processes: expansive argumentation, including problematizing of the state of affairs and creating discursive resonance with the prevailing discourses and values; building momentum by generating attention and exerting pressure in networks of actors; and seizing opportunities to negotiate with the other side. Thus, we challenge previous research on settlements by showing how they should not merely be seen as endpoints but rather as stepping-stones in a trajectory of institutional change. More specifically, we develop a process model explaining how progressive legitimation unfolds in a series of settlements.

**Research Question**
How can peripheral actors succeed in progressively increasing their dispositional legitimacy in a series of settlements? The paper examines this question through the Cree First Nation's long-term struggle to legitimate Indigenous rights in and through successive settlements with Quebec, Canada, and Hydro-Quebec.

**Mechanism Process**
- Core phenomenon: progressive legitimation in a trajectory of settlements, where each settlement both formalizes legitimacy and becomes a basis for later legitimation work.
- Focal legitimacy construct: dispositional legitimacy, defined in the paper as recognition of the interests, rights, or voice of an actor in a given field.
- Process 1: expansive argumentation, consisting of problematizing the state of affairs and creating discursive resonance with broader discourses and values.
- Process 2: building momentum, through generating attention, rallying support, and exerting pressure in networks of actors.
- Process 3: seizing negotiation opportunities, including knowing when to negotiate, delay, refuse, or accept imperfect settlements.
- Process outcome: increased dispositional legitimacy, shown in the progressive formalization, broadening, defense, reinterpretation, and extension of Cree Indigenous rights across four settlements.

The process model explains legitimation as cumulative and temporally patterned. Ongoing expansive argumentation gives the Crees a basis for building momentum; that momentum creates or strengthens negotiation opportunities; and negotiated settlements formalize legitimacy in ways that increase voice, network centrality, and bargaining position for later rounds. The paper therefore treats settlements not as terminal outcomes but as stepping-stones that enable further institutional change.

**Data & Measures**
Empirical material came from three streams, summarized in the paper's Table 1: documents, interviews, and observations. Documentary material included approximately 6,000 pages of governmental documents; federal parliamentary and senate discussions plus over 100 hours of National Assembly of Quebec debate in verbatim transcript, located through the archival database's "Debates Journal Index" under themes such as "Indian" (early 1970s), "Aboriginals" (late 1990s and early 2000s), "James Bay," "Natural resources," and "Great Whale"; the agreements themselves (the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement of 1975, the Cree-Naskapi Act of 1984, the Memorandum of Understanding of 1995, and the Paix des Braves of 2002); laws passed, including the Cree-Naskapi Act and the constitutional amendment of 1982; court archives such as the 1973 Calder decision and Malouf's decision of the same year; government-ordered reports running from the 1969 federal White Paper through the Tait Report (1982), the Penner Report (1983), the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1996), and the Truth and Reconciliation Report (2015); and approximately 5,500 pages of corporate archives, comprising Hydro-Quebec reports plus Grand Council of the Crees material (letters exchanged with ministers and Hydro-Quebec officials, chiefs' discourses between 1980 and 2000, submissions to the United Nations, and speaking notes of grand chiefs). Media material comprised 343 newspaper articles gathered systematically from Quebec, Canadian, and North American outlets (Washington Post, Globe and Mail, New York Post, La Presse, Le Devoir, National Post, The Montreal Gazette, Le Droit, Le Soleil, Le Journal de Montreal, and the Cree newspaper The Nation) via three databases, Eureka, the National Archives of Quebec, and the Google News Archive, using English and French keywords over the period 1970-2018 within a search window limited to 1940-2018. Historical books and analyses added approximately 8,500 pages and 12 hours, including the work of the anthropologist Feit and the journalist Boyce, alongside video documentaries. With triangulating searches of relevant websites (Hydro-Quebec annual reports, Grand Council and Cree government reports and archives), this material amounted to over 14,000 pages.

The first author conducted 112 semi-structured interviews with 94 informants, distributed as Hydro-Quebec 11, Quebec government 8, Niskamoon 20, scholars and experts 6, Grand Council of the Crees / Cree government / local band office employees 16, and land users, tallymen, and local communities 51. Interviews ranged from 30 minutes to four hours, with the majority lasting about 75 minutes; they were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in the language of origin (English or French) where possible and otherwise captured in notes, and were supplemented in some cases by email and telephone follow-up, with informal exchanges with key actors exceeding 35 hours. Interview focus shifted across three rounds of fieldwork: the first (2011-2012) on the James Bay hydroelectric project and its impact on local communities, the second (2016) on the internal dimension of the controversy, its impact on Cree institutions and traditional values, and the building of the Cree Nation, and the third (2018-2019) on the societal and macro context of Indigenous rights, Canadian and international politics, and environmentalism. Observation totalled two months across seven Cree communities (Mistissini, Nemaska, Waskaganish, Eastmain, Wemindji, Ouje-Bougoumou, and Waswanipi) in four rounds: two weeks in Mistissini (August 2016); one week each in Nemaska and Waskaganish (November 2016); five days in Nemaska including in-depth discussions with a tallyman and participation in traditional activities on his trapline (September 2017); and almost one month across Eastmain, Wemindji, Ouje-Bougoumou, and Waswanipi (July and August 2018). This was accompanied by over 150 hours of involvement in joint research projects and a conference with Niskamoon, a joint Hydro-Quebec/Cree organization (June 2016-2019). Observation served to refine the interview questions, guide the archival search and analysis, and validate the authors' findings and interpretations.

Constructs were operationalized interpretively rather than measured, as set out in the paper's Table 2 on the operationalization of constructs based on historical analysis. Dispositional legitimacy was operationalized as recognition of the rights of the Crees, as reflected in the settlements and as perceived by various actors; expansive argumentation as problematizing the state of affairs and creating resonance with broader discourses and values; creating momentum as generating attention to rally support and exerting pressure on the other side; and seizing negotiation opportunities as the Crees' decisions to act or not to act. Analysis followed an inductive logic with several iterations, in three steps. Step 1 constructed a chronological map of key events and main actors, which revealed that the trajectory was punctuated by settlements and, after iteration, narrowed the more than 60 agreements signed during the study period to the four most significant settlements, each linked to several formal agreements and selected as a "turning point," understood as a "critical moment during an interaction or development process" (Druckman & Olekalns, 2013). Step 2 identified and analyzed the three progressive legitimation processes and the specific Cree actions within them. Step 3 developed the process model, articulating the relationships among the three processes, their temporality, and the boundary conditions of the case. The design is an interpretive historical process study; it reports no statistical estimates and makes no causal-identification claim.

**Key Findings**
The Crees progressively increased their dispositional legitimacy across a trajectory of four settlements, and the paper records the increase settlement by settlement: the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (1975) delivered the initial formalization of Indigenous rights, which the government had not recognized before; the Cree-Naskapi Act (1984) broadened the scope of those rights, giving a legal basis for protecting Cree traditions and values and bringing increased visibility and recognition; the Memorandum of Understanding (1995) came with defending Indigenous rights and reinterpreting their meaning, as the previous settlements and an international media campaign against the government and Hydro-Quebec strengthened recognition of the Crees at the international level; and the Paix des Braves (2002) extended Indigenous rights by renegotiating previous settlements, increasing the Crees' status and legitimacy as a distinct nation with rights. Three interrelated processes account for the progression: expansive argumentation, building momentum, and seizing opportunities to negotiate. Each is intertwined with the others rather than forming a fixed stage sequence, but the analysis identifies a usual pattern in which ongoing expansive argumentation provides a basis for building momentum, which in turn enables the creation and seizing of negotiation opportunities.

Within expansive argumentation, problematization evolved rather than settling into normalization. It moved from protecting their way of life, argued when the notion of protecting Indigenous rights did not yet exist in Canadian discourse (settlement 1), to problematizing the previous settlement and the necessity of broadening its scope, especially its self-governance facet (settlement 2), to problematizing previous settlements and reinterpreting Indigenous rights against the backdrop of the Great Whale project and the settlement's poor implementation (settlement 3), to focusing on the necessity of extending Indigenous rights by renegotiating settlements the Crees deemed unfair (settlement 4). The authors read this as an evolving process in which new problematizations are based on the normalized ones, rather than as problematization followed by normalization in the manner of Maguire and Hardy (2009). Discursive resonance shifted in parallel: first with the nascent discourse on ancestral rights, then with the growing discourse on self-determination, then with environmentalism and sustainability, and finally with economic considerations, in which the Crees positioned themselves as real economic partners in a nation-to-nation relationship rather than as opponents of development per se. The authors present this shift as an expansion of the very basis of legitimation.

Building momentum was more situational and combined generating attention with exerting pressure. Attention-generation ran from building initial public awareness by connecting with newspaper, radio, and television outlets and emerging Indigenous groups, to media stunts that deliberately internationalized the struggle, including a meeting between the grand chief and the pope, to a worldwide media campaign directed at international institutions, environmentalists, and human rights activists, to continued national and international public relations work in forums concerned with Indigenous, human, and environmental rights. Pressure was exerted through legal and political channels: two lawsuits in 1972 to have the James Bay project stopped, entry into politics to negotiate implementation of the first settlement's self-governance provisions, more active engagement in international politics alongside legal challenges to Great Whale, and a series of lawsuits in the late 1990s (launched between 1998 and 2000) over the government's failure to fulfil its settlement obligations. The paper finds that peripheral actors may be rather reactive in initial settlements because of their limited role in networks, and can become more proactive generators of momentum over time by repeatedly engaging with stakeholders in the network sphere. Seizing negotiation opportunities varied by settlement and turned on timing: at settlement 1 the Crees seized the hard-won opportunity created by Judge Malouf's decision but delayed their responses to maximize bargaining power; at settlement 2 they captured an opportunity before it disappeared by positioning themselves in connection with the government's agenda; at settlement 3 they moved quickly, negotiating to receive community development funding once mounting pressure had caused the government to back down from Great Whale; and at settlement 4 they refused a first, less attractive offer and then entered secret negotiations at a favorable moment. Such opportunities are not treated as emerging by themselves but as usually created by persistent expansive argumentation and momentum-building.

The increase in dispositional legitimacy fed back into the next round in three ways: it gave the Crees a stronger voice in societal discussions, which helped them develop new problematizing arguments and benefit from discursive resonance with broader discourses and values; a more central position in the networks of actors relevant to building momentum, which was key to orchestrating media stunts and engaging in international politics; and a stronger position in negotiations with the other side. The three processes carry distinct temporalities, with expansive argumentation a long-term process, momentum created more situationally, and the seizing of emerging opportunities hinging on calls of timing (kairos), so succeeding in progressive legitimation is not simply a question of offering the right argument at the right time or of acting upon opportunities, but of multifaceted efforts with different time orientations. Over time, peripheral actors may become increasingly better at defining the terms of the negotiations because they can build upon previous settlements while more proactively seizing subsequent opportunities. The study therefore concludes that settlements should not be seen as endpoints but as stepping-stones of progressive legitimation in a trajectory of institutional change, and that this cumulative view is especially useful for understanding how peripheral actors may at least partially overcome the power asymmetry they face in achieving settlements.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances research on institutional settlements by shifting attention from individual settlements as endpoints to settlements as part of a longer trajectory of institutional change. It contributes to legitimacy and legitimation research by placing dispositional legitimacy at the center and showing how recognition of interests, rights, or voice can accumulate through formal settlements. It also extends research on peripheral and marginalized actors by theorizing how actors with limited initial power can use argumentation, momentum-building, and negotiation timing to progressively improve their position.

**Practical Implication**
For peripheral actors seeking recognition of rights, the study implies that moral argumentation alone is insufficient: actors also need to build momentum through media, political, legal, and network activity, and then judge when to formalize gains through imperfect settlements. The analysis also suggests that settlements are not final closure points; unresolved issues and partial recognition may become the basis for later claims and renegotiations.

**Limitations**
The authors emphasize that the model is grounded in a specific cultural-institutional context: peripheral actors dealing with a government organization in a Western democracy. The case is also a relatively successful trajectory in which four settlements increased dispositional legitimacy, so the model may not apply unchanged to less successful, regressive, authoritarian, or more politically unstable settings. The study focuses on the Cree side of the process, leaving the other side and internal divisions among key parties less fully examined.

**Future Research**
Future research could compare the Cree case with other institutional and cultural contexts, including authoritarian governments, major political transitions, less successful legitimation efforts, and movements such as Black Lives Matter or Palestinian self-determination. The authors also call for deeper research on dispositional legitimacy, including its dimensions, relationship to reputation or stigma, and effects on voice and negotiation. Further studies could examine micro-level rhetorical strategies, the role of history and memory, outright power dynamics, actors' skills and learning, the other side of negotiations, and internal divisions within stakeholder groups.
