# Seeing Oneself as a Valued Contributor: Social Worth Affirmation Improves Team Information Sharing

**Abstract**
Teams often fail to reach their potential because members’ concerns about being socially accepted prevent them from offering their unique perspectives to the team. Drawing on relational self and self-affirmation theory, we argue that affirmation of team members’ social worth by trusted people outside the team helps them internalize an identity as a valued contributor, thereby reducing social acceptance concerns and facilitating information sharing in teams. We devised three intervention studies to demonstrate the causal effect of social worth affirmation in teams. In Study 1, senior executive teams in which members experienced social worth affirmation performed better (compared to control teams) on a crisis simulation that required information sharing in teams. In Study 2, with U.S. military cadets, we examined social acceptance concerns as a mechanism by which social worth affirmation influences information sharing. In Study 3, we showed that social worth affirmation improves virtual teams’ ability to share information by exchanging unique information cues. Our results suggest that affirmation of the social worth of team members through their personal relationships broadens their sense of self, thereby reducing their social concerns about being accepted by other members. This, in turn, leads to better information sharing in teams.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether affirming team members' social worth through narratives from close relationships outside the team reduces social acceptance concerns and improves information sharing in teams. It also asks whether social acceptance concerns explain the effect of social worth affirmation on team information sharing.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Social worth affirmation from close relationships reduces one's concerns about social acceptance (negative).
- H2a: Social worth affirmation from close relationships increases a team's unique information sharing (positive).
- H2b: The relationship between social worth affirmation and information sharing in teams is explained by social acceptance concerns (mediation).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Social worth affirmation from close relationships.
- DV(s): Team information sharing, unique information sharing, direct information requests, and team performance in tasks requiring information integration.
- Mediators: Social acceptance concerns.
- Moderators: None in the hypothesized model. Validation Study 2 manipulated team threat and tested its interaction with valued contributor identity, but no interaction was found (p = .912).

The mechanism links external relational resources to team processes. Narratives from trusted personal contacts affirm members as valued contributors, broadening their working sense of self before or during team entry. This reduces concern about whether other team members will accept them, making members more willing to volunteer unique information and directly request information from others. Across the studies, the intervention improved team performance in a crisis simulation, problem solving in the Mt. Everest hidden-profile simulation, and exchange of unique cues in virtual teams.

**Data & Measures**
Five samples across three randomized intervention studies and two validation studies. The intervention studies are experimentally identified rather than associational: teams (Studies 1 and 2) or participants and then teams (Study 3) were randomly assigned to treatment or control, an approach the paper describes as "using an experimental design to allow for causal inference." Each study's sample, site, and measures are reported separately below and are not pooled.

- Study 1 sample: executives attending a leadership development program at a top government school in the United States, comprising career civil servants (the majority worked for the U.S. government) and current or former military officers, described as leaders from different government agencies around the world. Data came from four sessions across two years: October-November 2013 (N = 31), February-March 2014 (N = 70), April-May 2014 (N = 80), and October-November 2014 (N = 65), for 246 individuals in total (Mage = 48.47, SD = 7.13; 73% male), organized into 42 groups of five or six members (22 treatment, 20 control).
- Study 1 manipulation and measures: all participants completed a pre-arrival three-step best-self assignment; treatment teams then received the compiled report of narratives from their own social network plus a worksheet prompting a self-portrait, while control teams received a worksheet preparing a leadership-communication discussion. DV: expert-rated team performance on a 10-day public-health crisis simulation, scored by panels of three to four experts (16 experts in total, all blind to condition) on effective communication, creativity, clarity, feasibility, and overall value; ratings were standardized within sessions as z scores and averaged into one summary variable (α = .97; rWG = .77, average deviation = .46, ICC1 = .69). Controls: cohort dummies, team size, mean age, gender composition. Analysis: treatment-control t-test plus multiple regression at the team level (N = 42).
- Study 2 sample: 330 U.S. Air Force cadets (Mage = 28.26, SD = 3.98; 82% male) in 66 five-person teams receiving pre-commissioning training at the Officer Training School in Montgomery, Alabama, across two eight-week classes (June 28 to August 25, 2017 and July 5 to August 31, 2017). Cadets who did not nominate a network (n = 13; 4%) formed a separate group, and two teams did not complete the simulation because of technical issues, leaving 64 teams in the analyses.
- Study 2 manipulation and measures: treatment teams received an electronic best-self narrative report and sat together for 30 minutes sharing narratives; control teams instead recalled and reflected on a positive event, a positive-affect comparison condition. Manipulation check: felt social worth, 3-item scale from Grant and Gino (2010) (α = .92). Mediator: concerns about social acceptance, 4-item scale adapted from Leary's (1983) brief version of the "fear of negative evaluation" scale (α = .89). DV: team information sharing, operationalized as the likelihood of solving the problem in each of the three Mt. Everest simulation challenges, each of which could only be solved if all members supplied unique information. Alternative mechanisms: positive and negative affect (10-item scale from Barsade, 2002; α = .93 and .91), self-worth (8-item scale from Critcher & Dunning, 2015; α = .94), psychological safety (6-item scale from Edmondson, 1999; α = .69), and trust in teams (7-item scale adapted from Robinson & Rousseau, 1994; α = .86). Controls: mean age, gender composition, and class; team size was omitted because it did not vary. Mediation: bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals.
- Study 3 sample: a month-long Amazon Mechanical Turk study of working adults; of 159 eligible participants, 123 (Mage = 32.78, SD = 11.26; 31% male) took part in 41 three-person virtual teams (21 treatment, 20 control) and received a $15 Amazon.com gift card after a follow-up survey a month later.
- Study 3 manipulation and measures: treatment teams received their best-self narratives the night before the session and control teams after the follow-up survey; unlike Studies 1 and 2, teams neither sat together nor exchanged non-task information. Teams met simultaneously in a Chatzy.com chat room and worked a modified hidden-profile task adapted from Graetz, Boyle, Kimble, Thompson, and Garloch (1998), playing purchasing executives who had to rank three restaurant locations from partly unique checklists. DVs: numbers of unique and communal information cues, coded from the full chat transcripts by two coders (average ICC1 = .75, ICC2 = .83, rWG = .99). Exploratory DVs: counts of direct versus indirect information requests (average ICC1 = .82, ICC2 = .89, rWG = .96). Manipulation check: the same 3-item social worth scale as Study 2 (α = .92), administered after the team task. Controls: mean age and gender composition.
- Validation Study 1 sample and measures: 237 working employees (Mage = 44.67, SD = 14.59; 37% female) recruited through the alumni association of a Midwestern university in the United States, paid a $5.00 Amazon.com gift card. A new 3-item measure of social worth beliefs ("I bring something valuable to my social network," "I help others in my social network by being myself," "I feel that I bring unique value to others") was compared with self-affirmation (4-item scale from Critcher & Dunning, 2011; α = .87), self-verification (4-item scale from Wiesenfeld, Swann, Brockner, & Bartel, 2007; α = .88), self-efficacy in strengths (6-item scale adapted from Tsai et al., 2014; α = .90), and self-esteem (4 items from Rosenberg, 1965; α = .77), all on 7-point scales; analysis used structural equation modeling in Stata 15. The 3-item scale's reliability was separately checked on a distinct sample of 301 full-time MTurk employees (Mage = 38.80, SD = 10.64; 55% female; α = .89).
- Validation Study 2 sample and measures: 198 analyzed participants with full-time work experience (200 recruited, two removed for not following instructions; Mage = 31.53, SD = 11.04; 64% female) sourced from Prolific Academic and paid $2.00 for a 15-minute study. Participants completed the social worth beliefs scale and then a simulated group decision-making task adapted from Swaab, Phillips, and Schaerer (2016: Study 2b), acting as hiring committee members evaluating two candidates, with random assignment to a team threat versus no team threat condition implemented through scripted teammate messages. DV: number of information cues (up to 8) included in the participant's written recommendation (M = 2.71, SD = 1.4), analyzed with Poisson regression and robust standard errors.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 tested no hypothesis directly and used expert-rated team performance as a proxy for information sharing. Treatment teams outperformed control teams (Mtreatment = 0.32, SD = 0.98 vs. Mcontrol = -0.35, SD = 0.93), t(40) = 2.26, p = .029, and the effect remained robust after controlling for cohort, team size, mean age, and gender composition (b = .66, SE = 0.28, p < .05). The authors note that in this study the link between information sharing and team performance was implicit rather than observed.
- Study 2, H1 supported: the treatment reduced teams' average concerns about social acceptance (Mtreatment = 3.46, SD = 0.62 vs. Mcontrol = 3.82, SD = 0.60), b = -.35, SE = 0.15, p = .029.
- Study 2, H2a supported: the treatment increased team information sharing, that is, the number of Mt. Everest challenges solved (Mtreatment = 1.64, SD = 0.71 vs. Mcontrol = 1.15, SD = 0.79), b = .48, SE = 0.19, p = .014.
- Study 2, H2b supported: bootstrapping with 1,000 iterations showed that social acceptance concerns explained the relationship between social worth affirmation and information sharing (bias-corrected 95% CI [0.018, 0.104]).
- Study 2, alternative mechanisms eliminated: team average positive and negative affect did not differ across conditions (ps > .203), nor did self-worth (p = .799). Psychological safety was marginally higher under treatment (Mtreatment = 5.68 vs. Mcontrol = 5.49), b = .19, SE = 0.10, p = .059, and trust was significantly higher (Mtreatment = 6.22 vs. Mcontrol = 5.87), b = .36, SE = 0.10, p = .001, but none of the five alternative measures correlated significantly with information sharing and none mediated the effect.
- Study 3, H2a supported: the treatment increased the number of unique cues shared (Mtreatment = 14.71, SD = 2.00 vs. Mcontrol = 12.85, SD = 2.87), b = 1.78, SE = 0.78, p = .028. Communal cues did not differ significantly (Mtreatment = 12.33, SD = 2.41 vs. Mcontrol = 13.2, SD = 1.40), b = -.88, SE = 0.63, p = .168, and the total number of cues shared was unchanged (Mtreatment = 27.05 vs. Mcontrol = 26.05), b = .90, SE = 1.15, p = .439.
- Study 3, exploratory question-asking analysis: direct information requests were higher under treatment (Mtreatment = 9.62, SD = 3.98 vs. Mcontrol = 6.55, SD = 2.80), b = 2.95, SE = 1.07, p = .009, indirect requests were lower (Mtreatment = 3.52, SD = 2.18 vs. Mcontrol = 5.95, SD = 3.47), b = -2.40, SE = 0.85, p = .008, and total requests did not differ (Mtreatment = 13.14 vs. Mcontrol = 12.5), b = .55, SE = 1.48, p = .712, suggesting a shift in questioning style from indirect to direct rather than more questioning overall.
- Support pattern across studies: H2a was supported in Study 2 and again in Study 3; H1 and H2b were tested only in Study 2, so the mediation evidence rests on that single study; Study 1 supplies only indirect support through team performance.
- Validation Study 1: social worth beliefs were established as a distinct construct. The 3-item scale had good internal consistency (α = .81) and retained a single factor (eigenvalue = 2.18; 72.73% of variance), the five-factor model fit the data well (χ²(179) = 424.47; RMSEA = .08; CFI = .91; TLI = .90; SRMR = .08) with no lower-factor model fitting better, and all related constructs correlated positively with social worth beliefs but no correlation exceeded .37 (self-esteem r = .37, p < .001; self-efficacy in strengths use r = .35, p < .001; self-verification r = .33, p < .001; self-affirmation r = .20, p = .002).
- Validation Study 2: the more participants believed they brought value to their own social network, the more unique information they shared with a new team (b = .07, robust SE = .03, p = .034), and the relationship held when controlling for team threat (b = .07, robust SE = .03, p = .037). Team threat itself did not predict information sharing (b = -.04, robust SE = .07, p = .561) and did not interact with valued-contributor identity (p = .912).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to relational self theory by showing how close relationships outside a team can make a valued-contributor identity salient inside a new team. It extends self-affirmation theory by shifting affirmation from private reflection on personal values to social worth affirmed through others' narratives. It also contributes to team information-sharing research by identifying social acceptance concerns as a micro-level barrier that can be reduced before teams begin their work.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations can prepare members of new or temporary teams by prompting them to reflect on credible narratives of how they have contributed value to others. The intervention is especially relevant when teams must quickly pool unique expertise but have little time to build a shared team identity. Employers and team leaders can use social worth affirmation as a way to support communication and information sharing without relying only on within-team relationship building.

**Limitations**
The paper does not directly measure the identity internalization process that it theorizes, so the psychological shift toward seeing oneself as a valued contributor remains inferred from the intervention and downstream measures. The operationalization of social worth affirmation varies across studies, including differences in whether participants wrote self-narratives, shared narratives with teammates, or received different control exercises. The intervention was examined at the team level, so the studies cannot show whether affirming only one or two team members would produce similar effects for the whole team.

**Future Research**
Future research could directly measure how social worth affirmation changes valued-contributor identity and distinguish affirmation of preexisting self-views from incorporation of newly revealed reflected self-views. Studies could compare different sources, formats, durations, and frequencies of social worth affirmation, including recognition programs or thank-you notes. Future work could also examine whether social worth affirmation from close relationships interacts with affirmation or exclusion from the team itself.
