# Keep Your Eye on the Ball or on the Field? Exploring the Performance Implications of Executive Strategic Attention

**Abstract**
Scholars have highlighted executive attention as a vital organizational resource. However, as prior studies have examined different facets of attention, a theoretical tension has emerged as to whether attention to a broader or narrower set of strategic issues better facilitates firm performance. On the one hand, a narrower breadth of strategic attention limits the risk of managerial cognitive overload. On the other hand, a greater breadth of strategic attention helps managers avoid blind spots and identify more or better opportunities. We argue that this balance shifts based on the quantity and quality of opportunities available in the market and on firms’ recently demonstrated ability to execute against these opportunities effectively and efficiently. Using a novel text-analysis tool that we develop, validate, and make available, we find support for our arguments within a random sample of Standard & Poor’s (S&P) 500 companies. We find that broader strategic attention is associated with enhanced performance in weaker opportunity environments, with firms using their resources efficiently, or with firms’ failure to utilize their capabilities effectively. In supplementary analyses, we also provide new insights into the importance of following attention with subsequent organizational action.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether a greater breadth of executive strategic attention helps or hinders firm performance. It further asks how this relationship depends on the quantity and quality of external opportunities and on firms' recent effectiveness and efficiency in executing against those opportunities.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The quantity of opportunities that a firm has in its environment negatively moderates the relationship between the breadth of strategic attention and firm performance, such that the relationship is more negative when a firm faces an environment with a higher quantity of opportunities.
- H2: The quality of opportunities that a firm has in its environment negatively moderates the relationship between the breadth of strategic attention and firm performance, such that the relationship is more negative when a firm faces an environment with higher-quality opportunities.
- H3: The current effectiveness of usage of a firm's capabilities negatively moderates the relationship between the breadth of strategic attention and firm performance, such that the relationship is more negative as a firm uses its capabilities more effectively.
- H4: The current efficiency of usage of a firm's resources positively moderates the relationship between the breadth of strategic attention and firm performance, such that the relationship is more positive as a firm uses its resources more efficiently.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Breadth of strategic attention, measured as strategic breadth across 13 categories of strategic issues in executives' quarterly earnings-call communications.
- DV(s): Firm performance, measured as return on assets.
- Mediators: Not directly tested; the theory centers on the tradeoff between opportunity identification and cognitive overload, with supplementary analysis examining whether resource allocation follows changes in attention.
- Moderators: Industry munificence, average industry ROA, lagged firm performance, and current assets turnover.

The paper theorizes that broad strategic attention helps executives avoid blind spots and identify scarce or better-fitting opportunities, but also increases cognitive load and can dilute evaluation and execution. Broader attention is expected to be more beneficial when external opportunities are fewer or lower quality, when recent performance suggests poor fit between capabilities and opportunities, or when resource-use efficiency creates capacity to pursue more initiatives. The empirical results support all four moderation hypotheses and suggest in supplementary analyses that broader attention is more valuable when followed by increased resource allocation; the paper states that these supplementary results "only apply for R&D and capital expenditures" and not to other forms of expenditure.

**Data & Measures**
Single archival study (no multi-study design). Data: quarterly earnings-call transcripts for a random sample of 250 S&P 500 companies over 2008-2015, obtained from Seeking Alpha and yielding 7,090 firm-analyst call events. The 250 firms were drawn from a list of all S&P 500 firms in 2008, excluding banks, by assigning each remaining firm a random value and taking the 250 highest. Earnings-call data were supplemented with BoardEx, Compustat, CRSP, and IBES. The final estimation sample is 1,373 firm-year observations over 2009-2015, which is 627 fewer than the potential 2,000 (250 firms over eight years) because of lagged variables that eliminate the first year of data, missing values for key variables such as missing call transcripts, and firms dropping out through merger and acquisition activity.

- IV — Strategic breadth (breadth of strategic attention): a Herfindahl measure of how broadly managerial attention is allocated across 13 categories of strategic issue, computed as 1 minus the Herfindahl index of the word proportions associated with each issue category in a quarterly earnings call, with each proportion adjusted for the size of that issue's dictionary. Only the top management team's prepared statements and executives' responses to analyst questions are analyzed; analysts' own questions and commentary are excluded. Word counts are generated with Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software, and the quarterly values are averaged over the fiscal year to give the annual measure (mean 0.83, SD 0.038). The Herfindahl is subtracted from 1 so that larger values correspond to greater breadth.
- Measure development and validation: the 13 issue categories were built inductively and deductively from Strategic Management Journal (2012-2014), McKinsey Quarterly (2012-2014), and a survey of 12 management consultants from six leading strategy consulting firms, yielding 256 unique strategy terms that were coded into 10 categories and then expanded to 13 after two rounds of review by five senior scholars. The associated dictionaries began with 380 word roots and were validated by 36 scholars from 24 unique universities (five to eight raters per dictionary, average 5.6), which eliminated 80 words and left 300 words across the 13 dictionaries (minimum 10, maximum 40 per dictionary).
- DV — Performance: return on assets (ROA) from Compustat, winsorized at the 1% level (mean 0.069, SD 0.059).
- Moderator (H1) — Industry munificence: five-year revenue growth of the industry, estimated by regressing annual revenues on year and dividing by the mean five-year revenue value for firms in that industry, measured at the four-digit GICS level.
- Moderator (H2) — Average industry ROA: rolling five-year average ROA for all firms in each four-digit GICS industry.
- Moderator (H3) — Lagged performance: the firm's one-year lagged ROA, used as the observable indicator of whether the firm is effectively matching its capabilities to available opportunities.
- Moderator (H4) — Current assets turnover: firm annual revenues divided by average current assets in the year, lagged one year, used as the indicator of recent efficiency in resource utilization.
- Controls (Table 2): size (log of firm revenues), R&D intensity, slack, debt-to-equity, diversification (Herfindahl of revenue proportions across four-digit GICS codes), SG&A, CEO tenure, firm age, earnings surprise, analyst rating, analyst breadth, analyst tone, dynamism, industry concentration, and average industry R&D intensity. Firm-level and stock-market controls are lagged one year except firm age and CEO tenure; the five industry-level variables are not lagged, to reflect current market conditions.

Identification is associational rather than experimental. The winsorized DV is continuous and normally distributed, so hypotheses are tested with OLS regressions using one-year lagged values of strategic breadth, firm and year fixed effects, and standard errors clustered at the firm level; strategic breadth and the four moderators are mean-centered to aid interpretation of the interaction terms, and all VIFs are below the recommended level of 10. A Hausman test rejected the null of no difference between firm fixed-effects and random-effects models (p = 0.00). The authors state that strategic breadth is an endogenous choice, leaving its main coefficient prone to omitted-variable bias (a lesser concern for the interaction terms), and address this with an instrumental variable two-stage least squares (IV 2SLS) robustness check that instruments strategic breadth with the lagged average number of words in management's commentary and responses per quarterly analyst call, combined with split-sample regressions on high and low values of each moderator to avoid needing two instruments. Non-lagged strategic breadth values, a non-winsorized and industry-adjusted ROA, and an alternative annual construction of strategic breadth are also examined as robustness tests.

**Key Findings**
All four hypotheses are supported. Coefficients below are OLS estimates from Table 4 (n = 1,373 firm-years in every model; firm and year fixed effects; firm-clustered standard errors in parentheses). Asterisks marking the p < .05 and p < .01 levels are lost in the extracted text, so only the coefficients the table daggers at p < .10 are labeled with a level here; the paper's text describes the remaining interaction coefficients as statistically significant.

- H1 supported: strategic breadth x munificence is negative and statistically significant (Model 3: -5.265 (0.865); fully saturated Model 7: -4.427 (1.077)) — broader strategic attention has a more favorable performance impact when firms face fewer opportunities in their external environment.
- H2 supported: strategic breadth x average industry ROA is negative and statistically significant (Model 4: -8.610 (2.731); Model 7: -4.721 (2.456), p < .10).
- H3 supported: strategic breadth x lagged performance is negative and statistically significant (Model 5: -2.352 (0.803); Model 7: -1.803 (0.686)).
- H4 supported: strategic breadth x current assets turnover is positive and statistically significant (Model 6: 0.037 (0.021), p < .10; Model 7: 0.054 (0.023)).
- All four interactions keep their hypothesized signs and remain supported in the fully saturated Model 7 (R2 = 0.200, against 0.101 for the controls-only Model 1).
- Main effects: munificence and average industry ROA are positively associated with performance. The main effect of strategic breadth is not statistically distinguishable across Models 2-7, but in supplementary IV regressions specified without interaction terms its coefficient is negative and statistically significant, which the authors read as indicating that on average a greater breadth of strategic attention is associated with reduced performance.
- Effect size (Table 5, using Model 7): moving from the bottom to the top decile of a moderator, a one-standard-deviation change in strategic breadth shifts firms' ROA by between 0.8 and 1.2 percentage points, against a sample mean ROA of 6.9%.
- Supplementary resource-allocation analysis (Table 6): the interaction between the change in strategic breadth (t-2 to t-1) and the subsequent change in R&D intensity (0.130 (0.055), n = 1,272) or in capital intensity (0.048 (0.024), n = 1,272) is positive and statistically significant, so an increase in strategic breadth followed by increased R&D or capital investment enhances performance, and the greater the resource increase the greater the performance improvement. The equivalent interactions for advertising intensity, SG&A, and the PPE ratio are not statistically significant.
- Supplementary environment-split analysis (Table 7, p-values in parentheses): the negative moderation by lagged performance is stronger under high munificence (-4.976 (0.000)) than under low munificence (-0.794 (0.641)), and stronger under strong (-1.833 (0.047)) than weak (-1.532 (0.306)) average industry ROA. The positive moderation by current assets turnover is larger under low munificence (0.093 (0.285)) than high munificence (0.015 (0.607)) and under weak (0.070 (0.052)) than strong (0.024 (0.280)) average industry ROA, though the authors note that the reduced split-sample sizes leave the munificence pair statistically insignificant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the attention-based view and managerial cognition literatures by specifying when broad versus narrow strategic attention is performance-enhancing. It reconciles prior arguments favoring focus with arguments favoring broad responsiveness by showing that the attention-performance relationship depends on external opportunity conditions and internal execution conditions. It also contributes a validated text-analysis tool for measuring attention across 13 strategic issue categories in executive communications.

**Practical Implication**
Executives should not treat focus or breadth as universally superior. The paper suggests broadening attention in weaker opportunity environments, when recent performance indicates poor capability-opportunity fit, or when efficient resource use creates room for additional initiatives. It also warns that attention alone is insufficient: broader attention can harm performance if it is not followed by resource allocation that enables action.

**Limitations**
The paper does not investigate time in significant depth, especially whether firms attend to some strategic issues in the short term and others in the longer term. The strategic breadth measure can take the same value through different combinations of the 13 issue categories, so it does not fully distinguish coherent from incoherent attention portfolios. The supplementary resource-allocation analysis is limited because the data do not provide a precise breakdown of resource allocation by the specific strategic issues receiving managerial attention.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine longer time horizons and compare firms that adapt their breadth of strategic attention appropriately with firms that do not. Scholars could also study which individual strategic issues, or which combinations of issues, shape performance under different conditions. Additional work could connect attention to issue-specific resource allocation rather than only aggregate resource changes.
