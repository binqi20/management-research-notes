# Guilt and Corporate Philanthropy: The Case of the Privatization in China

**Abstract**
Firms’ harm-inflicting decisions/actions can make firm leaders feel guilt toward those who are harmed, which then motivates them to make restitution through corporate philanthropy. Our primary setting for investigating this proposition is the privatization of Chinese firms. We argue that massive layoffs resulting from privatization triggered guilt among firm leaders to drive corporate philanthropy. The analysis of a national survey of Chinese private firms shows that on average privatized firms made approximately 26% more charitable contributions than firms founded as private. This was particularly the case when (a) privatization resulted in layoffs (vs. no layoffs), (b) the firms’ provinces experienced greater unemployment at the time of privatization, (c) the firms’ leaders were directly involved in privatization, (d) the influence of Confucianism was stronger in their provinces, and (e) firm leaders had stronger ties with laid-off workers. An interview-based field study of 25 firm leaders involved in privatization provides qualitative evidence of the influence of guilt on privatized firms’ philanthropy. Finally, a vignette experiment conducted on EMBA students shows the mediation of guilt between privatization and firms’ philanthropic intention. These findings establish guilt as a potent emotional driver of corporate philanthropy, helping redirect the attention of corporate philanthropy research toward nonfinancial drivers.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether firms' harm-inflicting decisions and actions can make firm leaders feel guilt toward harmed others and thereby increase corporate philanthropy as restitution. It studies this question in the context of Chinese SOE privatization, where layoffs created harm to workers and their families.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Privatized SOEs are likely to undertake more corporate philanthropy than firms founded as private.
Hypothesis 2a. The positive effect of experiencing privatization on corporate philanthropy is stronger when the privatization of an SOE resulted in more layoffs.
Hypothesis 2b. The positive effect of experiencing privatization on corporate philanthropy is stronger for a privatized SOE located in an area with a higher rate of unemployment at the time of privatization.
Hypothesis 3a. The positive effect of experiencing privatization on corporate philanthropy is stronger when the leader of a privatized SOE was involved in the privatization.
Hypothesis 3b. The positive effect of experiencing privatization on corporate philanthropy is stronger in areas with a stronger Confucian influence.
Hypothesis 4. The positive effect of experiencing privatization on corporate philanthropy is stronger when the leader of a privatized SOE had a longer tenure in the SOE.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Experiencing privatization, especially privatization with layoffs, in formerly state-owned or collectively owned Chinese firms.
- DV(s): Corporate philanthropy, measured primarily as charitable contributions in the national survey and as philanthropic intention in the vignette experiment.
- Mediators: Guilt toward laid-off SOE workers is theorized as the central mechanism and is directly tested as a mediator in the vignette experiment.
- Moderators: Severity of harm through layoffs and provincial unemployment, responsibility through leader involvement in privatization and Confucian influence, and concern for the harmed through leader SOE tenure and ties with workers.

The paper argues that privatization could arouse guilt because firm leaders saw themselves as implicated in layoffs that harmed SOE workers. Guilt then motivates restitution, which can be made through the firm as corporate philanthropy when personal assistance is too limited for the scale of harm. Study 1 tests the observable implications of this mechanism, Study 2 provides interview evidence that leaders linked layoffs, guilt, and restitution, and Study 3 experimentally tests guilt as a mediator between privatization with layoffs and philanthropic intention.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (survey study). Data come from the national survey of Chinese private firms conducted since 1993 by the United Front Work Department, the All-China Federation of Industry and Commerce, and the State Administration for Industry and Commerce through face-to-face structured interviews with owners of private firms randomly sampled across all provinces and industries; the 2006 and 2008 questionnaires asked about total charitable contributions made since registration as private. The final data from the two surveys contain observations for 3,071 firms without missing values on variables of interest, supplemented with archival data from the National Statistics Bureau of China.
- DV, corporate philanthropy: the logarithm of the charitable contributions a firm reported having made since its registration as a private firm.
- IV, privatization: a dummy coded 1 if the firm reported being privatized from an existing state-owned or collectively owned firm and 0 if it was founded as private.
- Moderator, privatization with layoffs: a dummy coded 1 if the current owner obtained the firm through a "zero" acquisition — paying nothing to take over an SOE by using its assets to pay off debts and reemploying laid-off employees. The surveys did not ask how many workers were laid off.
- Moderator, province unemployment rate: the unemployment rate in the province where the privatized SOE was located in the year before its registration as private, with location taken from firms' reported postal codes.
- Moderator, involvement in privatization: a dummy coded 1 for firms choosing either of the two answers to "How did you obtain your firm?" that imply involvement (obtained by the original leaders of the SOE; obtained by the SOE's employees or managers with major ownership).
- Moderator, province Confucian influence: a historical proxy based on the keju civil-service examination — 26,746 Qing-dynasty jinshi (all 112 national exams) assigned to provinces by residential address, and in a second specification 531 weike, each weighted by 1880 provincial population.
- Moderator, owner SOE tenure: estimated as registration year minus (birth year plus start age), with birth year taken as survey year minus reported age and start age set at 18 (results also checked with 19, 20, and 21). The authors note the measure overestimates tenure for leaders who did not remain in the same SOE, making support for Hypothesis 4 harder to find.
- Controls: firm size (logarithm of registered assets), firm age, firm profitability (return on equity), an owner-CEO dummy, percentage government ownership, a North/South location dummy, owner age, education, gender, logged income, self-reported economic and social status, ascribed and achieved political ties, CCP membership, and provincial GDP per capita in the year before registration.

Study 1 is estimated with a multilevel model that treats cross-province mean differences as a random effect and estimates robust standard errors clustered on province, with industry and year fixed effects in all models; OLS produced consistent results. The design is observational rather than experimental. Because whether to lay off employees during privatization "might not be entirely random," the authors add propensity score matching (logit model, nearest-neighbor matching with replacement, caliper of 0.25 standard deviation, matching ratios of 1:1 through 1:5) to construct balanced control and treatment groups, and they test observable implications of public pressure, renqing, and mianzi as alternative mechanisms.

Study 2 (field study). Interviews with 25 leaders of privatized SOEs, secured through social ties, with each additional informant selected from a new industry or province where possible; the final sample covers 18 industries and 12 provinces. Interviews were conducted by at least two interviewers, 4 face to face and 21 by phone, totaling 1,368 minutes, recorded when permitted and otherwise captured in detailed notes; informants knew only that the research concerned the privatization of SOEs. Analysis was theory-guided content analysis: identifying constructs and their relationships in each transcript, organizing the material by construct, and tabulating responses across informants for layoff, harm, guilt, and corporate philanthropy, with three authors coding separately using the same rules.

Study 3 (vignette experiment). 131 EMBA students with executive experience above the vice-president level participated online. They read a short text and watched a three-minute video vignette about privatization in China and the distress of laid-off SOE employees, answered attention-check questions, and were randomly assigned to imagine themselves as firm leaders involved in privatization either with layoffs (treatment, 73 subjects) or without layoffs (control, 58 subjects). After excluding 26 subjects who failed the attention check, the final sample was 105 subjects (43 control, 62 treatment); the excluded subjects did not differ systematically from those retained on gender, age, SOE experience, CCP membership, or working years. Guilt items were adopted from Kugler and Jones (1992) and philanthropic intention items from Smith and McSweeney (2007), placed on separate screens with a new imagined condition to reduce common method bias; reliabilities were Cronbach's alpha = 0.83 for guilt and 0.90 for philanthropic intention. The data were analyzed with a multilevel model with robust standard errors to account for nonindependence among students from the same class, following a conventional four-step mediation approach plus the Sobel-Goodman mediation test.

**Key Findings**
The three studies provide converging evidence that firm leaders' guilt is at least partly responsible for the observed effect of privatization on corporate philanthropy, but support in Study 1 is not uniform across hypotheses.

Study 1 (multilevel regressions; N = 3,071 in the full-sample models, N = 677 in the privatized-SOE subsample models).
- H1 supported: the coefficient of privatization is positive and significant (Model 2); all else equal, privatized SOEs made approximately 26% more charitable contributions than firms founded as private.
- H2a not supported in the main model: the coefficient of the privatization-with-layoffs dummy is positive as expected but not significant (Model 3). A post hoc test of the mean difference between the two types of privatized SOEs is significant (t = 2.726, df = 675) in the expected direction, and propensity-score-matched Models 9-13 show consistent evidence that firms with layoffs make more charitable contributions, with Model 9 implying approximately 197% more than privatized SOEs without layoffs. The authors nonetheless describe Table 3 as providing "mostly suggestive evidence for Hypothesis 2a," given matched samples of only 44 to 96 observations.
- H2b supported: the interaction of privatization with the provincial unemployment rate is positive and significant (Model 4); compared with firms founded as private, privatized SOEs made approximately 57% more charitable contributions in low-unemployment provinces (one SD below the mean) but approximately 100% more in high-unemployment provinces (one SD above the mean), a net difference of about 43%.
- H3a supported: the coefficient of owner involvement in privatization is positive and significant (Model 5); privatized SOEs whose owners were involved in the privatization made approximately 26% more charitable contributions than privatized SOEs whose owners were not.
- H3b supported: both measures of Confucian influence have significant, positive moderating effects (Models 6 and 7). The discussion reports that privatized SOEs in provinces with a strong Confucian influence made 58% (jinshi measure) to 65% (weike measure) more charitable contributions than those in provinces with a weak Confucian influence. The authors did not find consistent support for a direct effect of Confucianism on corporate philanthropy — only for its moderating role.
- H4 supported: the coefficient of owner SOE tenure is highly significant with the expected positive sign (Model 8); an increase of 10 years in owner SOE tenure increases corporate philanthropy by 20% on average.
- Robustness and alternative explanations: propensity-score-matched and endogeneity models (Table 4) produced results largely consistent with Table 2. Further analyses of the survey data did not support the derived implications of a public-pressure mechanism, found support for neither of the two renqing interactions (turnover of provincial officials and provincial GDP growth), and found no support for either mianzi moderator (whether the leader grew up in the countryside or the city; separation of headquarters and operation locations).

Study 2 (25 informants, coded in Table 5). 24 of 25 informants confirmed that their SOEs underwent significant downsizing, 23 of 25 that laid-off workers experienced great distress, 21 of 25 that they experienced a feeling of guilt or nei jiu, and 18 of 25 that guilt influenced subsequent engagement in philanthropy. Restitution was made predominantly through corporate philanthropy rather than personally — only 1 of the 18 acted mainly through personal endeavors — and informants identified their firm's improved financial condition as an important condition for helping laid-off workers. Among the seven informants who did not report a philanthropic response, two said no significant corporate philanthropy was undertaken, and four, while feeling guilty and sympathetic, held that their firm's responsibility was to provide the best products and services and that helping those harmed by the reform was the government's responsibility. Informants mostly reported guilt rather than shame, emphasizing that they had been following central-government policy.

Study 3 (vignette experiment, N = 105). Privatization with layoffs had a significant, positive effect on guilt (Model 21) and a significant, positive effect on philanthropic intention (Model 22), and the effect of guilt on charitable intention was highly significant and positive (Model 23). Model 24 shows the mediation effect of guilt, as confirmed by the Sobel-Goodman mediation test (indirect effect coefficient = 1.27, p = 0.05). Testing the reverse path with philanthropic intention as the mediator between privatization and guilt found no support for mediation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to corporate philanthropy research by establishing guilt as a nonfinancial emotional driver of firm giving, complementing rational accounts focused on external pressure or financial returns. It also connects values and emotions by showing that Confucian influence strengthens the effect of privatization on philanthropy, suggesting that moral values shape whether harm-inflicting strategic actions arouse guilt. More broadly, it brings top managers' aroused emotions into strategic management research by showing how guilt can affect subsequent firm-level strategic action.

**Practical Implication**
For firms, the findings suggest that expressing guilt and making restitution can help managers respond to harmed stakeholders and may counter cynical interpretations of philanthropy when the expression is genuine. For the public, the study suggests that appealing to firm leaders' guilt may sometimes complement pressure-based tactics such as condemnation, protest, or boycott in encouraging firms to address social harm.

**Limitations**
The paper notes that guilt does not automatically arise from every harm-inflicting action; leaders must be aware of harm and feel responsible for it, and these conditions are shaped by severity, proximity, social ties, and values. It also notes that guilt may not always translate into philanthropy because firms' financial condition and leaders' views of corporate responsibility can constrain restitution. The authors test several alternative explanations, including public pressure, renqing, and mianzi.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine other sources of guilt beyond dramatic harm-inflicting actions, including survivor guilt, outdoing guilt, privilege guilt, and trait-based guilt. The authors also call for more systematic research on when guilt is aroused, when it translates into corporate philanthropy, and how long its influence lasts. They further suggest studying whether guilt affects other strategic decisions, such as avoiding or modifying actions that may harm stakeholders.
