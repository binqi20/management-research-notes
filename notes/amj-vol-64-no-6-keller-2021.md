# In With the Old? Examining When Boomerang Employees Outperform New Hires

**Abstract**
As most careers now span across organizations, former employees represent a growing source of potential hires for many organizations. Yet, we know little about whether and when firms benefit by rehiring former employees. To answer these questions, we adopt a knowledge-based view of hiring to develop new theory about how returning former employees’ (“boomerangs”) post-hire performance might differ from that of external hires who have no previous experience with the firm (“new hires”). We theorize that, relative to new hires, boomerangs’ familiarity with the organization’s social system will allow them to more effectively engage in coordination and overcome internal resistance from organizational incumbents. As a consequence, boomerangs should have a particular advantage in roles that require a higher degree of coordination and in units that are likely to be more resistant to outsiders. Comparing the post-hire performance of 2,053 boomerangs and 10,858 new hires over an eight-year period in a large health care organization, we find that, upon being (re)hired into the organization, boomerangs outperform new hires in their initial job spell and that this performance advantage is larger in jobs requiring greater internal coordination and in contexts characterized by greater internal resistance to external hires.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether former employees who return to an organization perform differently from external hires with no prior experience in the firm. It also asks when boomerangs' prior familiarity with the organization's social system creates a larger post-hire performance advantage.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Boomerangs will have higher performance than new hires do during their initial job spells in the organization (positive).
- H2a: The degree of internal interdependence required by a job will moderate the relationship between boomerang status and individual performance, such that this positive relationship will be stronger in jobs with high demand for internal interdependence.
- H2b: The degree of relational demands imposed by a job will moderate the relationship between boomerang status and individual performance, such that this positive relationship will be stronger in jobs with high relational demands.
- H2c: The level of administrative coordination required by a job will moderate the relationship between boomerang status and individual performance, such that this positive relationship will be stronger in jobs with high demand for administrative coordination.
- H3a: The number of recent new hires in a work unit will moderate the relationship between boomerang status and individual performance, such that this positive relationship will be stronger in work units where the number of recent new hires is low.
- H3b: Managers' tenure in the firm will moderate the relationship between boomerang status and individual performance, such that this positive relationship will be stronger as the tenure of a new entrant's manager increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Boomerang status, defined as being a former permanent employee rehired by the same organization, compared with new-hire status.
- DV(s): Standardized annual individual performance rating during the first job spell after entry or reentry.
- Mediators: Not directly tested; the paper theorizes familiarity with the organization's social system, improved coordination, and reduced internal resistance as mechanisms.
- Moderators: Job internal interdependence, relational demands, administrative coordination, percentage of recent new hires in the work unit, and manager firm tenure.

The argument extends the knowledge-based view from organization-level coordination to individual entry and reentry. Boomerangs are expected to retain tacit, firm-specific knowledge of norms, routines, communication expectations, and social-system boundaries, allowing them to coordinate work more effectively and frame their ideas in ways that incumbents accept. The results show an average boomerang performance advantage, with stronger support in jobs with high relational demands and administrative coordination and in units where internal resistance is likely higher. The predicted moderation by internal interdependence is not supported.

**Data & Measures**
Annual personnel data for the years 2009 to 2016 from the U.S. offices of a Fortune 500 health care company the authors call "Asclepius". The final sample covers the first job spells of 2,053 boomerangs and 10,858 new hires upon (re)entry, giving 25,686 person-year observations spanning 44 operating companies, 124 functions, 13 hierarchical pay grades, and 27 states; the analysis is limited to pay grades and functions filled by both boomerangs and new hires during the observation period. The design is archival and observational, and the authors state plainly that "the archival nature of our study means that our results are associational"; they name no identification strategy and report that they were unable to locate a plausible source of exogeneity in staffing.

- DV: standardized performance rating, based on the annual rating each employee receives for each calendar year. Observations early in the window used a 0-to-9 scale (n = 12,890; mean = 5.29, SD = 0.99) before the firm moved over three years to an 8-point two-component system (n = 12,796; mean = 6.07, SD = 0.89); to make the scales compatible, ratings were transformed to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 within division, pay grade, and year.
- IV: boomerang, a dichotomous indicator coded 1 for individuals who previously worked full time for Asclepius and returned, and 0 for new hires who had not worked for the firm before entering during the study period. Boomerangs were 12.6% of all external hires firm-wide and 15.9% of external hires in the analysis sample.
- Coordination moderators, coded from O*NET scores mapped to each of the 124 functions in the sample through a four-step matching process (two independent coders, review by two further authors, consensus discussion, and validation by a firm expert): internal interdependence = mean of two of the three role relationships items in the work-context domain, excluding the external-customer item (alpha = .84); relational demands = mean of the three interpersonal orientation items in the work styles domain — "cooperation," "concern for others," and a "social orientation" (alpha = .88); administrative coordination = mean of the three administering items in the work activities domain — "monitoring and controlling resources," "staffing organizational units," and "performing administrative activities" (alpha = .87).
- Internal-resistance moderators from personnel records: percentage of recent new hires in the work unit = number of unit employees with fewer than two years of firm tenure divided by work unit size (a raw count with unit size controlled gave qualitatively similar results); manager firm tenure = number of years the employee's direct manager had been employed at Asclepius.
- Controls: job complexity (mean of "processing information" and "analyzing data or information" from the mental processes scale; alpha = .90), job autonomy (mean of "degree of structured versus unstructured work" and "freedom to make decisions" from the structural job characteristics scale; alpha = .91), unit size (natural log of unit headcount), job spell tenure, and dummies for the 13 pay grades, 17 functional areas, 44 operating companies, states, and years. Employee demographics were unavailable because of the firm's data-privacy concerns.
- Estimation: ordinary least squares with robust standard errors clustered by individual, each observation being a person-year within the worker's first job spell. On the firm's advice, observations of workers (re)hired in or after July of a year were excluded because managers could rate them on at most three months of work, dropping roughly 15% of boomerangs (n = 404) and 15% of new hires (n = 2,457), plus 3,041 further observations.
- Supplementary analyses use their own distinct samples and are not pooled with the main person-year sample: 12,911 first-rating-only observations (Table 3); 79 boomerangs with observable performance during their first spell before initial exit, and 509 boomerangs (about one-quarter of the sample, the rest left-censored) with observable final pre-exit jobs and ratings; 37,491 observations comparing boomerangs' pre-exit ratings with all other employees in the same job (Table 4); 874 observations of those 509 boomerangs, with years away and its square added and pay band and division substituted for pay grade and operating company (Table 5); 15,628 observations comparing those 509 boomerangs with new hires (Table 6); Cox event-history models of subsequent promotion, lateral move, demotion, and voluntary and involuntary exit reported as odds ratios (Table 7); a rough six-category measure of outside (non-Asclepius) work experience added as a control (Table A1); and a matched set of 4,880 individuals (266 boomerangs and 4,614 employees who remained, about 17 stayers per boomerang) for the career-advancement comparison.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: boomerangs received performance ratings approximately 9% of a standard deviation higher than new hires during their first job spell (b = .092, p = .000; Model 1, Table 2), which the authors describe as a modest but perceptible effect.
- H2a not supported: Model 2 shows no significant interaction between boomerang status and the job's level of internal interdependence.
- H2b supported: a positive and significant boomerang-by-relational-demands interaction (b = .009, p = .033; Model 3). A one standard deviation increase in a job's relational demands increases the boomerang advantage to approximately 12% of a standard deviation.
- H2c supported: a positive and significant boomerang-by-administrative-coordination interaction (b = .004, p = .012; Model 4). A one standard deviation increase in administrative coordination increases the advantage to approximately 15% of a standard deviation.
- H3a supported: a negative and significant boomerang-by-percentage-recent-new-hires interaction (b = -.255, p = .006; Model 6), that is, the boomerang advantage is larger in work units with fewer recent new hires.
- H3b supported: a positive and significant boomerang-by-manager-firm-tenure interaction (b = .006, p = .015; Model 7). A one standard deviation increase in manager tenure increases the advantage to approximately 12% of a standard deviation.
- Specification sensitivity: results are consistent when the coordination interactions are entered jointly (Model 5), when both internal-resistance interactions are entered jointly (Model 8), and in the first-rating-only models (Table 3). In the fully saturated Model 9, however, the interactions for managerial tenure (p = .053) and recent new hires (p = .072) fall just short of the standard significance test, so support for H3a and H3b is not uniform across specifications. Adding the outside-work-experience control leaves the main effect significant but roughly halves it (b = .045, p = .048), a reduction the authors attribute to bias in that measure rather than to mediation.
- Higher boomerang ability is ruled out as the explanation. Boomerangs' ratings in the year they exited were significantly lower than those of colleagues in the same job (b = -.100; Table 4); the 79 boomerangs with observable first-spell data averaged 0.57 of a standard deviation below the mean versus 0.50 for comparable new hires, a difference that was not statistically significant; and boomerang and new-hire jobs did not differ significantly in internal interdependence (75.1 vs. 75.8), relational demands (66.7 vs. 67.2), administrative coordination (38.2 vs. 39.3), complexity (61.1 vs. 62.6), or autonomy (80.8 vs. 79.8).
- Within-boomerang heterogeneity (509-boomerang subsample, Table 5): returning to work for the same manager predicted higher performance (b = .345, p = .013), while returning to the same work unit did not (b = .166, p = .354). Prior performance rating (b = .117, p = .000) and voluntary rather than involuntary exit from the previous job (b = .292, p = .000) both predicted post-rehire performance, indicating the firm could have made better hiring decisions from information it did not exploit. The average boomerang advantage held in this subsample (b = .095, p = .038) and survived dropping boomerangs who returned to the same manager or the same unit (Table 6).
- Subsequent mobility (Table 7): boomerangs had higher rates of promotion (OR = 1.55, p = .000) and lateral moves (OR = 1.24, p = .001) than new hires but, contrary to expectations, were not significantly less likely to be demoted or to exit voluntarily, and were significantly more likely to be involuntarily terminated during the first job spell (OR = 1.33, p = .023).
- Compared with employees who stayed, boomeranging was not a better route up the ladder: on return, boomerangs performed worse (mean standardized rating = -0.29) than matched employees who remained (mean = -0.01), only 35% outperformed their matched stayers, boomerangs returned an average 0.71 pay grades above their previous job against 1.24 grades of advancement for stayers, and only 32% jumped ahead of their matched stayers; regressions with comparison-group fixed effects confirmed both the lower performance and the lower pay grade on return.
- Interacting boomerang status with job tenure produced no significant interaction, so the advantage persisted across the first job spell, though the authors caution against strong conclusions because the average first spell lasted only just over two years.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to strategic human capital research by explaining why boomerang employees differ from other external hires and by specifying the job and work-context conditions under which their prior organizational experience matters. It extends the knowledge-based view by showing how familiarity with an organization's social system can shape individual performance during hiring and socialization, not only collective organizational capability. It also advances source-of-hire research by arguing that the value of a hiring source depends on role demands and local social context.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that organizations should not evaluate boomerang hiring only as a general alumni-recruitment practice; its value is greater in roles requiring relational subtlety, administrative coordination, and navigation of resistant internal contexts. Employers may benefit from using prior employment information more carefully, because supplementary analyses show that prior performance and voluntary exit predict performance after rehire even though the focal organization did not strongly exploit that information. The paper also suggests that firms with strong social systems may need more intensive integration support for external hires who lack prior organizational familiarity.

**Limitations**
The authors caution that the eight-year observation window prevents them from assessing longer-term employment outcomes after later lateral or upward moves. They also lacked full external work-history and individual-difference data for all workers, so boomerangs and new hires may differ in ways not captured by the personnel records. Because the study uses archival observational data from one organization, the findings are associational and cannot conclusively prove that social-system knowledge causes the observed performance differences.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine boomerang and new-hire outcomes over longer periods and after subsequent job changes. Additional studies could test how rehiring boomerangs affects colleagues, work groups, and perceptions of loyalty or resistance. The authors also call for work on whether the findings generalize across organizations that vary in social-system strength and openness to returning employees.
