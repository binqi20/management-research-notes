# The Catch-22 of Countering a Moral Occupational Stigma in Employee-Customer Interactions

**Abstract**
Past research has suggested that individuals in dirty work occupations can manage their self-views so as to derive positive self-definitions that allow them to perform their tasks with less of the burden of stigma. Results from our three studies show that this may not necessarily be the case when they try to manage how occupational outsiders view them. Findings from our studies show that in terms of this external perspective, rather than thwarting the occupational stigma, active stigma management by the stigma bearer can unintentionally reinforce the stigma. Drawing on the expectancy-confirmation framework from the stereotyping literature, we explain the cognitive mechanism that underlies this inadvertent stigma reinforcement in the stigma perceiver, and investigate the job performance implications for the stigma bearer. We test our hypotheses across two field studies and one experimental study, involving cross-industry and cross-sectional data, as well as one longitudinal data set including 128,549 employee–customer transactions. Our results contribute to research on dirty work by revealing that stigma management can be a double-edged sword with unintended negative consequences for the stigmatized worker.

**Research Question**
The paper asks whether moral occupational stigma can be effectively countered when frontline employees try to manage how occupational outsiders view them. It specifically examines whether social comparisons and social proofing change customers' stereotypes of immorality and thereby affect frontline employees' job performance.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1a. A morally stigmatized frontline employee's inclination to use social comparisons is negatively associated with the frontline employee's job performance.
Hypothesis 1b. A morally stigmatized frontline employee's inclination to use social proofing is positively related with the frontline employee's job performance.
Hypothesis 2a. The negative relationship between a frontline employee's inclination to use social comparisons and job performance will be mediated by customers' occupational stereotypes of immorality.
Hypothesis 2b. The positive relationship between a frontline employee's inclination to use social proofing and job performance will be mediated by customers' occupational stereotypes of immorality.
Hypothesis 3a. There is a conditional indirect effect between a morally stigmatized frontline employee's inclination to use social comparisons and job performance via customers' stereotypes of immorality, such that the negative indirect effect of social comparisons on job performance is less negative when the occupational stigma is mild compared to when it is strong.
Hypothesis 3b. There is a conditional indirect effect between a morally stigmatized frontline employee's inclination to use social proofing and job performance via customers' stereotypes of immorality, such that the positive indirect effect of social proofing on job performance is less positive when the occupational stigma is mild compared to when it is strong.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Frontline employees' use of stigma-countering tactics, specifically social comparisons and social proofing.
- DV(s): Frontline employee job performance, measured as transaction value, actual customer purchase, and customer purchase intention across the three studies.
- Mediators: Customers' occupational stereotypes of immorality.
- Moderators: Moral stigma intensity of the frontline job in Study 3.

The paper argues that moral occupational stigma activates customers' stereotypes of immorality, shaping how they interpret a frontline employee's attempts to counter the stigma. Social comparisons can backfire because customers interpret downward "us versus them" comparisons through the lens of the initial stigma, confirming rather than disconfirming stereotypes of immorality. Social proofing, by contrast, points to value created for other customers and can weaken the applicability of the immoral-salesperson stereotype. The experimental evidence further suggests that these indirect effects operate mainly when the frontline job carries high moral stigma intensity.

**Data & Measures**
Three studies, each with its own sample and design.

Study 1 is a longitudinal field study in the fashion retailing industry, run with an apparel brand in Europe that owns its manufacturing facilities and its network of retail stores. Every frontline employee of the brand was surveyed about job perceptions and about their inclinations to use social proofing and social comparisons; 131 usable employee responses were obtained (79% response rate) and matched to the firm's archival transaction records through personal identification numbers, yielding 128,549 customer transactions over one year nested in 131 frontline employees from 35 stores. Employees averaged 981.29 transactions in the focal year (minimum 732, maximum 3,556), average age 33.7 years, average job experience 7.8 years, 73% female.
- Study 1 IVs: social proofing and social comparisons, each a three-item 7-point Likert index of the employee's self-reported inclination to practice the tactic (new scales built by blending Ashforth and Kreiner's countering tactics with semi-structured interviews with salespeople from three industries; social proofing α = 0.70, social comparisons α = 0.70), introduced by the stem "If you feel that your customers harbor negative stereotypes toward you ... what do you do to rebut these stereotypes?"
- Study 1 DV: transaction value per customer transaction, taken from the firm's database as the monetary amount purchased per transaction and logged to normalize its distribution.
- Study 1 controls: employee-level customer orientation (six items from Thomas, Soutar, and Ryan 2001; α = 0.91), perceived competitive intensity (single item from Jaworski and Kohli 1993), job experience, age, and gender; transaction-level number of purchased articles and 11 month dummies with January as the reference month.
- Study 1 estimation: two-level hierarchical linear path model in Mplus 7 with a full-information maximum likelihood estimator and robust standard errors, plus a sandwich estimator to adjust standard errors for the nesting of employees within stores; continuous predictors grand-mean centered. A null model placed 10% of transaction-value variance at the employee level (ICC = 0.10).

Study 2 is a cross-sectional, cross-industry field study of salespeople and their own customers across retail contexts (car retailers plus other industries; data from electronic retailers were excluded), part of which came from a larger data collection by the second author whose non-car-retail portion was reported as Study 1 of Alavi, Habel, Schmitz, Richter, and Wieseke (2018). Research-team members administered questionnaires to salespeople in their branches and approached customers directly after a sales encounter; 45% of approached customers and 100% of salespeople participated, questionnaires were matched by code numbers, and after list-wise deletion of 32 salespeople with missing data on both countering tactics the sample was 535 customers and 161 salespeople.
- Study 2 IVs: the same social proofing and social comparison scales as Study 1, reported by the salesperson (social proofing α = 0.70, social comparisons α = 0.72).
- Study 2 mediator: customers' occupational stereotypes of immorality, measured with stereotype content model items on the moral facet of warmth (Fiske et al., 1999, 2002) worded positively ("Salespeople are ... trustworthy / sincere / good-natured") and then reverse coded (α = 0.90).
- Study 2 DV: actual purchase, a single dichotomous item on whether the customer had actually bought the product they came to the shop for.
- Study 2 controls: relationship length, customer age, customer gender, customer risk aversion, customer category experience, perceived sentiment of the interaction, perceived product quality, perceived customer orientation, and frontline employee age, plus industry fixed effects.
- Study 2 estimation: multilevel path model with a binary outcome (customers at the within level nested in frontline employees at the between level, themselves nested in branches and industries) in Mplus 7, robust ML with Monte Carlo integration, a sandwich estimator for the third-level nesting, and grand-mean centering of continuous predictors. 30% of purchase-behavior variance resided at the salesperson level (ICC = 0.30).

Study 3 is a randomized scenario experiment in a furniture retailing context developed in cooperation with a local furniture store: a 3 (stigma-countering tactic: control, social proofing, social comparisons) × 2 (frontline job: high versus low moral stigma intensity) between-subjects design, with sample size set by an a priori power analysis. 860 U.K.-based participants were recruited through Prolific for 0.40 GBP; 23 were dropped for failing an attention check and 17 for missing values on the dependent variables, leaving 820 participants (70.7% female, mean age 38.46 years). Participants assumed the role of a customer visiting a furniture store to buy a couch and read a written scenario in which the frontline worker consulted them on product options.
- Study 3 manipulations: the tactic manipulation had the worker give product information only (control), mention that past customers were satisfied and continued to patronize the store as documented by positive ratings on customer review websites (social proofing), or state that unlike other furniture stores where salespeople focus only on closing a deal this store focuses on high-quality consultation (social comparisons). Moral stigma intensity was manipulated by calling the person a "salesperson" (high intensity) versus an "employee" (low intensity); a pretest with 59 U.K.-based Prolific respondents confirmed higher concerns about the salesperson's morality than the employee's, and a separate pretest with 151 U.K.-based Prolific participants confirmed that the tactic manipulations worked as intended.
- Study 3 measures: customers' occupational stereotypes of immorality with the same three reverse-coded 7-point items as Study 2 (α = 0.84; AVE = 0.65, composite reliability = 0.85); job performance operationalized as purchase intention with a single 7-point item on the likelihood of buying at this store, from very low to very high; plus a 7-point semantic-differential negative stereotype confirmation item used to scrutinize the mechanism. Treatment factors entered the analysis as group-coded dummies (social proofing dummy, social comparison dummy, and a frontline job dummy with the high-stigma job coded 1).
- Study 3 estimation: analyses of variance with post hoc contrasts in STATA 15.1, and a path model in Mplus 7 with bootstrapped standard errors and bootstrapped confidence intervals for the conditional indirect effects.

On identification, only Study 3 is a randomized experiment. Studies 1 and 2 measure inclinations to use the tactics and relate them to archival or customer-reported outcomes, so their estimates are associational; the paper itself notes that Study 2's multiple-source, cross-industry data would still not allow causal inferences about the role of stigma and countering tactics, and gives this as the reason for running the experiment. Study 1 matches employees' survey responses to their transaction value across the focal year, which the paper says lets it assess the temporal continuity of the effects; it does not state that the transactions postdate the survey.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses were supported, each in the study designed to test it: H1a and H1b in Study 1, H2a and H2b in Study 2, and H3a and H3b in Study 3. No single study tested all six.
- Study 1 (H1a and H1b supported): social comparisons had a negative effect on logged transaction value per customer transaction (β = -0.034, SE = 0.008, p < .01) and social proofing a positive effect (β = 0.027, SE = 0.012, p < .05). The authors report that results remained stable with and without control variables.
- Study 2 (H2a and H2b supported): employees' inclination to practice social comparisons was associated with stronger customer occupational stereotypes of immorality (β = 0.087, p < .05), whereas a stronger inclination to practice social proofing was associated with weaker stereotypes (β = -0.082, p < .05), and stereotypes of immorality were negatively related to customers' purchase behavior (β = -0.249, p < .01). Mediation analysis gave a negative indirect effect of social comparisons on purchase behavior via stereotypes (β = -0.022, 90% CI [-0.043, -0.001]) and a positive indirect effect of social proofing (β = 0.020, 90% CI [0.001, 0.041]).
- Study 3 (H3a and H3b supported): the tactic × stigma-intensity interaction was significant both for customers' purchase intentions (F(2, 814) = 3.82, η² = 0.009, p < .05) and for stereotypes of immorality (F(2, 814) = 13.98, η² = 0.033, p < .01), while the main effect of the tactics was only marginal and the main effect of stigma intensity nonsignificant for purchase intentions. Conditional indirect effects were significant only for the high-stigma-intensity job: -0.075 (95% CI [-0.131, -0.018]) for social comparisons and 0.091 (95% CI [0.028, 0.154]) for social proofing, both reported as full mediation. For the low-stigma-intensity job neither tactic had a significant indirect effect (social comparisons 0.022, 95% CI [-0.032, 0.077], n.s.; social proofing -0.032, 95% CI [-0.090, 0.025], n.s.).
- Study 3 post hoc contrasts, reported with their signs: for the high-stigma job, social comparisons produced significantly higher negative stereotypes of immorality than the control condition (ΔM = 0.32, SE = 0.12, p < .05) and social proofing significantly lower ones (ΔM = -0.38, SE = 0.12, p < .01); social proofing also raised purchase intentions above control (ΔM = 0.31, SE = 0.15, p < .05), whereas the drop in purchase intentions under social comparisons was in the predicted direction but not significant (ΔM = -0.20, SE = 0.15, n.s.). For the low-stigma job, neither tactic significantly affected stereotypes or purchase intentions.
- The moderation is therefore a genuine boundary condition rather than a graded effect in these data: the mediation chains held only where the frontline job was strongly stigmatized, and the authors read the drop to nonsignificance under low stigma intensity as support for their theorized logic that the tactics work through stereotypes of immorality.
- Supplementary results: adding an interaction between social proofing and social comparison claims in Study 2 produced a significant interaction on occupational stereotypes of immorality (β = 0.048, SE = 0.017, p < .01), which the authors describe as social comparisons attenuating the effect of social proofing; the Study 2 results also remained stable when dealer reputation was controlled. In Study 3, adding customers' negative stereotype confirmation as a further mediator corroborated the previous results and indicated a mediating role for stereotype confirmation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends dirty work and stigma management research by shifting attention from inward-facing self-affirmation to outward-facing interactions with occupational outsiders. It shows that tactics previously treated as helpful for stigmatized workers' self-views can have different consequences when used to gain customer approval. The paper also applies the expectancy-confirmation framework to explain how deliberate stigma-countering can unintentionally reinforce the very stereotypes it seeks to reduce.

**Practical Implication**
For managers of customer-facing employees in morally tainted roles, the findings imply that not all stigma-countering tactics should be encouraged. Social proofing appears more useful because managers can equip employees with customer testimonials, satisfaction evidence, reviews, or other credible third-party signals. Supervisors should be cautious about employees' use of downward social comparisons, because these may harm customer perceptions and job performance in strongly stigmatized occupations.

**Limitations**
The paper notes that it focuses on interpersonal implications of stigma-countering in business exchanges, while intrapersonal consequences for the stigma bearer may also be positive or negative. It studies moral occupational stigma and is optimistic about broader relevance to occupational stigmas generally, but additional research is needed to test whether the same mechanism applies beyond moral stigma and beyond sales-like settings. The field studies measure inclinations to engage in stigma-countering rather than specific dyadic instances of each tactic, limiting claims about reciprocal employee-customer dynamics.

**Future Research**
Future research could integrate intrapersonal and interpersonal outcomes of stigma-countering, including emotional exhaustion, self-image preservation, and customer approval. Additional studies could test whether the mechanism generalizes to other stigmatized occupations such as investment banking, bill collection, telemarketing, real estate, insurance, or sex work. The paper also calls for dyadic designs that capture specific episodes of social proofing and social comparisons, research on triggers such as false consensus, and examination of other occupational contexts, status conditions, task complexity, and nonexhaustive stigma-countering tactics.
