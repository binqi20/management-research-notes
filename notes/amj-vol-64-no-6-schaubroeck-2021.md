# Struggling to Meet the Bar: Occupational Progress Failure and Informal Leadership Behavior

**Abstract**
We integrate theory and research about individuals’ responses to failures and develop a model in which occupational progress failures precipitate ruminative processes that limit the extent to which individuals subsequently act as informal leaders. Our first study, an experiment with a sample of advanced accounting students, found that manipulating poor performance on a simulated certification test promoted ruminative thoughts about the test, which were negatively related to peer ratings of informal leadership behavior during a subsequent task. A separate field study using a regression discontinuity design in a 14-week military training program found that failure to pass the required physical fitness examination early in group formation influenced psychosomatic symptoms—an indirect measure of sustained rumination—and consequently hindered enactment of informal leadership behavior. We also theorized and found that neuroticism enhanced the positive effect of failure on rumination in Study 1 and psychosomatic symptoms in Study 2. We discuss the implications for developing theories concerning how disruptive personal events may interfere with employees’ engaging in informal leadership behavior.

**Research Question**
How do occupational progress failures affect individuals' subsequent informal leadership behavior among peers? The paper also asks whether rumination or psychosomatic symptoms explain this relationship and whether neuroticism strengthens the effect of failure on those intrapsychic responses.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Occupational progress failure is positively related to ruminative thinking. (In Study 2 this prediction is tested on psychosomatic symptoms, which the paper treats as an indirect measure of sustained rumination; failure is expected to be positively related to psychosomatic symptoms.)
- Hypothesis 2: Occupational progress failure has a negative indirect effect on informal leadership behavior through ruminative thinking (through psychosomatic symptoms in Study 2).
- Hypothesis 3: The positive relationship between occupational progress failure and ruminative thinking is stronger among persons with higher levels of neuroticism (the failure-psychosomatic symptoms relationship in Study 2).
- Hypothesis 4: The negative indirect effect of occupational progress failure on informal leadership behavior is stronger among persons with higher levels of neuroticism.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Occupational progress failure, operationalized as manipulated poor performance on a simulated CPA diagnostic test in Study 1 and failure to pass a diagnostic Army Physical Fitness Test in Study 2.
- DV(s): Peer-rated informal leadership behavior during a leaderless accounting case discussion in Study 1 and peer-rated informal leadership behavior at the end of military training in Study 2.
- Mediators: Ruminative thinking in Study 1 and psychosomatic symptoms as an indirect measure of sustained rumination in Study 2.
- Moderators: Neuroticism, measured before the failure event in both studies.

The paper argues that an unresolved occupational benchmark failure keeps the failed goal cognitively active, producing self-focused rumination about the failure and its implications. Drawing on the resource allocation model, the authors argue that this off-task cognitive demand leaves fewer surplus attentional resources for complex, discretionary behavior such as informal leadership. The two studies support a negative indirect relationship from failure to informal leadership through rumination-related states, with stronger effects among individuals higher in neuroticism.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data source: a laboratory experiment with 99 accounting students recruited from a private university in the Southeast United States, all within a few months of graduating and taking the CPA exam (46 male, 53 female; 73 master's students, 26 undergraduate seniors; mean age 22.9). Students preregistered for one of three two-hour sessions and were paid $100.
- Study 1 IV, occupational progress failure: manipulated by random assignment to a 30-minute "CPA diagnostic" accounting test that was either very difficult (11 of 15 questions drawn from past Becker CPA preparation tests the publisher rated most difficult, four from other CPA preparation tests) or easy (questions used in the host university's introductory and intermediate accounting classes), followed by accurate performance feedback. Failure was indexed as 1 = difficult exam condition, 0 = easy exam condition. Manipulation checks: mean correct answers 4.49 (SD = 1.75, n = 49) versus 13.6 (SD = 1.33, n = 50), F(1, 97) = 849.17, p < .001; self-rated test performance 1.92 versus 3.96, F(1, 97) = 100.47, p < .001; lower accounting self-efficacy in the difficult condition.
- Study 1 mediator, ruminative thinking: a 5-item self-report measure adapted from McCullough, Bono, and Root (2007) to the test context (e.g., "I couldn't get thoughts of the accounting test out of my mind even though I wanted to"), administered in the post-discussion survey.
- Study 1 DV, informal leadership behavior: four peer-rated items (two general leadership behaviors plus two written for the task) aggregated across teammates after a 40-minute leaderless four-person accounting-fraud case discussion; 85% of participants were rated by three teammates, the rest by two (M r-wg(j) = .74; ICC(1) = .15, ICC(2) = .34).
- Study 1 moderator and controls: neuroticism measured before the manipulation with the IPIP-NEO-120 (Johnson, 2014) as a higher-order composite of the anxiety, depression, and vulnerability facets; extraversion and conscientiousness measured with the mini-IPIP (Donnellan et al., 2006); overall GPA. Individual task performance (correctly identified "red flags" out of 28, M = 10.6, SD = 5.13) was graded by an accounting instructor and a research assistant for a supplementary analysis.
- Study 1 estimation: Mplus structural equation models with a sandwich (cluster) estimator to account for 99 participants nested in 26 discussion groups; indirect effects tested with Monte Carlo confidence intervals based on 20,000 resamplings (Selig & Preacher, 2008). Randomized manipulation supports a causal reading of the failure effect; the rumination-to-leadership link is measured, not manipulated.
- Study 2 data source: a field study of one company-sized U.S. Army infantry unit in a combined 14-week residential program (10-week Basic Combat Training plus four-week Advanced Infantry Training), with trainees held in the same squads throughout. All participants were male (the infantry was male-only at the time), ages 17-39 (M = 21.2, SD = 3.6). Surveys were administered on the second day (Time 1), in Week 7 (Time 2), and shortly before graduation in Week 14 (Time 3). 243 trainees consented at Time 1 and 218 (90%) provided data at all three periods; pairwise analysis n ranges from 182 (leadership) to 217 (failure and exercise scores). Data were collected within a broader project; the paper states that apart from drill-instructor-rated performance and perceived peer respect, which this study uses in supplementary analyses, there is no overlap between its variables and those of the project's two prior studies.
- Study 2 IV, occupational progress failure: failing the diagnostic Army Physical Fitness Test administered in Week 5, coded 1 when a trainee scored below the uniform 50-point passing criterion on any of the three standardized components (push-ups, sit-ups, two-mile run) and 0 otherwise. 46 trainees (21%) in the final analysis sample failed; one of these was dropped as an influential outlier.
- Study 2 mediator, psychosomatic symptoms: an eight-item briefer version of the somatic complaints scale used by Ganster, Fox, and Dwyer (2001), rating how often over the past few weeks trainees experienced symptoms such as an upset stomach, poor sleep, headache, and poor appetite; measured at Time 1 (baseline) and Time 2, two weeks after the diagnostic test.
- Study 2 DV, informal leadership behavior: three items rated at Time 3 on a 7-point scale by squad peers (each trainee rated at least three of five pseudo-randomly assigned squad peers), covering inspiration and motivation, purpose and direction, and encouraging the team to think about values and ethics as a soldier (M r-wg(j) = .76; ICC(1) = .32, ICC(2) = .58).
- Study 2 moderator and controls: neuroticism from four mini-IPIP items at Time 1; baseline (Time 1) psychosomatic symptoms; perceived peer respect at Times 1 and 2 (6-item scale adapted from Tyler & Blader, 2000) as an alternative mediating pathway; drill-sergeant interpersonal justice at Time 2 (Colquitt, 2001, 4 items); extraversion and conscientiousness; and drill-sergeant-rated overall performance at Time 3 (three items adapted from Motowidlo & Van Scotter, 1994).
- Study 2 estimation and identification: the paper claims causal identification through a regression discontinuity design, arguing that because the fitness examination is uniformly scored and a single explicit threshold determines passing, the effect of failure on an outcome after also regressing the outcome on the three component deviation scores represents a treatment effect - an abrupt change in intercept at the passing threshold. Models were estimated in Mplus with the sandwich estimator for trainees nested in squads, full-information maximum likelihood for missing data, and Monte Carlo confidence intervals for indirect effects. Because the mediator and moderator are measured rather than assigned, the mediating and moderating links are associational.

**Key Findings**
All four hypotheses were supported in both studies, with the mediating state differing by study (ruminative thinking in Study 1, psychosomatic symptoms in Study 2).
- Study 1, Hypothesis 1 supported: failure was positively related to ruminative thinking (a = .45, SE = .10, p < .001).
- Study 1, Hypothesis 2 supported: ruminative thinking was negatively associated with informal leadership behavior (b = -.25, SE = .08, p < .01), and the indirect effect of failure on informal leadership behavior through ruminative thinking was negative (ab = -.11, SE = .04, p < .01; Monte Carlo 95% CI -.21 to -.04). Failure had no significant direct effect on informal leadership behavior (.07, SE = .13).
- Study 1, Hypothesis 3 supported: the failure-by-neuroticism interaction predicting ruminative thinking was positive and significant (a = .36, SE = .17, p < .05). The simple slope of failure was significant at higher neuroticism (+1 SD: .68, SE = .18, p < .001) but not at lower neuroticism (-1 SD: .20, SE = .11, p = .06). Region-of-significance analysis put the effect significant for 74.7% of participants.
- Study 1, Hypothesis 4 supported: the conditional indirect effect was significant at higher neuroticism (ab = -.17, SE = .06, p < .01; 95% CI -.30 to -.06) but not at lower neuroticism (ab = -.05, SE = .03, p = .11; 95% CI -.09 to .004).
- Study 1 supplementary: failure did not influence individual task performance on the non-discretionary red-flag task (b = -.43, SE = 1.13, p = .71), neuroticism did not moderate that effect, and the indirect effect through ruminative thinking was not significant (ab = -.58, SE = .40, p = .14). Neither extraversion (a = 0.03, p = .82) nor conscientiousness (a = 0.07, p = .50) moderated the failure-rumination effect.
- Study 2, overall effect: diagnostic test failure was negatively and significantly related to peer-rated informal leadership behavior at the end of training (b = -.70, SE = .28, p < .01).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 1 supported: failure was positively related to psychosomatic symptoms at Time 2 (a = .36, SE = .09, p < .01).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 2 supported: psychosomatic symptoms (Time 2) were negatively related to informal leadership behavior (b = -.34, SE = .13, p < .01), and the indirect effect was negative and significant (ab = -.12, SE = .06, p < .05; Monte Carlo 95% CI -.26 to -.02), with the alternative peer-respect pathway modeled simultaneously.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 3 supported: the failure-by-neuroticism interaction predicting psychosomatic symptoms was positive and significant (a = .35, SE = .12, p < .01). The simple slope was significant at higher neuroticism (+1 SD: .58, SE = .09, p < .001) and absent at lower neuroticism (-1 SD: .10, SE = .14, p = .48); the effect was significant at neuroticism levels of 1.87 or higher, representing 72.6% of participants.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 4 supported: the indirect effect of failure through psychosomatic symptoms was negative at higher neuroticism (ab = -.22, SE = .09, p < .05; 95% CI -.41 to -.07) and absent at lower neuroticism (ab = -.04, SE = .05, p = .40; 95% CI -.16 to .06).
- Study 2 supplementary: extraversion (a = -.26, SE = .12, p < .05) and conscientiousness (a = -.30, SE = .10, p < .01) also moderated the failure-symptoms relationship, with stronger positive relationships at lower levels of each trait. There were no significant quadratic or cubic effects of the component scores, and accounting for the fitness test of record did not change the conclusions. Most trainees who failed the diagnostic test passed the test of record three weeks later, and failure had no effect on psychosomatic symptoms measured at the conclusion of training (b = .11, SE = .18, p = .52), yet the earlier symptom elevation still predicted lower end-of-training informal leadership ratings. Ruminative thinking was not related to task performance in Study 1, and failure did not affect rated core training performance in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to informal leadership research by theorizing occupational progress failure as an event that can inhibit would-be leaders' attempts to claim leadership, beyond stable individual differences and interpersonal factors. It links goal progress theory of rumination with resource allocation theory to explain why a personally important, reversible failure can reduce discretionary leadership behavior without necessarily reducing core task performance. It also shows that neuroticism conditions this process by intensifying rumination-related responses to failure.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that organizations should recognize that temporary failure on important occupational benchmarks can suppress informal leadership behavior even when the individual remains capable of performing core tasks. Managers may reduce these costs by helping employees prepare for benchmark requirements, providing growth-oriented support after failure, and offering renewed leadership opportunities after the failure has been reversed. The paper also cautions that low informal leadership after a failure may not indicate low leadership potential or motivation.

**Limitations**
The authors note that the studies did not directly test the full serial mediation process from occupational progress failure to rumination, psychosomatic symptoms, and informal leadership over time. Study 2 uses psychosomatic symptoms as a proxy for persistent rumination, so the inferred psychological process rests on theory, prior research, and convergence with Study 1 rather than a direct repeated rumination measure. The samples were U.S.-based, limiting conclusions about whether the same failure-leadership process operates similarly in cultures where responses to failure may differ.

**Future Research**
The paper calls for studies that randomly administer goal progress failure and track daily rumination, psychosomatic symptoms, and informal leadership behaviors over time. Future work could examine whether occupational progress failure harms core task performance in settings that require careful monitoring, detail work, or avoidance of errors, and whether ethical or safety behavior is similarly affected. The authors also encourage research on how failure base rates and cultural context shape ruminative responses and informal leadership outcomes.
