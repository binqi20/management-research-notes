# The Impact of Leader Eye Gaze on Disparity in Member Influence: Implications for Process and Performance in Diverse Groups

**Abstract**
One important benefit of teamwork is the exchange and integration of diverse knowledge, experience, and opinions group members bring to the table. However, demographic attributes—such as race, gender, and functional background—may create asymmetric influence patterns between group members in diverse groups because these demographic characteristics are often associated with status differences. In the current research, we examine how to attenuate this disparity in member influence in diverse groups by focusing on the role of a leader’s gazing behavior. Across two studies, we found that asymmetric influence patterns in which high-status members tend to wield greater influence in group decision-making processes were attenuated when a leader increased their visual attention toward low-status members in the group. This reduced disparity in member influence which in turn improved group information elaboration and group performance in a collective decision-making task. Theoretical and practical implications for leaders’ visual attention, diversity, group decision-making processes, and group performance are discussed.

**Research Question**
How can a leader's visual attention attenuate disparity in member influence between high-status and low-status members in diverse groups? The paper also asks whether reduced disparity improves group information elaboration and, in turn, group performance in collective decision-making tasks.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. When a member with a low-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention from a leader than a member with a high-status diverse characteristic in a diverse group, the disparity in member influence is lower than when both members receive equal visual attention or when a member with a high-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention.
Hypothesis 2. When a member with a low-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention from a leader than a member with a high-status diverse characteristic in a diverse group, group information elaboration is higher than when both members receive equal visual attention or when a member with a high-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention.
Hypothesis 3. The positive effect of a leader's greater visual attention toward a member with a low-status diverse characteristic (compared to a leader's equal visual attention or a leader's greater visual attention toward a member with a high-status diverse characteristic) on group information elaboration is mediated by disparity in member influence.
Hypothesis 4. When a member with a low-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention from a leader than a member with a high-status diverse characteristic in a diverse group, group performance is higher than when both members receive equal visual attention or when a member with a high-status diverse characteristic receives greater visual attention.
Hypothesis 5. The positive effect of a leader's greater visual attention toward a member with a low-status diverse characteristic (compared to a leader's equal visual attention or a leader's greater visual attention toward a member with a high-status diverse characteristic) on group performance is mediated in sequence by disparity in member influence and by group information elaboration in a diverse group.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader visual attention condition: greater attention to the low-status member, greater attention to the high-status member, or equal attention to both members.
- DV(s): Group performance in a collective decision-making task, measured through desert survival ranking accuracy in Study 1 and correct candidate choice in a hidden-profile task in Study 2.
- Mediators: Disparity in member influence and group information elaboration, modeled sequentially.
- Moderators: Not directly tested; the paper discusses diversity characteristic type across studies by comparing race and educational background.

The paper argues that demographic characteristics can operate as status cues, producing asymmetric influence patterns in which high-status members exert more influence in group decisions. A leader's greater positive visual attention toward a low-status member signals recognition and support, encouraging the low-status member to speak up while making the high-status member more likely to attend to that member's input. Reduced disparity in member influence then increases the group's motivation and opportunity to process and integrate diverse information, improving performance on tasks that require collective decision-making.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (racially diverse dyads; desert survival task). Two hundred and twenty-six undergraduates (60 male, 166 female) at a Midwestern and an East Coast university participated; three participants who suspected the leader's attention had been contrived, and their groups, were excluded, leaving a final sample of 220 participants across 38 low-status attention groups, 36 equal attention groups, and 36 high-status attention groups. A group was defined as a unit of three persons — two participants (one White, one Black) plus a trained White confederate leader who delivered the task guidelines but did not join the decision — with all groups, including leaders, matched on gender.
- IV, leader visual attention: three randomly assigned conditions (low-status attention, high-status attention, equal attention). In the unequal conditions the confederate leader looked at the member assigned to receive more attention for roughly six seconds before shifting to the other member for roughly two seconds, repeated over the roughly three minutes needed to explain the task; in the equal condition the leader alternated gaze between the two members approximately every four seconds. Condition was coded low-status attention = 0, equal attention = 1, high-status attention = 2, so the low-status attention condition served as the PROCESS baseline.
- Mediator 1, disparity in member influence: observer-coded from videotapes of the group discussion by two independent coders blind to the hypotheses, using the five-point scheme in Table 1 built on talking time, making decisions and suggestions, and leading or controlling discussions (ICC1 = .68, ICC2 = .81, p < .001; mean rwg = .88), then averaged.
- Mediator 2, group information elaboration: a second pair of independent coders blind to the hypotheses rated two items capturing processing and integration of information and perspectives on scales from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) (ICC1 = .71, ICC2 = .83, p < .001; mean rwg = .84), then averaged. The exchange-of-information component was deliberately excluded so the measure would stay distinct from disparity in member influence.
- DV, group performance: the group's joint ranking of the 15 desert survival items (Lafferty & Pond, 1974) compared with expert rankings, with each discrepancy counted as an error and the error sum subtracted from a constant following Littlepage et al.'s (1995) formula.
- Control: individuals' actual competence, computed the same way from each participant's individual ranking and aggregated to the group level.
- Analysis: ANOVA with post hoc comparisons, plus the PROCESS macro Model 4 for Hypothesis 3 and Model 6 for Hypothesis 5. A robustness check reanalyzed disparity using the absolute value of the difference between the two members' self-rated influence (three items).

Study 2 (educationally diverse dyads; hidden-profile task). The participants were 200 Asian undergraduates (66 male, 134 female) from a business school at a university in China, half from an advanced program and half from a regular program, where entry to the advanced program requires much higher entrance exam scores. Two participants who suspected the manipulation, and their groups, were excluded, leaving a final sample of 196 participants across 32 low-status attention groups, 34 equal attention groups, and 32 high-status attention groups (n = 98 groups); because seven participants declined to be videotaped and two discussions failed to record, the sample for disparity in member influence and group information elaboration comprised 89 groups. The confederate leaders were one Asian male and one Asian female, and groups were gender-matched as in Study 1.
- IV, leader visual attention: the same three conditions and the same gaze-duration manipulation as Study 1, with program affiliation made salient by introducing members by program name and by name placards on the table; coded low-status attention = 0, equal attention = 1, high-status attention = 2.
- Mediator 1, disparity in member influence: the same Table 1 coding scheme, rated by two independent coders (ICC1 = .65, ICC2 = .79, p < .001; mean rwg = .79), then averaged.
- Mediator 2, group information elaboration: a second pair of coders blind to the hypotheses and conditions scored each of the 18 unique information items on a five-point scale from 1 (not attentive) to 5 (very attentive), using a scheme modified from Homan et al. (2007) and van Ginkel and van Knippenberg (2008); item scores were summed into total elaboration (average ICC = .88).
- DV, group performance: a dichotomous variable for whether the group chose the correct candidate in a modified "PB technology" hidden-profile task (Peterson, 2001), in which each member's packet favors a different candidate but pooling all information identifies Candidate C as the best choice (1 = correct, 0 = not correct).
- Analysis: ANOVA with post hoc comparisons for the two mediators, chi-square tests for the dichotomous performance measure, and PROCESS macro version 3.3 Model 6 for the sequential mediation, again with the low-status attention condition as baseline; the self-rated-influence robustness check was repeated (alpha = .73).

Both studies are randomized laboratory experiments, which the authors say enabled them to examine causal mechanisms, observe and code group-level behavior, and systematically control a leader's visual attention. The gaze manipulation is experimentally assigned, but the two mediators are measured rather than manipulated, so the sequential mediation rests on model-based bootstrapped indirect effects rather than on separate experimental identification of each link.

**Key Findings**
Across both studies, a leader's greater visual attention toward the low-status member lowered disparity in member influence and, through it, raised group information elaboration and group performance. Support is not uniform at the pairwise level: the comparison against the high-status attention condition fell short of significance for information elaboration in Study 1 and for group performance in Study 2.

Study 1 (racially diverse dyads).
- H1 supported. Disparity in member influence differed across the three conditions (F[2, 107] = 7.74, p = .001, η² = .13) and was lower in the low-status attention groups (M = 2.28, SD = .79) than in the equal attention groups (M = 2.94, SD = .73, p = .001) and the high-status attention groups (M = 2.71, SD = .69, p = .04).
- H2 supported against equal attention only. Group information elaboration differed across conditions (F[2, 107] = 6.22, p < .01, η² = .10) and was higher in the low-status attention groups (M = 4.24, SD = 1.29) than in the equal attention groups (M = 3.36, SD = .94, p < .01); the comparison with the high-status attention groups (M = 3.68, SD = .97) was p = .07, which the paper reports as not statistically significant.
- H3 supported. The relative indirect effect of leader visual attention on group information elaboration through disparity in member influence was significant against the equal attention groups (b = -.39, SE = .14, 95% bias-corrected CI [-.70, -.16]) and against the high-status attention groups (b = -.25, SE = .12, 95% bias-corrected CI [-.51, -.05]).
- H4 supported. Group performance differed across conditions (F[2, 106] = 6.54, p < .01, η² = .11) controlling for individuals' actual competence, and was better in the low-status attention groups (M = 53.50, SD = 13.94) than in the equal attention groups (M = 44.17, SD = 11.44, F[1, 71] = 11.65, p = .001, η² = .14) and the high-status attention groups (M = 48.17, SD = 10.91, F[1, 71] = 4.60, p = .04, η² = .06).
- H5 supported. The sequential relative indirect effect on group performance, through disparity in member influence and then group information elaboration, was significant against the equal attention groups (b = -1.41, SE = .64, 95% bias-corrected CI [-2.93, -.39]) and against the high-status attention groups (b = -.91, SE = .51, 95% bias-corrected CI [-2.08, -.12]).

Study 2 (educationally diverse dyads).
- H1 supported. Disparity in member influence differed across conditions (F[2, 86] = 7.64, p = .001, η² = .15) and was lower in the low-status attention groups (M = 2.08, SD = .93) than in the equal attention groups (M = 2.97, SD = .73, p = .001) and the high-status attention groups (M = 2.75, SD = 1.08, p = .02).
- H2 supported. Group information elaboration differed across conditions (F[2, 86] = 4.69, p = .01, η² = .10) and was higher in the low-status attention groups (M = 23.65, SD = 9.66) than in the equal attention groups (M = 17.13, SD = 8.23, p = .02) and the high-status attention groups (M = 18.16, SD = 8.76, p = .05).
- H3 supported. Relative indirect effects on group information elaboration through disparity in member influence were significant against the equal attention groups (b = -3.18, SE = 1.24, 95% bias-corrected CI [-5.91, -1.11]) and the high-status attention groups (b = -2.40, SE = 1.21, 95% bias-corrected CI [-5.15, -.42]).
- H4 supported against equal attention only. Group performance differed across conditions (χ²[2, n = 98] = 6.41, p = .04); a higher percentage of low-status attention groups chose the right candidate (44.7%) than equal attention groups (25.5%) (χ²[1, n = 66] = 6.07, p = .01), whereas the comparison with the high-status attention groups (29.8%) was χ²[1, n = 64] = 3.09, p = .08, which the paper reports as not significant.
- H5 supported. Sequential relative indirect effects on the dichotomous performance outcome were significant against the equal attention groups (b = -.31, SE = .18, 95% bias-corrected CI [-.77, -.08]) and the high-status attention groups (b = -.23, SE = .16, 95% bias-corrected CI [-.64, -.03]).

Sign convention. All reported indirect effects carry negative coefficients because the low-status attention condition was coded as the lowest number (0) and therefore became the PROCESS baseline; the paper states that its theoretical model represents the positive effect of the low-status attention condition on group information elaboration (Hypothesis 3) and group performance (Hypothesis 5).

Additional results. In both studies there was no significant difference between the high-status attention and equal attention conditions on disparity in member influence, group information elaboration, or group performance. Supplementary analyses of self-rated influence indicate that disparity falls primarily through the low-status member's increased contribution: low-status members' influence was higher in the low-status attention condition than in the equal attention condition (Study 1: M = 4.97 vs. M = 4.46, F[1, 72] = 5.92, p = .02, η² = .08; Study 2: M = 4.95 vs. M = 4.37, F[1, 64] = 6.31, p = .02, η² = .09), while high-status members' influence was largely unchanged. Robustness checks using the absolute self-rated difference score reproduced the observer-rated pattern in both studies, although in each the low-status versus high-status attention comparison on disparity was not statistically significant (Study 1 p = .09; Study 2 p = .08).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to diversity research by directly examining disparity in member influence as a process through which demographic diversity can undermine group information elaboration and performance. It identifies leader visual attention as a subtle nonverbal intervention that can reduce asymmetric influence patterns without relying on more burdensome procedural systems. It also extends work on gazing behavior into organizational research and develops an observer-rated measure of disparity in member influence.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that leaders in diverse groups should attend to how they distribute visual attention during initial group interactions, especially when some members are likely to carry lower status. Providing more positive visual attention to low-status members can create a more equalized communication atmosphere and improve information elaboration in collective decision-making tasks. The authors present gaze regulation as a trainable behavior that can be improved through interventions such as video feedback or sensitivity training.

**Limitations**
The studies used newly formed three-person groups consisting of two members and one leader, so the findings may not generalize directly to larger, ongoing, or more complex teams. The studies assume shared perceptions of status-relevant diversity characteristics, but leaders and members may not always agree about which characteristics create lower or higher status. The tasks were short-duration collective decision-making tasks, leaving open whether the effects persist, diminish, or strengthen over time and whether they apply to more structured, repetitive, or coordination-oriented work.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine group size as a moderator and test whether leader visual attention produces different dynamics in larger groups or multicultural and geographically distributed teams. Additional studies could investigate how a leader's own demographic characteristics shape the effect of visual attention on group dynamics. The authors also call for within-team designs and individual-level outcomes to understand how leader visual attention changes individual perceptions and influence over time.
