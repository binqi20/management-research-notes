# When Victims Help Their Abusive Supervisors: The Role of LMX, Self-Blame, and Guilt

**Abstract**
Studies on abusive supervision typically posit that targets of abuse will either directly blame the perpetrating supervisor or indirectly blame the organization for allowing the abuse, and as a result reduce their cooperativeness at work. We pivot from this predominant logic and argue that, under certain circumstances, targets of abusive supervision may blame themselves, feel guilty, and then try to make it up to their abusive supervisors by helping them more. Drawing on the emotional process theory of abusive supervision and the more general sociofunctional perspective of emotions, we specify that such a dynamic is more likely to ensue when subordinates otherwise experience the relationship with their supervisors as good (high LMX). Two studies—an experiment and a twoweek bi-daily experience sampling study—provide support for our reasoning. As such, our study extends theorizing on the consequences of abusive supervision, which has typically found that it reduces cooperative behaviors. Moreover, it contributes to previous speculations that leaders may engage in abusive supervision because it has beneficial consequences for them.

**Research Question**
When and why might targets of abusive supervision blame themselves, feel guilty, and respond by helping the abusive supervisor rather than reducing cooperation? The paper asks whether high leader-member exchange (LMX) makes this self-blame and guilt pathway more likely.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The positive effect of abusive supervision on self-blame is stronger when LMX is high (vs. low).
Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between abusive supervision and guilt is mediated by self-blame. This indirect relationship is stronger when LMX is high (vs. low).
Hypothesis 3. The positive relationship between abusive supervision and supervisor-directed helping is serially mediated by self-blame (first mediator) and guilt (second mediator). This indirect relationship is stronger when LMX is high (vs. low).
All three hypotheses were tested in both Study 1 and Study 2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Abusive supervision, manipulated as low versus high in Study 1 and measured as daily abusive supervision in Study 2.
- DV(s): Supervisor-directed helping, assessed as intended helping toward the supervisor in Study 1 and within-day supervisor-directed helping in Study 2.
- Mediators: Self-blame as the first mediator and guilt as the second mediator.
- Moderators: LMX as the boundary condition strengthening the abusive-supervision-to-self-blame path.

The paper argues that abusive supervision can be interpreted differently when it occurs inside an otherwise high-quality supervisor-subordinate relationship. In high-LMX dyads, subordinates may treat abusive episodes as inconsistent with the broader relationship, look inward for the cause, and blame themselves for possibly threatening the relationship. That self-blame produces guilt, and guilt motivates relationship-repair behavior, expressed here as increased supervisor-directed helping. Study 1 supports the model in a vignette experiment, and Study 2 finds the same moderated serial mediation pattern in daily work data while controlling for self-perceived performance.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1: 200 high-reputation US MTurk participants were randomly assigned to a 2 (abusive supervision: low vs. high) x 2 (LMX: high vs. low) between-subjects vignette design, with both factors manipulated through scenario text about a supervisor named Chris; the LMX wording was derived from the LMX scale by Scandura and Graen (1984) and the abusive supervision wording from the behaviors in Tepper's (2000) scale. Manipulation checks in a separate MTurk sample (n = 51) showed main effects for both manipulations, F(1, 49) = 27.62 and F(1, 49) = 25.91, both p < .001, and no interaction. Self-blame used three items the authors developed for this study ("I think that I did something that jeopardized my relationship with my supervisor"), 7-point Likert (Cronbach's alpha = .92); its content validity was checked on 100 further MTurk respondents and its convergent/discriminant validity through a CFA on a new sample of 303 MTurkers. Guilt used the five guilt items of the state shame and guilt scale (Marschall et al., 1994) on a 5-point likelihood scale (alpha = .94), and supervisor-directed helping used the six items of Dalal et al. (2009) asking how likely participants would be to help Chris, 5-point (alpha = .91). All hypothesized paths were estimated simultaneously with structural equation modeling, using 10,000 bootstrap samples for the indirect effects and the moderated-mediation indices.

Study 2: a two-week bi-daily experience-sampling field study. 300 MTurk workers were recruited for an intake survey; 25 completed no daily assignment, leaving 275 respondents (92% retention), and because both daily surveys had to be completed on the same day the usable sample was 2,215 daily observations (respondents completed both surveys on 8.1 of the 10 working days on average). LMX was measured once, in the intake survey a week earlier, with the seven items of Scandura and Graen (1984) on 5-point scales (alpha = .92) and grand-mean centered; all daily variables were person-mean centered. Measured mid-workday (T): abusive supervision with Tepper's (2000) 15-item scale rated for that day on a 6-point frequency scale from 1 (never) to 6 (five or more times) ("My boss ridicules me"; within-person omega = .76) and self-blame with the Study 1 three-item measure (omega = .87). Guilt and supervisor-directed helping were measured both mid-workday (T) and after work (T+1) — guilt with the same state shame and guilt items on a 6-point intensity scale (omega = .91 and .89) and helping with the six Dalal et al. (2009) items on the 6-point frequency scale (omega = .86 and .88). Self-perceived performance (four items, Pearce & Porter, 1986) was included as a covariate in every hypothesis test, and supervisor-directed blame (three self-developed items), anger (Diener et al., 1995), and supervisor-directed deviance (10 items, Mitchell & Ambrose, 2007) were measured for supplementary analyses. Hypotheses were tested with random-intercept, random-slope multilevel regression in Stata 15.0, modeling residuals as moving averages; multilevel SEM failed to converge, so indirect effects were assessed with Monte Carlo simulations of 50,000 samples.

Identification: Study 1 is a randomized scenario experiment, which the authors say lets them draw causal inferences with a high degree of internal validity while not showing that people would actually respond this way in their real jobs. Study 2 is not an experiment; it is a within-person field design that time-separates predictors from outcomes inside the working day (T predictors, T+1 outcomes, controlling for the T level of the outcome) and, through person-mean centering, rules out stable between-person differences, but its daily associations are not experimentally identified.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (N = 200) supported all three hypotheses. H1: the abusive supervision x LMX interaction on self-blame was significant (b = 1.07, p < .01); when LMX was high, self-blame was higher under high than under low abuse (M = 3.44 vs. M = 2.62; z = 3.60, p < .001), whereas the corresponding difference under low LMX was not significant (z = -0.97, ns). H2 was fully supported: the index of moderated mediation was significant (coefficient = .83, 95% CI [0.31, 1.39]), and the indirect effect of abusive supervision on guilt via self-blame was positive at high LMX (indirect effect = .66, 95% CI [0.27, 1.04]) but not at low LMX (indirect effect = -.19); self-blame predicted guilt (b = 0.78, p < .001). H3 was also fully supported: the index was significant (coefficient = .16, 95% CI [0.06, 0.38]), and the serial indirect effect on supervisor-directed helping was positive at high LMX (indirect effect = .12, 95% CI [0.03, 0.28]) but not at low LMX (indirect effect = -.03, 95% CI [-0.15, 0.02]); guilt predicted helping (b = 0.19, p < .01).

Study 2 (2,215 daily observations from 275 individuals), controlling for self-perceived performance, also reported all three hypotheses as fully supported, but with a different conditional pattern from Study 1: the conditional effects were positive and significant at both levels of LMX, merely weaker at low LMX, rather than significant only at high LMX. H1: the daily abusive supervision x LMX interaction on self-blame was significant (coefficient = .46, p < .05), with a positive slope at high LMX (coefficient = 1.11, p < .001) and a smaller but still significant slope at low LMX (coefficient = .42, p < .05), the two slopes differing significantly (z = 2.18, p < .05). H2: the index of moderated mediation was significant (coefficient = .02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.05]); the indirect effect on guilt (T+1) via self-blame was positive and significant at high LMX (indirect effect = .06, 95% CI [0.02, 0.11]) and less positive at low LMX (indirect effect = .02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.05]); self-blame (T) predicted guilt (T+1) (coefficient = .05, p < .001). H3: the index was significant (coefficient = .0020, 95% CI [0.0001, 0.0052]); the serial indirect effect on supervisor-directed helping (T+1) was positive and significant at high LMX (indirect effect = .0049, 95% CI [0.0001, 0.0147]) and less positive at low LMX (indirect effect = .0019, 95% CI [0.0001, 0.0049]); guilt (T+1) predicted helping (T+1) (coefficient = .08, p < .01).

A supplementary Study 2 analysis reproduced the literature's conventional retaliation path alongside the new one: abusive supervision (T) raised supervisor-directed blame (T) (coefficient = .58, p < .001), which in turn raised supervisor-directed deviance (T+1) (coefficient = .01, p < .05) and mediated the abusive-supervision-to-deviance link (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.09]); anger (T) also mediated that link (indirect effect = .01, 95% CI [0.00, 0.02]), while supervisor-directed blame (T) did not affect anger (T+1).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the emotional process theory of abusive supervision by adding a self-blame and guilt pathway that can produce relationship-promotive behavior, not only retaliation, withdrawal, fear, anger, or sadness. It also qualifies the dominant victim-blame-supervisor narrative in abusive supervision research by showing that self-blame and supervisor-directed blame can coexist. By identifying supervisor-directed helping as a possible outcome, the paper contributes to debates about why abusive supervision may persist despite its harmful and morally unjustified character.

**Practical Implication**
The findings imply that increased helping after abuse should not be read as evidence that abusive supervision is effective or harmless. In high-LMX relationships, a subordinate's extra helping may reflect guilt-driven relationship repair rather than healthy cooperation, and it may hide a potentially dysfunctional pattern in which the target blames themselves for mistreatment. The paper explicitly states that it does not endorse abusive supervision even if some short-term supervisor-benefiting outcomes appear.

**Limitations**
Study 1 used a hypothetical scenario, so it could establish a possible causal process with internal validity but could not show that employees would actually respond this way in their real jobs. Study 2 covered only two weeks, which the authors state is too short for testing long-term consequences of helping an abusive supervisor. The authors also note that they do not know how supervisors perceive subordinates' helping efforts, because supervisors and employees may disagree about whether the behavior is extra-role helping or part of formal job requirements.

**Future Research**
The authors call for work on when self-blame versus supervisor-directed blame dominates over time, including dispositional factors such as locus of control and attribution styles and situational factors beyond LMX. They also suggest research on social comparisons in abusive supervision, shame as another possible route to helping, and the long-term consequences of helping an abusive supervisor. Future studies should examine whether supervisors intentionally use abusive behavior because they expect it to elicit help or other productive responses from subordinates.
