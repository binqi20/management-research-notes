# From Grace to Violence: Stigmatizing the Medical Profession in China

**Abstract**
This study examines how the once prestigious medical profession in China has become the subject of criticism and accused of pervasive impropriety to the point of inducing widespread violence from patients. Drawing on archival documents, media articles, interviews, and secondary sources, we trace the lengthy and uneven trajectory by which the profession moved away from enjoying widespread collective approval toward ambivalence and eventual stigmatization. We develop a process model of professional stigmatization that identifies the dynamics that precipitate and then catalyze that trajectory and highlight the underpinning mechanisms. By unpacking the complex process of stigmatization, the study extends existing understanding of how aspects of stigma emerge and are attached by different stakeholders to a profession and the consequences that follow. We conclude by highlighting the implications for research on stigma and the challenge of stigma containment.

**Research Question**
The paper asks: What is the process by which stigma emerges and attaches to a profession? It examines this question through the case of the Chinese medical profession's movement from collective approval, through ambivalence, toward partial stigmatization and patient violence.

**Mechanism Process**
- Core phenomenon: professional stigmatization, defined as stakeholders perceiving the profession as a category with a deep-seated flaw.
- Precipitating dynamics: pervasive professional transgressions combined with discernible damage to primary stakeholders, especially patients facing rising medical costs.
- Amplifying dynamics: a spiral of voice in which media coverage and patient reactions made dissatisfaction more visible, legitimate, and imitable.
- Catalyzing dynamics: authoritative government judgment shifted blame from individual misconduct toward the profession as a whole, encouraging collective labeling by media and patients.
- Divergent punishments: secondary stakeholders emphasized shaming, regulation, and restraint, while primary stakeholders sometimes prescribed harsher punishments, including violence.
- Underpinning mechanisms: impotent dependence of patients on medical expertise and moral resonance of health care with socialist public-welfare values.
- Countermoves: government, media, professional associations, individual physicians, SARS, and COVID-19 could temporarily contain or reverse stigma, but the process remained unsettled.

The process model explains professional stigmatization as nonlinear and oscillating rather than as a simple shift from legitimacy to stigma. Ethical transgressions alone did not trigger stigmatization; they became consequential when patients experienced damage and when secondary stakeholders amplified dissatisfaction. Government denunciation then catalyzed collective labeling, while patients' dependence on physicians and expectations that medicine should embody core societal values made some punishments unusually harsh.

**Data & Measures**
Four data streams were assembled to build a chronological, cross-level narrative of the 1985-2015 period. Archival documents comprised national-level government documents outlining regulatory interventions in the health care system, provincial-level reports describing how those changes were implemented and prioritized on the ground, internal documents from two hospitals in a major coastal city covering changes to physician incentive systems and drug pricing (whose representativeness was checked by discussing them with elite interviewees), and documents from the official websites and regional branches of the Chinese Medical Doctor Association (established 2002) and the Chinese Hospital Association (established 2006). Media articles came principally from the People's Daily: using combinations of Chinese key phrases and synonyms for "professional ethics (zhiye daode)" with "physicians (yisheng)" or "hospitals (yiyuan)" and "medical professional ethics (yi de/yi feng)", the authors collected 2,104 articles published between 1985 and 2015 and, after removing those that merely mentioned rather than actually commented upon professional ethics, compiled a dataset of 1,390 articles. A separate People's Daily dataset on patient dissatisfaction covering 1985 to 2015 was built from a phrase list derived through consultations with interviewees and two professors who study doctor-patient relationships ("medical dispute" (yiliao jiufen), "doctor-patient dispute" (yihuan jiufen), "doctor-patient conflict" (yihuan chongtu), and "medical disruption" (yi nao)), which identified 756 articles. Corroborating searches covered the Guangming Daily and the Economic Daily (available only from 2000 onward), two large governmental news websites (Xinhua Net and China News Service), three large private news websites (Tecent, NetEase, and Sina), and the Financial Times (Chinese).

Interviews were semi-structured and conducted in three waves, each with its own population: 10 pilot interviews with physicians about their experience and understanding of patient relationships; 28 medical professionals about the profession's perspective; and 30 patients about their experiences with medical professionals. Informants were identified by snowball sampling. Physician interviewees spanned professional ranks from chief to resident physician, leadership positions, and provincial, municipal, and district hospital affiliations; 19 had entered the profession before 1985 and so could compare conditions before and after the reforms, and five held multiple senior positions and were treated as "elite interviewees" (all five had been hospital presidents, four had also served as government health department officials, and three had presided over regional branches of professional associations). Approximately half the patients were born before the 1970s. All interviews were conducted onsite in two major municipalities in two province-level regions, in Mandarin Chinese, lasting 40 to 90 minutes apart from four elite interviews of two hours each; they were recorded, transcribed, and translated, and the first author and a research assistant jointly conducted 16 of them to minimize interviewer bias. Emergent insights were separately presented to physicians who had practiced in 14 other province-level regions and to patients who had worked or studied in 16 province-level regions, to gauge scope. Secondary materials comprised statistics from the National Bureau of Statistics' Health Yearbooks and surveys by the Ministry of Health, the Chinese Hospital Association, and the Chinese Medical Doctor Association covering non-reimbursable personal health expenses, the frequency of verbal abuse and physical assault in hospitals, and the percentage of physicians wanting to quit (the 2012 Chinese Hospital Association survey, for example, drew responses from 8,388 physicians in 316 hospitals across 30 provinces), together with editorials in medical journals such as The Lancet.

Constructs were coded interpretively rather than measured psychometrically. Media portrayal of professional ethics was operationalized by having the first author read and code each article as "negative" (a disapproving view of professional ethics), "positive" (an approving view), or "neutral" (ethics-related facts without immediate connotations, or an impartial view), following Piazza and Perretti (2015); two of the authors and a field expert formalized the scheme by independently coding a subsample of 50 articles and reconciling divergences, and a Chinese physician reclassified a random subsample of 100 articles, yielding a Cohen's k of 0.83. Articles were further differentiated by whether blame was attributed to individual members of the profession or to the profession as a whole, which is the operational marker of collective labeling. Patient evaluations were captured first through media articles reporting doctor-patient disputes and then through the patient interviews: the 756 dispute articles were separated into those involving violence and those without, with violence defined, following the World Health Organization's (2002b) definition of workplace violence in health services, as incidents in which medical professionals are abused, threatened, or assaulted, covering both physical violence (beating, kicking, slapping, stabbing, shooting) and psychological violence (verbal abuse, bullying, mobbing, harassment, threats); applying these criteria yielded 220 articles reporting violent disputes, of which 163 involved physical violence. Core concepts, their data sources, and illustrative data are mapped in the paper's Table 2. Trustworthiness rested on cross-source triangulation, member checks presenting preliminary findings to elite interviewees, and a variant of the insider-outsider approach in which the first author had intimate knowledge of the setting through family members in the Chinese medical profession, the third author held a distant "devil's advocate" position, and the second author acted as a sounding board. The design is inductive, exploratory, and interpretive; counts, percentages, and survey statistics are reported descriptively, and the paper makes process rather than causal-identification claims.

**Key Findings**
The profession moved away from widespread collective approval, through ambivalence, toward partial stigmatization along a lengthy, uneven, and nonlinear trajectory, which the analysis reports in three phases. In the first phase (1985-1997), ethically questionable practices spread, notably overprescribing encouraged by a 15% drug markup, special-demand services outside the insurance scheme, and profit-linked incentive systems, yet approval held. Of the 469 People's Daily articles referring to professional ethics between 1985 and 1997, only 64 (14%) were critical while 291 (62%) were highly positive, and 45 of the 64 negative articles criticized specific physicians or hospitals rather than the profession. Only 14 People's Daily articles reported doctor-patient disputes between 1985 and 1998, and a government survey of 100 major hospitals recorded a yearly average of 2.3 disputes per hospital. The finding the authors draw is that pervasive transgressions alone did not precipitate stigmatization.

In the second phase (1998-2004), the new health insurance scheme was the tipping point because it inflicted discernible damage on patients, who could no longer claim full reimbursement for regular medical expenses; in the Shenzhen pilot patients became responsible for roughly 30% of their clinical bills, and from 1998 to 2003 non-reimbursable medical expenses rose about 13% annually, making health care the third largest personal expense after food and education. Disputes then increased noticeably: the People's Daily published 101 dispute articles in this phase against 14 in the previous one, and the Guangming Daily and Economic Daily published 106 articles between 2000 and 2004 describing patients confronting physicians. Media evaluations turned ambivalent rather than hostile, with positive articles falling from 62% to 43% and negative articles rising to one-third (120) of 359 articles between 1998 and 2004, and 74 of those 120 negative articles criticizing the moral behavior of the profession rather than of individual professionals. A "spiral of voice" amplified the shift: patients learned from media reporting how others were expressing dissatisfaction and inferred that confrontation and abuse were acceptable, the reverse of a spiral of silence. The 2002-2003 SARS epidemic gave the profession only a short-lived respite before media criticism resumed, and physicians responded with "strained tolerance" rather than collective pushback, partly because the Chinese Medical Doctor Association was established only in 2002 and lacked the capacity to police the profession or challenge the media.

In the third phase (2005-2015), government surveys showing that 57% of urban residents avoided hospital because of cost preceded public denunciation of the profession's transgressions and attribution of blame to the profession as a whole, an "authoritative judgment" that catalyzed collective labeling. Media and patients adopted the government's stance and at times its language: 291 (52%) of 562 People's Daily articles between 2005 and 2015 took a critical view of professional ethics, with 229 explicitly blaming the profession rather than individual professionals, whereas most of the 114 (20%) positive articles praised individual professionals. The same shift held within specific transgressions: of the 21 articles critical of overprescribing in the first phase, only 4 (20%) blamed the profession, against 157 (82%) of 192 articles in the third phase. Stigmatization was partial, targeting the ethical dimension of professional behavior rather than expertise or competence; over the same period the percentage of patients whose health issues were effectively treated rose from 95.5% to 96.8%, and the percentage of physicians obtaining postgraduate degrees grew from 4.3% in 2005 to 11.4% in 2013.

Primary and secondary stakeholders converged in collective labeling but diverged in the punishments they prescribed. Between 2005 and 2015 reports of doctor-patient disputes increased six-fold and incidents of violence rose sharply: by 2012 nearly two-thirds of hospitals had reported physical violence, 60% of physicians had suffered verbal abuse and threats, and 13% had experienced physical abuse; nearly 11% of physically violent incidents involved physicians with whom the patient had had no prior interaction, and in one online survey 65% of 6,161 respondents chose "happy" rather than "sympathetic", "sad", or "angry" about the murder of a physician by a patient he had never treated. Secondary stakeholders, and the government in particular, instead emphasized shaming the profession, regulatory supervision, penalties including loss of licence, and public appeals to respect physicians, with the Supreme People's Court declaring that crimes against medical professionals would be severely punished. Two mechanisms account for the harsher primary-stakeholder response: "impotent dependence", the significantly imbalanced professional-client relationship in which patients rely on expertise they cannot appraise or readily substitute, and "moral resonance", the closeness of the profession to core societal values, so that failure to live up to health care's socialist public-welfare meaning was highly consequential and unintentionally fueled anger. Countermoves by the government, the media, professional associations, and individual physicians, together with the SARS and COVID-19 outbreaks, produced only temporary respite: the epilogue reports that despite harsher penalties, including death sentences for perpetrators, the Chinese Medical Doctor Association found in 2018 that 66% of physicians had experienced verbal abuse or physical violence, down only 7% from 2015. The authors conclude that the stigmatization process remains partial, unsettled, and oscillating rather than stabilized.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes an empirically grounded process model of professional stigmatization that specifies precipitating, amplifying, and catalyzing dynamics. It extends stigma theory by showing that stigma can be partial, focused on a profession's ethical dimension rather than its expertise, and that different stakeholder groups may converge in collective labeling while diverging in preferred punishments. It also theorizes why professional stigmatization may be especially severe when primary stakeholders experience both impotent dependence and moral resonance.

**Practical Implication**
The study shows that authoritative stakeholders can unintentionally accelerate professional stigmatization: in this case the government "unintendedly shifted the debate from an emphasis upon the transgressions of individual professionals to placing responsibility upon the profession as a whole," broadening the target of stigma where prior models had depicted a narrowing. The paper treats containment as an open question rather than a settled prescription, noting that the respite from stigmatization observed after the SARS epidemic proved short-lived.

**Limitations**
The authors identify the Chinese medical profession as an extreme case, so the model needs confirmation and development in other professional and national contexts. The study focuses on one profession whose ethical transgressions had unusually high moral resonance in a socialist public-welfare setting, which may limit transferability to professions with different stakeholder dependencies and societal meanings. The epilogue also shows that the stigmatization process remained unsettled rather than fully stabilized.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how professional stigmatization can be contained or reversed, including whether stronger professional associations or alternative government responses can prevent collective labeling from escalating. They also propose examining whether timing matters, such as whether interventions during health crises can produce durable restoration of respect. Further research should compare professions and countries to assess when moral resonance leads to aggressive punishment and whether similar mechanisms appear in law, religion, politics, accounting, or other professions.
