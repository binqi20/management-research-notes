# Longing for the Road Not Taken: The Affective and Behavioral Consequences of Forgone Identity Dwelling

**Abstract**
The literature on identity construction has argued that employees work to create coherent and distinctive identities that encapsulate individual characteristics and professional and organizational memberships. Models of identity construction have suggested that a past career decision—choosing one professional “road” while leaving behind a “road not taken”—should lose its salience over time. We challenged that consensus by introducing the construct of forgone identity dwelling—thinking about and reflecting on a professional path that could have been. Our field and experimental studies showed that employees did dwell on forgone professional identities, even when the decision events that resulted in them were years in the past. Drawing on cognitive-motivational-relational theory, we showed that forgone identity dwelling resulted in longing—a yearning for something missing. Such longing created a “double-edged sword” for the behavioral consequences of forgone identity dwelling. On the one hand, longing was associated with increased withdrawal behavior and decreased helping behavior because employees were distracted from the here and now. On the other hand, longing was associated with job crafting that in turn reduced those reactions—particularly for employees with an internal locus of control. We discuss the implications of our work for models of identity construction.

**Research Question**
Do employees continue to dwell on forgone professional identities—the occupational "roads not taken"—long after the identity construction process should have rendered them moot, and if so, what are the affective (longing) and behavioral (withdrawal, helping, job crafting) consequences of such dwelling?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Forgone identity dwelling has a positive indirect effect on withdrawal behavior through longing.
- H2: Forgone identity dwelling has a negative indirect effect on helping behavior through longing.
- H3: Forgone identity dwelling has a positive indirect effect on job crafting through longing.
- H4: Forgone identity dwelling has a negative serial indirect effect on withdrawal behavior through longing and job crafting.
- H5: Forgone identity dwelling has a positive serial indirect effect on helping behavior through longing and job crafting.
- H6: The positive relationship between longing and job crafting is moderated by the locus of control, such that the relationship is more positive for employees with an internal locus than for employees with an external locus.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Forgone identity dwelling (Study 1 measured; Study 2a manipulated)
- DV(s): Withdrawal behavior, helping behavior, and job crafting / intentions to job craft (Study 2b manipulated longing → job crafting)
- Mediators: Longing (proximal); job crafting (serial mediator linking longing to reduced withdrawal and increased helping)
- Moderators: Internal locus of control (moderates the longing → job crafting relationship)

Drawing on Lazarus's (1991) cognitive-motivational-relational theory, the authors argue that dwelling on a forgone professional identity triggers primary and secondary appraisals that produce longing—a secondary emotion blending sadness and happiness. The sadness component creates an avoidance action tendency (more withdrawal, less helping because the employee is distracted from the here and now), while the happiness/approach component motivates job crafting to recover unfulfilled values from the forgone path. Because internal-locus employees believe they can shape their own work lives, longing more strongly translates into job crafting for them, which in turn circulates back to reduce withdrawal and increase helping (a serial indirect effect). The field study tested the full set of linkages, while the two experimental-causal-chain studies each manipulated one segment of the model.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (multi-wave, multi-source field study): recruitment through online classified ads posted in 10 major metropolitan areas in the United States (429 registrants) plus emails to members and alumni of executive and professional master's programs at a large southeastern university (99 registrants), requiring at least 30 hours of work per week and coworkers willing to participate; all participants were paid $10 per survey. Of the 528 employees who registered, 458 completed Time 1, 444 Time 2, 434 Time 3, and 364 coworkers completed Time 4; listwise deletion left 348 employee-coworker dyads, and screening for failed attention checks (12), unusual completion times (9), and thin or off-topic identity descriptions (11) produced a final usable sample of 316 dyads. The design used four time periods separated by three weeks plus a registration survey, with withdrawal and helping behavior rated by the coworker the employee worked with more often. Unless otherwise noted, measures used a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
- Study 1 measures: forgone identity dwelling (IV, Time 1) = three items created for this study, anchored 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always), e.g., "think about your alternative self?" (alpha = .91), content-validated with Hinkin and Tracey's (1999) approach on a separate 160-participant Mechanical Turk sample (htc = .88, htd = .27 against disappointment and daydreaming); longing (mediator, Time 2) = three items created for this study ("longed for what could have been," "yearned for how things could have turned out," "been wistful for what could have happened"; alpha = .96; htc = .87, htd = .40 against sadness and happiness); job crafting (mediator, Time 3) = Niessen, Weseler, and Kostova's (2016) three-item task crafting scale (alpha = .86); withdrawal behavior (DV, Time 4, coworker-rated) = five items from Fox and Spector's (1999) scale on 1 (not at all) to 7 (a great deal) anchors (alpha = .87); helping behavior (DV, Time 4, coworker-rated) = four items from Podsakoff, MacKenzie, Moorman, and Fetter (1990) on the same 7-point anchors, with one item dropped for cross-loadings (alpha = .91); internal locus of control (moderator, Time 1) = seven items from Levenson (1974), with one item dropped for cross-loadings (alpha = .72).
- Study 1 controls and estimation: forgone identity life satisfaction and current identity life satisfaction (adaptations of four Diener, Emmons, Larsen, and Griffin (1985) items; alpha = .88 and .86), sadness and happiness from Fredrickson, Tugade, Waugh, and Larkin (2003) (alpha = .88 and .91), trait negative and positive affectivity (10 PANAS items each; alpha = .94 and .83), proactive personality (Bateman & Crant, 1993; alpha = .86), intrinsic motivation (Guay, Vallerand, & Blanchard, 2000; alpha = .93), forgone timing, socioeconomic status, and education, each entered only in the linkages listed in the paper's Table 3. Hypotheses were tested with structural equation modeling in Mplus 8.30 using a partially latent approach (scale scores as single indicators with loadings fixed to the square root of reliability), with the longing x locus of control product term formed from centered variables and indirect effects tested via MODEL INDIRECT with 10,000 bootstrap samples. The authors describe this study as correlational and note the design "cannot rule out several threats to internal validity."
- Study 2a (randomized writing-reflection experiment): 207 Mechanical Turk participants recruited as full-time employees working at least 30 hours a week and aged 30-45, paid $2.00; exclusions for failed attention checks (4), unusual completion times (11), and not following the writing prompt (9) left a final usable sample of 183 employees. Forgone identity dwelling was manipulated by random assignment to a prompt instructing participants to dwell on and write at least five sentences about their alternative identity versus a control prompt about what they had done in their day so far. Manipulation check = the Study 1 dwelling scale (alpha = .91); longing = an adaptation of the Study 1 scale asking how much participants felt "a longing," "a yearning," and "a wistfulness" right now (alpha = .90). Analyzed with ANOVA; random assignment meant the Study 1 covariates did not alter the model tests and were excluded from the reported results.
- Study 2b (randomized writing-reflection experiment): 240 Mechanical Turk participants recruited on the same eligibility and payment terms, but without any reference to current or forgone identities (framed as research on attitudes about work in general); exclusions for failed attention checks (7), unusual completion times (30), and not following the writing prompt (29) left a final usable sample of 174 participants. Longing was manipulated by random assignment to write about things participants had a high versus a low degree of longing for in their working life. Manipulation check = the Study 2a longing scale (alpha = .96); locus of control = six items from Brewin and Shapiro (1984) (alpha = .80), a different scale from the Levenson (1974) items used in Study 1; job crafting = the Study 1 scale adapted to behavioral intentions ("During the next few weeks, in order to make it so that the job I do suits me, I will . . ."; alpha = .84). Analyzed with ANOVA and with ANCOVA using locus of control as a covariate. Studies 2a and 2b implement Spencer, Zanna, and Fong's (2005) experimental-causal-chain approach, so each experiment establishes one segment of the model by random assignment rather than testing the full serial chain.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (316 employee-coworker dyads) supported all six hypotheses. H1 supported: forgone identity dwelling was positively related to longing (b = .22, p < .01) and longing was positively related to withdrawal behavior (b = .10, p < .05), with a positive and significant indirect effect (unstandardized indirect effect = .02, 95% CI [.002, .061]).
- H2 supported: longing was negatively related to helping behavior (b = -.22, p < .01), with a negative and significant indirect effect (unstandardized indirect effect = -.05, 95% CI [-.138, -.003]).
- H3 supported: longing was positively related to job crafting (b = .17, p < .01), with a positive and significant indirect effect (unstandardized indirect effect = .04, 95% CI [.003, .101]).
- H4 supported: job crafting was negatively related to withdrawal behavior (b = -.20, p < .01), and the serial indirect effect of dwelling on withdrawal behavior through longing and job crafting was negative and significant (unstandardized indirect effect = -.01, 95% CI [-.026, -.001]).
- H5 supported: job crafting was positively related to helping behavior (b = .73, p < .01), and the serial indirect effect of dwelling on helping behavior through longing and job crafting was positive and significant (unstandardized indirect effect = .03, 95% CI [.002, .083]).
- H6 supported: the longing x locus of control product term predicted job crafting (b = .20, p < .05), and adding that path improved fit (-2 log-likelihood difference (1) = 5.64, p < .05); the longing-job crafting slope was more positive for employees with an internal locus (b = .25, p < .001) than an external locus (b = .11, p < .05), with the slopes significantly different (b difference = .14, p < .01).
- Descriptively, forgone identity dwelling correlated positively with forgone identity life satisfaction (r = .40) and negatively with current identity life satisfaction (r = -.19), and was more frequent among employees with lower socioeconomic status (r = -.11) and no college education (r = -.16); it was unrelated to every dispositional variable collected, including locus of control, trait positive and negative affectivity, and proactive personality.
- Study 2a (n = 183) replicated only the dwelling-to-longing segment: the dwelling prompt raised the dwelling manipulation check (F [1, 181] = 52.65, p < .001, M = 4.23 vs. 3.24, partial eta-squared = .23) and raised longing (F [1, 181] = 6.18, p < .05, M = 3.36 vs. 2.96, partial eta-squared = .03).
- Study 2b (n = 174) replicated the longing-to-job crafting segment and its moderation: the high-longing prompt raised longing (F [1, 172] = 289.84, p < .001, M = 4.13 vs. 1.90, partial eta-squared = .63) and job crafting intentions (F [1, 172] = 5.45, p < .05, M = 3.98 vs. 3.68, partial eta-squared = .03), and the longing prompt x locus of control interaction was significant for job crafting (F [1, 170] = 3.96, p < .05, partial eta-squared = .02), with a positive slope for employees with an internal locus (b = .53, p < .05) but a non-significant slope for those with an external locus (b = .05, p = .764). Locus of control did not predict the longing manipulation check (F [1, 170] = 2.57, p = .111), nor did the prompt x locus interaction on that check (F [1, 170] = 1.52, p = .219).
- Support pattern across studies: only Study 1 tested all six hypotheses, and it did so with correlational, self-report measures of dwelling and longing. Studies 2a and 2b each replicated one segment of the model by random assignment, so the withdrawal and helping outcomes and both serial indirect effects (H4, H5) rest on the field study alone.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the "forward progress" assumption in identity construction theory by showing that "identity echoes" from forgone professional identities continue to shape employee reactions after a current work identity is established, representing a lingering incoherence such models assume is resolved. It extends Obodaru's (2012, 2017) work on alternative selves by introducing identity dwelling as the mechanism through which forgone identities exert influence, and it foregrounds an affective construct—longing—to complement the predominantly cognitive and qualitative identity construction literature, addressing its noted "methodological myopia."

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that employees increasingly carry forgone professional identities and dwell on "what could have been," and should surface these through mentoring, coaching, or developmental conversations. They can then craft work contexts—providing flexibility, autonomy, and delegating tasks aligned with employees' forgone identities—to channel longing toward beneficial job crafting. Locus-of-control training that cultivates a more internal mindset may further encourage positive behavioral reactions to forgone identities.

**Limitations**
Participants may have faced multiple forks in the road and thus multiple forgone identities, but only the most salient was assessed, so findings may miss nuance. The forgone identity dwelling and longing measures were created for this study and need continued use to establish broader construct validity. There may also be reciprocal causation in the dwelling–longing relationship, since cognition and emotion are highly integrated.

**Future Research**
Researchers could examine dwelling on other selves (past, ideal, ought selves) and the cognitive—not just affective—consequences of identity dwelling, drawing on work on repetitive thought. They should examine whether dwelling and longing have iterative effects, with longing encouraging subsequent dwelling, using experimental methods and cross-lagged analyses with panel data; probe whether job crafting feeds a virtuous cycle of further crafting or ends in disenchantment and exit; and consider how identification strength and occupational prestige shape who dwells on forgone identities.
