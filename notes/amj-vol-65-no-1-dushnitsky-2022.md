# Here Comes the Sun: The Impact of Incidental Contextual Factors on Entrepreneurial Resource Acquisition

**Abstract**
This paper focuses on investment decisions in nascent, seed-stage startups, where information is scarce and uncertainty is intense. Entrepreneurship literature has shown that investment is a function of the investor–startup pair; some studies have investigated startup characteristics, while others have focused on investor traits. We complement extant work by studying factors beyond the investor–startup pair; namely, incidental contextual factors such as changes in the physical environment. Drawing on affect-as-information theory, we conjecture that, in the face of intense uncertainty, sunnier days may affect investors' mood and result in a greater likelihood of investment. Archival and experimental analyses inform our predictions. The former utilizes proprietary data on 1,335 startups graduating from European accelerators. Every accelerator holds a "Demo Day," where startups pitch to investors. We find that graduating on a sunnier day increases the likelihood of investment. This effect is stronger under intense uncertainty, where (a) startups are nascent, or (b) founders have limited human capital. An experimental study where prospective investors evaluate a Demo Day pitch yields further insights. We find a positive sunshine–investment association, and show that the association is mediated by investors' mood. Our findings complement extant work and contribute insights regarding seed-stage investment decisions.

**Research Question**
What explains seed-stage investment patterns, and what factors affect investors' decisions to fund highly uncertain startups? The paper asks specifically whether incidental contextual factors outside the investor–startup pair — namely a daily change in sunshine on Demo Day — systematically shape seed-stage resource acquisition.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: For a given startup graduating on a given Demo Day, the sunnier the Demo Day (i.e., when the sunshine duration is higher relative to the previous day), the greater the likelihood of raising a Demo Day round.
- H2: Startup maturity will moderate the positive association between sunnier Demo Day and subsequent funding success; the effect will be stronger for younger startups compared to more mature ones.
- H3: Founders' human capital will moderate the positive association between sunnier Demo Day and subsequent funding success; the effect will be stronger for founders with limited human capital compared to those who possess substantial human capital.

The sunshine–mood–investment mediation tested in Study 2 is presented as the paper's underlying theoretical mechanism rather than as a numbered hypothesis.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Sunnier — the interday change in realized sunshine hours between Demo Day and the preceding day (archival); a sunny-vs-cloudy weather mood-induction manipulation (experiment).
- DV(s): Demo Day funding (binary, whether a startup secures a funding round within 90 days); investment attractiveness and intended investment amount (experiment).
- Mediators: Investors' mood (PANAS-SF mood valence) mediates the sunshine–investment association in the experiment.
- Moderators: Startup maturity (younger startups → stronger effect) and founders' human capital (limited human capital → stronger effect), both capturing the level of uncertainty investors face.

Affect-as-information theory holds that people confuse incidental feelings (e.g., a sunshine-induced good mood) with integral feelings about the focal task, so that under intense uncertainty an investor's mood "tips the balance" toward more favorable subjective probability assessments of a startup. A sunnier Demo Day lifts investors' mood, which raises their evaluation of an otherwise opaque seed-stage venture and increases the likelihood of funding; the effect attenuates as uncertainty falls (mature startups, high-human-capital founders) because more objective information then displaces mood as the input to judgment.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (archival; unit of analysis is the startup): a proprietary dataset of 1,335 startups graduating from European accelerators, which pitched during 171 Demo Day events in about a dozen major cities across Europe. It was built by combining data from multiple venture capital databases (Crunchbase, triangulated with Beauhurst) and hand-collecting data from primary and secondary sources, including email questionnaires to the managers of the sample accelerators; of about 55 candidate European entities, 24 were dropped as misclassified, inactive, too new, or lacking documented Demo Day dates, leaving 31 accelerators. Estimation samples in Table 3 are 1,335 startups (Models 1-4) and 1,321 (Models 5-6, which add the Sunnier x Human capital interaction term; Human capital itself enters all six models as a control).
- Study 1 DV: Demo day funding, which takes a value of 1 if a startup secures a Demo Day funding round and 0 otherwise, where a Demo Day round is any financing round recorded within a 90-day period of the Demo Day date.
- Study 1 IV: Sunnier, the difference between the hours of actual sunshine realized on a Demo Day and that on the previous day, computed from Weather Online historical weather data validated against National Oceanic & Atmospheric Administration data wherever available, at a precision of up to a quarter of an hour.
- Study 1 moderators: Startup maturity (venture age), the time elapsed in months between the startup's founding date and its Demo Day date; and Human capital, a startup-level continuous score between 0 and 1 calculated as the mean across a startup's founders of a binary indicator scored 1 if the founder had prior entrepreneurial experience and held a master's degree or higher, using names and professional profiles from Beauhurst and Crunchbase complemented with the founders' LinkedIn accounts.
- Study 1 controls and estimation: DD sunshine (the absolute realized sunshine hours on the Demo Day itself), Team avg. age, Team incl. female, Prior funding, Cohort size, and cohort averages of startup maturity and prior fundraising amount, plus startup city, startup sector, and accelerator fixed effects. Because the dependent variable is binary, the paper estimates binary logistic regressions with errors clustered at the cohort level, and reports robustness to probit and to linear probability model (LPM) OLS estimation. The archival design is associational rather than an identified causal design: Sunnier is described as a stochastic variable arising from prescheduled Demo Day dates, and the paper reports sunshine–investment correlations.
- Study 2 (randomized, between-subjects experiment): participants were sourced through the Prolific Academic panel and prescreened on their overall profile and prior investment experience. After excluding those who failed the manipulation and attention checks and those with missing or inconsistent entries, the final pool was 92 participants, with about equal numbers randomly assigned to each of the two scenarios; 54% were male, average age was 34, average work experience was over 11 years, and about a third worked at a startup. A separate robustness experiment run without the PANAS instrument used 90 nonoverlapping participants.
- Study 2 manipulation and IV: a mood induction combining visual and textual weather cues. One scenario showed two sunny San Francisco images (the Golden Gate Bridge and a hilltop panorama) with the text "Imagine today is a sunny day. Yesterday's clouds are nowhere to be seen."; the other showed the same vista points under cloudy skies with the matching cloudy text. Sunnier is a binary variable equal to 1 for the scenario described as sunnier than the previous day and 0 otherwise. All participants then watched the same embedded video of a Demo Day pitch recorded at a recent Demo Day at a leading West Coast accelerator, selected because it had secured a funding round shortly after Demo Day.
- Study 2 mediator and DVs: Mood is mood valence from the 10-question PANAS short form (PANAS-SF), administered immediately after the manipulation but before the pitch was shown. Attractive measures investment attractiveness on a 7-item Likert scale where 1 denotes a highly unattractive and 7 a highly attractive opportunity; Log(Amount) is the natural log of the intended investment plus 1, elicited on a sliding scale with dollar amounts ranging from $0 to over $20,000.
- Study 2 estimation: ordered probit for Attractive and OLS for Mood and Log(Amount), with three mediation tests common in the literature: a three-step mediation regression (Baron & Kenny), a Sobel test, and bias-corrected bootstrap mediation based on 1,000 bootstrapped samples.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supports Hypothesis 1. Sunnier is positive and statistically significant (Table 3, Model 2: coefficient 0.08, p < 0.01) and remains positive and significant when the absolute Demo Day sunshine control is excluded (Model 3: coefficient 0.05, p < 0.04). The economic magnitude is material: a one-standard-deviation increase (4.2 hours) in the daily change of Demo Day sunshine hours is associated with a 3.6% increase in the likelihood of funding, against a population average of 17% for Demo Day funding, which the paper later describes as about 21% of the sample average.
- Hypotheses 2 and 3 are not supported by their interaction coefficients. In Model 4, the Sunnier by Venture age interaction is positive yet not statistically significant at conventional levels (p < 0.77), while Venture age remains negative and insignificant (p < 0.62) and Sunnier stays positive and significant (p < 0.04). In Model 5, Human capital and the Sunnier by Human capital interaction are both insignificant (p < 0.31 and p < 0.67), while Sunnier remains positive and significant (p < 0.01).
- The moderator support the paper reports rests on graphical net-effect and split-sample analyses, which it labels supplementary. The net effect of a sunnier Demo Day rises monotonically as startup age falls and is statistically significant (p < 0.1) for startups aged 50 months or less, about 80% of the sample; the net effect is likewise significant for startups whose founders possess lower levels of human capital, about two-thirds of the sample. In LPM OLS split samples the Sunnier effect is positive and significant for younger ventures and for founders with limited human capital, but not for mature ventures or those with high human capital. The paper reads these as boundary conditions: the sunshine–investment association is greatest when uncertainty is high.
- Study 2 converges with Study 1 on the main effect. Investment attractiveness was higher in the sunnier condition (sunnier M = 5.1, SD = 1.6; not-sunnier M = 4.3, SD = 1.4; t = 2.4, p = 0.02), as was the logged investment amount (sunnier M = 5.7, SD = 3.2; not-sunnier M = 4.3, SD = 3.5; t = 2.0, p = 0.04), and mood valence differed across conditions in the predicted direction (sunnier M = 1.8, SD = 1.0; not-sunnier M = 1.3, SD = 1.1; t = 2.1, p = 0.03).
- Study 2 supports the mood mechanism. Sunnier positively predicts Attractive (ordered probit, p = 0.001) and Mood (OLS, p = 0.022). In the full ordered-probit specification Mood is positive and significant (p = 0.00) alongside a still-significant Sunnier coefficient (p = 0.017), which by the paper's own stated criterion is partial rather than full mediation; in the OLS replication of the attractiveness models Mood remains significant (p = 0.00) while Sunnier becomes insignificant (p = 0.11). Sobel test statistics are significant across the three panels (t = 4.21, t = 3.94, and t = 4.41, all p = 0.00), and the bias-corrected bootstrap indirect effects do not straddle zero: 0.105 (95% CI 0.094 to 0.118) for attractiveness under ordered probit, 0.083 (95% CI 0.071 to 0.097) for attractiveness under OLS, and 0.409 (95% CI 0.244 to 0.576) for investment amount. The paper concludes that mood mediates the association between sunshine and both investment attractiveness and investment amount.
- Robustness. The additional experiment run without the PANAS instrument on 90 nonoverlapping participants yielded similar results, which the paper reads as evidence that the sunshine–investment associations are not an artifact of the experimental design. In the archival study, analyses of daily sunshine changes on the five days before and after the Demo Day show that in each full model the coefficient for Demo Day Sunnier is significant and higher than the other coefficients, and splined models for Sunnier are also reported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces a third class of explanation for seed-stage investment patterns — incidental contextual factors situated outside the investor–startup pair — to complement the two dominant streams that locate variation in startup characteristics or investor traits. It demonstrates that funding-outcome variation can emerge even across a homogeneous pool of investors and startups, simply because some pairs are randomly exposed to contextual shocks, and it imports affect-as-information theory into entrepreneurship to specify mood as the mechanism, bounded by the level of uncertainty.

**Practical Implication**
Rather than ignoring incidental contextual factors, investors and accelerators should heed their effect, since transient conditions such as weather accrue in a foreseeable way on funding decisions. While one cannot control the sun, accelerators could design Demo Days (e.g., refreshment choices or "walking" Demo Days) to manage investors' mood, and scholars/practitioners should collect data on the context in which pairs are situated, not only on startups and investors.

**Limitations**
The arguments are calibrated to seed-stage investment and its intense uncertainty, so the affect-as-information logic need not extend to later-stage deals where a VC committee already knows the startup. The archival analysis captures funding outcomes for hundreds of accelerator graduates but lacks detailed data on the individual investors who attended, and the outcome is measured only as a binary funding variable.

**Future Research**
Future work could trace individual investors' movements to directly map how incidental contextual factors shape their decisions, and examine whether daily sunshine operates through entrepreneurs as well as investors. It could also test generalizability to other geographies and other contextual factors (e.g., choice of refreshment), and explore richer outcome measures such as premoney valuation.
