# “I Go Here…But I Don’t Necessarily Belong”: The Process of Transgressor Reintegration in Organizations

**Abstract**
When organizational members violate important organizational standards, they may face termination, or they may instead be retained by the organization and given a second chance. Retained transgressors experience the tension of liminality: they maintain their affiliation to the organization, making them structural insiders, but they have committed a transgression, making them moral outsiders. How might transgressors attempt to reintegrate and feel like full organizational insiders once again? And what makes transgressors feel more, or less, reintegrated? Previous work has studied reintegration from victims’ or third-parties’ perspectives, but little is known about transgressor reintegration. To build theory on transgressor reintegration, I studied transgressors at a military service academy. Through waves of qualitative data collection and inductive analyses, I find that transgressions threaten transgressors’ integration, leading transgressors to feel precarious in their perceptions of membership and their feelings of belonging. Transgressors attempt to restore both elements, but use distinct approaches for each. Because belonging restoration requires positive interactions with many organizational actors, transgressors can—and, in my data, frequently do—experience restoration of membership but not belonging. Therefore, it may be relatively rare for transgressors to feel highly reintegrated following transgressions, even in organizations that devote considerable resources to reintegration.

**Research Question**
How do retained transgressors—organizational members who violated core values and so became "structural insiders but moral outsiders"—attempt to reintegrate and come to feel like full insiders again, and what makes them feel more, or less, reintegrated?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper — this is a longitudinal, inductive grounded-theory study with no a priori hypotheses and no formally numbered propositions. The paper poses research questions rather than predictions (how transgressors attempt to reintegrate and feel like full insiders once again, and what makes them feel more, or less, reintegrated), and states that because of the inductive nature of the research the bulk of the theorizing is presented at the end of the paper.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: A transgression decouples membership and belonging, throwing the retained transgressor into liminality (structural insider, moral outsider) and producing precarious membership and precarious belonging.
- Restoration attempts (membership): Compliance behaviors aimed at obtaining institutional endorsements to feel secure in perceptions of membership.
- Restoration attempts (belonging): Investment in relationships aimed at strengthening relational belonging, which stems from positive treatment by personal acquaintances, while institutional belonging is triggered by positive treatment from institutional actors.
- Emergent outcome: Belonging splits into relational belonging and institutional belonging; both, plus restored membership, are necessary for high reintegration.
- Boundary condition: Because belonging restoration depends on positive perceived interactions with many actors, transgressors often restore membership but not belonging, so high reintegration is rare (only 3 of 23 cadets).

Frey advances a process-with-variance model in which reintegration is an intrapsychic, perceptual process driven by transgressors’ own interpretations of interactions rather than by objective, triangulated behaviors. Transgressors use distinct logics—compliance to restore membership versus relational investment to restore belonging—and because lack of restoration in even one element (membership, relational belonging, or institutional belonging) blocks full reintegration, feeling highly reintegrated is difficult to achieve even in resource-intensive programs.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and site: a longitudinal, qualitative, inductive study of cadets who had committed transgressions at a military service academy in the United States (pseudonymized as "the Academy"), which trains future military officers and has a strict honor code forbidding lying, cheating, or stealing in professional and personal life. Guilt is determined by a jury of peers and punishment by the superintendent; most cadets found guilty are retained but have graduation delayed by six to 12 months, lose rank and privileges, and must complete a six-month-long remediation program with an assigned high-ranking officer as mentor. The highly formalized and heavily used nature of that program makes the Academy an "extreme exemplar" (Eisenhardt & Graebner, 2007; Yin, 1994) of structural insiders but moral outsiders attempting to become full insiders.
- Participants: 23 cadets, 10 of their mentors, and four honor officers agreed to participate. Roughly 70% of the honor violations in the sample were related to academic issues (academic cheating, insufficient citations, plagiarism); the rest involved some type of lying. All cadets in the sample successfully completed the remediation program and had their formal standing restored. Only volunteers were included per IRB policy, and mentors were invited only if their cadet mentee had been recruited, which is why there are more cadets than mentors.
- Interview corpus: 111 interviews with participating cadets, mentors, and honor officers over the course of 48 months. Up to five hour-long, semi-structured interviews per cadet, at roughly the beginning, middle, and end of the remediation program, with the final interview typically after the cadet had been returned to full rank and standing; the first interview covered background, violation, and adjudication, the middle interview(s) the program itself, and the last the period after the superintendent restored rank and privileges. Up to three interviews per consenting mentor at the same three points, used primarily as a comparison point for the narratives cadets told. Single interviews with honor officers administering the Honor Program, recruited 24 months after the cadet and mentor interviews were completed, so the author could not "triangulate" or "verify" any specific cadet's experience with them; these were used to situate cadets' narratives in the larger institutional context. Interview protocols are reproduced in Appendix C.
- Secondary data (Appendix A, Table A2): an initial online survey completed after consent, covering extracurricular involvement at the Academy, a brief description of the violation, and a number of scales about ethical judgments, moral identity, and commitment — used only to familiarize the author with each cadet before the first interview, not as an analytic measure; cadet (and where applicable mentor) reflection exercises adapted from Kahneman and colleagues' Day Reconstruction Method (2004) at up to three points in time, to capture the most recent mentoring meeting; journal entries, which four cadets provided at least one of; and informal on-site observation of classes, meals, athletic events, officer get-togethers, officer promotions, and graduation events.
- How constructs were operationalized: inductively coded, not measured. Analysis was informed by grounded theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1965; Strauss & Corbin, 1998), iterating between data and theory. Open coding began after about one third of the interviews and produced codes close to the data (e.g., "feeling like part of the corps," "treated like a 'normal' cadet," "not allowed to represent the Academy"); what emerged also prompted a protocol revision so that later interviews asked cadets explicitly about their feelings of belonging.
- Axial coding grouped open codes into categories: codes such as "feeling included by friends," "allowed to play on athletic team," and "fitting in with others in corps" were grouped into the category of "feeling integrated," as these codes all had to do with feelings of inclusion in a collective; codes about insecurity in future affiliation or structural position ("feeling like 'second class citizen,'" "not allowed to have leadership role," "unsure when he would graduate") were placed into a category called precarious membership, drawing on scholarship about the indicators of membership; and more emotionally based codes about not being valued or accepted ("feels like he didn't belong," "friend stopped talking to her," "felt like 'leper'") were subsumed into a category called precarious belonging, drawing on scholarship about the indicators of belonging.
- Trajectory mapping and comparison: after the final interview for each cadet, the author followed Langley (1999) to build detailed narratives, reading each cadet's transcripts chronologically to infer the "high" and "low" points of the experience and mapping those on per-cadet timelines. Each theoretical category was then tracked within a cadet from first to last interview and compared across cadets, with memo-writing to record emerging patterns.
- Theory building and corroboration: in the framework-building phase the category "perceptions of restored belonging" was split into two subcomponents, relational belonging and institutional belonging, and the categories "compliance" and "investment behaviors" were linked to precarious membership and precarious belonging respectively. Informal member checks plus mentor interviews, honor officer interviews, and cadets' journal entries were used to corroborate, disprove, or modify the framework.
- Outcome classification: Appendix B (Table B1) records, for each cadet, demographics, tenure at time of violation, type and self-perceived severity of violation, pre-violation integration on the individual indicators of attraction to the organization, satisfaction with the organization, and social interactions (plus an overall rating), and a reintegration outcome coded High, Partial, or Low.
- Identification: none claimed. The design is inductive and interpretive, yielding a process-with-variance model rather than estimated effects, and the paper states explicitly that the emerging theory was a perceptual process based on cadets' interpretations of interactions rather than on "objective" behaviors that were triangulated and agreed upon by all involved parties.

**Key Findings**
- One shared process, variance at the end: the data suggest all transgressing cadets experienced the same process of reintegration — perceiving initial integration, experiencing liminality and the decoupling of membership and belonging, attempting to restore membership, attempting to restore belonging, and perceiving reintegration (Figure 1). Variation appeared at the end, in how far belonging was restored, which is why the author presents a process-with-variance model rather than a uniform stage model.
- Pre-violation baseline: all cadets recalled similar levels of integration and felt secure in both their perceptions of membership and their feelings of belonging; because both were likely formed together during socialization, the two components appear to have been psychologically coupled, with cadets experiencing them simply as "integration."
- Liminality decouples the two components: after being accused and then retained, cadets remained structural insiders (their formal affiliation was intact) but felt like moral outsiders, describing being "in limbo" or in "purgatory." All cadets experienced this liminality in two distinct ways — precarious belonging and precarious perceptions of membership — and it was liminality that led them to talk about and experience membership as distinct from belonging.
- Membership restoration ran through compliance and institutional endorsements: cadets sought signals that the Academy viewed them as undisputed members (getting rank back, a set graduation date, rejoining a team, leadership opportunities, restored privileges) and pursued them through compliance behaviors — saying what they believed leadership wanted to hear and following every rule. All cadets in the sample eventually received institutional endorsements, and regardless of how many they received, all described feeling more secure in their perceptions of membership; since cadets had technically remained structural insiders throughout, it was the endorsements rather than formal status that restored the perception.
- Belonging restoration ran through a different logic: cadets invested in relationships and closely monitored whether others treated them the same or differently. Because restoration hinged on interpretations of often ambiguous treatment, the heuristics cadets held about others' intentions (positive, negative, or mixed) shaped what they concluded; mentors' accounts sometimes diverged from cadets' own, which is what led the author to characterize transgressor reintegration as an intrapsychic, perceptual process rather than one driven by objectively verified behavior.
- Belonging splits into two types that complement rather than substitute: the data differentiate relational belonging (feeling valued by personal acquaintances) from institutional belonging (feeling valued by the organization as a whole, triggered by positive treatment from institutional actors). High reintegration required substantial restoration in membership, relational belonging, and institutional belonging together, each of which requires endorsement or affirmation from distinct organizational actors; a lack of restoration in even one element blocked high reintegration.
- The headline asymmetry: because belonging restoration requires positive interactions with many organizational actors, transgressors "can—and, in my data, frequently do—experience restoration of membership but not belonging." Only three out of the 23 cadets felt highly reintegrated by the end of the process; Table B1 classifies 16 of the remaining cadets as Partial and four as Low.
- Outcome patterns illustrated by three archetypal cadets: Cadet Kila was highly restored in both relational and institutional belonging (plus membership) and felt highly reintegrated; Cadet Romo received institutional endorsements and some restoration in institutional belonging but very little in relational belonging, concluding "I go here … but I don't necessarily belong," and was only partially reintegrated; Cadet Ecker felt highly restored in perceptions of membership yet experienced a lack of restoration in both types of belonging and ultimately felt he had not reintegrated. Partial reintegration also occurred in the mirror-image case (relational but not institutional belonging restored, e.g., Cadet Nova) and where a cadet felt only somewhat restored in both (Cadet Lima).
- Severity was not the driver: although self-perceived severity of the transgression varied, that variation appeared unrelated to cadets' reintegration outcomes or experiences.
- No recoupling: even cadets who felt highly reintegrated continued to treat membership and belonging as psychologically distinct — the two did not appear to become "recoupled" — suggesting that once integration has been psychologically decomposed into its constituent parts, those parts may never be "unseen" again.
- Victimless transgressions: because the honor violations had no single victim, the findings show that transgressor reintegration does not necessarily require victim involvement or victim-directed amends such as apologies at all, even though such actions may still matter for victims' and third-parties' perceptions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study conceptualizes transgressor reintegration from the transgressor’s own perspective, foregrounding its liminal starting point and the driving forces (restoring membership and belonging) rather than merely listing features of reintegration. It recasts integration from a static construct into a dynamic, perceptual, and negotiated process that can be formed, threatened, modified, and regained, and it differentiates belonging into relational and institutional types that are complements, not substitutes—offering new leverage for research on socialization, undersocialization, belonging, and nested relational phenomena.

**Practical Implication**
To give employees a genuine second chance, managers must support both restoring perceptions of membership (e.g., bestowing institutional endorsements such as desirable assignments, promotions, and access to perks) and fostering belonging (e.g., coaching peers and supervisors on how to interact with and affirm the transgressor). Managers should also balance transgressor affirmation against good-standing members’ justice perceptions to avoid backlash.

**Limitations**
Pre-violation integration data are retrospective because first interviews occurred after violations, so cadets’ recollections of initial integration may be inaccurate or colored by present feelings. The author could not collect data from transgressors’ interaction partners (personal acquaintances or institutional actors), leaving open whether transgressors’ perceptions are accurate, and the data are not fine-grained enough to definitively map specific actors to specific types of belonging.

**Future Research**
Survey-based quantitative work could test whether transgressors’ recollections of pre-transgression integration are accurate and whether their perceptions of others’ treatment generally align with reality. Researchers could more systematically identify which types or features of organizational actors foster relational versus institutional belonging, and examine how the observed dynamics differ in organizations with little or no formal structure around transgressor reintegration.
