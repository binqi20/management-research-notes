# Joining Forces: How Can Founding Members’ Prior Experience Variety and Shared Experience Increase Startup Survival?

**Abstract**
In extant work, two arguments have emerged on the relationship between founders’ experience and startup survival. First, founders’ diverse experiences should foster startup survival because the combination of various experience-based knowledge components drives the needed innovation. Second, founders’ shared experience in an incumbent firm in the industry of the startup should positively relate to startup survival because of the transfer of shared knowledge and routines. This paper reconciles both streams of research by proposing that startups can combine both types of experience-based advantages when founding teams are composed of founders who individually possess a variety of firm experiences and other founders who jointly transition from the same industry incumbent. Using a sample of startups in technological manufacturing industries, I test the direct effect of the two types of experiences and their complementarity in decreasing the likelihood of startup failure. The results show that such complementarities exist so that startups are less likely to fail when their founding teams combine within-founder experience variety and across-team shared experience. I discuss the implications of these findings for startup survival and founding team literature streams.

**Research Question**
How can founding teams combine two distinct experience-based advantages—within-founder experience variety and founders’ shared experience from the same industry incumbent—to reduce the likelihood of startup failure, thereby reconciling the experience-variety and spinout literatures?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1a. The number of founders with a variety of firm experiences negatively relates to the startup’s likelihood of failure.
Hypothesis 1b. The number of founders with a variety of firm experiences positively relates to the startup’s likelihood of failure. (H1a and H1b are developed as competing predictions about the same variable.)
Hypothesis 2. The shared experience of founders transitioning from the same industry incumbent negatively relates to the startup’s likelihood of failure.
Hypothesis 3. The shared experience in the industry incumbent moderates the relationship between the number of founders with a variety of firm experiences and startup failure, such that the likelihood of failure decreases as the number of years of shared experience increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Number of founders with a variety of firm experiences (within-founder experience variety aggregated to the team level); length (years) of shared experience of founders transitioning from the same industry incumbent.
- DV(s): Startup failure (coded 1 in the year the startup fails, 0 in years it is alive).
- Mediators: None tested; the knowledge-integration and routine-transfer logic is theorized but not measured as a mediator.
- Moderators: Shared industry-incumbent experience moderates the relationship between the number of variety founders and failure (the focal interaction, Hypothesis 3).

Drawing on social cognition theory and organizational knowledge theory, the paper argues that founders who frequently changed jobs accumulate diverse, loosely connected knowledge components that fuel innovation but are costly to integrate, so adding such founders alone raises failure risk (H1b supported). Founders who jointly leave the same incumbent transfer a shared knowledge base and well-oiled routines that lower failure risk (H2 supported). When the two combine, shared routines supply common ground that lets the team integrate the variety founders’ components, so the interaction is negative—startups are least likely to fail when within-founder variety is paired with across-team shared experience (H3 supported).

**Data & Measures**
Single quantitative study, supplemented by semistructured interviews of seven entrepreneurs (personal acquaintances and local entrepreneurs) conducted with the Appendix A questionnaire to provide rich illustrations and anecdotes. The analysis dataset is an extract of the confidential U.S. Census Bureau Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics (LEHD) employee–employer linked file, built from unemployment insurance records and covering 1990 to 2008 (1990–2000 is used only to compute the team experience variables, 2007–2008 only to compute startup performance). The frame is all new establishments created between 2000 and 2006 in five three-digit NAICS technological manufacturing industries in 18 U.S. states, identified as a new establishment identification number accompanied by a new firm tax identification number; establishments with strictly more than 30 employees at the end of their first year (or last quarter) were discarded, employees averaging less than $10,000 a year over their career were dropped, and the sample was restricted to startups with at least two employees, whose early employees constitute the founding team. Team and startup characteristics are measured one year after the startup’s birth, or in the last quarter of existence if it did not survive a full year, and only each member’s main job (highest earnings in a given year) enters the experience variables. The final sample is 6,000 startups and 31,000 startup-year observations, rounded per U.S. Census Bureau rules.
- DV — startup failure: coded 0 in any year the startup is alive and 1 the year the startup fails; the yearly failure rate is 5% and 27% of the startups failed over 2000–2008. Acquired startups are coded 0 in their last observed year as an independent firm and later years are excluded.
- IV — number of founders with a variety of firm experiences: the count of founders who, in the five years before joining the startup, worked in at least two firms and did not transition from the same industry incumbent as the other founding members, restricted to founders whose earnings did not decrease when they changed jobs and whose average yearly earnings over the five years were at least $10,000 (M = 0.46, SD = 0.90). The five-year window fits the U.S. employee median tenure over the period.
- IV / moderator — shared experience: the number of years the team worked together in the same incumbent firm right before creating the startup, with same-industry status established at the four-digit NAICS level; the largest set of team members from the same incumbent is used, with ties broken by the set with the highest earnings in the year before the startup’s creation (M = 0.35, SD = 1.19).
- Controls: team’s average experience, team’s average number of previous startups, share of teammates with a college degree, share of male teammates, teammates’ average age, team size, log of the team’s average earnings prior to the startup, startup age and age squared, the yearly number of new establishments in the venture’s county and its square, plus year, industry, and region dummies.

Identification is associational rather than causal: a discrete-time hazard model with one-year discrete periods, estimated as a linear probability model with standard errors clustered by industry (out-of-bound predictions checked to be under 4%) and re-estimated with a complementary log-log regression. The author states that mutual selection among team members cannot be ruled out. Robustness checks substitute alternative measures of within-founder variety (founders’ average number of previous employers, an outsider count, team-level average previous employers and a Blau index), vary the earnings constraint, and replace shared experience with the team’s average industry experience.

**Key Findings**
The two competing main-effect predictions resolve in favor of H1b, and both H2 and H3 are supported.
- H1b supported, H1a rejected: the number of founders with a variety of firm experiences is significant at 5% and positively related to failure (b = 0.0033 in Model 3 of Table 2; b = 0.0039 in Model 4 with the interaction). Including one founder of this type increases the likelihood of failure by 6.6%.
- H2 supported: shared experience is significant at 1% and negatively related to failure (b = −0.0024, Model 3). An additional year of shared experience reduces the failure rate by 4.8%.
- H3 supported: the interaction between the number of variety founders and shared experience is negative and significant at 5% (b = −0.0024, Model 4). Re-running every model as a complementary log-log regression (Table 3) yields the same results.
- Total marginal effects: a startup with one variety founder teaming up with cofounders who had two years of shared experience sees its likelihood of failing decline by 9%, and for teams with four years of such experience the inclusion of two variety founders decreases the likelihood of failing the following year by 36%. Without prior shared experience, an increasing number of variety founders raises the marginal effect of failing; with three years of prior shared experience, that marginal effect plummets.
- Robustness and boundary conditions: the interaction holds when variety is measured as those founders’ average number of previous employers, when founders who had worked for a startup in the preceding five years are excluded, and when previously excluded founders who had also worked at the same incumbent as the rest of the team are included. It is insignificant at the conventional level (p > 0.10) for "outsiders" (founders sharing no work experience with the team who worked for only one firm in the window) and for team-level variety measured as the team’s average number of previous employers or an experience Blau index — both of which still increase the likelihood of failure, indicating that variety spread across the team cannot benefit from prior shared experience. Team industry experience is itself significant and negatively related to failure, but its interaction with variety founders is very small and insignificant (p > 0.10), so the complementarity requires founders transitioning from the same industry incumbent. The complementarity also vanishes when founders whose earnings decreased from one job to the next are included, though it holds under a 3% decrease limit and for three of the four earnings-split subgroups; in the fourth (no decrease and at least one increase of 3% or more) the interaction is negatively related to failure but, in the paper's words, "the p-value is slightly above the conventional level of significance."

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study bridges the experience-variety and spinout literatures by integrating knowledge-combination and knowledge-transfer mechanisms under a single organizational-knowledge-and-routines framework, introducing the construct of within-founder experience variety (variety encapsulated in a single founder’s career rather than spread across the team). It shows that the level at which human-capital resources are measured matters: complementarities emerge only when variety is concentrated within individual founders and the shared experience is specifically tied to a common industry incumbent, refining prior work that relied on teams’ total number of prior experiences or any industry experience.

**Practical Implication**
Founders who frequently changed jobs before founding should team up with cofounders who jointly transition from the same industry incumbent; variety founders lower failure risk only when at least part of the team supplies shared knowledge and routines. Practically, founders with a variety of experiences are associated with lower startup failure when other founders share at least two years of prior work in the same industry incumbent, helping resource-constrained teams assemble complementary human capital.

**Limitations**
The findings derive from U.S. employer–employee LEHD data covering technological manufacturing startup survival from 2000 to 2008, so implications should be treated with caution in other institutional and economic contexts. The LEHD excludes pre-incorporation, pre-hiring, and self-employment stages, so average survival is likely overstated and implications apply only to "advanced" startups. Mutual selection among team members cannot be fully ruled out, and the data do not capture each member’s functional role or non-employment shared work relationships.

**Future Research**
Future work could model the mutual selection among all team members using newer techniques such as many-sided matching models to better isolate selection effects on startup performance. Building new surveys (e.g., with the U.S. Census Bureau) that span all entrepreneurial stages from idea inception to first hires would broaden generalizability, and linking team members’ occupations and broader forms of shared work relationships could reveal additional human-capital complementarities within founding teams.
