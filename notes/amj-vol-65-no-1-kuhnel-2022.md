# There Is a Time to Be Creative: The Alignment between Chronotype and Time of Day

**Abstract**
We examine the influence of chronobiological processes on creativity, specifically the influence of a person’s chronotype. Chronotype refers to the setting of a person’s biological clock that gives rise to a distinctive pattern of sleep habits and preferred diurnal activity. We propose a synchrony effect and predict that people are creative when the external clock is aligned with their internal, biological clock. According to our model, positive mood and creative self-efficacy act as affective and cognitive mechanisms of this synchrony effect. We present three studies that test our theorizing: a quasi-experimental field study with 260 employees, a day-reconstruction study with 238 employees, and a one-day experience sampling study with 319 employees. Across the studies, we find that chronotype moderates the effect of time of day on creativity. Overall, late chronotypes were more creative in the late afternoon and early chronotypes tended to be more creative in the morning. The alignment between chronotype and time of day also gave rise to positive mood and creative self-efficacy; however, the studies provide only partial support for the hypothesis that positive mood acts as a mediating mechanism. We discuss the implications of these findings against the background of an embodied cognition perspective on creativity.

**Research Question**
Does within-day fluctuation in employees’ creativity depend on the alignment between their chronotype and the time of day (a "synchrony effect"), and do positive mood and creative self-efficacy operate as the affective and cognitive mechanisms transmitting this chronobiological influence on creativity?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Chronotype moderates the effect of time of day on creativity such that (a) earlier chronotypes are more creative in the morning compared to in the late afternoon, and (b) later chronotypes are more creative in the late afternoon compared to in the morning.
H2. Chronotype moderates the effect of time of day on positive mood such that (a) earlier chronotypes experience higher positive mood in the morning compared to in the late afternoon, and (b) later chronotypes experience higher positive mood in the late afternoon compared to in the morning.
H3. The relationship between time of day and creativity that is moderated by chronotype is mediated by positive mood: (a) earlier chronotypes are more creative in the morning than in the late afternoon because they experience higher positive mood in the morning; (b) later chronotypes are more creative in the late afternoon than in the morning because they experience higher positive mood in the late afternoon.
H4. Chronotype moderates the effect of time of day on creative self-efficacy such that (a) earlier chronotypes display higher creative self-efficacy in the morning compared to in the late afternoon, and (b) later chronotypes experience higher creative self-efficacy in the late afternoon compared to in the morning.
H5. The relationship between time of day and creativity that is moderated by chronotype is mediated by creative self-efficacy: (a) earlier chronotypes are more creative in the morning than in the late afternoon because they display higher creative self-efficacy in the morning; (b) later chronotypes are more creative in the late afternoon than in the morning because they display higher creative self-efficacy in the late afternoon.

Studies 1 and 2 tested H1-H3; Study 3 tested the entire conceptual model (H1-H5), adding creative self-efficacy as a second, parallel mediator.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Time of day (Level 1); chronotype (Level 2, continuous, measured via MCTQ and rMEQ).
- DV(s): Creativity (objective alternative uses test in Study 1, scored on three facets — creative fluency, cognitive flexibility, and originality; self-rated work-related creativity in Studies 2 and 3).
- Mediators: Positive mood (PANAS items) and creative self-efficacy (Tierney & Farmer, 2002).
- Moderators: Chronotype moderates the within-person effect of time of day (the synchrony effect); chronotype is modeled as a cross-level moderator of the time-of-day slope.

Drawing on Borbély's two-process model of sleep regulation and an embodied cognition perspective, the paper argues that when the time of day matches a person's chronotype, the executive functions that enable creativity are most accessible, allowing the person to form remote associations and recombine elements in novel ways. This synchrony state is theorized to elevate both positive mood (an affective pathway, via the circadian reward-motivation system) and creative self-efficacy (a cognitive pathway, as awareness that one can access one's creative potential), which in turn promote creativity. Empirically, chronotype reliably moderated the time-of-day effect on creativity, but positive mood mediated only in Studies 2 and 3 and creative self-efficacy mediated for earlier (not later) chronotypes, indicating the mediating processes—especially for late chronotypes—are more complex than a simple parallel-mediation model implies.

**Data & Measures**
Three separate within-person field studies, all analyzed with multilevel models in Mplus 8.2 (Level-1 measurement occasions nested within Level-2 persons), with time of day as the Level-1 predictor and chronotype grand-mean centered as a cross-level moderator of the time-of-day slope. Neither time of day nor chronotype is exogenously manipulated: Study 1 is described as quasi-experimental and randomizes only the order of the two sessions, and Study 3 separates the measurement of the mediators from the dependent variable by one hour "for improved causal inferences," so the within-person relationships are associational rather than causally identified.

Study 1 (repeated-measures field study; combined N = 260 employees, response rate 57%, 520 occasions; nonshift workers from companies in diverse industries recruited by students through their personal networks and companies in the region):
- Design: Group 1 (N = 80) attended two sessions on consecutive workdays at their workplace, one in the morning between 8 a.m. and 10 a.m. and one in the late afternoon between 3:30 p.m. and 5:30 p.m.; Group 2 (N = 180) completed two online questionnaires during work time, one between 9 a.m. and 10 a.m. and one between 5 p.m. and 6 p.m. Participants were randomly assigned to morning-first or late-afternoon-first order. Time of day was coded 0 = morning, 1 = late afternoon.
- Chronotype (Level-2 moderator): the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire (MCTQ; Roenneberg et al., 2003), which derives chronotype from the midpoint of sleep on work-free days corrected for oversleep; continuous (M = 3.91, SD = 1.10), with 19% early, 66% intermediate, and 15% late chronotypes.
- Creativity (DV): the alternative uses test (Guilford, 1967), administered in both sessions with two object versions (brick, newspaper) counterbalanced across sessions and a five-minute time frame. Two independent raters, blind to condition, chronotype, and hypotheses, scored creative fluency (number of unique ideas), cognitive flexibility (number of seven a priori content categories used), and originality (1 = not original at all to 7 = very original); interrater reliability ICC(2,2) was above .80 for all measures, and z-standardized facet scores were averaged into a composite.
- Positive mood (mediator): six PANAS items (Watson et al., 1988) as used by Sonnentag et al. (2008) - active, interested, excited, strong, inspired, alert - referring to the present moment on a 5-point scale; "inspired" was dropped in Group 1 to improve alpha. Cronbach's alphas were .72 and .74 (Group 1, morning and late-afternoon sessions) and .83 and .81 (Group 2).
- Controls: previous night's sleep duration (self-reported hours and minutes) and single-item sleep quality on a 5-point scale derived from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.
- Group 2 only (Appendix A): self-rated work-related creativity measured with the five-item version (Bledow et al., 2013) of the Tierney et al. (1999) scale on a 7-point scale, alphas .88 and .86.

Study 2 (day-reconstruction study; N = 238 full-time employees doing creative work such as architects, designers, artists, and creative directors, response rate 69%, yielding 849 days and 2,716 performance episodes, M = 3.2 episodes per day):
- Design: over one work week, participants received an end-of-day questionnaire, selected all performance episodes during which they had worked, wrote down what they did, and rated mood and creativity for each episode. Time of day is the performance episode, coded 0 (6 a.m.-9 a.m.), 1 (9 a.m.-noon), 2 (noon-3 p.m.), 3 (3 p.m.-6 p.m.), and 4 (6 p.m.-9 p.m.).
- Chronotype: the MCTQ as in Study 1 (M = 3.78, SD = 1.04), with 24% early, 65% intermediate, and 11% late chronotypes.
- Creativity (DV): work-related creativity, the five-item version (Bledow et al., 2013) of the Tierney, Farmer, and Graen (1999) scale on a 7-point scale, worded to the time frame of the respective performance episode; alphas ranged between .86 and .90.
- Positive mood (mediator): the same items as in Study 1, referring to how the person felt during a specific performance episode; alphas ranged between .83 and .90.
- Controls: previous night's sleep duration and sleep quality, plus day-specific time pressure and day-specific job control (three items each; Semmer, Zapf, & Dunckel, 1999).

Study 3 (one-day experience sampling study; N = 319 full-time employees recruited via Amazon's Mechanical Turk, 638 matched measurement occasions; of 562 who answered the entry questionnaire, 339 provided data on all occasions (60% completion) and 20 were excluded for very short answering times):
- Design: an entry questionnaire followed by four same-day surveys at 8 a.m. (T1a), 9 a.m. (T1b), 4 p.m. (T2a), and 5 p.m. (T2b), with the mediators measured at T1a and T2a and creativity measured one hour later at T1b and T2b. Time of day was coded 0 = morning, 1 = late afternoon.
- Chronotype: the reduced Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ; Adan & Almirall, 1991), five questions, alpha = .75, continuous with values from 4 to 25 (M = 13.64, SD = 3.86); 33% early (< 12), 50% intermediate (12-17), and 17% late (> 17). The MCTQ was also collected and correlated r = .711 (p < .001) with the rMEQ, but the rMEQ scores were used for the analyses because it required no exclusions.
- Creativity (DV): the same work-related creativity items as in Studies 1 and 2, referring to the last hour, on a 5-point scale; alpha = .91 on both occasions.
- Positive mood (mediator): the same PANAS items, with respondents indicating how they felt "right now"; alphas .91 (T1a) and .89 (T2a).
- Creative self-efficacy (mediator): the three items of Tierney and Farmer (2002) adapted to capture within-person variation (e.g., "Right now, I have confidence in my ability to solve problems creatively") on a 5-point scale; alphas .91 (T1a) and .92 (T2a).
- Controls: previous night's sleep duration and sleep quality (person-level in this one-day design) and last-hour workload (single item from Amabile, Conti, Coon, Lazenby, & Herron, 1996).

Mediation in all three studies was tested with conditional indirect-effect models following Preacher and colleagues (Preacher, Zhang, & Zyphur, 2011; Preacher, Zyphur, & Zhang, 2010), estimating the within-person indirect effect of time of day on creativity at +1 SD and -1 SD of chronotype and supplemented by regions of significance, because the +/-1 SD values are sample specific and differ from the cut-offs for early and late chronotypes.

**Key Findings**
Chronotype moderated the effect of time of day on creativity in all three studies, but which half of the predicted crossover reached significance differed by study, and the mediation hypotheses received only partial support. The authors' own summary is that the synchrony effect "appeared most consistently for late chronotypes" and that positive mood mediated the effect only in Studies 2 and 3.
- H1 (moderation on creativity) - the cross-level interaction was significant in every study: Study 1 on the alternative uses test composite (estimate = 0.12, SE = 0.04, t = 2.79, p < .01), Study 2 (estimate = 0.07, SE = 0.03, t = 2.70, p < .01), and Study 3 (estimate = 0.03, SE = 0.01, t = 2.53, p < .05). The conditional halves were asymmetric and not consistent across studies. H1b was supported and H1a not supported in Study 1 (later chronotypes at +1 SD more creative in the late afternoon, estimate = 0.17, SE = 0.07, t = 2.48, p < .05; earlier chronotypes' creativity did not differ, estimate = -0.10, SE = 0.07, t = -1.47, p = .142) and in Study 2 (increase for later chronotypes, estimate = 0.12, SE = 0.04, t = 3.13, p < .01; earlier chronotypes stable, estimate = -0.02, SE = 0.04, t = -0.64, p = .525). Study 3 reversed the pattern: H1a was supported (earlier chronotypes more creative in the morning, estimate = -0.18, SE = 0.07, t = -2.65, p < .01) and H1b was not (later chronotypes, estimate = 0.07, SE = 0.07, t = 0.93, p = .350).
- H1 facets and replication in Study 1 - the moderation held for creative fluency (estimate = 0.13, SE = 0.05, t = 2.45, p < .05) and cognitive flexibility (estimate = 0.14, SE = 0.06, t = 2.28, p < .05), but for originality only at the 10% level (estimate = 0.10, SE = 0.05, t = 1.88, p = .060). In Group 2's supplementary analysis, the moderation replicated on self-rated work-related creativity (estimate = 0.34, SE = 0.13, t = 2.62, p < .01), again with H1b supported (later chronotypes, estimate = 0.83, SE = 0.20, t = 4.21, p < .001) and H1a not supported (earlier chronotypes, estimate = 0.06, SE = 0.19, t = 0.32, p = .745).
- H2 (moderation on positive mood) - significant in all three studies: Study 1 (estimate = 0.11, SE = 0.05, t = 2.42, p < .05), Study 2 (estimate = 0.05, SE = 0.01, t = 3.28, p < .01), Study 3 (estimate = 0.08, SE = 0.01, t = 6.84, p < .001). H2a was supported in all three (Study 1 estimate = -0.38, SE = 0.07, t = -5.21, p < .001; Study 2 estimate = -0.08, SE = 0.02, t = -3.79, p < .001; Study 3 estimate = -0.24, SE = 0.06, t = -3.90, p < .001). H2b was not supported in Study 1 (later chronotypes' mood did not differ, estimate = -0.13, SE = 0.07, t = -1.80, p = .071), received partial support in Study 2 (flat at +1 SD, estimate = 0.02, SE = 0.02, t = 0.96, p = .336, but increasing for chronotypes later than +1.87 SD), and was supported in Study 3 (estimate = 0.36, SE = 0.06, t = 5.79, p < .001).
- H3 (mediation via positive mood) - not supported in Study 1 for the alternative uses test, where positive mood was not a significant within-person predictor of creativity (b path estimate = 0.02, SE = 0.06, t = 0.27, p = .787) and neither conditional indirect effect was significant. In Group 2's work-related-creativity analysis, positive mood did predict creativity (b path estimate = 0.80, SE = 0.16, t = 5.11, p < .001) and H3a was supported (indirect effect for earlier chronotypes = -0.30, SE = 0.08, t = -3.65, p < .001) while H3b was not (later chronotypes = -0.10, SE = 0.06, t = -1.70, p = .089). Study 2 supported H3a (indirect effect for earlier chronotypes = -0.085, SE = 0.023, t = -3.77, p < .001) and gave H3b partial support (not significant at +1 SD, estimate = 0.023, SE = 0.024, t = 0.96, p = .338, but positive for chronotypes later than +1.89 SD). Study 3 supported both halves: the indirect effect via positive mood was positive for later chronotypes (estimate = 0.064, SE = 0.026, p = .015) and negative for earlier chronotypes (estimate = -0.043, SE = 0.020, p = .028).
- H4 and H5 (creative self-efficacy, tested in Study 3 only) - chronotype moderated the effect of time of day on creative self-efficacy (estimate = 0.05, SE = 0.02, t = 3.39, p < .01). H4a was supported (earlier chronotypes higher in the morning, estimate = -0.40, SE = 0.08, t = -4.89, p < .001) and H4b was not (later chronotypes, estimate = -0.01, SE = 0.08, t = -0.09, p = .932). Creative self-efficacy predicted creativity within person (estimate = 0.16, SE = 0.05, t = 2.94, p < .01), and the indirect effect was negative for earlier chronotypes (estimate = -0.063, SE = 0.025, p = .012) but nonsignificant for later chronotypes because the a path was nonsignificant, so H5a was supported and H5b was not. With both indirect effects in the model, the direct effect (c') of time of day on creativity was nonsignificant.
- Robustness - the pattern of results did not change when day-specific sleep quality and sleep duration were included in any study; neither was a significant predictor of creativity in Studies 1 or 2, which lets the authors rule out the explanation that late chronotypes simply slept longer or better before the late-afternoon session. In Study 2, day-specific time pressure and job control were significant positive predictors of creativity at both levels but did not change the pattern; in Study 3, workload was positively related to creativity (Level 1 estimate = 0.12, SE = 0.03, t = 3.60, p < .001; Level 2 estimate = 0.22, SE = 0.07, t = 3.20, p < .01) without altering the pattern, and sleep quality explained morning-to-afternoon differences in positive mood and creative self-efficacy over and above chronotype while chronotype remained a significant cross-level moderator. In Study 1, group membership predicted neither creativity nor positive mood, and conditional indirect effects for the two groups did not differ from the combined sample.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends creativity research by showing that within-person variation in creativity and its cognitive and affective antecedents are grounded in the current state of the body, calling for theories of creativity to attend to the mind–body interface from an embodied cognition framework. It contributes to the two-process model of sleep regulation by expanding its scope to creativity in a high-stakes work context and specifying affective (positive mood) and cognitive (creative self-efficacy) mechanisms, and shows that chronobiological drivers explain within-day fluctuations in work performance over and above day-specific sleep quality and duration.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that employees' ability to be creative is not a time-invariant trait but varies over the day and differs across individuals' chronotypes. Organizations should give employees autonomy to schedule creativity-demanding tasks for their state of synchrony (shielding that time from low-value meetings) while assigning routine tasks to other times; employees also need awareness of their own chronobiological preferences to make "wise" scheduling decisions.

**Limitations**
Creativity was operationalized via an objective alternative uses test and self-rated creativity, and the self-report measures may be subject to social-desirability and recollection biases (Study 2 used day reconstruction). Extreme chronotypes were underrepresented because start- and end-of-work times had to be set as participation requirements, yielding a conservative test, and Studies 1 and 3 took only two daily "snapshots" of creativity rather than continuously monitoring its temporal dynamics. The studies also neglected the social environment in which employees are creative.

**Future Research**
Future work should disentangle and directly measure the executive functions assumed to underlie the synchrony effect, examine why late chronotypes' creativity depends less on positive mood (e.g., links to thinking/decision-making styles and right-hemisphere processing), and capture the temporal dynamics of creativity across the entire workday without relying on employee recollection. Researchers should also extend measurement to earlier and later times of day, integrate the joint impact of the synchrony effect and sleep, and study how embodied processes interact with the social context.
