# The Distinct Effects of Wealth- and CSR-Oriented Shareholder Unrest on CEO Career Outcomes: A New Lens on Settling Up and Executive Job Demands

**Abstract**
Conceptualizing shareholder unrest as the aggregate scale and gravity of shareholder dissatisfaction with company practices, we examine the effects of two distinct types of shareholder unrest—wealth-oriented and corporate social responsibility (CSR)-oriented unrest—on CEOs’ career outcomes. Drawing on the concept of settling up, we hypothesize that CEOs are negatively sanctioned for both types of unrest in terms of reduced pay and increased likelihood of dismissal. Drawing on executive job demands theory, we argue that incumbent CEOs feel burdened by both types of unrest, leading them to voluntarily depart, and that new CEOs of unrest-laden firms receive larger pay packages as compensation for dealing with these inherited job demands. Overarching all these expectations, we argue that the effects of CSR-oriented unrest will be greater than the effects of wealth-oriented unrest, as CSR-oriented unrest occurs in a domain where alternative performance indicators are few and noisy, causing CSR-oriented unrest to be seemingly more meaningful. Testing our ideas on a longitudinal sample of U.S. corporations, we find considerable but not total support for our hypotheses, which we then interpret and discuss.

**Research Question**
How do two distinct forms of shareholder unrest—wealth-oriented and CSR-oriented—differentially affect CEOs' career outcomes (incumbent pay adjustments, dismissal, voluntary departure, and new-CEO pay), and which form is more consequential for boards versus CEOs themselves?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1a. The greater the recent CSR-oriented shareholder unrest, the less favorable the CEO's current pay adjustment.
H1b. The greater the recent wealth-oriented shareholder unrest, the less favorable the CEO's current pay adjustment.
H2a. The greater the recent CSR-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the likelihood of CEO dismissal.
H2b. The greater the recent wealth-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the likelihood of CEO dismissal.
H3. The relationship between recent CSR-oriented unrest and the CEO's current pay adjustment is more strongly negative than the relationship between recent wealth-oriented unrest and that pay adjustment.
H4. The relationship between recent CSR-oriented unrest and the likelihood of CEO dismissal is more strongly positive than the relationship between recent wealth-oriented unrest and that likelihood.
H5a. The greater the recent CSR-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the likelihood of CEO voluntary departure.
H5b. The greater the recent wealth-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the likelihood of CEO voluntary departure.
H6. The relationship between recent CSR-oriented unrest and the likelihood of CEO voluntary departure is more strongly positive than the relationship between recent wealth-oriented unrest and that likelihood.
H7a. The greater the recent CSR-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the compensation of a new CEO.
H7b. The greater the recent wealth-oriented shareholder unrest, the greater the compensation of a new CEO.
H8. The relationship between pre-succession CSR-oriented unrest and the compensation of a new CEO is more strongly positive than the relationship between pre-succession wealth-oriented unrest and that compensation.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CSR-oriented shareholder unrest; wealth-oriented shareholder unrest (each a standardized composite of four indicators—number of resolutions, aggregate voting support, distinct institutional-investor sponsors, and ISS endorsements—coded over the two prior years)
- DV(s): Incumbent CEO pay change; CEO dismissal; CEO voluntary departure; new CEO compensation (TDC1)
- Mediators: None formally tested (informativeness / job-demands logic is theorized, not modeled)
- Moderators: None; the central comparative claim is the difference between the two unrest coefficients (Wald tests)

The paper positions settling up and executive job demands as "two sides of the same coin." Under settling up, boards make post hoc pay and retention decisions after observing shareholder unrest, sanctioning CEOs blamed for unrest on their watch; because CSR-oriented unrest arises in a domain where alternative performance metrics are few and noisy, it conveys more distinct information and so should provoke stronger board sanctions. Under executive job demands, both forms of unrest add quantitative and qualitative load that pushes incumbents to voluntarily depart and forces firms to pay successors a premium. Empirically, boards sanctioned CSR-oriented unrest (pay cuts and dismissal) but not wealth-oriented unrest, whereas CEOs voluntarily departed in response to wealth-oriented unrest but not CSR-oriented unrest—revealing a divergence in the indicators boards and CEOs weight. New-CEO pay rose equally with both forms of pre-succession unrest.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: Standard and Poor's 1500 firms, 2005-2016. Compensation from Execucomp, financial and market performance from Compustat and CRSP, institutional-investor data from Thomson Reuters, shareholder-resolution data from Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS), and CSR performance from KLD. Dismissal codings were taken from Peters and Wagner (2014) and Jenter and Kanaan (2015), who followed Parrino (1997) in triangulating media reports, executive profiles, and web searches.
- IV(s): CSR-oriented and wealth-oriented shareholder unrest, each built from four indicators measured over the two years before the focal year—number of resolutions filed (including those withdrawn or omitted), aggregate support (percentage of votes cast in favor, summed across all resolutions voted on), number of distinct institutional-investor sponsors, and number of resolutions endorsed by ISS. The four indicators cohered strongly (Cronbach's alpha = .90 for CSR-oriented and .91 for wealth-oriented unrest), so each set was standardized and averaged into a composite index.
- DV(s): incumbent CEO pay change = percentage change in the CEO's annual total compensation (Execucomp TDC1: salary, bonus, restricted stock, Black-Scholes-valued options, and other income) from t-1 to t; CEO dismissal = binary, coded from media-triangulated departure events partitioned by age-based retirement proneness; CEO voluntary departure = binary, derived from the same departure coding after excluding preplanned or mandatory retirements (CEOs aged 65 or older) and departures attributable to health or death; new CEO compensation = first-year TDC1, prorated when the CEO joined mid-year, with interim CEOs excluded.
- Controls: firm size (logged net sales), diversification (Palepu's 1985 entropy measure), ROA, TSR, market-to-book ratio, Altman's Z, CSR performance (KLD strengths minus concerns across employee relations, diversity, product quality, environment, and community relations), CEO duality, board outsider ratio, independent directors' shareholdings, board size, board turnover, institutional ownership, inherited CSR- and wealth-oriented unrest, CEO tenure, CEO age, CEO credentials, insider status, and CEO shareholdings, plus calendar-year and 4-digit GICS industry fixed effects in all models. Pay change, prior-year pay change, CEO shareholdings, TSR, ROA, and market-to-book were winsorized at the 3% level.
- Identification: associational, not causal. GEEs estimate the three incumbent-CEO outcomes and OLS estimates new CEO pay, with firm-level clustering of error terms and robust standard errors. Omitted-variable concerns are addressed with impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable (ITCV) calculations and with 2SLS instrumental-variable models using median industry and median state unrest as instruments; because the Durbin-Wu-Hausman tests were not significant, the authors conclude that the unrest variables are not endogenous, "making the 2SLS regression unnecessary."

**Key Findings**
- Settling up (board-side outcomes): H1a supported—CSR-oriented unrest was negatively and significantly related to incumbent CEO pay change (b = -1.561, p < .01), a pay penalty; H1b not supported—wealth-oriented unrest was not significant (b = -0.441). H2a supported—CSR-oriented unrest raised the likelihood of dismissal (b = 0.156, p < .05), with its marginal effect positive and significant over the full range of the variable; H2b rejected—wealth-oriented unrest was not significant. H3 supported: the Wald test of the difference between the two pay coefficients was significant (χ² = 3.91, p < .05). H4 received only marginal support: the corresponding Wald difference for dismissal reached significance only at p < .10 (χ² = 2.92), which the authors describe as lending some support.
- Executive job demands (CEO-side outcome): H5a not supported—CSR-oriented unrest was not significant for voluntary departure; H5b strongly supported—wealth-oriented unrest was positive and significant (b = 0.165, p < .01) over the full range of the variable. H6 not supported: the Wald difference was significant (χ² = 4.7, p < .05) but ran opposite to the prediction, with wealth-oriented rather than CSR-oriented unrest exerting the stronger effect.
- New CEO pay: H7a and H7b both supported—pre-succession CSR-oriented and wealth-oriented unrest were each positively and significantly associated with new CEO pay (b = 0.144 and b = 0.135, p < .01). H8 not supported: the Wald test showed the difference between the two coefficients was not significant.
- Reported effect sizes: a high (+1 SD) level of CSR-oriented unrest increases the likelihood of CEO dismissal by 9.8% relative to the average level, and new CEOs entering firms with high (+1 SD) pre-succession CSR-oriented and wealth-oriented unrest are paid, on average, 8.1% and 8.9% more respectively than peers joining firms with average unrest. The authors summarize the overall pattern as considerable but not total support: boards act on CSR-oriented unrest, whereas CEOs themselves act on wealth-oriented unrest.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study elevates "shareholder activism" (the binary filing of a resolution) to "shareholder unrest"—a continuous, multifaceted construct capturing the scale and gravity of dissatisfaction—and develops a novel index to measure it. It advances settling-up theory by showing boards weight non-financial, CSR-related information more heavily than wealth-related information when its incremental informativeness is high, and it extends executive job demands theory by surfacing a board–CEO divergence in attended indicators and a predecessor–successor linkage in which inherited unrest buoys new-CEO pay.

**Practical Implication**
The paper offers no practical-implications section. Its findings indicate that boards treat CSR-oriented shareholder unrest as informative about CEO ability, reducing pay and raising dismissal likelihood, and that successor CEOs hired into firms with prior CSR-oriented unrest command higher pay, which the authors interpret as compensation for heightened job demands.

**Limitations**
Not reported in paper. The paper contains no limitations section. Its own caveats are methodological: for binary dependent variables a significant coefficient is insufficient "to infer the true nature of the relationship," so marginal effects are examined; and because the Durbin-Wu-Hausman tests were not significant, the authors treat the unrest variables as not endogenous, "making the 2SLS regression unnecessary."

**Future Research**
Scholars could examine whether other publicly registered stakeholder assessments—media criticism, boycotts, Yelp/Glassdoor ratings—affect CEO labor-market outcomes, and whether CSR-oriented unrest becomes less informative as CSR metrics improve. Promising directions include longer-term career consequences of unrest, effects on directors themselves, and spillover effects on the CEOs of non-targeted neighboring firms.
