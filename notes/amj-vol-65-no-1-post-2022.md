# What Changes after Women Enter Top Management Teams? A Gender-Based Model of Strategic Renewal

**Abstract**
The question of what changes when women enter upper-echelons teams has long frustrated upper echelons and gender researchers. We build on the dynamic strategic renewal literature, combine it with upper echelons theory insights, and integrate knowledge about female executives’ career strategies to theorize how and when female appointments into top management teams (TMTs) cause firms to change their approach to knowledge-related strategic renewal. In doing so, we reconcile the tension among extant mediating processes invoked to explain how female TMT representation might affect strategic decisions: change orientation and risk-taking propensity. Estimating a dynamic ordinary least squares model on panel data from 163 multinationals, we find that following female (but not male) TMT appointments, TMT cognitions shift, becoming more change oriented and less risk seeking. Subsequently, these TMT cognitive shifts cause a decrease in mergers and acquisitions and an increase in research and development. Our model of female TMT appointments as catalysts that cause shifts in TMT cognitions, which, in turn, redirect knowledge-related strategic renewal from a buying to a building approach, is a novel effort at advancing research on women at upper echelons to examine time-dependent, within-firm mechanisms linking women in upper echelons and firm outcomes.

**Research Question**
How and when do female (versus male) appointments into top management teams cause firms to change their approach to knowledge-related strategic renewal? The paper asks what changes after women enter TMTs, moving the female-TMT–firm-outcome literature from descriptive ("What is the link?") to explanatory ("How does the linkage occur?") by specifying time-dependent, within-firm mechanisms.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: When TMT appointments occur within a firm, following female (but not male) TMT appointments, there is a subsequent increase in the TMT's change orientation.
- H2: When TMT appointments occur within a firm, following female (but not male) TMT appointments, there is a subsequent decrease in the TMT's risk-taking propensity.
- H3a/H3b: When TMT appointments occur within a firm, there is an interaction effect between female (but not male) TMT appointments and TMT female incumbency on the subsequent shifts in TMT cognitions; specifically, female TMT appointments combined with TMT female incumbency lead to a greater subsequent (a) increase in the TMT's change orientation and (b) decrease in the TMT's risk-taking propensity.
- H4a/H4b: The moderating effects of TMT female incumbency described in Hypotheses 3a and 3b are attenuated at high levels of TMT female incumbency.
- H5a/H5b: When TMT appointments occur within a firm, there is an interaction effect between female (but not male) TMT appointments and the size of the cohort of new TMT appointees on subsequent shifts in TMT cognitions; specifically, female TMT appointments combined with a small (compared to a large) cohort of new TMT appointees lead to a greater subsequent (a) increase in the TMT's change orientation and (b) decrease in the TMT's risk-taking propensity.
- H6: Following female TMT appointments, the greater the increase in TMT change orientation, the greater the subsequent increase in R&D.
- H7: Following female TMT appointments, the greater the decrease in TMT risk-taking propensity, the greater the subsequent decrease in M&A.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Female TMT appointments (count of newly appointed female executives in a given year); male TMT appointments as a comparison.
- DV(s): Within-firm change in renewal strategy — change in R&D (capability "building") and change in technology-based M&A activity (capability "buying").
- Mediators: Shifts in two TMT cognitions — increase in TMT change orientation and decrease in TMT risk-taking propensity (year-to-year, measured from letters to shareholders).
- Moderators: TMT female incumbency (number of incumbent women, with diminishing returns / threshold at four or more) and the relative size of the incoming cohort of new TMT appointees (smaller cohorts amplify the shifts).

Female appointments act as catalysts: because female executives' life experiences and accession paths to the C-suite favor transformative, change-oriented values while their heightened scrutiny and career-derailment risks favor caution, their entry (but not men's) raises TMT change orientation and lowers TMT risk-taking propensity. These cognitive shifts then redirect renewal from buying (M&A) toward building (R&D). Integration conditions (female incumbency, small cohorts) determine whether the unique values of female appointees are voiced, heard, and incorporated, drawing on social identity theory's logic that similar in-group members are more readily integrated.

**Data & Measures**
- Data: an unbalanced firm-year panel of 1,911 observations on 163 multinational firms headquartered in 20 OECD countries over 1998-2012, with firms observed on average 11.7 years (historical annual reports were missing for some firms in some years). For each industry the authors selected the firms with the largest sales presence in the European market, all actively involved in strategic innovation. Sources: annual reports to identify top executives plus BoardEx for their demographic and job-related information (information on over 4,000 TMT members); nearly 2,000 letters to shareholders (LTS); Compustat and Datastream for firm-level data; the Securities Data Company and Zephyr databases for M&A; the European Patent Office's PATSTAT for patent applications; ILOSTAT for female representation in senior and middle management; and the World Economic Forum (2015) report for the gender gap index. TMT membership was defined as holding the rank of vice president or above (chairman/chairperson, vice chairman, CEO, CFO, CTO, COO, executive vice president, senior vice president) or a board executive directorship.
- Mediators (TMT cognitions): TMT change orientation and TMT risk-taking propensity were measured by content-analyzing each firm's LTS with LIWC, counting the percentage of words capturing risk taking (e.g., audacious, precarious) and change orientation (e.g., create, transform) using dictionaries validated by Short, Broberg, Cogliser, and Brigham (2010) and by McClelland, Liang, and Barker (2010) respectively; both were first-differenced (t-1 to t) to capture cognition change, and censored at zero when a letter used no dictionary words. Validity checks: TMT risk-taking propensity is significantly positively related to firm risk (.07, p = .00) while TMT change orientation is not (-.04, p = .11); TMT change orientation correlates .71 (p = .00) with a separately coded innovation-orientation measure but risk-taking propensity does not (-.01, p = .69); and LIWC and CAT Scanner versions of the two measures correlate .93 and .97.
- DVs (renewal strategy): within-firm change in M&A activity is the first difference (from t to t+1) of the annual count of technology-based M&A, that is, targets with patent activities; within-firm change in R&D is the yearly growth of the focal firm's R&D stock, with knowledge capital constructed by the perpetual inventory method using a 15% depreciation rate, so the DV captures growth of the R&D stock beyond replenishment and depreciation.
- IVs: female and male TMT appointments are the counts of female and male executives who represent new additions to the TMT in a given year.
- Moderators: TMT female incumbency is the total number of women on a TMT, lagged one year; the relative size of the cohort of incoming TMT appointees is total male plus female new TMT appointments during a year divided by the number of TMT members in the previous year, split at the sample mean of 0.14 into small and large cohort subsamples.
- Controls (all in first differences unless noted): CEO tenure, CEO age, TMT social stratification, TMT nationality diversity (Blau index), TMT size, female board representation, LTS word count, firm size (log of employees), R&D intensity (R&D expenditures/sales), degree of product diversification (Blau index of product segmentation), country-level women in management, the gender gap index, and 12 industry dummies.
- Identification, in the paper's own terms: dynamic OLS on the unbalanced panel, controlling for unobserved heterogeneity with fixed industry effects and by first-differencing all time-varying explanatory variables, each lagged one year; past levels of TMT cognitions are included so cognition shifts are modeled as a partial adjustment process. Moderation (H3, H5) is tested by estimating the cognition models on subsamples (zero vs. positive female incumbency; small vs. large cohort) and comparing coefficients with Wald and Chow tests rather than multiplicative interaction terms, while H4 uses threshold interactions with incumbency dummies. Mediation (H6, H7) uses "a standard instrumental variable approach" whose exclusion restrictions are the past levels of cognition: the predicted cognition shifts from Models 3 and 4 are entered in the M&A and R&D models, with bias-corrected confidence intervals for the indirect effects and an SEM re-estimation as a robustness check. The evidence base is observational secondary panel data; the authors treat this design as licensing causal inference in a panel setting.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported (Table 2, Model 3): female TMT appointments increase TMT change orientation (b = 0.051, p = .018) while male TMT appointments do not (b = -0.003, p = .479), and a Wald test indicates the two coefficients differ (p = .017).
- H2 supported (Table 2, Model 4): the female-appointment coefficient on TMT risk-taking propensity is negative and significant (b = -0.009, p = .025) while the male coefficient is not (b = -0.001, p = .361), though the Wald test on the difference is only marginally significant (p = .079). The estimates imply that female TMT appointments on average decrease TMT propensity to take risks by 13.5% and increase TMT change orientation by 10.2%.
- H3a: the pattern is consistent with the hypothesis but the across-subsample test is not. Female appointments raise change orientation marginally when female incumbency is zero (b = 0.042, p = .093) and significantly when it is positive (b = 0.059, p = .035), where the female-versus-male difference also becomes significant (p = .029); however, the Chow test comparing the female-appointment effect across the two subsamples is not significant (p = .60).
- H3b supported: with positive female incumbency the female-appointment coefficient on risk-taking propensity is significant (b = -0.013, p = .003), whereas at zero incumbency both female and male coefficients are insignificant (female b = 0.006, p = .557), and the Chow test across subsamples is marginally significant (p = .085); the implied effect is a 19.5% decrease in TMT risk-taking propensity.
- H4a not supported at conventional levels: in the change-orientation threshold model (Table 4, Model 9) female appointments shift change orientation when exactly one other woman is present (b = 0.14, p = .029), but interactions with the other incumbency dummies are insignificant and Wald tests cannot reject equality across them, so the evidence for diminishing returns "does not reach conventional levels of statistical significance."
- H4b partially supported: in the risk-taking model (Table 4, Model 10) female appointments shift risk-taking propensity when at least one other woman is present (b = -0.02, p = .038), the effect is largest in absolute value with exactly two female incumbents (b = -0.03, p = .005), and it loses statistical significance at four or more female incumbents, with the Wald test rejecting equality only for that last pair (chi-square = 5.76, p = .02). The authors caution that these threshold estimates rest on small samples.
- H5a: again the pattern is consistent but not statistically confirmed. The female-appointment coefficient on change orientation is larger and marginally significant in the small-cohort subsample (b = 0.10, p = .053) than the insignificant large-cohort coefficient (b = 0.03, p = .251), yet the Chow test comparing the two does not reach conventional significance (p = .211).
- H5b supported: the female-appointment coefficient on risk-taking propensity is significant in the small-cohort subsample (b = -0.02, p = .002) but not in the large-cohort subsample (b = -0.00, p = .501), and the difference in effect sizes across the two models is statistically significant (p = .029); the small-cohort estimate implies a 28.2% decrease in TMT risk-taking propensity.
- H6 supported (Table 6, Model 17): the predicted shift in TMT change orientation raises R&D (b = 0.06, p = .041), and a one-standard-deviation increase in TMT change orientation is estimated to increase R&D stock by 1.1%, which the authors call substantial given a sample mean R&D stock of 6,538 million.
- H7 supported (Table 6, Model 19): the predicted shift in TMT risk-taking propensity is positively related to change in M&A (b = 1.42, p = .016); a one-standard-deviation increase in risk-taking propensity corresponds to a 10.1% increase in the likelihood of an additional M&A, indicating conversely a reduced likelihood of engaging in more M&A activity for TMTs that have become less prone to risk taking.
- Moderated mediation: the indirect effects of female TMT appointments on R&D (via a rise in TMT change orientation) and on M&A (via a drop in TMT risk-taking propensity) are statistically significant when TMT female incumbency is positive (z = 0.003, p = .038; z = -0.019, p = .049) and only marginally significant for small incoming TMT cohorts (z = 0.006, p = .088; z = -0.032, p = .075). Citing the statistical difficulty of detecting moderated mediation in coarse-grained secondary data, the authors interpret the pattern as consistent with — not proof of — renewal effects operating at least partially via cognition shifts.
- Robustness: re-estimating on the subsample of firm-years in which TMT appointments occur does not suggest that endogeneity or selectivity biased the results; an SEM specification yields largely similar results, with only two of six estimated residual correlations even marginally significant; and substituting firm-specific risk and innovation orientation for the two cognition measures reproduces the pattern, with female TMT appointments significantly reducing firm-specific risk, especially where there is female incumbency.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study makes three contributions to research on women in upper echelons, upper echelons theory (UET), and strategic renewal. First, it establishes shifts in TMT cognitions as a dynamic, within-firm mechanism linking female TMT appointments to strategic change, replacing static demographic-proxy models with a temporal model of antecedents of cognition. Second, it reconciles the apparent contradiction between women's higher risk aversion and their greater propensity for change by showing the two operate on distinct cognitive channels (risk-taking propensity → M&A; change orientation → R&D). Third, it extends UET and strategic-renewal research by identifying TMT integration as a boundary condition and offering a new temporal understanding of critical mass (combining the flow of new female appointees with the stock of female incumbents).

**Practical Implication**
CEOs and boards should not treat appointing women as "pixie-dust" that automatically yields favorable outcomes; female appointees reshape strategy only when integrated into the TMT. CEOs and boards counting on a female appointee to prompt new ways of thinking may find the effort more successful if they commit to diversifying their TMT more and are more receptive to new female appointees, so that renewal shifts (more R&D, fewer M&A) materialize. The paper separately advises female executives invited to join an all-male TMT to ascertain the CEO's beliefs about female executives, since teams more readily incorporate female tokens' input when the team leader's gender beliefs are more positive.

**Limitations**
The model offers two non-exclusive explanations for cognition shifts (direct value transmission versus diversity-driven team-process change) that the data cannot disentangle. Threshold (diminishing-returns) estimates rest on small samples because female appointments to TMTs with existing female incumbents are rare, so they must be interpreted with caution. The study isolates only one appointee characteristic (gender), and its use of letters to shareholders as a proxy for TMT cognitions cannot fully parcel out impression-management content.

**Future Research**
Future work could identify other dynamic models linking female entry into upper echelons to firm decisions via cognition shifts — for example, female appointments' effects on TMT moral sensitivities and corporate social responsibility, or on TMT affective tone. Researchers could probe the processes through which shifts occur (e.g., whether stereotypical risk ascriptions versus actual gender differences drive change), extend the reasoning to other appointee dimensions (race, age, national origin, social class), develop additional moderators (managerial discretion, sociocultural context, demographic fault lines), and disentangle cognition from impression management in shareholder letters.
