# OMG! My Boss Just Friended Me: How Evaluations of Colleagues’ Disclosure, Gender, and Rank Shape Personal/Professional Boundary Blurring Online

**Abstract**
We propose and test a relational boundary-blurring framework, examining how employees’ evaluations of colleagues’ characteristics drive their decisions to connect with colleagues as friends online. We use a multi-method approach across four studies to investigate how self-disclosure of personal information, gender, and rank shape warmth evaluations of colleagues and subsequent boundary blurring decisions on online social networks such as Facebook. Study 1, a large archival study using a nationally representative sample, finds that connecting as friends with colleagues online is prevalent. Study 2, examining employees across several industries, shows that people experience connecting as friends with colleagues online as boundary blurring. Two experimental studies (Studies 3 and 4) ascertain that employees are more likely to connect as friends online with colleagues who engage in more (vs. less) self-disclosure and are less likely to connect with bosses (vs. peers). Further, self-disclosure, gender, and rank interact, such that employees are more likely to connect with female bosses who disclose more, compared to those who disclose less, and compared to male bosses, regardless of self-disclosure. Our work contributes to boundary management research by demonstrating that employees’ decisions to blur the personal/professional boundary online crucially depends on whom they are blurring the boundary with.

**Research Question**
How do employees’ evaluations of a colleague’s self-disclosure, gender, and rank — characteristics that convey warmth — shape their decisions to blur the personal/professional boundary by connecting as friends with that colleague online? The paper shifts boundary management from the focal employee to the relational partner, asking with whom employees choose to blur the boundary.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Employees are more likely to connect as a friend online with colleagues who disclose more (vs. less) personal information.
H1a. The positive relationship between a colleague's self-disclosure and likelihood of connecting as a friend with this colleague online is mediated by perceived warmth.
H2. Employees are more likely to connect as a friend online with female (vs. male) colleagues.
H2a. The positive relationship between female colleagues and likelihood of connecting as a friend with colleagues online is mediated by perceived warmth.
H3. Employees are more likely to connect as a friend online with colleagues of symmetric rank (i.e., peers) than with those of asymmetric rank (i.e., subordinates or bosses).
H3a. The positive relationship between symmetric rank and likelihood of connecting as a friend with colleagues online is mediated by perceived warmth.
H4. Self-disclosure interacts with gender such that employees are more likely to connect as friends online with female colleagues who disclose more rather than less personal information, while disclosure will be less consequential for connecting as friends online with male colleagues.
H5. Self-disclosure interacts with asymmetric rank such that employees are more likely to connect as friends online with bosses and subordinates who disclose more versus less personal information, and equally likely to connect as friends online with peers regardless of disclosure.
H6. Gender and asymmetric rank interact such that employees are least likely to connect as friends online with male colleagues of asymmetric rank compared to all other conditions, and are less likely to connect as friends online with female colleagues of asymmetric rank compared to male and female colleagues of symmetric rank (i.e., peers).
H7. Disclosure, rank, and gender interact such that employees are more likely to connect as friends online with female colleagues of asymmetric rank who disclose more compared to those who disclose less and compared to male colleagues of asymmetric rank regardless of disclosure; employees will also be less likely to connect to female colleagues of asymmetric rank who disclose more compared to female colleagues of symmetric rank who disclose more; but employees are more likely to connect as friends online with both male and female colleagues of symmetric rank who disclose more compared to those who disclose less.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Colleague's self-disclosure (more vs. less); colleague's gender (female vs. male); colleague's rank (symmetric/peer vs. asymmetric/boss or subordinate); their two- and three-way interactions.
- DV(s): Likelihood of connecting as a friend with the colleague online (Study 3: stated likelihood of accepting a request; Study 4: actual Facebook connection, dichotomous).
- Mediators: Perceived warmth of the colleague (Fiske et al., 2002 scale).
- Moderators: Self-disclosure moderates the gender x rank interaction; specifically, disclosure ameliorates the negative effect of asymmetric rank for female but not male bosses.

Drawing on social cognition and person perception research, the authors argue that people evaluate others first along warmth, and that more self-disclosure, female gender, and symmetric (peer) rank each signal warmth and benevolent intent, lowering the perceived relational risk of granting a colleague visible, permanent, less-tailored access to one's personal life online. Warmth thus mediates the disclosure effect consistently, while gender and rank operate more conditionally. The three-way interaction reflects that disclosure most strongly improves connection odds for female bosses, whose hierarchical status otherwise violates gender-role expectations, whereas male bosses of asymmetric rank remain least likely to be connected with regardless of disclosure.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (archival, associational): an archival data set of 2,003 American adults surveyed by the Pew Research Center (2014) about their internet use, restricted to working adults aged 18-70 (n = 899) and then to the 586 respondents who reported using Facebook (65%). Online "friendship" connection was measured with a binary item — "Thinking about who is in your Facebook network, are you Facebook friends with ... work colleagues" (yes = 1, no = 0) — alongside parallel items for family and past friends, number of Facebook friends, frequency of posting (1 = "never" to 4 = "frequently"), and frequency of Facebook and LinkedIn use (1 = "less often" to 5 = "several times a day"); demographics were gender, age, and employment status. Analyzed with Spearman correlations.
- Study 2 (survey, associational): 676 full-time U.S. employees recruited through the Lucid platform from three of the largest U.S. industries by employee size (retail or wholesale, health care or social assistance, leisure or hospitality), all with both bosses and subordinates; 146 attention-check failures and 17 reCAPTCHA v3 scores below 0.9 were excluded, leaving 513. Measures were the same binary Facebook colleague-connection item as Study 1 plus number of colleagues connected with and extent of sending, accepting, and rejecting requests (1-5 scales); three established boundary-management scales (Dumas et al., 2013 integration behaviors, a = .70; Desrochers et al., 2005 work-family integration, a = .69; Edwards & Rothbard, 1999 segmentation preferences, a = .89); two new online scales (the Desrochers et al. scale adapted for social media, a = .63; a new four-item Facebook blurring scale, a = .88); and ratings of whether each of 10 online and offline activities blurs personal and professional life (1 = "strongly disagree" to 5 = "strongly agree"). Analyzed with correlations, paired t-tests, and an exploratory factor analysis (principal axis factoring, oblimin rotation).
- Study 3 (randomized between-subjects experiment): 659 U.S. working adults with at least two years of work experience recruited from MTurk; 45 excluded for bot-like responses or duplicate/non-U.S. IP addresses, leaving 614 (49% female, average age 32, 12 years of work experience). A 2 x 2 x 3 design manipulated disclosure (less, more), gender (female "Emily Jacobs" vs. male "Greg Jacobs" name and pretested photo), and rank (boss, peer, subordinate) through a mock but realistic Facebook profile of a fictitious colleague. DV: a single item on how likely the participant would be to accept a friend request from this colleague (1 = "very unlikely" to 7 = "very likely"). Mediator: perceived warmth, three items from Fiske et al. (2002) — "This person is [friendly/well-intentioned/sincere]" (a = .84). Six manipulation-check items covered disclosure, rank, and gender. Analyzed with ANOVA and PROCESS Model 4 mediation using 5,000 bootstrap samples and bias-corrected confidence intervals; robustness models added age, participant gender, years of work experience, years on Facebook, and number of Facebook friends.
- Study 4 (randomized between-subjects experiment on recalled real colleagues): 1,288 participants recruited via MTurk (TurkPrime); after removing 60 for bot-like responses, sub-five-minute completion, failed screening or attention checks, 2 who listed only one colleague, 31 whose described colleague did not match the assigned gender or rank, 1 who guessed the study purpose, and 454 who recalled former rather than current colleagues, the final sample was 740 (61% female, mean age 39, 16 years full-time and four years part-time experience). An adapted critical incident technique randomly assigned participants to one of 12 cells of the same 2 x 2 x 3 design: they listed three real colleagues matching their assigned gender and rank, ranked them on how much they disclose about their personal lives, and then answered about the colleague matching their assigned disclosure condition. DV: the dichotomous behavioral item "Are you already connected with [AB] on Facebook?" (1 = yes, 0 = no). Mediator: Fiske et al.'s (2002) full six-item warmth scale — warm/good-natured/sincere/trustworthy/friendly/well intentioned (a = .94). Analyzed with logistic regression using condition dummy variables and the Stata margins command, reporting odds ratios for effect size, PROCESS Model 4 bootstrapped mediation (5,000 samples), and a multinomial logit separating request senders from receivers; robustness models added the Study 3 controls plus length of relationship, part-time status, and rater-coded closeness (Cohen's k = .88, 36% coded close).

Studies 3 and 4 use random assignment to manipulated disclosure, gender, and rank, which the authors describe as enabling causal inference; Studies 1 and 2 are correlational and, as the paper states, were not designed to test the hypotheses.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (prevalence, descriptive): of working adults using Facebook, 66% were connected with colleagues, 96% with family, and 89% with friends from the past — establishing that connecting with colleagues online is prevalent. Older employees had fewer Facebook friends (r = -.43, p < .01) and used Facebook less (r = -.18, p < .01); women posted more (r = .11, p < .05) and used LinkedIn less (r = -.17, p < .01); full-time employees used Facebook less (r = -.10, p < .05) but were more connected with colleagues (r = .11, p < .05).
- Study 2 (boundary-blurring assumption): 79% of Facebook users were connected as friends with colleagues. Connecting with colleagues on Facebook (M = 3.51) was rated as blurring as employee-initiated social events (M = 3.51) and talking about personal life during work hours (M = 3.53), and significantly more blurring than talking about work after hours (M = 3.12) or company-sponsored social activities (M = 3.07); connecting on LinkedIn was rated lowest of these (M = 2.73). The binary colleague-connection measure was positively correlated with both established integration scales (r = .18 and r = .14, p < .01) and both new online blurring scales (r = .22 and r = .23, p < .01), and an EFA yielded two factors on which Facebook colleague connection loaded .61 with three offline blurring behaviors while LinkedIn connection loaded .56 on the interactive-professional factor.
- Study 3 (n = 614): H1 supported — main effect of disclosure, F(1, 609) = 9.27, p < .01, with more-disclosing colleagues more likely to be accepted (M = 4.95 vs. M = 4.53, Cohen's d = .23). H3 supported — main effect of rank, F(2, 609) = 14.60, p < .01, peers (M = 5.27) preferred to subordinates (M = 4.57, d = .41) and bosses (M = 4.37, d = .51). H2 not supported — no main effect of gender, F(1, 609) = 1.58, p = .21. H1a supported (positive indirect effect of more disclosure via warmth = .17, 95% CI [0.08, 0.28]); H2a partially supported (indirect effect of gender = .07, 95% CI [0.01, 0.14]) despite the null main effect; H3a not supported (the confidence intervals for the indirect effects of asymmetric rank included zero). The three two-way interactions were all non-significant, so H4 (F = .78, p = .38), H5 (F = .10, p = .91), and H6 (F = 1.02, p = .36) were not supported. H7 supported — the three-way interaction was significant, F(2, 602) = 4.39, p < .05: more disclosure ameliorated the negative effect of asymmetric rank for female but not male bosses (female boss more = 4.88 vs. less = 4.00, d = .45; male boss 4.33 vs. 4.25, d = .04); disclosing female bosses were still less likely to be accepted than more-disclosing female peers (5.66, d = .45) but similarly likely as less-disclosing female peers (4.85, d = .02); in the subordinate conditions disclosure did not shift acceptance for women (4.81 vs. 4.79, d = .01) but did for men (4.83 vs. 3.91, d = .52); and disclosure raised acceptance for female peers (5.66 vs. 4.85, d = .49) but not male peers (5.23 vs. 5.35, d = .08).
- Study 4 (n = 740; 50% were connected to the recalled colleague): H1 supported — Wald = 53.18, p < .01, with almost three times the odds of connection for more- versus less-disclosing colleagues (63% vs. 37%, OR = 2.96, p < .01). H2 supported in this study — Wald = 12.76, p < .01, almost twice the odds for female (57%) versus male colleagues (44%), OR = 1.69, p < .01. H3 partially supported — employees were significantly less likely to be connected to bosses (37%) than peers (59%), OR = .41, p < .01, and than subordinates (51%), OR = .56, p < .01, but peers and subordinates did not differ (OR = 1.38, p = .07). H1a supported (indirect effect of disclosure = .19, 95% CI [0.10, 0.30]); H3a partially supported (significant negative indirect effect of asymmetric rank for bosses, -.15, 95% CI [-0.29, -0.04], but not subordinates, .06, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.17]); H2a not supported (the confidence interval for the indirect effect of gender included zero). The two-way interactions were again all non-significant, so H4 (Wald = 1.10, p = .29), H5 (Wald = 3.12, p = .21), and H6 (Wald = 1.47, p = .48) were not supported. H7 supported — the three-way interaction was significant (Wald_male x peer x less = 7.73, p < .01; Wald_male x sub x less = 7.52, p < .01), again because more disclosure ameliorated the negative effect of asymmetric rank for female but not male bosses: employees had eight times the odds of being connected with female bosses who disclosed more (64%) than less (18%), OR = 8.33, p < .01, and about four times the odds relative to male bosses who disclosed more (33%, OR = 3.70, p < .01) or less (30%, OR = 4.24, p < .01), with no differences among those latter three conditions. Disclosing female bosses were less likely to be connected with than disclosing female peers (81%, OR = .43, p < .05) but similarly likely as female peers who disclosed less (52%, OR = 1.66, p = .17). For subordinates the pattern differed: disclosure doubled the odds of connecting with female subordinates (65% vs. 47%, OR = 2.11, p < .05) and almost tripled them for male subordinates (59% vs. 34%, OR = 2.74, p < .01); disclosure also raised the odds for male peers (69% vs. 30%, OR = 5.30, p < .01) and female peers (81% vs. 52%, OR = 3.88, p < .01).
- Across the two experiments: the disclosure main effect (H1), the lower likelihood of connecting with bosses than peers, and the H7 three-way interaction replicated in both, with the disclosure effect stronger in Study 4. The pattern was heterogeneous elsewhere — the main effect of gender (H2) held only in Study 4, warmth mediation of gender (H2a) only in Study 3, warmth mediation of asymmetric rank (H3a) only in Study 4 and only for bosses, and Study 3 found peers preferred to subordinates whereas Study 4 found peers and subordinates equally likely. Robustness checks indicated that disclosure, gender, and rank shaped both sending and accepting requests similarly.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces a relational boundary-blurring framework that reorients boundary management theory from the characteristics of the focal employee to evaluations of the relational partner, showing that boundary blurring is decided relationship by relationship rather than uniformly within a domain. It extends social cognition and status-characteristics research by demonstrating that warmth-conveying cues (disclosure, gender, rank) are not equivalent in their effects on boundary blurring, and that gender and rank are less consistently mediated by warmth than disclosure is. It also theorizes online social networks as an extreme case that amplifies the visibility, permanence, and relational risks of boundary blurring.

**Practical Implication**
Employees should recognize that online connection decisions may rest on stereotypes about colleagues' benevolence (especially regarding gender and rank) and deliberate more carefully about the risks and rewards of connecting; moderate, noncontroversial disclosure may suffice to signal warmth. Bosses should recognize that subordinates are often hesitant to connect and avoid initiating requests that create discomfort. Because reinforcing gender-role stereotypes can place disclosing female bosses at greater risk, organizations should avoid one-size-fits-all policies and pursue training and safeguards that protect privacy without perpetuating gender discrimination in workplace social connection.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 are correlational and not designed to test the hypotheses. Study 3 maximized control using fictitious profiles but lacked realism, while Study 4 used an adapted critical-incident recall design that gained external validity but introduced recall error and could not firmly establish that disclosure precedes the connection decision. Both experiments relied on single-item dependent measures and a shortened warmth scale in Study 3, and the main effect of gender varied across studies.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine the amount and type of online versus offline disclosure more extensively, including appropriate versus politicized or stigmatized content and the role of privacy settings, and further establish the temporal precedence of real-world disclosure. Scholars should investigate other status characteristics such as race and their interaction with gender and rank, the persistence of gender inequalities on OSNs (e.g., whether connections help or harm disclosing female bosses), downstream consequences of online boundary blurring for performance and offline relationships, and extensions to other platforms such as Twitter, Instagram, and WeChat.
