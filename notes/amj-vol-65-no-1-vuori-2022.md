# Regulating Top Managers’ Emotions during Strategy Making: Nokia’s Socially Distributed Approach Enabling Radical Change from Mobile Phones to Networks in 2007–2013

**Abstract**
Strategy making can be emotional for decision makers, especially when facing a major threat or a disruptive change. Yet, we know little about whether and how strategic decision makers’ emotions are regulated and how such regulation influences strategy making. Based on a longitudinal study of Nokia from 2007 to 2013, we develop a process model of socially distributed emotion regulation. This model shows how various organizational groups help regulate top managers’ emotions. Top managers contain their initial emotional reactions to strategic options thanks to activities performed by groups with power over top managers. This enables top managers to form data-informed reappraisals of strategic options, contributing to gradual changes in their emotions. The reappraisal process is aided by diverse groups performing distinct roles. Top managers’ revised emotions, in turn, enable them to form new, iterative data-informed reappraisals and ultimately enable radical strategic change. Our study contributes to research on emotions and strategy making by showing how socially distributed emotion regulation operates during strategy making and influences its outcomes. We contribute to the cognitive perspective on strategy by showing how cognition and emotion interact over time during strategy making.

**Research Question**
(a) Whether and how are strategic decision makers’ emotions regulated during strategy making in large organizations? (b) How does this regulation influence the strategy-making process?

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: A strategic situation (a major threat or disruptive change) triggers intense emotional reactions among top managers, activating action tendencies that can produce myopic, rigid, and biased behavior and strategic inertia.
- Structural affordances (regulating groups): Governance structures (board of directors → power to dictate norms/behaviors), advisory structures (analytical teams/consultants → impartial, specialized cognitive capacity), and boundary-spanning structures (potential partner firms → capacity to increase the salience/vividness of options).
- Three regulation mechanisms: (1) increasing top managers’ impulse control (containing initial emotions via formal practices and psychological safety), (2) adding emotionally neutral cognitive capacity (enabling data-informed reappraisals), and (3) increasing the mental vividness of options (amplifying emotional reactions toward specific options).
- Enabling condition: A shared appreciation of emotion regulation (born of the negative experience of emotional inertia in Period 1) makes top managers react favorably to others’ regulation efforts.
- Emergent outcome: A recursive loop in which contained initial emotions → wider/deeper search → data-informed reappraisals → gradually changing emotions → still deeper search, ultimately enabling a radical strategic choice the managers initially disdained.

Across two periods, unregulated emotional reactions (denial, avoidance of the iPhone/Android threat) drove strategic inertia in Period 1, whereas in Period 2 various organizational groups, coordinated through formal structures, distributed the work of regulating top managers’ emotions so that those emotions changed qualitatively over weeks and months. Because the process is socially distributed, each group performs a sub-task with compartmentalized information, and the overall emotion-regulating effect emerges from their joint, complementary efforts rather than from any single leader’s individual emotional intelligence.

**Data & Measures**
Theory-elaborating qualitative single-case design comparing two periods of Nokia’s strategy making (2007–2010 and 2012–2013). Data corpus: real-time documents, public materials, 121 in-depth interviews, about 300 informal discussions, and 60 follow-up emails; the interviews were conducted between 2012 and 2018, lasted 68 minutes on average, and 112 were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim, with detailed notes taken for the remainder. The interviews comprised 9 with board members, 20 with top managers, 21 with others who held substantial strategy-making roles (e.g., senior vice presidents in strategically relevant areas), and 71 with other knowledgeable informants (external advisors, other senior vice presidents, and software and other middle managers), Table 1 reporting interview counts rather than distinct individuals; with one exception, the 77 interviews conducted before 2015 were also reported in Vuori and Huy (2016). Constructs were coded rather than measured. Open coding of transcripts and other data generated over 3,000 first-order codes, which were reduced to about 200 relevant codes and then grouped into second-order themes and aggregate dimensions in a separate data structure for each period (Figures 1a and 1b). Emotions were coded predominantly from informants’ own reports of their emotional states, with appraisal theories of emotion (Lazarus, 1991) used to check that the self-reported emotions were plausible and to identify their causes and changes. Emotion regulation was coded from (a) informants’ statements about attempts to influence their own or others’ emotions, (b) existing emotion-regulation coding frameworks (Huy & Zott, 2019; Kaplan et al., 2014), and (c) Gross’s (1998) five mechanisms: situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation. Thirty tables, 220 memos, and 160 diagrams were used to explicate the causal logic of the argumentation. The design is interpretive and theory-elaborating rather than hypothesis-testing, with no statistical identification strategy; ex post rationalization bias was addressed by triangulating real-time public and private evidence, asking informants to describe concrete behaviors and events, and having informants in different organizational roles describe the same events for cross-checking.

**Key Findings**
In Period 1 (2007–2010) unregulated emotions produced strategic inertia: the prospect of laying off thousands of engineers and middle managers evoked sadness, guilt, and anxiety, so top managers clung to the prevailing Symbian strategy and avoided the iPhone and Android threat, while exciting initiatives (the services and mapping businesses, the NSN joint venture, a large 2008 reorganization) absorbed their attention. The board maintained rather than contained those impulses, reacting aggressively to people who raised threats and holding no in-depth strategy dialogue; analytical teams were tasked only with improving the prevailing strategy, and top managers rejected consultants’ critiques. Performance declined and the board replaced the CEO in September 2010. In Period 2 (2012–2013) the same initial avoidance appeared when Windows phone sales fell short of targets, but a shared appreciation of emotion regulation, learned from the Period 1 failure and reinforced by the chairman appointed in 2012 and the new CEO, made top managers receptive to regulation by other groups. Three complementary mechanisms operated: the board increased impulse control through formal practices (interaction norms, public support for the CEO, psychological safety, a structured process that took issues in "digestible" pieces, and a "What keeps me up at night" board slide); internal analytical teams, strategy consultants, and an investment bank added emotionally neutral cognitive capacity, at times with two firms analyzing the same option; and negotiations and prototype work with potential partners increased the mental vividness of options. These produced data-informed reappraisals that ran against top managers’ initial preferences (the handset business was losing money and had negative value; no discounted cash flow model made Android look better; NSN could be bought below market price), and those reappraisals changed emotions gradually, which in turn enabled still wider and deeper search. Table 4 traces this recursive loop option by option across the four main options considered. The outcome was the two choices top managers had initially disdained: selling the phone business to Microsoft for $7.2B and acquiring full ownership of NSN from Siemens for $2.2B, with emotions toward both turning positive only after repeated analytical cycles; Nokia’s market valuation grew from about $6B in June 2012 to $25B by December 2013. The study concludes that emotion regulation during strategy making was socially distributed across groups occupying distinct structural positions and unfolded cumulatively over weeks and months rather than in a single episode.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper enriches work on emotions and strategy making by theorizing emotion regulation as socially distributed across organizational groups and enabled by formal organizational structures, transcending the individual-level limitations that dominate prior emotion-regulation research. It shows how emotion regulation unfolds cumulatively over time (rather than as a one-off episode), and extends the cognitive-processual perspective by demonstrating how emotions shape the framing of strategic issues and how containing initial emotions can later support more adaptive, data-informed reappraisals.

**Practical Implication**
Management teams and boards should recognize that their governance functions naturally arouse emotions that influence strategy making. The authors recommend three practices: create processes that contain initial emotional reactions by letting executives study challenges in "digestible parts"; require executives to form data-informed reappraisals by generating and analyzing multiple strategic options (using consultants and outside advisors); and concretize options through interactions with potential partners, while nurturing patience as emotions and thinking evolve gradually.

**Limitations**
The model is context-dependent: the arguments assume emotions have fairly universal influences on biology and cognition, yet context-specific factors may shape how emotions translate into behavior and whether they can be regulated. Nokia was headquartered in Finland and listed on the NYSE, so its board could actively influence top managers; in other contexts boards may face more constraints. The study also focuses on the emotion-regulation roles of boards, analytical teams, and potential partner firms within a single firm.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine how structural arrangements (e.g., industry norms, ownership structure) shape the occurrence and effectiveness of socially distributed emotion regulation, and how various structural positions elicit the emotions of different groups and influence their emotion-regulation behaviors. Researchers should also explore whether and how organizational groups beyond boards, analytical teams, and partner firms contribute to emotion regulation, and how the timing, sequencing, combining, and rhythm of the actions enacted affect the effectiveness of the overall emotion-regulation process, which the paper argues may matter as much as using any specific approach.
