# Glue or Gasoline? The Role of Interorganizational Linkages in the Occurrence and Spillover of Competitive Wars

**Abstract**
Despite their prevalence and significance, competitive wars have received limited attention in the strategy literature. Our knowledge of how interorganizational linkages influence competitive wars is particularly lacking. Drawing on the social embeddedness perspective, we argue that both direct linkages (i.e., strategic alliances) and indirect linkages (i.e., common ownership ties and common analyst ties) reduce the likelihood of war, thereby functioning as the glue that binds firms together. Yet once competitive wars are launched in related markets, indirect linkages through common third-parties may continuously function as glue, reducing the likelihood of war spillover, whereas direct linkages, such as strategic alliances, may facilitate the spillover of competitive wars, akin to adding gasoline to a fire. Using data from the U.S. domestic airline industry between 1991 and 2010, our empirical evidence offers strong support for our predictions.

**Research Question**
How do direct interorganizational linkages (strategic alliances) and indirect linkages (common ownership ties and common analyst ties) influence both the occurrence of competitive wars within a market and their spillover into a focal market once wars have broken out elsewhere?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The number of strategic alliances among incumbents of a market is negatively related to the likelihood of a competitive war occurring in that market.
- H2a: The extent of common ownership ties among incumbents in a market is negatively related to the likelihood of a competitive war occurring in that market.
- H2b: The extent of common analyst ties among incumbents in a market is negatively related to the likelihood of a competitive war occurring in that market.
- H3: The proportion of incumbents in a focal market that were at war in other markets during the previous period is positively related to the likelihood of a competitive war starting in that focal market.
- H4: The number of strategic alliances among incumbents in a market positively moderates the relationship between the proportion of incumbents at war in other markets and the likelihood of competitive war occurring in the focal market.
- H5a: The extent of common ownership ties among incumbents in a market negatively moderates the relationship between the proportion of incumbents at war in other markets and the likelihood of competitive war occurring in the focal market.
- H5b: The extent of common analyst ties among incumbents in a market negatively moderates the relationship between the proportion of incumbents at war in other markets and the likelihood of competitive war occurring in the focal market.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Strategic alliances among incumbents (direct/relational linkage); common ownership ties (indirect/structural linkage via shared institutional investors); common analyst ties (indirect/structural linkage via shared financial analysts).
- DV(s): War occurrence — a dichotomous indicator that a price war began in a market-period (actual prices >20% below expected prices).
- Mediators: None modeled (theorized mechanisms are trust/resource interdependence for direct ties and third-party mediation/sanctioning for indirect ties).
- Moderators: Incumbents at war in other markets in the prior period (the spillover trigger); the three linkage types each moderate the spillover-to-focal-market relationship — strategic alliances amplify it (gasoline), while common ownership ties and common analyst ties attenuate it (stronger glue).

Drawing on the social embeddedness perspective, the paper argues that direct alliances suppress war onset because repeated interaction builds trust and resource interdependence, while indirect ties suppress onset because impartial common third-parties (institutional investors, analysts) mediate conflicts and can sanction deviating firms via resource holdings or socioeconomic influence. Once a war erupts in related markets, however, the mechanisms diverge: alliance partners' aligned, complementary operations plus war-induced distrust make spillover more direct and severe (alliances become gasoline), whereas non-partisan third-parties, whose own interests are harmed by spreading wars, intensify their mediating and sanctioning roles (indirect ties become stronger glue). All five focal hypotheses were supported in the airline data.

**Data & Measures**
- Data sources: five archival sources covering 1991-2010 — the DOT Airline Origin & Destination Survey (DB1A/DB1B) to identify city-pair markets and calculate prices; Aviation Daily, from which the authors manually content-analyzed 37,149 articles to code alliance announcements (coding only arrangements that were confirmed or would take effect shortly after announcement, and excluding multiparty global alliances such as Star Alliance); the Thomson-Reuters Institutional Holdings (13F) Database for common ownership; IBES for analyst coverage; and Air Carrier Financial Reports (Form 41) for other financial and operating data. A market is a city-pair route served by direct or one-stop flights; an incumbent is a carrier with more than a 5% market share or minimum efficiency scale of capacity (900 travelers per quarter) in that market.
- DV, war occurrence: a dichotomous variable coded 1 when actual prices in a market are more than 20% below expected prices in a period and 0 otherwise, with only the first period of a war's commencement coded 1 and continuation periods coded 0. Actual price is measured by passenger yield (average price per revenue passenger mile); expected price is predicted by autoregressive models employing lagged pricing data from the previous eight quarters (model R-square 95.9%). A war is treated as ending in the first period after onset with a positive residual. The measure was validated against 1,327 price-war-related press articles identified in Factiva out of 19,809 airline-industry articles mentioning price or fare.
- IV, strategic alliances: the total number of alliance relationships among all incumbent dyads in a market each quarter, scaled by the total number of rival pairs in that market.
- IV, common ownership ties: the average overlap in institutional investors among incumbent airlines in a market, calculated as percent common ownership for each rival dyad (the minimum ownership percentage among all common investors where a dyad has more than one), summed across dyads and divided by the total number of dyads; the measure ranges from 0 to 1.
- IV, common analyst ties: the average number of financial analysts in common among incumbent airlines in a market, where an analyst counts as following a firm if it issued at least one earnings forecast for that firm in the previous 12 months; the number of common analysts per rival dyad is scaled by the mean number of analysts covering that dyad, then averaged across all rival pairs.
- Moderating trigger, incumbents at war in other markets: the percentage of incumbent dyads in the focal market that were at war in other markets in the previous period, where a dyad counts as at war elsewhere only when price wars occurred in at least 5% of its shared markets.
- Controls: demand instability (percentage change in predicted demand from a five-factor demand regression), fuel cost (market incumbents' average cost of fuel per mile), number of new entrants, market concentration (Herfindahl index), and market size (gravity model).

Estimation is conditional fixed-effects logistic regression on 112,210 market-period observations with city-pair market and period fixed effects and robust standard errors, chosen over probit on log-likelihood grounds; a Breusch-Pagan Lagrange multiplier test rejected zero market and zero period effects, and Hausman tests rejected random effects in favor of fixed effects (p < .01). The design is associational panel estimation: the fixed effects absorb period-invariant market characteristics and market-invariant period shocks, and the paper claims no instrument, experiment, or discontinuity.

**Key Findings**
All seven hypotheses were supported. Descriptively, the authors identified 4,086 price wars in 2,066 markets (about 1.97 per market, with 82% of markets having no more than three wars); post-war prices averaged approximately 90.1% of pre-war levels and about 77.2% of wars ended with prices below the pre-war level.
- H1 supported: strategic alliances are negatively related to war occurrence (coefficient -0.33, p < .01). A market one standard deviation above the mean is 14.3% less likely to experience a war than one a standard deviation below, and 46.8% less likely comparing zero alliances with the sample maximum.
- H2a supported: common ownership ties are negatively related to war occurrence (-1.83, p < .001). High versus low is 27.3% less likely, rising to 56.1% comparing zero with full common ownership.
- H2b supported: common analyst ties are negatively related to war occurrence (-0.22, p < .01). High versus low is 14.3% less likely, rising to 19.6% comparing zero with full common analyst ties.
- H3 strongly supported: the proportion of incumbents at war in other markets in the prior period is positively related to war onset (1.86, p < .001). High versus low is 68.7% more likely, rising to 95.5% comparing no incumbents at war elsewhere with all incumbents at war.
- H4 supported (the gasoline effect): the interaction of incumbents at war elsewhere with strategic alliances is positive (1.46, p < .001). The spillover gap widens from 43.4% at low alliance levels to 72.7% at high levels, and the marginal-effect plot shows a crossover point at which alliances switch from deterring war to amplifying it.
- H5a supported: the interaction with common ownership ties is negative (-3.38, p < .001). The spillover gap narrows from 83.2% at low common ownership to 45.3% at high.
- H5b supported: the interaction with common analyst ties is negative (-1.06, p < .001). The spillover gap narrows from 74.8% at low common analyst ties to 47.5% at high.

Results held under alternative price-war cutoffs (10%, 15%, 25%) and war-end cutoffs (5%, 10%), alternative at-war-elsewhere thresholds (1%, 10%), a linear probability model, omission of the industrywide war years 1992, 1996, 2001 and 2009 and of major carriers' Chapter 11 periods, two-way clustering on both endpoint cities (with city-pair fixed effects dropped for tractability), and exclusion of the 395 market-quarters with more than one alliance clique. Auxiliary analyses locate the alliance effects in strong ties: strong alliance ties are negative and significant for occurrence (-2.16, p < .01) and their interaction with incumbents at war elsewhere is positive and highly significant (.83, p < .001), while both weak-tie terms are insignificant. Dedicated and transient common ownership ties each show negative main and moderating effects, with the dedicated-ties model producing the larger log-likelihood improvement. The common ownership effect also strengthened over time, from -0.37 (p < .10) using 1991-2000 observations to -2.07 (p < .001) using 2000-2010 observations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study advances competitive dynamics research by being, to the authors' knowledge, the first to systematically examine the occurrence and spillover of competitive wars as a distinct phenomenon from day-to-day rivalry. It extends social embeddedness theory by identifying the onset of a competitive war as a critical situational trigger that can transform direct linkages from constraining competition (glue) into a catalyst for escalation (gasoline), and by foregrounding the distinct mechanisms—trust/interdependence versus third-party mediation/sanctioning—through which direct and indirect ties operate. It also provides initial empirical evidence for the previously untested assumption underlying the multimarket mutual forbearance hypothesis that competitive interactions spread across markets when forbearance breaks down, and introduces common analyst ties as a novel form of embeddedness shaping interfirm competition.

**Practical Implication**
The findings indicate that interorganizational linkages have asymmetric, state-dependent effects on competitive escalation: common ownership and common analyst ties dampen the spread of destructive price wars, whereas strategic alliances—while preventing wars from starting—can accelerate their contagion once fighting begins. The authors note that when competition escalates into catastrophic warfare, common ownership ties can help prevent the situation from worsening, which is beneficial for the industry as a whole.

**Limitations**
The arguments carry boundary conditions: the theory applies most cleanly to industries with low differentiation and to markets without competing alliance cliques (where high alliance density may not lower rivalry), and the glue-gasoline story fits best alliances aimed at resource sharing such as airline code-sharing. The study examines only lose-lose wars detrimental to performance (e.g., price wars), not value-enhancing competitive wars, and analyzes spillover only across geographic—not product—markets. It also could not directly test whether passively managed index-fund ownership weakens common-ownership effects because the sample ends in 2010.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine how different alliance types and purposes shape war occurrence and spillover differently, study value-enhancing (e.g., marketing or product-development) wars whose dynamics differ from lose-lose wars, and extend the analysis to spillover across product markets. The authors also encourage borrowing from behavioral perspectives (prospect theory, the behavioral theory of the firm, the psychology of rivalry) and strategic-communication lenses, investigating the within-war process of how rivals actually interact, and exploring how competitive wars reshape subsequent cooperative and alliance relationships between rivals.
