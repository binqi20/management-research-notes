# Addressing Performance Tensions in Multiteam Systems: Balancing Informal Mechanisms of Coordination within and between Teams

**Abstract**
Due to their distinctive features, multiteam systems (MTSs) face significant coordination challenges—both within component teams and across the larger system. Despite the benefits of informal mechanisms of coordination for knowledge-based work, there is considerable ambiguity regarding their effects in MTSs. To resolve this ambiguity, we build and test theory about how interpersonal interactions among MTS members serve as an informal coordination mechanism that facilitates team and system functioning. Integrating MTS research with insights from the team boundary spanning literature, we argue that the degree to which MTS members balance their interactions with members of their own component team (i.e., intrateam interactions) and with the members of other teams in the system (i.e., interteam interactions) shapes team- and system-level performance. The findings of a multimethod study of 44 MTSs composed of 295 teams and 930 members show that as interteam interactions exceed intrateam interactions, team conflict arises and detracts from component team performance. At the system level, a balance between intrateam and interteam interactions enhances system success. Our findings advance understanding of MTSs by highlighting how informal coordination mechanisms enable MTSs to overcome their coordination challenges and address the unique performance tension between component teams and the larger system.

**Research Question**
How do informal coordination mechanisms—specifically, the balance of MTS members' intrateam versus interteam interpersonal interactions—influence component team and system-level effectiveness, and through what process do imbalances translate into performance consequences?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Imbalance between intrateam and interteam interactions is positively related to team conflict, such that team conflict is higher as interteam interactions exceed intrateam interactions (positive).
- H2: Imbalance between intrateam and interteam interactions is indirectly negatively related to team performance through team conflict (negative indirect effect).
- H3: Controlling for component team conflict and performance, imbalance between intrateam and interteam interactions is negatively related to system performance (negative; the paper argues an imbalance in either direction is problematic at the system level).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Imbalance between intrateam and interteam interpersonal interactions (operationalized via response surface / polynomial terms; "imbalance" = interteam exceeding intrateam, and incongruence in either direction at the system level)
- DV(s): Component team performance (within-team energy-transfer success rate) and MTS system-level performance (between-team energy-transfer success rate)
- Mediator: Team conflict (Jehn & Mannix's 9-item measure; channels the team-level imbalance effect to team performance)
- Moderators: None hypothesized; post hoc, coordination foci (adjacent vs. nonadjacent interteam interactions) and forms (concentration of interteam interactions in a subset of members) qualify the effects
- Controls: Team familiarity, team size, MTS size; system models also control average team familiarity, team performance, and team conflict

Drawing on the team boundary spanning principle that teams must strike "a balance between internal and external activities" (Choi, 2002), the authors argue that interteam interactions are necessary to coordinate across component teams but generate role overload, divergent task understandings, and intrateam conflict when not matched by corresponding intrateam interactions. At the team level, imbalance (interteam exceeding intrateam) raises team conflict (H1), which in turn depresses component team performance (H2). At the system level, balanced interpersonal interactions serve a coordination function that enables the MTS to integrate adjustments locally, so incongruence in either direction undermines system performance (H3). All three hypotheses were supported.

**Data & Measures**
Single multisource, multimethod field study (no multi-study design). Setting and sample: 44 multiteam systems formed as part of the laboratory component of a required undergraduate engineering course at a university on the East Coast of the United States, in which students worked in an MTS to design and build a Rube Goldberg machine across an 11-week course; each component team built a machine containing at least six prescribed energy-transfer events, and the machines of all teams in a laboratory section were linked into one system-level machine. The full sample was 295 teams and 930 members; teams had three or four members (M = 3.15, SD = 0.46), the MTSs consisted of between four and eight teams (M = 6.70, SD = 1.11), and participants averaged 19 years of age (SD = 1.68) and were 76% male. Data came from three sources to guard against single-source and single-method limitations: web surveys (Week 1 background, 913 responses, 98% response rate; Week 8 team conflict, 827 responses, 89%), wearable Bluetooth sensors worn by 912 participants (98%) during Weeks 5, 6, and 7, and trained observers who scored machine performance in Week 11. Hypotheses were tested on a subsample of 268 teams nested in 44 systems: 22 teams were excluded because device or system failures left them without interaction data, and 5 were excluded as interaction outliers (more than three standard deviations above the mean); the 27 excluded teams did not differ significantly from the remaining 268 on team size, familiarity, conflict, or performance.

- IVs (intrateam and interteam interpersonal interactions): operationalized as the amount of time individuals spent in relative physical proximity to one another, captured with wearable Bluetooth multisensor devices (Kim et al., 2012). Devices were randomly reassigned across participants week by week; raw Bluetooth detection (RSSI) values were used rather than software-derived metrics; a threshold of -80, the midpoint of the -91 to -69 range considered, classified a detection event as an interaction; and dyadic interaction counts were scaled by the number of minutes both members' sensors were simultaneously active. Intrateam interactions are the dyadic interactions among members of the same component team aggregated to the team level; interteam interactions are the dyadic interactions between members of one component team and members of other teams in the same MTS. The system-level variables are the system-level mean (across teams) of these two team-level measures. Analysis-sample descriptives: intrateam M = 0.92 (SD = 0.44), interteam M = 0.12 (SD = 0.08).
- Mediator (team conflict): Jehn and Mannix's (2001) 9-item measure, completed in Week 8, with team conflict using a 7-point scale ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (all the time); sample item, "How often are there disagreements about who should do what in your work group?" Team-level interitem reliability was 0.94, with aggregation justified by median rwg(j) = 0.91, ICC(1) = 0.21 (p < .01), and ICC(2) = 0.43 (the low ICC(2) attributed to the three- and four-member team size). The instrument carries three items each for task, process, and relationship conflict (mean intercorrelation 0.70); results were substantively the same across the three forms, so a single overall conflict measure is reported. M = 2.23 (SD = 0.81).
- Team-level DV (component team performance): the total percentage of successful within-team energy transfers across five performance trials scored by trained observers in Week 11, a transfer counting as successful when energy passed seamlessly from one event to the next within the same team's machine without manual intervention. Range 16.67% to 100%; M = 0.89 (SD = 0.10).
- System-level DV (system performance): the rate of successful energy transfers between component teams' machines in the system, computed across the same five Week 11 trials; between-team transfers occurred between adjacent teams in the sequentially interdependent MTS, and the measure also included the final event of transferring sugar to a cup of coffee. Range 73.33% to 100%; M = 0.90 (SD = 0.10).
- Controls: team familiarity (mean of round-robin Week 1 dyadic ratings of "I know this team member well" on a 7-point agreement scale, operationalized as an additive construct) and team size (number on the team roster) in the team-level models; MTS size (number of teams in the system) plus average team familiarity, team performance, and team conflict in the system-level models.
- Post hoc measures: coordination foci, built by demarcating whether a dyadic interteam interaction involved teams next to one another in the sequential workflow (adjacent) or teams separated by at least one other team (nonadjacent); and coordination form, measured as the concentration of interteam interactions within a component team, the coefficient of variation across a team's members in how much each interacted with members of other teams.

Estimation and identification: hypotheses were tested with polynomial regression and response surface analysis rather than difference scores or product terms, computing four surface parameters (a1 through a4), of which a3, the slope of the line of incongruence, is the focal test for H1, and a4, the curvature along the line of incongruence, is the focal test for H3. Team-level models predicting conflict and performance used clustered standard errors to adjust for teams nested within systems; the H2 indirect effect used a block variable approach with bootstrapped standard errors, creating weighted linear composites of the five polynomial estimates and then estimating a path model. Appendix B re-ran the focal models across 23 RSSI threshold values (-91 to -69) as a sensitivity check, and further sensitivity analyses added the number of people in each MTS and the performance of the lowest-performing team as controls. The design is associational rather than causal: although constructs were assessed at multiple time points, the authors state they were unable to establish causality given their methodology.

**Key Findings**
Team-level results come from Tables 3 and 4 (n = 268 teams nested in 44 systems; unstandardized coefficients, clustered standard errors, two-tailed tests) and system-level results from Table 5 (n = 44 systems). All three hypotheses were supported.

- H1 supported: predicting team conflict, the slope of the line of incongruence was significant and negative (a3 = -0.32, p < .01; Table 3 Model 3), indicating that team conflict increases when members spend more time in interteam interactions than in intrateam interactions. In the same model, intrateam interactions related negatively to conflict (B = -0.14, p < .05) and interteam interactions positively (B = 0.17, p < .05); results were robust to dropping the team size and familiarity controls (Model 4). R2 = 0.049.
- H2 supported: the indirect effect of the imbalance of intrateam and interteam interactions on team performance, through team conflict, was negative and significant (B = -0.02, SE = 0.01, p < .05, b = -0.04). Consistent with this, team conflict related negatively to team performance (B = -0.02, p < .01; Table 4 Model 5), while the interpersonal interaction terms had no significant direct effects on team performance (all four response surface parameters nonsignificant). The team-level imbalance effect on performance therefore runs through conflict rather than directly.
- H3 supported: controlling for component team conflict and performance, the relation between interpersonal interactions and system performance was curvilinear, with significant downward curvature along the line of incongruence (a4 = -0.07, p < .05; Table 5 Model 3, R2 = 0.355). System performance was higher when members balanced their engagement with their own team and with other teams, and declined as teams engaged disproportionately in either intrateam or interteam interactions, so at the system level imbalance in either direction was costly rather than only an excess of interteam interaction. Team familiarity was also negatively related to system performance (B = -0.08, p < .01).
- Post hoc, the foci of interaction mattered. The team-level imbalance effect on conflict held for nonadjacent interteam interactions (a3 = -0.34, SE = 0.08, p < .01) but was negative and nonsignificant for adjacent ones (a3 = -0.12, SE = 0.10, p > .10). At the system level, imbalance of nonadjacent interteam and intrateam interactions was negatively related to system performance (a4 = -0.06, SE = 0.03, p < .05), whereas the adjacent estimate was nonsignificant (a4 = -0.06, SE = 0.05, p > .10). The detrimental effects of imbalance were thus especially pronounced for interactions with teams the focal team does not directly integrate with in the system.
- Post hoc, the form of interaction cut differently across levels. Concentration of interteam interactions in a subset of team members was not significantly related to team conflict (B = -0.10, SE = 0.18, p > .10) but was positively related to team performance (B = 0.04, SE = 0.02, p = .05), and the system-level mean of concentration was not related to system performance (B = -0.07, SE = 0.05, p > .10). Concentrating boundary spanning therefore appears to benefit team performance through a channel other than team conflict, without costing system performance. In the same post hoc model, intrateam interactions remained significantly negatively related to team conflict (B = -0.26, SE = 0.11, p < .05), and the a priori hypothesis tests were robust to including concentration as a control.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends MTS theory by showing that informal coordination mechanisms—long viewed pessimistically as unworkable in large, distributed systems—can in fact facilitate MTS effectiveness when intrateam and interteam interactions are considered jointly rather than as independent, additive factors. By integrating MTS and boundary spanning literatures, it identifies the balance of interpersonal interactions as a coordination mechanism, names team conflict as the intermediary that transmits team-level imbalance to performance, and demonstrates the distinct, sometimes countervailing, effects of coordination at the team versus system levels of analysis.

**Practical Implication**
Managers and members of MTSs should consciously manage with whom and how much they interact internally versus externally: interteam interactions must be coupled with at least a similar level of intrateam interactions so that externally acquired information is integrated within the team. Post hoc evidence further suggests focusing external interactions on directly interdependent (adjacent) teams, and that concentrating boundary spanning in a subset of members may benefit team (though not system) performance. Social network and interaction-tracking tools can help monitor and rebalance informal interactions.

**Limitations**
The sample comprised U.S. engineering students completing a standardized sequential-interdependence task, which limits generalizability to organizational, cross-cultural, and other MTS structures, and the system-level sample of 44 limits statistical power. Interaction was operationalized only as time in physical proximity, so the quality and content of interactions and the presumed system-level knowledge-transfer mechanism were not directly measured. Causality could not be established, and conflict measured after the action phase leaves open recursive conflict–interaction dynamics.

**Future Research**
The authors call for studying the intersection of formal and informal coordination mechanisms (including semi-structured forms) in MTSs, examining informal coordination across the full MTS lifecycle and transition phases, and directly measuring system-level mediating mechanisms such as knowledge transfer and interteam conflict. They also encourage examining the absolute volume of interactions, the adjacent/nonadjacent foci distinction, and the use of computational modeling or experiments to identify causal effects in other cultural and organizational contexts and larger samples.
