# Top Management Team Experiential Variety, Competitive Repertoires, and Firm Performance: Examining the Law of Requisite Variety in the 3D Printing Industry (1986–2017)

**Abstract**
This study develops and tests a thesis derived from the law of requisite variety. We contend that the greater the experiential variety of a top management team, the more likely it is that the complexity and consistency of the firm’s competitive repertoire will be calibrated to relevant external variety. In addition, for firms that achieve such calibrated repertoires, we expect that their financial performance will be superior to that of their peers. We then integrate these arguments and examine whether top management team experiential variety indirectly, through calibrated repertoires, contributes to firm performance. Analyzing hand-collected data for firms operating in the 3D printing industry over the past three decades (1986–2017), we find support for the overall thesis and associated hypotheses. The discussion section elaborates on the study’s contributions, limitations, and future research potential.

**Research Question**
Does top management team (TMT) industry experience variety shape firm performance indirectly, by enabling firms to calibrate the complexity and consistency of their competitive repertoires to the external variety embodied in top performers' actions, as predicted by the law of requisite variety (LRV)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. TMT industry experience variety will be positively associated with calibrated repertoire complexity.
H2. TMT industry experience variety will be positively associated with calibrated repertoire consistency.
H3. Calibrated repertoire complexity will be positively associated with firm performance.
H4. Calibrated repertoire consistency will be positively associated with firm performance.
H5. Through calibrated repertoire complexity, TMT industry experience variety will have an indirect effect on firm performance.
H6. Through calibrated repertoire consistency, TMT industry experience variety will have an indirect effect on firm performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): TMT industry experience variety (Shannon-entropy index of distinct NAICS-coded prior industry experiences across team members)
- DV(s): Calibrated repertoire complexity (H1) and calibrated repertoire consistency (H2) as proximal outcomes; firm performance, return on assets / ROA (H3–H6) as the distal outcome
- Mediators: Calibrated repertoire complexity (complexity path, H5) and calibrated repertoire consistency (consistency path, H6) jointly mediate the variety → performance relationship
- Moderators: None hypothesized; supplementary analyses probe TMT industry experience skew, within- vs. between-firm variance, and the calibration time frame

Drawing on the LRV, the authors argue that experientially varied TMTs possess a richer reserve of cognitive categories and behavioral routines, reducing both "routinized" (over-consistent, under-complex) and "headless chicken" (over-complex, under-consistent) competitive misfires. This allows such firms to calibrate the complexity and consistency of their repertoires to the external variety signaled by contemporaneous top performers, which in turn improves performance. The variety → performance relationship is fully indirect: TMT experiential variety operates through repertoire calibration rather than directly, consistent with the LRV's claim that system performance depends on matching internal to external variety.

**Data & Measures**
Single study, single industry: a hand-collected, unbalanced longitudinal panel of commercial 3D printer manufacturers in operation from 1986 through 2017, with observations admitted only from 1991 forward, once enough firms were trading for top performers to be identified. The sampling frame comprised all publicly traded firms covered by Wohlers Associates plus several privately held firms; a firm entered a given year on recording at least one commercial 3D printer sale (units priced $5,000 or greater).
- Competitive actions (the input to both repertoire measures): structured content analysis of press articles and news reports compiled by Factiva, with all flagged articles totaling over 17,500. A quanteda dictionary of 3-grams tracked 102 specific actions across 10 categories (pricing, marketing, product, capacity, service, signaling, legal, market expansion, strategic alliances, acquisitions); overall agreement was 98.5%, validated by manual coding of a random sample of 175 articles (1%), with a Perreault and Leigh reliability index of .75. Repertoire vectors were generated for 245 firm-years and comprised 1,590 coded actions (an average of 6.49 per firm-year).
- Calibrated repertoire complexity (DV in H1, IV in H3): the firm-year Shannon entropy of action types normalized by ln(10), differenced against a standard given by the fitted linear trend in the repertoire complexity of the three firms with the highest current sales volumes (linear trend b = 0.02, SE = 0.002, p < .001, explaining 65% of the variation); calibration = 1 − |firm value − standard|.
- Calibrated repertoire consistency (DV in H2, IV in H4): a normalized displacement vector capturing the magnitude and direction of year-to-year change in a firm's repertoire in 10-dimensional space relative to prior years, differenced the same way against a standard built from the significant linear, quadratic, and cubic trend components of top performers' consistency (70% of the variance).
- TMT industry experience variety (IV in H1, H2, H5, H6): the Shannon entropy of the distinct NAICS codes (to six-digit fidelity) across the full career histories of current TMT members, normalized to the theoretical maximum ln(249). The TMT is defined as the top two levels of the organizational structure. Of 468 individuals identified, 343 were determined to be TMT members and career histories were located for 285 of them (83%); the population spans 249 distinct industries and 12,385 person-years of experience.
- Firm performance (DV in H3–H6): return on assets, with prior-year ROA included to mitigate reverse causality and deliberately kept distinct from the sales-volume criterion used to identify top performers.
- Controls: total competitive actions, new action types, media mentions; TMT industry experience separation (coefficient of variation of member industry tenure) and skew, functional-background, gender, and age diversity, average member age, TMT size, total 3D printing experience, average experience relatedness; board size, CEO industry tenure, firm age, firm size (sales), public-firm status; industry concentration, industry sales growth, and a four-component accumulated-industry-experience index (α = .93).

Estimation is associational panel modeling rather than a causal-identification design. Random-effects models with robust standard errors test H1–H4; Table 2 reports 245 firm-years across 23 firms for calibrated complexity, 227 across 22 firms for calibrated consistency, and 175 across 21 firms (Models 5–6) and 161 across 20 firms (Model 7) for ROA, while Table 1 separately reports ROA descriptives for 183 firm-years. Fixed-effects and hybrid within/between models serve as robustness checks, and the indirect effects in H5–H6 are estimated with Preacher and Selig's Monte Carlo method (20,000 replications) and triangulated with SEM using bootstrapped and cluster-robust standard errors.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses were supported in the primary random-effects models.
- H1 supported: TMT industry experience variety is positively and significantly associated with calibrated repertoire complexity (b = 0.40, SE = 0.16, p < .05; Model 2). Moving from one standard deviation below the mean to one above reduces the magnitude by which firms miscalibrate by approximately 30%; the simple effect without controls is also positive (b = 0.20, SE = 0.06, p < .01).
- H2 supported: the same variety measure is positively and significantly associated with calibrated repertoire consistency (b = 0.83, SE = 0.35, p < .05; Model 4), corresponding to a 40% reduction in miscalibration magnitude across the same one-standard-deviation range (simple effect b = 0.29, SE = 0.13, p < .05).
- H3 supported: calibrated repertoire complexity is associated with higher performance as measured by ROA (b = 25.65, SE = 10.31, p < .05; Model 6); a one-standard-deviation increase in complexity calibration translates to nearly a four percentage-point boost to ROA, which is substantial given a sample average ROA of approximately 0%.
- H4 supported: repertoires closer to calibrated consistency levels are likewise beneficial to performance (b = 11.96, SE = 5.19, p < .05; Model 7). Unrestricted piecewise tests show the complexity and consistency effects are symmetric — performance declines when a firm deviates in either direction from the standard.
- H5 and H6 supported: the Monte Carlo indirect effects are positive with 95% confidence intervals excluding zero for both the complexity path (b = 9.49, CI [0.88, 23.94]) and the consistency path (b = 8.96, CI [0.31, 24.42]). The authors describe these as largely corroborated by the SEM specifications, for which they report that the 90% — not the 95% — confidence interval excludes zero in all cases. The direct effect of TMT industry experience variety on ROA is not significant (b = −20.19, SE = 33.84, p > .10), which is the basis for concluding that the effect on performance is indirect.

The authors report their own robustness qualifications. Most of the variance in TMT industry experience variety lies between firms (ICC(1) = .76), and in fixed-effects specifications the within-firm effect of variety on calibrated complexity was "muted and not significant" (b = .05, SE = 0.27, p > .10) while its within-firm effect on calibrated consistency reached only p < .10 (b = .90, SE = 0.49). Within-firm calibrated complexity does not significantly predict ROA (Model 12: b = 9.93, SE = 8.26, p > .10), whereas within-firm calibrated consistency does (Model 13: b = 11.28, SE = 4.96, p < .05); hybrid models find the between- and within-firm estimates statistically indistinguishable (Wald tests, p > .10). Supplementary analyses show that TMTs whose experience skews toward 3D printing hold repertoires less complex than the standard (b = −0.11, SE = 0.05, p < .05), with no evidence that they are systematically more consistent than the standard (b = 0.11, SE = 0.12, p > .10); no interaction between within- and between-person variety was found; and calibrating against top performers' contemporaneous repertoires had the most poignant effect on performance, with the effect of future-year standards diminishing and becoming insignificant after three years.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces and operationalizes "calibrated competitive repertoires," shifting the LRV from an undefined theoretical-maximum notion of external variety to a measurable standard anchored in top-performing competitors, and extends the law beyond complexity to encompass repertoire consistency (a temporal component). It provides one of the first direct empirical tests of the LRV's performance thesis, modeling the full chain from TMT experiential variety through calibration to performance, and shows that "more is better" is too simplistic—what matters is matching, not maximizing, variety. For upper echelons theory it reframes attention from why executives act to how managerial perceptions produce calibration misfires.

**Practical Implication**
Firms should not pursue maximal complexity or rigid consistency in competitive action, but should calibrate both to the moving standard set by industry top performers; either under- or over-shooting that standard degrades performance. Because calibration is hard to achieve, assembling top management teams with a broad spread of qualitatively different prior industry experiences gives firms the cognitive breadth needed to track shifting external variety and avoid competitive inertia or scattershot responses.

**Limitations**
The overall sample is relatively small, which constrains the power to detect firm-performance effects and to jointly examine earlier and later life-cycle stages. The single-industry design buys less-confounded testing at the expense of cross-industry generalizability, and other TMT experience characteristics (e.g., functional or educational diversity) could yield similar predictions. ROA is a summary financial proxy for adaptation, and the authors cannot directly observe TMTs discriminating among stimuli and constructing their action repertoires.

**Future Research**
Examine whether an inherent trade-off exists between calibrating complexity and calibrating consistency, and which is more valuable when firms are forced to choose. Identify the behavioral routines and rigidities (beyond cognition) that drive miscalibration, and study how different TMT diversity types produce distinct forms of miscalibration—how firms miscalibrate may matter as much as how much. Finer-grained, more micro-level decision-making data could parse the perceptual and selection processes the theory posits.
