# Identity–Society (Mis)Alignment and the Instrumentalization of Firm Creation: Creative Destruction and Creative Reconstruction

**Abstract**
Research on founder identity has significantly advanced our understanding of entrepreneurship and related literatures. By departing from the widely held—though often implicit—assumption that culture defines the parameters of identity formation, this paper investigates how the alignment, or misalignment, between a founder’s identity and their perceived sociocultural context influences new firm creation. We engage in an in-depth qualitative study of 49 founders in Taiwan that reveals how founders’ identity structures can be (mis)aligned with their perceived social structures, and how the reinforcement or tensions generated by (mis)alignment can fuel the creation of fundamentally different types of ventures—including those that challenge the perceived sociocultural status quo with a social mission intended to push society toward a new equilibrium. Our theory of founder identity–society (mis)alignment is therefore able to specify a key mechanism linking the founder’s identity with perceived sociocultural context. Furthermore, we advance the influential Schumpeterian theory of creative destruction beyond the purely economic context to address the broader sociocultural environment and to incorporate the concept of creative reconstruction, in which entrepreneurs seek to restore elements of society past.

**Research Question**
How does the alignment, or misalignment, between an entrepreneur’s identity and perceived surrounding sociocultural context influence new venture creation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper — this is an inductive, interpretive qualitative study that poses a research question rather than a priori predictions, and it states no numbered hypotheses and no formal propositions anywhere in the text. The constructs of identity structure and perceived social structure enter the analysis abductively in Stage 2 as theoretically predefined constructs used to induce (mis)alignment, not as tested predictions; the two venture-outcome dimensions, the six venture types, and the extension of creative destruction into creative reconstruction all emerge from the coding.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: founder *identity structure* (tradition-, inner-, image-/discovery-directed) juxtaposed against perceived *social structure* (traditional, industrial, postmodern); (mis)alignment is the (in)congruence between the forces driving the two.
- Intermediate psychological outcomes: alignment yields a sense of societal *reinforcement* (emotional, cognitive, social); misalignment generates *tensions* (low to high) reflecting dissatisfaction with the perceived "rules of the game."
- Venture-outcome dimensions: (1) the *sociopolitical dimension* embedded in the venture (absent → dominant, i.e., whether it challenges the sociocultural status quo) and (2) *temporal orientation* (inventing the future vs. restoring the past).
- Emergent typology: six venture types mapping founders onto the two outcome dimensions, including high-tension misaligned founders who build social-mission ventures.

The paper theorizes a micro-level mechanism: when founders perceive misalignment between their identity structure and their surrounding social structures, the resulting tensions push them—via a logic of appropriateness rather than purely rational choice—to instrumentalize firm creation as a vehicle to change society, embedding a sociopolitical dimension oriented either toward a new future (creative destruction of the sociocultural environment) or toward restoring a valued past (creative reconstruction). Aligned founders, experiencing reinforcement, instead venture within the prevailing rules of the game and challenge only the economic status quo.

**Data & Measures**
- Setting and case-inclusion criteria: the study was conducted on the island of Taiwan and delimited to the cultural products and services industry — the "cultural and creative" (*wen chuang*) market category that by 2015 comprised 15 sectors, over 62,000 companies, and 25 billion U.S. dollars in annual turnover (Austrade, 2020). The industry was chosen because it predisposes founders to awareness of their sociocultural context, is likely to attract founders with various social motivations, and has starting-capital requirements low enough not to predispose founders to a disproportionately profit-oriented approach. Included ventures were independently held, eight years old or less, and carried public-facing brands on a business-to-consumer model (in a few cases business-to-business as well); new brands or business units created for or by family factories taken over by a new generation of entrepreneurs were included where the entrepreneur building the brand had full control over it.
- Sampling frame and recruitment: a database of new ventures established between 2010 and 2017 in the Taiwanese fashion and textile sector was built from media reports, industry blogs and awards, online stores, and social media. Cold emails inviting participation went to 86 firms and produced 18 responses (21% response rate). Founders then completed a screening survey collecting demographic and venture data to confirm they met the participation criteria before interviews were scheduled. A theoretically driven snowball strategy followed — each founder was asked at the end of the interview to recommend other entrepreneurs, especially those with different experiences and perspectives, leveraging the *guanxi* of the person making the introduction — aimed at maximum variation in social motivations and in perceptions of the sociocultural context. Sampling also targeted diversity in age, gender, and urban (e.g., Taipei) versus rural (e.g., Hualian) settings; the sample comprised mostly founders of the Han ethnic majority (95% of the Taiwanese population) but also ethnic Taiwanese minority founders (2.3% of the population; CIA, 2018).
- Interview corpus as collected: one of the authors conducted semistructured interviews with a total of 58 founders at 46 firms, accompanied by one of two local interpreters engaged for the study. Formal recorded interviews lasted 45–120 minutes and were generally preceded or followed by tours of the founder's store or workspace with time to examine products, much informal storytelling (captured in two completely filled notebooks) and in some cases a joint meal, for a total of 2–6 hours of face-to-face interaction; each interview was followed by 30–120 minutes of debriefing with the interpreter when present. The interview guide sought narrative answers on the founder's journey to starting up, self-conceptualization along various dimensions of identity (Brewer & Gardner, 1996; Erikson, 1959), venture attributes and business model, and perceptions of the sociocultural context in which they live, interact, and do business; phrasings were chosen to reduce power distance, every interview was supplemented by spontaneous probes, and emergent themes were added to the agenda throughout data collection.
- Language and transcription: interviews were conducted in the language preferred by the interviewee, with responses usually in Mandarin. Audio captured the researcher's questions, the interpreter's translation where needed, the founder's response, and subsequent clarifying exchanges in Mandarin. Each file was transcribed and all Mandarin and Taiwanese translated into English; as an alternative to back translation, the live interpreter's simultaneous translation was supplemented by a second professional translator, with the occasional divergence resolved by discussion and, in one case, by direct verification with the founder.
- Analytic sample: three firms were dropped because they did not fit the selection criteria (in one case a venture run by an intrapreneur hired to launch a new brand for a pivoting factory, who therefore lacked full decision-making power), leaving a final sample of 49 founders at 43 firms, with founding teams interviewed at six ventures.
- Triangulation and member checks: the transcribed interactions form the analyzed corpus and were triangulated with founders' screening-survey responses, information from cofounders and employees, company websites, founders' publications on personal social media, newspaper articles, brochures and other strategic documents supplied by founders, and research-journal notes on unrecorded interactions. "Immediate and informal" member checks were conducted with key informants throughout the analysis (Lincoln & Guba, 1985: 314), and in Stage 3 all founders were invited to react — in their preferred language — to a one-page rich description of the interview, observations, and subsequent analyses that used the study's first- and second-order categories.
- Coding, Stage 1 (inductive): two rounds of inductive coding (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013) focused on how the founder perceived their sociocultural context and how they perceived themselves within it, with founder identity (Fauchart & Gruber, 2011) and social identity (Brewer & Gardner, 1996) used as sensitizing concepts (Blumer, 1954).
- Coding, Stage 2 (abductive) — how the focal construct was operationalized: identity structure, defined as the filter through which an individual determines which experiences are important to identity and how they are handled (Marcia, 1993), was set against perceived social structure, the normalized patterns of interaction that prescribe which information and experiences are identity-relevant (Côté, 1996a, 1996b; Giddens, 1984). Alignment or misalignment was induced by comparing founders' statements about "who I am" and "how I define myself" with their statements about "who society says I should be" and "how society says I should define (or find) myself." Three overarching forces emerged — tradition, individual agency, and image or discovery — and the same three forces were used to label identity structures (tradition-directed, inner-directed, image- or discovery-directed) and perceived social structures (traditional, industrial, postmodern). Founders were coded aligned when the force(s) guiding their identity structure were the same force(s) they perceived to be driving their surrounding social structures, and misaligned on any divergence; hybrid identity structures and multiple perceived social structures were handled by inferring alignment from overlap or divergence in the overall range of forces the founder referenced. The misaligned group was then further divided according to whether the data conveyed high or low levels of tension. Table 1 reports the first-order categories, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions; Table 2 gives sample quotes per structural theme with founder IDs; Table 3 lists all cases with their identity structure(s), perceived social structure(s), (mis)alignment, associated tension or reinforcement, and venture outcomes.
- Coding, Stage 3 (firm-level implications): each interview was coded for the activities founders performed in the venture — what they produced, how, with whom they produced and delivered, and to whom they marketed and sold — after which "stacking comparable cases" (Miles, Huberman, & Saldaña, 2013: 103) produced written rich descriptions of every case followed by matrices to identify cross-cutting variables. Two venture-outcome dimensions were coded from this: the sociopolitical dimension in the venture, as a spectrum running from "absence" to "dominance," and temporal orientation, poled between "inventing the new future" and "restoring the past." Every case was then revisited for a second, more nuanced set of rich descriptions triangulated across sources including member checks, and each venture was mapped onto the two dimensions in Figure 1. Constant comparison within and between cases (Miles et al., 2013) yielded six distinct venture types; after 38 interviews had been iteratively coded and compared, no further insights emerged with regard to the identity–social-structure configurations and venture types, which the authors treat as theoretical saturation (Strauss & Corbin, 1998).
- Identification: none claimed, and none available by design. There are no measured variables, no estimation model, and no causal-identification strategy; the interpretive approach deliberately privileges founders' *perceptions* of their surroundings over any formal or intersubjective measure of the environment. The paper does use mechanism and driving-force language and, in the Discussion, describes its framework as providing an "identity-based causal explanation" of why institutional and cultural entrepreneurs engage to change social environments, but that claim rests on inductive and abductive cross-case interpretation, not on a design that isolates an effect. The authors also note explicitly that the venture "choices" arrayed in Table 9 were not true rational design choices in their study but were prescribed by the nature of founders' fundamental (dis)content with the prevailing sociocultural context.

**Key Findings**
- No hypotheses were tested and no coefficients, effect sizes, or significance levels are reported; the findings are the emergent constructs, the venture typology, and the reframed theoretical framework, presented through five prototypical cases plus corroborating evidence in Tables 5–8.
- First core finding (individual level): founders differ in the extent to which they experience (mis)alignment between their identity structure and their perceived social structure(s). Contrary to sociology's suggestion that culture defines the parameters of identity formation (Côté, 1996a, 1996b), misalignment is reported as "in fact, a frequent phenomenon in entrepreneurship."
- Second core finding: alignment was associated with a sense of societal reinforcement for founders — emotional, cognitive, and social — whereas misalignment was associated with tensions regarding the way founders feel, act, and interact with others, tensions that represent fundamental dissatisfaction with the "rules of the game" the founder perceives to govern their sociocultural context. The authors note that reinforcement from alignment on some elements can mitigate tension from misalignment on others, so a partly misaligned founder may register either high or low tension.
- Third core finding (venture level): those individual-level differences are reflected in variation in the types of ventures founders create, along two dimensions — the sociopolitical dimension embedded in the venture (absent to dominant) and the venture's temporal orientation (inventing the new future versus restoring the past). Mapping all ventures on these axes (Figure 1) produced six types; Types 5 and 6 are discussed together, so five prototypical cases are presented.
- Types 1 and 2 — aligned founders, reinforcement, sociopolitical dimension absent: primarily economic ventures that operate within the existing rules of the game and challenge only the industry/economic status quo, differentiating either through forward-looking innovation (Type 1; prototypical case Adam [12], whose design objects use a signature high-performance concrete) or through a past-oriented, traditional-craft niche (Type 2; Jason and Sirena's handmade-shoe company [23]). The paper reports Type 2 as relatively less common and states that this venture "was the only one in our sample to pursue a past orientation out of a primarily economic logic."
- Types 3 and 4 — misaligned founders, high tension, dominant sociopolitical dimension: founders channel their tensions into ventures that challenge the sociocultural status quo, oriented either toward building a new future (Type 3; Jeff and Stella's designer-furniture venture [53], whose stated main purpose is "not to make money" but to correct people's inappropriate concepts and educate Taiwanese people about individual choice) or toward restoring elements of the past (Type 4; Lu's custom shoemaking venture [42]). An important qualification: these founders did not set out to create "social ventures" — a label not strongly established in Taiwan — but "achieved this by simply being who they are."
- Types 5 and 6 — the middle ground, limited sociopolitical dimension: here the sociopolitical dimension was "by no means the raison d'être" of the venture, but once established the venture became a natural means of extending the founder's vision for society alongside profit-making, expressed as the adoption of sociopolitical best practices. The middle ground is explicitly mixed rather than uniform in its origin: just over half of these ventures were created by founders with partial misalignment and some (low) tension who were not deeply affected by it (prototypical case Lina [26], a wedding-gown designer bringing functional, modular, repurposeable gowns to a traditional industry), while the other half were started by aligned founders who conveyed no identity-related tensions of their own but empathized with others they observed experiencing such tensions. Type 6, the past-oriented middle ground, contains a single venture [31] — a chef trained in classical Japanese cuisine whose restaurant serves both innovative fusion dishes and a traditional 19-course banquet. In the Discussion these middle-ground founders are characterized not as originators of a new sociocultural vision but as "early adopters of business practices that support the revised vision of society put forth by high-tension misalignment founders."
- Founding-team extension: in the six team ventures, cofounders were homogeneous in their (mis)alignment — both aligned [14, 23, 33], both misaligned with high tension [53], or both misaligned with low tension [6, 49] — which is why each team appears as a single point in Figure 1. The sister team [49] shows that homogeneity in (mis)alignment does not mean identical tensions: one sister went through a process of concession over the venture's direction and found a separate outlet (blogging for an inner-directed audience) for her own desire to see change in society, which the authors read as evidence that pursuing sociopolitical change alongside the venture can alleviate tension within a founding team.
- Outlier extension — the individual-to-venture pattern does not hold universally: three misaligned, low-tension founders [25, 40, 51] created Type 1 ventures with no sociopolitical dimension, i.e., ventures more characteristic of aligned founders. They completely disengaged from challenging the sociopolitical status quo in the context of their venture, expressing dissatisfaction instead as defeat and indifference [25, 51] or disdain [40]. The authors label these three cases outliers with respect to the general patterns in the data and offer, as a conjecture rather than a finding, that they may not have understood or been convinced of entrepreneurship's potential for addressing sociopolitical aims.
- Framework-level result (Table 9): crossing identity–society (mis)alignment with temporal orientation reframes Schumpeter (1934). Aligned founders engage in creative destruction of the *industry* status quo — with novel solutions (future-oriented) or with past solutions (past-oriented) — operating within the existing sociocultural system without challenging it and pursuing economic goals. Misaligned founders engage in creative destruction of the *sociocultural* status quo with novel sociopolitical measures (future-oriented) or in creative reconstruction of it by reinvigorating elements of a past sociocultural setting (past-oriented), challenging the existing sociocultural system and pursuing primarily sociopolitical goals coupled with economic goals. Consistent with the second core finding, the authors attribute these patterns to a logic of appropriateness (March & Olsen, 2011) rather than rational, choice-based decision-making.
- Ancillary suggestion, flagged by the authors as beyond what their data can establish: the extended framework may supply origins for previously studied anomalies such as the craft brewery resurgence (Carroll & Swaminathan, 2000) and the survival of legacy-technology Swiss watchmaking (Raffaelli, 2019) — industry-wide movements may begin with misaligned founders enacting creative reconstruction with an express sociopolitical aim and then, after proof of concept, be joined by aligned founders who identify the revival of the past as lucrative strategic positioning. The authors state they cannot explain this at the level of organizational sociology.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study specifies founder identity–society (mis)alignment as a driving mechanism in entrepreneurship that produces qualitatively different venture types, including social ventures, thereby relaxing the implicit assumption that founders’ identities are congruent with their sociocultural context. It extends Schumpeter’s theory of creative destruction beyond the economic domain to the sociocultural environment and introduces the complementary concept of *creative reconstruction*, in which entrepreneurs seek to re-establish elements of a past sociocultural environment. It also contributes to research on organizational emergence by showing early-stage strategic choices follow a logic of appropriateness rather than rational, choice-based decision-making.

**Practical Implication**
The (mis)alignment lens can help founders better understand themselves and prospective partners when forming venture teams, since divergence in identity structures or perceptions of the sociocultural environment shapes the tensions that arise. Recognizing that social-mission ventures often originate in identity-based tensions (rather than a deliberate aim to become "social entrepreneurs") clarifies the motivational roots of socially oriented venturing.

**Limitations**
The study mainly observed ventures created by single individuals (with only a few two-founder teams whose members had similar identities), precluding analysis of how founders with divergent identity structures co-create ventures and the tensions that arise between them. Not all misaligned founders acted on their tension to the same degree; in-depth analysis of why three outlier founders disengaged was outside the study’s scope, and other factors such as self-efficacy and personality were not examined.

**Future Research**
Longitudinal designs could examine the dynamics of identity–society alignment over time and the role of identity-structure shifts, since founders recounted life events that moved them between identity structures. Researchers could also drill into specific identity-structure × social-structure configurations (nine pure permutations) and investigate which levels of sociocultural context (immediate surroundings, industry, nation, global region, global society) are most salient for reinforcement or tension.
