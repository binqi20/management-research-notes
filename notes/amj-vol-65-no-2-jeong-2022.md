# Strategic Nepotism in Family Director Appointments: Evidence from Family Business Groups in South Korea

**Abstract**
Controlling families of family firms strive to preserve their socioemotional wealth (SEW) through family involvement in company leadership. Prior research points to a potential trade-off of family involvement, as underqualified and underprepared family members’ involvement would bring SEW gains at the expense of financial wealth. We examine how controlling families seek to shape this trade-off to be in their favor by systematically appointing family members to board positions favorable for building managerial competencies through what we call “strategic” nepotism. We find that compared to nonfamily insiders, family members are preferentially appointed to prestigious firms that provide opportunities for cultivating knowledge, skills, and reputation in a protected organizational environment. We also find that controlling family members are kept away from stigmatizing appointments that would undermine their managerial resource-building efforts. Consistent with an effort to overcome intergenerational deterioration of managerial talent and skills, we find that strategic nepotism is concentrated on later-rather than earlier-generation family members.

**Research Question**
What determines and drives family director appointments in family business groups — that is, where and when do controlling families appoint family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to boards, and how does this "strategic nepotism" vary across family generations?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Firm market performance is positively related to the likelihood of appointment of family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to the board of directors.
- Hypothesis 2a. Firm reputation is positively related to the likelihood of appointment of family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to the board of directors.
- Hypothesis 3a. Intragroup board interlock centrality is positively related to the likelihood of appointment of family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to the board of directors.
- Hypothesis 4a. Financial distress is negatively related to the likelihood of appointment of family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to the board of directors.
- Hypothesis 5a. The number of existing family directors is negatively related to the likelihood of appointment of family members (versus nonfamily insiders) to the board of directors.
- Hypothesis 1b. Firm market performance is more positively related to the likelihood of appointing later- versus earlier-generation family members.
- Hypothesis 2b. Firm reputation is more positively related to the likelihood of appointing later- versus earlier-generation family members.
- Hypothesis 3b. Intragroup board interlock centrality is more positively related to the likelihood of appointing later- versus earlier-generation family members.
- Hypothesis 4b. Financial distress is more negatively related to the likelihood of appointing later- versus earlier-generation family members.
- Hypothesis 5b. The number of existing family directors is more negatively related to the likelihood of appointing later- versus earlier-generation family members.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm market performance (Tobin's Q); firm reputation ("most admired" list); intragroup board interlock centrality; financial distress (negative net income); number of existing family directors on the focal board.
- DV(s): Likelihood a board appointment is a family member versus a nonfamily inside director (binary probit); and, for generational tests, a categorical appointment outcome (nonfamily insider / earlier-generation / later-generation family member).
- Mediators: None modeled (resource-building and stigma-avoidance are theorized but not directly measured).
- Moderators: Family generation (earlier vs. later) — operationalized via the multinomial-probit contrast with earlier-generation appointments as baseline (Hypotheses 1b–5b).

Building on the SEW perspective and the "nepotism mixed gamble," the paper argues that controlling families proactively shape the long-run trade-off between SEW and financial wealth by deliberately routing family heirs to board positions that build human, reputational, and social capital (resource-building nepotism: high-performance, high-reputation, highly interlocked firms) while steering them away from positions that would taint those resources (stigma-avoiding nepotism: financially distressed firms and boards already crowded with family directors). Because founder-specific advantages erode across generations ("intergenerational deterioration"), later-generation members most need such grooming, so strategic nepotism is predicted — and found — to concentrate on them. Empirically, intragroup centrality (H3a), financial-distress avoidance (H4a), and avoidance of family-crowded boards (H5a) are supported; market performance (H1a) is only marginally supported and firm reputation (H2a) is not, but the generational tests show market performance (H1b), reputation (H2b), and distress avoidance (H4b) operate more strongly for later-generation members.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and data sources: an archival panel study of publicly traded firms affiliated with family-controlled business groups (chaebol) in South Korea, 2004–2009 (inclusive). Family-controlled business groups were identified from the Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) classification, and any group that appeared at least once on that list during the sample period was treated as a business group throughout the panel; the group's controlling family also had to be matchable to the authors' family-tree data. Firm- and director-level data came from the Korea Listed Companies Association (KLCA) and Korea Investors Services (KIS) databases, with KIS preferred when the two disagreed about when a board member was appointed. A director counted as appointed in a focal year if he or she was not a board member of that firm in the previous year. Controlling-family trees came from *The Chaebol of Korea* (Kim et al., 2005) and *Chaebol-Ga* (Seoul News Corporation, 2005), supplemented by media reports and internet search engines; director human-capital data came primarily from KIS and secondarily from the Joins search engine. Private firms and financial-sector firms were excluded. The resulting structure is 33 family-controlled business groups and 155 firms.
- Two analytic samples, each with its own count. The primary sample for the pooled probit and multinomial probit models (Table 3) is 603 board appointment events, of which 7.96% (n = 48) were family members and the remainder were nonfamily insiders. The supplementary discrete-time event history analysis (Table 4) uses a separately constructed firm-year panel of 756 firm-year observations, in which family member appointment is treated as a firm-level event without comparison to nonfamily appointments.
- Dependent variables. Pooled probit (H1a–H5a): binary, 0 = appointment of a nonfamily inside director, 1 = appointment of a family director. Pooled multinomial probit (H1b–H5b): categorical, 0 = nonfamily inside director, 1 = earlier-generation family member, 2 = later-generation family member; Model 5 of Table 3 takes earlier-generation family appointments as the baseline (comparison) outcome. Discrete-time event history models: binary indicator = 1 if there was a family member appointment in the firm-year, 0 otherwise. Nonfamily *inside* directors, not outside directors, were the baseline comparison group because they are more comparable to family directors in their potential to be groomed for leadership.
- Construct coding of the family. Controlling family members are the descendants of the business group's founder, including those born into the family (children, siblings) and those who joined by marriage (spouses, in-laws); three appointees in the sample joined by marriage. Following Fiss and Zajac (2004), the first and second generations were coded as the "earlier generation" and family members beyond the second generation as the "later generation."
- Independent variables. Firm market performance = an approximation of Tobin's Q (Chung & Pruitt, 1994), calculated as the sum of the market value of common stock, book value of preferred stock, and total debt over total assets, chosen as a forward-looking rather than accounting-based measure. Firm reputation = a binary variable coded 1 if the firm appeared on the "Korea's Most Admired Companies" list published annually by Korea Management Association Consulting (the South Korean equivalent of *Fortune*'s "Most Admired Corporations" list), which rests on survey ratings by executives, financial analysts, and consumers across social, shareholder, employee, customer, image, and innovation value. Intragroup board interlock centrality = degree centrality in the intragroup board interlock network, calculated as the total number of board positions that a focal firm's board members held at other firms within the same business group, entered in logarithmic form. Financial distress = a dummy equal to 1 if net income was negative in the year prior to the appointment event. Number of existing family directors = a count of existing family members serving on the focal firm's board; because board size is always controlled, the variable assesses the number of existing family directors scaled by board size.
- Controls. Firm level: firm age, firm size (log of total assets), sales growth, leverage (total debt over total assets), stock market beta, R&D intensity, current ratio, board size, number of outside directors, controlling-family voting rights, and institutional ownership. Business group level: number of affiliated firms and family size (controlling family members aged 35–65). Individual level, in the pooled probit models only: director age, work experience, work experience within the business group, education level, elite degree, MBA degree, foreign degree, and initial appointment. Year fixed effects were included in the Table 3 models but not reported.
- Estimation and identification. Pooled probit models test H1a–H5a; pooled multinomial probit models test H1b–H5b; random effects probit discrete-time event history models (Allison, 1984) provide an additional test of H1a–H5a on the firm-year panel and absorb unobserved firm heterogeneity. Fixed-effects models were not feasible because many sample firms experienced no family appointment event during the period. All models use robust standard errors clustered at the firm level, and the board appointment events comprising the dependent variables were always measured one year after the predictors (t + 1), which is equivalent to lagging all predictors and which the authors describe as mitigating reverse-causality concerns. No instrumental variable, difference-in-differences, or other causal-identification strategy is claimed, so the design is associational. The highest mean VIF in any model was 1.8 and the maximum VIF for a single variable (firm size) was 4.11.
- Robustness checks reported: using all nonfamily directors (inside and outside) as the comparison group; an industry-adjusted Tobin's Q; the proportion of family directors on the board instead of the count; separate controls for family ownership and affiliated-firm ownership; omitting the three appointees who joined the family by marriage; and a logit rather than a probit link (probit fit better on the Bayesian information criterion, with similar support under logit).

**Key Findings**
- Support for the five main hypotheses (family versus nonfamily insider appointment) is partial, not uniform: H3a, H4a, and H5a are supported, H1a is only marginally supported, and H2a is not supported.
- H1a (firm market performance) — marginally supported. Tobin's Q is positive and marginally significant in the pooled probit (b = 0.27, p < .1; Model 1 of Table 3) and positive and marginally significant in the discrete-time event history analysis (b = 0.21, p < .1; Model 1 of Table 4); the authors state that this pattern "lends marginal support" rather than full support.
- H2a (firm reputation) — not supported. The relationship is not statistically significant in Model 1 of Table 3 or Model 1 of Table 4.
- H3a (intragroup board interlock centrality) — supported. Positive and significant in the pooled probit (b = 0.68, p < .01) and in the event history analysis (b = 0.67, p < .01). Moving centrality from a low level (no interlocks) to a high level (mean + 2 SD) raises the predicted probability of appointing a family member from 4% to 19% (Model 1 of Table 3).
- H4a (financial distress) — supported, with the predicted negative sign. Negative and significant in the pooled probit (b = −0.90, p < .05) and in the event history analysis (b = −1.08, p < .01).
- H5a (number of existing family directors) — supported, with the predicted negative sign. Negative and significant in the pooled probit (b = −0.35, p < .05) and in the random effects probit estimation (b = −0.43, p < .05). The predicted probability of a family board appointment falls from 11% when there are no existing family directors to 4% at a high number of existing family directors (mean + 2 SD).
- The generational hypotheses, tested by multinomial probit with earlier-generation family appointments as the baseline (Model 5 of Table 3), are likewise only partly supported: H1b supported (Tobin's Q b = 1.21, p < .05), H2b supported (firm reputation b = 4.39, p < .01), H4b supported (financial distress b = −2.49, p < .05), while H3b is not supported (intragroup centrality not statistically significant) and H5b is not supported (number of family directors not statistically significant).
- Generational effect sizes (Model 3 of Table 3). Moving Tobin's Q from a low (25th percentile) to a high (75th percentile) level raises the predicted probability of a later-generation appointment from 3% to 8%, and Tobin's Q is not significantly related to earlier-generation appointments (Model 2 of Table 3), so the authors read the preferential appointment of family members to well-performing firms as driven by later generations. Moving from a low-reputation to a high-reputation firm raises the predicted probability of a later-generation appointment from 3% to 12%. A later-generation family member's predicted probability of joining a firm falls from 5% when the firm is not in financial distress to 1% when it shows visible signs of distress.
- Two of the five patterns do not differ by generation. Family members' selection into highly interlocked firms (H3) is not driven by generation, and avoidance of companies with existing family directors (H5) also does not differ across generations.
- Unhypothesized result: family members are less likely to be appointed to boards of firms where institutional investors hold higher levels of ownership, and this tendency is stronger for later- than for earlier-generation family members — a pattern the authors describe as consistent with an agency theory perspective.
- Overall, the authors conclude that they "found support for our strategic nepotism view": family members are preferentially given access to attractive boards where they can build and protect managerial resources, and are systematically less likely to join potentially stigmatizing board positions, with strategic nepotism particularly salient for later-generation family members. Because the design is associational and lagged rather than causally identified, these are estimated associations, not identified causal effects.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study introduces "strategic nepotism" to extend the SEW/nepotism-mixed-gamble literature by recasting controlling families as proactive actors who manage the endogenous process of family director selection rather than passive beneficiaries of nepotism. It opens the "black box" of how family involvement arises, brings the SEW perspective into business-group research, reframes family business groups as internal labor markets for grooming family heirs, and supplies a previously neglected SEW-based account of director selection in contexts where family principals dominate.

**Practical Implication**
Families controlling businesses are advised to approach board appointments and family members' career paths strategically and dynamically — especially for later generations — since deliberately nurtured heirs are more likely to develop into qualified executives with firm-specific human and social capital, helping reconcile tensions between SEW and financial wealth.

**Limitations**
The study relies on archival data, so it infers underlying motives (e.g., stigma avoidance) from observed patterns rather than measuring them directly, and real-world motives may be more complex (e.g., avoiding intra-family conflict or dividing labor among kin). Because the data are drawn solely from South Korea, the generalizability of the theory and findings to family firms and business groups in other institutional and cultural contexts is an open question.

**Future Research**
Future work could use surveys, interviews, and ethnographic observation to uncover controlling families' fine-grained motives and intentions, and test the theory cross-nationally to establish institutional and cultural boundary conditions. Scholars could also extend strategic nepotism to multi-business family firms (e.g., assigning kin to favorable business units), and examine whether strategic nepotism actually yields the intended downstream outcomes — improved managerial resources and more positive stakeholder evaluations.
