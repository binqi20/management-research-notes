# The Fragility of Experts: A Moderated-Mediation Model of Expertise, Expert Identity Threat, and Overprecision

**Abstract**
Experts play a crucial role in modern organizations, but evidence regarding the soundness and reliability of their decision-making is mixed and often contradictory. We develop and test a moderated-mediation model of expert decision-making linking expertise, identity threat, and overprecision to understand when and why experts offer overly precise judgments, and how they can cope with disconfirming feedback. We find support for this model in a series of lab experiments which show that (a) experts are more likely than novices to double down and produce overly precise predictions following disconfirming feedback, (b) this feedback-induced overprecision by experts is mediated by perceived level of expert identity threat, (c) the source of the feedback matters for identity threat and overprecision, and (d) self-affirmation attenuates identity threat and reduces overprecision. We supplement these experimental findings by investigating experts' response to disconfirming feedback in two real-world settings: Major League Baseball umpiring and chief financial officer predictions of stock market returns. Our model and results show that feedback can harm expert decision-making by leading experts to be overly precise in their judgment, challenging existing notions on the ability of expert decision-makers, and providing insight into when and why experts should be relied upon in organizational decisions.

**Research Question**
When and why do experts make sound versus unsound decisions following negative performance feedback? Specifically, the paper asks whether disconfirming feedback leads experts (more than novices) to "double down" and become overprecise in their judgments, whether perceived expert identity threat mediates this effect, and how the feedback source and self-affirmation moderate it.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Experts generate more overprecise predictions after they receive disconfirming feedback than before they receive disconfirming feedback, and this effect is greater than in novices.
- H2: Expert identity threat mediates the positive relationship between expertise and overprecision following disconfirming feedback.
- H3: The indirect effect of negative feedback on expert overprecision via identity threat is stronger when the credibility of the source of the feedback is higher.
- H4: The indirect effect of expertise on overprecision via identity threat is weaker when experts engage in self-affirmation prior to receiving negative feedback.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Expertise (expert vs. novice, operationalized via domain experience in Study 1 and via priming/manipulation in Studies 2-4); disconfirming (negative) performance feedback (pre- vs. post-feedback, within-subjects).
- DV(s): Overprecision (excessive certainty in one's judgments), measured primarily via the Subjective Probability Interval Elicitation (SPIES) method and an overprecision-count alternative; in archival studies, umpire "border certainty" (deviation from 0.5) and CFO confidence-interval miscalibration.
- Mediators: Perceived expert identity threat.
- Moderators: Source credibility of the feedback (group of experts vs. group of novices vs. actual results); self-affirmation (vs. control); monetary reward (tested, but ineffective).

Negative performance feedback poses a salient threat to people's expert identity; because experts' self-concept is grounded in superior knowledge and competence, they experience a more acute threat than novices and respond by reaffirming self-worth—boosting confidence and issuing narrower, overly precise predictions. This identity-threat mechanism is amplified when feedback comes from credible sources (other experts or actual outcomes) and attenuated when experts self-affirm core values beforehand, which restores global self-integrity and removes the need to assert expert identity through overconfidence.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (baseball play prediction, pre-registered): 191 participants recruited from a behavioral lab session in exchange for course credits; 48 excluded for failing the attention check and one for not agreeing to participate, leaving 142 usable participants (44% male, mean age 20.92). Design: 2 (expertise: expert vs. novice) x 2 (feedback timing: pre vs. post) mixed, with feedback timing within subjects. Expertise = self-reported years watching or playing baseball or softball, treated categorically at the sample mean (five-year cutoff; experts M = 10.74, SD = 4.49; novices M = 0.73, SD = 1.14). Expert identity threat = four 7-point items ("threatening," "challenging," "disappointing," "threatening to your sense of competence") from Green et al. (2017), averaged (alpha = .87, M = 2.80, SD = 1.71). Overprecision = SPIES (Haran, Moore, & Morewedge, 2010), calculated as the average difference between peak confidence and the confidence assigned to the correct outcome; robustness measure = overprecision count, the inverse of the number of options chosen. Disconfirming feedback = a computer-generated message that the participant's predictions had very low agreement with four experts. Controls: self-reported age and the short PANAS scale.
- Study 2 (entrepreneurial pitch evaluation, pre-registered): 224 recruited; 42 excluded for failing the attention check and nine for prior exposure to a pitch video, leaving 173 usable participants (42% male, mean age 20.90). Design: 3 (expert-prime: expert vs. novice vs. control) x 2 (feedback timing) mixed, expertise manipulated between subjects by a writing task. Manipulation checks: perceived expertise (Tormala & Petty, 2007; 9-point) and two expert-identification items from Doosje, Ellemers, and Spears (1995). Four pitches drawn from TechCrunch Disrupt Startup Battlefield; prediction accuracy scored against each startup's operating and funding status as of December 2019 from Crunchbase. Same SPIES dependent variable as Study 1.
- Study 3 (source of disconfirming feedback, pre-registered): 135 recruited; 28 excluded for failing the attention check and three for prior exposure to a pitch video, leaving 104 usable participants (47% male, mean age 22.04). Design: 3 (feedback source: group of experts vs. group of novices vs. actual results) x 2 (feedback timing) mixed, with all participants completing the expertise-priming writing task. Source credibility manipulation check adapted from Smith, De Houwer, and Nosek (2013) ("trustworthy," "informative"; alpha = .96, M = 4.08, SD = 2.06).
- Study 4 (self-affirmation and rewards, pre-registered): 140 recruited; 29 excluded for failing the attention check and one for prior exposure to a pitch video, leaving 110 usable participants (39% male, mean age 21.50). Design: 2 (self-affirmation: yes vs. no) x 2 (reward: yes vs. no) x 2 (feedback timing) mixed. Self-affirmation = choosing four of six values excerpted from Cohen et al. (2000), then a 15-minute expressive writing task describing incidents of upholding them, administered before the feedback; control participants wrote about what they ate or drank in the past 48 hours. Reward = notice that winners would receive a $5 Starbucks gift card. Mediation in Studies 1-4 estimated with Hayes's (2012) PROCESS macro (Model 4) and 95% bias-corrected bootstrapped confidence intervals.
- Study 5 (MLB umpires, archival): pitch data downloaded from MLB's Gameday for the 408 games in which an umpire ejected a player or coach between 2014 and 2018, as recorded by the Umpire Ejection Fantasy League Portal, each matched to a control game, for a total sample of 816 games. Dependent variable = "border certainty," the absolute deviation from .5 of the called-strike probability estimated by a generalized additive model fitted separately to pre- and post-ejection pitches. Estimation = difference-in-differences OLS on observed pitches on or near the rule-book border with umpire fixed effects (Model 1a) or fixed effects for each of the 705 games in that estimation sample (Model 1b), both on 1,738 observations, plus a model over 1,616 hypothetical on-border pitches with location fixed effects. This is a matched-sample quasi-experiment, not a randomized design.
- Study 6 (CFO forecasts, archival): the Duke CFO Global Business Outlook Survey, which polls between 2,000 and 3,000 U.S. CFOs each quarter, using their point forecast of the average annual S&P 500 return and its 80% confidence interval from 2004 to 2019, giving 61 quarterly observations. Overprecision = whether the true returns in the subsequent year fell outside the CFOs' stated 80% confidence interval; disconfirming feedback = a dummy for whether the annualized three-month return before survey t fell short of the prediction made at survey t-1, with the absolute shortfall as an alternative independent variable and interval width as an alternative dependent variable; all models include quarter fixed effects.

**Key Findings**
- H1 (expertise x feedback timing) was supported in Study 2 but only partially in Study 1. In Study 1 the interaction was significant (unstandardized b = 20.51, SE = 5.49, t = 3.73, p < .001) and the pre-to-post change was larger for experts than for novices (p = .007), but post-hoc Tukey tests showed that experts' own increase was not statistically different (29.45% to 32.10%; MD = 2.65, SE = 4.35, p = .93) while novices' overprecision fell significantly (40.09% to 22.22%; MD = 17.86, SE = 3.36, p < .001), which the authors describe as "partially supporting Hypothesis 1." In Study 2 expert-primed participants rose from 16.28% to 27.14% overprecision (MD = 10.86, SE = 3.96, p = .037, Cohen's d = .52) and exceeded novice-primed participants post-feedback (MD = 13.95, SE = 3.83, p = .002, d = .69), with a significant interaction (b = 20.10, SE = 5.42, t = 3.71, p < .001).
- H2 (mediation by expert identity threat) was supported in both experiments, but in a different form in each: full mediation in Study 1 (total effect 9.88, SE = 4.38, p = .03; direct effect nonsignificant at 3.30, SE = 3.60, p = .36; indirect effect 6.58, SE = 3.00, CI [1.97, 14.08]) and only partial mediation in Study 2, where the direct effect of expertise remained significant (8.85, SE = 3.14, p = .01) alongside a significant identity-threat path (b = 6.10, SE = 1.30, p < .001).
- H3 (source credibility) was supported in Study 3 on the SPIES measure but not on the alternative overprecision-count measure. Post-feedback overprecision was 37.74% with expert-source feedback and 34.50% with actual-results feedback, versus 17.19% with novice-source feedback (MD expert vs. novice source = 20.55, SE = 6.94, p = .011, d = .73; MD actual results vs. novice source = 17.31, SE = 7.09, p = .043, d = .64), and the indirect effects through expert identity threat were 18.51 (SE = 5.52, CI [9.47, 31.76]) and 12.57 (SE = 5.58, CI [3.74, 26.68]) with both direct effects nonsignificant. The overprecision-count results were in the expected direction but not statistically significant (p = .072 and p = .212).
- H4 (self-affirmation) was supported in Study 4: self-affirmed experts' post-feedback overprecision was 14.34% versus 31.75% for controls (MD = 17.42, SE = 4.58, p = .001, d = .61), the total effect of self-affirmation on SPIES was negative and significant (effect = -17.42, SE = 5.56, t = -3.13, p < .001), and its direct effect became nonsignificant (effect = -9.80, SE = 5.27, t = -1.86, p = .07) once expert identity threat was accounted for. Monetary rewards did not mitigate doubling down (mean difference 9.12, SE = 4.48, p = .18) and did not interact with self-affirmation (p = .995).
- The two archival settings are consistent with H1 but with weaker statistical support. In Study 5 the post-ejection by ejection-game interaction on umpire border certainty was positive and significant in the model with umpire fixed effects (.03, p = .04), larger but not statistically different from zero at the .05 level with game fixed effects (.04, p = .07), and significant for the hypothetical on-border pitches (p < .001); a footnote also reports that border confidence was significantly lower in ejection games overall. In Study 6, when the prior quarter's returns fell below what CFOs had projected, the chance that their prediction was overprecise increased by 26% (p = .044), while the disconfirming-returns dummy had no relevant effect on interval width (p = .88) and larger shortfalls were associated with wider confidence intervals.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces expert identity threat as a motivational (not merely cognitive or task-based) factor explaining when experts exhibit sound versus distorted decision-making, integrating identity-threat and self-affirmation theory into the literature on expertise and overconfidence. It reframes overconfidence as a motivated, identity-protective response to threatened valued identities rather than only a heuristic bias or strategic response to external incentives, and shows that performance feedback—long viewed as a debiasing tool—can paradoxically worsen expert judgment.

**Practical Implication**
Managers and organizations that rely on experts (e.g., professional service firms, hospitals) should recognize that bluntly confronting experts with their errors can backfire by triggering identity-protective overprecision. Framing feedback as constructive learning opportunities, deploying self-affirmation interventions that reaffirm individual and organizational values, and cultivating psychological safety can mitigate identity threat and reduce experts' tendency to double down. Monetary incentives, by contrast, did not curb the bias.

**Limitations**
The prediction tasks studied were genuinely difficult with low accuracy rates (often under 40%, and under 20% in the investment task), so a ceiling effect means the findings may not generalize to high-accuracy, routine judgment tasks—an important boundary condition. Providing identical negative feedback to all participants regardless of actual performance limits external validity and raises the possibility of demotivation/frustration artifacts, though identity threat fully mediated the effect. The study examined only one defensive response (overprecision) among many possible forms of defensiveness.

**Future Research**
Future work could extend the identity-threat model to other forms of identity threat (e.g., status loss, rivalry, race- or gender-based stereotype threat) and to inequality within organizations, and could test the model with other defensive behaviors such as rejection, denigration, discounting, derogation, or reframing. Researchers could also examine how the correctness of feedback interacts with identity concerns, particularly for tasks with higher baseline accuracy.
