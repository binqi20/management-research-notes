# No Change Is an Island: How Interferences between Change Initiatives Evoke Inconsistencies That Undermine Implementation

**Abstract**
Organizational change research has concentrated on the challenges of implementing isolated changes, paying little attention to the interactions among concurrent change initiatives. Our longitudinal real-time study of a multinational technology firm examines how two corporate change initiatives interfered with each other. The interfering initiatives provoked inconsistency judgments (cognitive, normative, and procedural) and the emergence of collective emotions that undermined change performance. Top managers’ responses fueled the sharing of inconsistency judgments and emotions that fed into a recursive process that, over time, provoked emotional uncertainty, elicited moral emotions, and eroded emotional attachment to change. Our process model reveals inconsistency judgments as a previously overlooked socio-psychological mechanism underpinning interferences between change initiatives. We reveal the limitations of examining organizational change in terms of isolated initiatives and call for research that considers the dynamics between change initiatives.

**Research Question**
How do interferences between change initiatives emerge? And what are the mechanisms linking those interferences to change outcomes?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper — this is an inductive/abductive, longitudinal real-time single-case study with no a priori hypotheses and no formally numbered propositions. The authors pose two research questions and build a process model (Figure 3) after the fact; the figure's own note describes its solid arrows as "suggested main relationships between the themes" and its dashed arrows as "suggested feedback loops," and the analysis is explicitly described as moving in iterative cycles between literature and empirical themes rather than testing stated predictions.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: Interfering organizational change initiatives — two quasi-concurrent strategic initiatives (Project Foundation, an internally focused culture/process change, and Project Rebranding) whose separate, quasi-autonomous execution generated friction rather than fit.
- Key constructs / judgments: Perceived inconsistencies between initiatives, coded as cognitive (lack of agreement in content), procedural (lack of agreement in required actions), and normative (lack of agreement in norms and values).
- Emotional reactions: Three shared emotional processes — uncertainty-related (confusion, worry), moral-related (indignation, shock), and detachment-related (annoyance, apathy).
- Social sharing activities: Challenging, reproaching, and distancing — recursive sharing that spreads cues and fosters collective emotions, amplified by internal social media.
- Top managers’ responses: Ambiguous top-down communication and restricted bottom-up communication that fueled rather than calmed negativity.
- Emergent outcome: Collective (dis)engagement from change initiatives, producing underperformance in speed, costs, and impact.

Employees socially construct the meaning of concurrent initiatives using consistency criteria; when initiatives are judged inconsistent, this triggers negative emotions that are shared and converge into collective emotions. Top managers’ responses act as triggers that amplify or dampen this sharing, and the resulting collective (dis)engagement — not abstract cognition alone — drives change outcomes through a recursive, self-reinforcing process operating over time.

**Data & Measures**
- Site and design: one site only — TechCorp, a business-to-business technology manufacturer with revenues of more than $10 billion per year, part of a multi-industry conglomerate, with a strong engineering tradition and a culture several employees described as hierarchical and bureaucratic. The study is a longitudinal, real-time single case (Yin, 1994) of the interaction between two change initiatives, supported by an in-depth case description (Langley, 1999).
- Interviews (Table 2, N = 94): 94 semi-structured interviews with 65 informants drawn from different hierarchical levels and functions, including top managers, middle managers, change agents, and employees, conducted over a period of three years and distributed as n = 19 in year 1 (initiation of Project Foundation), n = 35 in year 2 (during initiation of Project Rebranding), and n = 40 in year 3. Interviews lasted between 20 minutes and 2 hours, were audio-recorded and transcribed, focused on the change initiatives (protocol in Appendix A), and core informants were interviewed two or three times to capture evolving reactions over time.
- Open survey responses (Table 2, N = 1,684): anonymous open-ended responses across three waves — n = 343 in year 1 (initiation of Foundation), n = 687 in year 2 (interference of Rebranding), and n = 654 in year 3 (interaction of the initiatives). One survey ran several months before the rebranding was announced and all the others after it; questions are in Appendix B. Footnote 1 states the questionnaire also contained quantitative measures that are not part of this analysis.
- Archival, observational, and video data (Table 2): 29 corporate documents (slides on both initiatives, employee email newsletters, further change communication material such as posters and storyboards, intranet/social-network content such as FAQs); 53 further archival items (newspaper articles, comments on web blogs, annual reports, plus external documents consulted to understand the competitive environment); 84 hours of observations and field notes from middle-manager/change-agent meetings, informal post-meeting discussions, telephone conferences, change-implementation trainings and workshops, and member-checking feedback sessions (Table 2 records 64 pages of notes while the Field observations passage records 63 pages); and 2 videos (the employee rebranding ceremony and middle managers' video messages promoting Foundation). Quotes are labelled by source: Doc_ (documents), Int_ (interviews), FiOb_ (field observations), Sur_ (employee surveys), Vid_ (video material).
- Coding procedure: abductive analysis (Alvesson & Kärreman, 2007; Bamberger, 2018) moving in iterative cycles (Miles & Huberman, 1994) between the relevant literature and empirical themes. The first step was to code interviews and open survey comments using an open coding approach (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) and to collect all comments related to both initiatives; quotes relevant to the research question were then compared with one another and used to develop more abstract themes (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), producing the Figure 1 data structure of first-order concepts, second-order themes, and aggregate dimensions.
- Inconsistency judgments (focal construct): defined as "the subjective perception that two (or more) change initiatives lack agreement, and their relationship is somewhat contradictory," collected from informants and then categorized in an iterative coding process as "cognitive" (related to the initiatives' content), "normative" (related to their values and norms), or "procedural" (related to their required actions); Table 3 states each category's definition and gives illustrative interview, survey, and field-observation excerpts.
- Emotions (focal construct): specific emotional expressions were coded on the basis of cognitive appraisal theories of emotion (Ellsworth & Scherer, 2003), using appraisal dimensions of pleasantness, control, certainty, goal congruence, and compatibility with social norms, and then aggregated into three broader categories. Table 4 records each category's appraisal profile and illustrative quotes — for example, "Uncertainty-related emotions: unpleasant feeling states; uncertainty about goals and outcomes; rather skeptical about impact."
- Remaining themes coded from the same corpus: social sharing activities (challenging, reproaching, distancing), top managers' responses (ambiguous top-down communication; restricted bottom-up communication), and outcomes (collective (dis)engagement from change initiatives; change initiative outcomes, including underperformance in terms of speed, costs, and impact).
- Supplementary quantitative check (footnote 3, not the main analysis): a two-wave survey in one business unit, analyzed with paired t tests on matched data from 170 employees five months before and five months after the rebranding, using statements rated on a 1-to-5 agreement scale.
- Identification: none claimed. This is an interpretive design and the paper makes no causal-identification claim; the model's links are labelled "suggested" relationships and feedback loops. Trustworthiness was pursued through triangulation across sources (Eisenhardt, Graebner, & Sonenshein, 2016), repeated site visits by the first and third authors, member checking with a contact team that was not part of top management, and real-time collection intended to reduce ex post rationalization and retrospective bias (Golden-Biddle & Locke, 2007). Data collection stopped once the authors judged the data sufficient to support their theorizing. The country of the site is not reported.

**Key Findings**
- Origin of the interference: top managers thought of Foundation and Rebranding as coherent sub-initiatives under the umbrella of one strategic vision, but delegated responsibility for driving them to different departments — the strategy department (with local HR support) for Foundation, and a team of top managers with marketing support for Rebranding — so the two were developed and enacted separately. That separation resulted in their quasi-autonomous execution, with the middle managers in charge focusing narrowly on their own initiative and ignoring how its activities might affect the other. Rebranding was announced one year after Foundation and was a surprise to everyone outside top management (including the researchers); its introduction caused internal "paralysis" and Foundation lost momentum.
- Temporal shift in the data: in the early phases the authors found few inconsistency codes and several positive emotional codes; they report a substantial increase in inconsistency judgments and negative emotions following the introduction of Rebranding.
- Three types of inconsistency judgment emerged (Table 3). Cognitive — a perceived lack of agreement in the initiatives' content (the "what": motives, objectives, scope); employees used words such as "unaligned," "clashing," and "conflicting," construed the initiatives as substituting for or competing with one another, and one leader saw "more friction than fit." Procedural — a perceived lack of agreement in the activities required to reach each initiative's objectives (the "how": integration, coordination, resources); Foundation follow-up meetings were cancelled for lack of clarity on how to proceed, HR plans built on Foundation had to be reworked around new employer-branding content, and departments clashed over money and employees' time, ending up executing different activities. Normative — a perceived lack of agreement in the values and norms the two initiatives conveyed; the live-streamed rebranding ceremony, with dancers and a corporate song, was widely judged a poor fit for the culture articulated in Foundation, with employees invoking values such as "lean" and "listening," high-quality work, long-term orientation, and engineering excellence.
- Inconsistency judgments elicited three shared emotional processes (Table 4): uncertainty-related emotions (confusion, worry), particularly salient among middle managers expected to enact the changes amid the turmoil; moral-related emotions (indignation, shock), with employees repeatedly invoking "hypocrisy" and the contradiction between what Foundation "preached" and their experience of Rebranding; and detachment-related emotions (annoyance, apathy), including among middle managers who had felt excited and optimistic about Foundation early on and now described a "cooling down."
- Social sharing made these judgments and emotions collective through three activities: challenging (questioning the initiatives' content in meetings, intranet posts, and emails), reproaching (criticism aimed at top managers — including an anonymous letter of complaint to the board that was posted on the intranet, shared through social media, and leaked to the press), and distancing (verbal dismissal of the changes as "uninspiring," "generic," and "vague," plus observable low-attachment actions such as senior managers not attending workshops labelled "high priority"). Internal social media, group instant messaging, and blogs intensified and accelerated the process, and conversations about inconsistencies often ended with employees feeling more negative about the initiatives, even among groups that had been relatively neutral at the start.
- Top managers' responses amplified rather than calmed the negativity. Ambiguous top-down communication — hesitant, delayed, non-transparent responses, a communication-vetting decision that further delayed messages, and the decision to keep the rebranding without conveying the reasoning after middle managers called for its reversal — spurred sharing activities. Restricted bottom-up communication — little engagement with employees' upward communication, attempts to restrict intranet debate, and the deletion of a critical open letter to the CEO whose author was confronted, relieved of duties, and finally fired — widened perceived distance between levels and amplified feelings of uncertainty and detachment. The authors argue top managers could instead have used sensebreaking or sense specification, but appear to have engaged in "sensehiding."
- First outcome — collective (dis)engagement: over time an increasing number of employees disengaged. Employees who had been active in implementing change doubted its vision and relevance, ignored requests, ended or put on hold ongoing activities in their units, and withdrew support and resources; even people who initially championed the change became disengaged and hesitant, caught between delivering results for top managers and absorbing criticism from below.
- Second outcome — change underperformance in terms of speed, costs, and impact, with Foundation suffering in particular: three years after initiation its progress was considerably behind expectations, more than a few manufacturing sites had not undergone planned training or performance-management interventions, several key activities were cancelled or left incomplete because of scoping conflicts and lack of resources, and the adjustments, legal branding issues, restructurings, and communication efforts resulted in "exceeding budgets."
- Remedial adaptations partly backfired: top managers sanctioned middle managers in charge (one communication manager's abrupt and forced exit was perceived as a "cheap" attempt to deflect responsibility), consolidated responsibility for both initiatives into a single team working closely with them, and invested in a consistent connecting narrative and a brand education program. When that failed to lift progress, they launched additional change projects, some framed under new labels but in fact addressing the same topics as Foundation — which refuelled the perception and sharing of inconsistency rather than resolving it.
- Supplementary quantitative corroboration (footnote 3): in the two-wave survey of one business unit, paired t tests on 170 matched employees showed agreement significantly lower five months after the rebranding announcement than five months before — "All in all, Project Foundation will add value for TechCorp" moved from M = 3.66 (SD = 1.01) to M = 3.46 (SD = 1.12), t(169) = 2.52, p < .01, and "Overall, I could name more opportunities than challenges in relation to Project Foundation for TechCorp" moved from M = 3.71 (SD = 0.90) to M = 3.46 (SD = 1.00), t(169) = 3.01, p < .01. The authors present this only as validating their observation that collective responses shifted, not as a test of the process model.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends theories of navigating large-scale change by developing a consistency perspective on interacting change initiatives, identifying inconsistency judgments (cognitive, normative, procedural) as a previously overlooked socio-psychological mechanism through which one change project interferes with another. It also contributes to the literature on emotions and organizational change by showing how perceptions of inconsistency trigger emotional reactions and how social sharing and top managers’ responses amplify the emergence of collective emotions during change.

**Practical Implication**
Top managers should anticipate that strategic change carried out through multiple initiatives can breed perceptions of inconsistency, and should respond to recipients’ emotional cues with purposeful sensegiving (sensebreaking, sense specification) rather than restricting dialogue. Establishing a centralized transformation team to monitor cross-initiative interactions and address both task- and emotion-related issues can attenuate inconsistency perceptions and sustain momentum.

**Limitations**
The study has the usual limitations of a single case study, so its insights need validation in other settings. The authors cannot entirely rule out that the observed outcomes were also driven by other factors, such as the cultural context of the studied firm.

**Future Research**
The authors invite research testing whether similar inconsistency judgments arise when a change is perceived as inconsistent with an organization’s history or identity (rather than another change), and on hypocrisy between espoused values and leaders’ cues. They also call for studies of "enabling interactions" in strategic initiative portfolios, a narrative/rhetorical lens on the temporal interplay of framing and retelling, and the dynamics of positive, future-oriented emotions (e.g., hope, optimism) — with social media as a contextual factor.
