# Big Fish versus Big Pond? Entrepreneurs, Established Firms, and Antecedents of Tie Formation

**Abstract**
Entrepreneurial and established firms form collaborative relationships to commercialize products. Through such ties, entrepreneurs seek (a) development help to hone ideas into marketable products and (b) access to markets. In most cases, entrepreneurs face a trade-off: they can be a big fish in a small pond, getting more attention and development help from a smaller firm with less market access, or a small fish in a big pond, getting less attention and help from a larger firm with more market access. Our study investigates what goes into choosing between these options. Drawing from resource dependence theory and an empirical study of tie formation between developers and publishers of PlayStation 2 video games, we develop and test a framework that identifies the key decision variables and focuses on two moderators—resource need evolution and resource uncertainty related to competition—that explain whether a big fish (more development help) or a big pond (more market access) drives tie formation. Our findings point to prospective peers as one of the significant decision criteria at tie formation and highlight the dynamic nature of resource dependence. Altogether, the results give resource dependence theory a dynamic element it has lacked in the past.

**Research Question**
What conditions lead an entrepreneur to become a big fish in a small pond (prioritizing development help) versus a small fish in a big pond (prioritizing market access) when forming a tie with an established firm? Specifically, how do anticipated development help and market access drive tie formation, and how are these weightings moderated by an entrepreneur's evolving resource needs and competition-induced resource uncertainty?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The more development help the entrepreneur anticipates from the established firm, the more likely the tie between the entrepreneur and the established firm.
Hypothesis 2. The higher the market access that the entrepreneur anticipates from the established firm, the more likely the tie between the entrepreneur and the established firm.
Hypothesis 3. The more evolved the entrepreneur's resource needs, the more heavily market access (big pond) is weighted over development help (big fish) in tie formation decisions between entrepreneurs and established firms.
Hypothesis 4. The higher the resource uncertainty due to competition, the more heavily development help (big fish) is weighted over market access (big pond) in tie formation decisions between entrepreneurs and established firms.
The interview component states no separate propositions of its own; the paper reports the interviews as grounding for the quantitative analyses of these four hypotheses.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Anticipated development help (H1); anticipated market access (H2)
- DV(s): Tie formation between developer (entrepreneur) and publisher (established firm) in year *t*
- Moderators: Entrepreneur's resource need evolution (product experience) (H3); competition-induced resource uncertainty (anticipated game overlap) (H4)
- Controls: Prior collaboration, in-house development, quality difference, geographic distance, same country, established firm size/experience/portfolio size, entrepreneur size, year fixed effects

Resource dependence drives entrepreneurs and established firms to form ties to satisfy mutual resource needs: entrepreneurs supply novel ideas but lack development resources and market access, which established firms control. Because personalized development help is rationed in rank order across the established firm's portfolio of prospective peers, while market access is scalable, entrepreneurs face the "big fish, big pond" trade-off. The paper argues resource dependence is dynamic: as entrepreneurs gain experience their needs shift from internal (development) to external (market) resources (H3), and as competitive intensity rises the value of personalized, differentiating development help increases (H4), so the relative weighting of the two resources at tie formation shifts accordingly.

**Data & Measures**
The quantitative data are a dyad-year panel built on the complete global population of firms that commercialized PlayStation 2 games, 367 developers and 170 publishers, over the 10-year period from 2000 to 2009, containing 1,416 observed ties. MobyGames was the primary source, triangulated with Factiva, GiantBomb, IGN, AllGame, Wikipedia and individual firm websites; the "unseen64" database supplied games cancelled after an initial tie had formed (26 such ties were added in an alternate test), and the Internet Archive covered firms that were no longer active. The analysis unit is a yearly developer-by-publisher matrix of all firms at risk of forming a tie, so unrealized dyads are included alongside realized ones. The qualitative component is a separate corpus of 30 interviews, conducted in two waves with informants on both sides of the relationship (publisher executives, publisher-developer and developer staff from founder-CEOs to programmers, game-engine developers) plus industry experts such as journalists, a venture capitalist and industry researchers, each listed with role, industry experience, location and interview length in Table 6; two of the authors also attended game conventions. Every interview used the same set of open-ended questions about expected and offered partner resources and partner worries. The paper reports no formal coding scheme for this material and presents it as illustrative quotes that ground the quantitative model.

Measures. The DV, tie formation, is a dyad-year dummy equal to 1 if a developer and a publisher formed a tie to develop one or more PS2 games in year t and 0 otherwise, with the game release year used as the year of collaboration (a count of ties produced similar results). Anticipated development help (H1) is the focal developer's rank inside the prospective publisher's portfolio: the number of "inferior" developers divided by the total number of developers affiliated with that publisher (0.6 if three of five portfolio developers scored lower), set to 1 when there were no other developers, where developer quality comes from MobyGames review scores normalized to a 1-100 scale and averaged over the three years prior to year t using a Bush and Mosteller (1955) average-updating procedure that weights consistent quality more heavily. Anticipated market access (H2) is the average number of reviews (in hundreds) received by the publisher's games in the three years prior to year t, reviews divided by games published, logged to reduce skew; the sample contains 148,638 reviews in 1,088 magazines. Resource need evolution (H3) is the developer's product experience, the number of games it launched in prior years including non-PS2 games, with high values indicating high external resource needs. Competition-induced resource uncertainty (H4) is the anticipated count of published PS2 games overlapping the focal developer's genres, counted over the last three years and averaged. Controls are prior collaboration, in-house development (genre overlap with the publisher's own games), quality difference (squared difference in average review scores), geographic distance in thousands of miles from the Google Maps API, same country, established firm size (average employees involved in game commercialization), established firm experience (games released on any platform in the prior three years), established firm portfolio size (yearly count of portfolio developers), entrepreneur size (number of developer employees), three unreported developer-location dummies, and year fixed effects.

Estimation is conditional logit on split and full samples plus random-effects logit with robust standard errors, two-way clustered on the entrepreneur and the established firm in the full-sample models. Split samples are cut at the mean of each moderator (robust to medians), and because conditional logit cannot report developer-invariant or industry-invariant variables, H3 and H4 are tested by comparing the ratio of the market-access to the development-help coefficient across subsamples per Train (1998) rather than from a single interaction term alone. Reported Ns differ by analysis: the descriptive statistics use n = 74,628 dyads (Table 1); the conditional logit split samples use 57,092 (internal resource needs), 16,627 (external resource needs), 44,873 (low resource uncertainty) and 28,846 (high resource uncertainty) observations (Table 2); the random-effects split samples use 57,742, 16,886, 45,338 and 29,290 (Table 4); the full-sample conditional logit models rest on 73,719 dyads with tie formation occurring in 795 dyads (Table 3), and the full-sample random-effects models on 74,628 dyads with the same 795 tie-forming dyads (Table 5). The design is associational and the paper claims no causal identification strategy; robustness instead rests on rare-events (relogit) models, re-estimation with 5, 7 and 10 randomly selected unrealized dyads per realized dyad, propensity score matching of unrealized to realized dyads, and a two-sided matching model with Bayesian MCMC estimation (Sørensen, 2007) that the authors use as a robustness check only because it requires assumptions about second-stage outcomes.

**Key Findings**
All four hypotheses are supported. The authors first verify the big fish, big pond trade-off itself with four tests: a simple negative correlation between development help and market access (r = -0.22, p < 0.01); regressing the two variables on each other, where market access had a negative and significant coefficient predicting development help and development help had a negative coefficient predicting market access; Hwang's (1991) clustering of regression residuals, whose cluster centers traced a 45-degree line with negative slope; and splines on combined high-high and low-low cases, which produced positive and negative coefficients predicting tie formation respectively.

Hypotheses 1 and 2 supported. Across the models in Tables 2-5, anticipated development help (b = 0.96, p < 0.01 in model 4 of Table 3) and anticipated market access (b = 2.06, p < 0.01 in model 4 of Table 3) are each significantly and positively associated with tie formation.

Hypothesis 3 supported. In the conditional logit split sample (Table 2), the ratio of the market-access coefficient to the development-help coefficient rose from 1.33 for inexperienced entrepreneurs (internal resource needs, model 1) to 5.08 for experienced ones (external resource needs, model 2), which the authors read as an experienced entrepreneur being willing to give up almost four times as much development help for market access as an inexperienced entrepreneur would. The random-effects split sample (models 1 and 2 of Table 4) and the full-sample interaction models (models 5 and 7 of Tables 3 and 5) are consistent, with a positive market access by resource needs coefficient of 0.04 in Table 3 and 0.05 in Table 5.

Hypothesis 4 supported. In the same split-sample comparison the ratio fell from 2.46 under low resource uncertainty (model 3) to 1.77 under high resource uncertainty (model 4) in Table 2, indicating a shift in preference from market access toward development help as competition intensifies. The random-effects split sample (models 3 and 4 of Table 4) and the full-sample interaction models (models 6 and 7 of Tables 3 and 5) are again consistent, with a positive development help by resource uncertainty coefficient of 0.01.

Controls and qualitative results. Across all analyses a tie is more likely when developer and publisher are geographically proximate, of similar quality, and prior partners. The interviews ground the rationing mechanism behind H1: publishers form ties with many developers because each game idea is highly uncertain but cannot devote equal attention to all of them, pay more attention to the relatively strong rather than the weak in the pack, and work from contracts that describe resource access only in general terms, leaving scope for preferential treatment, while a standard 70/30 revenue split varies little across relationships on a single console. Developers correspondingly report weighing the publisher's existing competitive set. Consistent with that account, the quantitative data show that a developer ranked below average was 26% more likely to have its projects cancelled than higher-ranked peers.

Established-firm side and robustness. Because ties require mutual agreement, the authors also test the publisher side and report that established-firm preferences are consistent with entrepreneurs'. Qualitatively, small-pond publishers can gain from inexperienced developers while publishers with high market access prefer experienced ones whose proven products suit a large customer base. Quantitatively, relaxing the rank-order preferential-treatment assumption by restricting the risk set to the second-highest game-expert personnel quartile left development help significant; controlling for developer-publisher power imbalance and mutual dependence with Casciaro and Piskorski's (2005) measures supported the original findings; and reversing the direction of choice by treating the publisher as the chooser strongly supported the development-help findings, although main effects of market access could not be tested that way. The rare-events models, the sampled and matched unrealized dyads, and the first stage of the two-sided matching model all yielded results the authors describe as strongly paralleling their original findings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study gives resource dependence theory a dynamic element it has lacked, showing that both resource needs (organizational dynamism) and resource uncertainty (environmental dynamism) evolve over time and reshape partner choice. It spotlights the neglected role of prospective peers as a source of competition at the *ex ante* tie-formation stage, demonstrating that partner choice is not simply "find the biggest pond" and that the partner with the best resources is not always the most sought-after.

**Practical Implication**
Resource-constrained, inexperienced entrepreneurs can attract partners and stand out by emphasizing their relative standing among an established firm's prospective peers, seeking "big fish" ties that offer personalized development help. Small, up-and-coming established firms or platforms lacking market access can compete for partners by offering personalized development help, differentiating themselves from larger, more rigid players.

**Limitations**
The conditional logit specification cannot report developer-level (e.g., resource needs) or industry-level (e.g., resource uncertainty) variables that are invariant across a developer's partner choices in a year. Realized ties were rare, requiring rare-events and matching robustness checks. The findings are bounded by conditions specific to "markets of know-how," including limited scalability of established-firm resources, heterogeneity of established firms, and the unpredictability of breakthrough ideas.

**Future Research**
The "big fish, big pond" framework could inform ecosystem and platform scholarship on which platforms attract complementors and which funding partners start-ups prefer, and whether established firms develop reputations as desirable partners. Studying the effectiveness and outcomes of formed ties (not just their formation), and how accumulated dyadic choices reshape alliance portfolios and industry networks, are promising directions.
