# Which Ideas for Change Are Endorsed? How Agentic and Communal Voice Affects Endorsement Differently for Men and for Women

**Abstract**
This paper explores how gender affects idea endorsement. We depart from the existing approach, which considers the effect of gender and other factors in isolation, and propose that endorsement depends on who suggests which ideas and in what ways. Drawing upon expectancy violations theory, we argue that "positive counter-stereotypical voice"—conceptualized as an idea that signals desirable traits stereotypically associated with the opposite gender—is more likely to be endorsed compared to "positive yet stereotypical voice" (i.e., that signals desirable traits consistent with stereotypical expectations of the speaker's gender). We argue that communal voice for men and agentic voice for women constitute positive counter-stereotypical voice, whereas agentic voice for men and communal voice for women constitute positive stereotypical voice. Then, we examine why positive counter-stereotypical voice results in positive evaluations—endorsement—by arguing that it affects attributions of competence and benevolence, albeit differently for men and for women. We test our hypotheses across three studies—one field and two experimental. Combined, we contribute to both the voice and gender literatures and shed new light on our understanding of how and why women and men get their ideas endorsed.

**Research Question**
How does the gender of a speaker, combined with the content and presentation style of the idea they raise, affect whether that idea for change is endorsed—and through which attributional mechanisms (competence vs. benevolence) does this effect operate for women versus for men?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: For women, positive counter-stereotypical voice in the form of agentic voice is more positively related to perceived competence compared to communal voice.
H2: For men, positive counter-stereotypical voice in the form of communal voice is more positively related to perceived benevolence compared to agentic voice.
H3: For women, perceived competence mediates the relationship between positive counter-stereotypical voice and endorsement.
H4: For men, perceived benevolence mediates the relationship between positive counter-stereotypical voice and endorsement.
Additional unnumbered post hoc prediction: contrary to the primary hypotheses and findings, negative counter-stereotypical voice (a negative violation) will lead to backlash compared to negative stereotypical voice, where backlash is defined as lowered perceptions of benevolence for women, competence for men, and endorsement for both.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Positive counter-stereotypical voice (agentic voice for women = task content + assertive language; communal voice for men = relational content + polite language), versus positive stereotypical voice; manipulated/coded against speaker gender.
- DV(s): Endorsement of the idea for change (five-item scale adapted from Burris, 2012).
- Mediators: Attributed competence (for women) and attributed benevolence (for men).
- Moderators: Speaker gender (moderates which attributional pathway links voice to endorsement; indexed via moderated mediation).

Grounded in expectancy violations theory (EVT) and social role theory, the paper argues that behaviors violating gender expectations draw heightened perceptual attention, and that *positive* violations are evaluated more favorably via the augmentation principle. When women voice agentically (counter-stereotypical), evaluators credit unanticipated competence; when men voice communally (counter-stereotypical), evaluators credit unanticipated benevolence. These attributions, in turn, raise the likelihood of endorsement, but the operative dimension of trustworthiness differs by gender. Support was consistent for women (competence pathway) across all studies; for men, the benevolence pathway emerged only in Study 3 when both content and language use jointly signaled communality.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (field, event-contingent design): 169 full-time U.S. managers recruited through the online panel company ROI Rocket, each reporting on one subordinate's idea for change raised within the previous two weeks; regressions were run separately on the 92 female-speaker and 77 male-speaker events. IV: positive counter-stereotypical voice, coded 0/1 by crossing the manager's classification of the idea's content (task vs. relational) with speaker gender, so relational = 0 and task = 1 for women, task = 0 and relational = 1 for men. DV: endorsement, five items from Burris (2012), alpha = .87, 5-point Likert. Mediators: competence (five items, alpha = .85) and benevolence (five items, alpha = .88) from Mayer and Davis (1999). Controls: manager gender, manager capability (three items based on Spreitzer's 1995 competence scale, alpha = .85), speaker tenure with the manager in years, size of the change suggested (1 = extremely small to 5 = extremely large), and whether a clearly definable solution was present (0/1).
- Study 2 (audio-vignette experiment): 299 usable responses from full-time working adults recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk after attention-check exclusions (mean age 35, SD = 9.71; 18.7 years of work experience; 44.8% women). 2 x 2 between-subjects manipulation of speaker gender (an audio clip attributed to "Eric" or "Erica") and voice content (agentic = a task idea about redistributing client/product work vs. communal = a relational idea about considering colleagues' feelings) in a financial services team scenario. Endorsement (alpha = .92), competence (alpha = .87), and benevolence (alpha = .87) used the Study 1 items adapted to the experiment; a separate pilot of 107 MTurk workers validated the content manipulations.
- Study 3 (audio-vignette experiment): 309 full-time working adults recruited on MTurk (mean age 34.7, SD = 10.03; 19.9 years of work experience; 47% women). Same 2 x 2 gender-by-content design as Study 2 but with language use added to the manipulation, so agentic voice combined task content with assertive, confident language and communal voice combined relational content with polite, tentative language (hedges, hesitations, tag questions). All variables were assessed with the same measures used in Study 2.
- Post hoc experiment: mimicked the Study 2 and 3 design, using Study 2's agentic and communal content revised to include aggressive or melodramatic, self-focused language, yielding aggressive-agentic scenarios (negative stereotypical for men, negative counter-stereotypical for women) and melodramatic-communal scenarios (negative stereotypical for women, negative counter-stereotypical for men); the paper places the detailed design and results in an Online Appendix and reports its backlash contrasts as t(147) for women and t(143) for men.
- Analysis: confirmatory factor analyses using Satorra-Bentler scaled chi-square difference tests, OLS regression and planned contrasts, and PROCESS-macro (Hayes, 2017) mediation and moderated mediation with 95% confidence intervals from 5,000 bootstrap resamples. Studies 2 and 3 use randomized experimental designs, which the authors describe as establishing causality of the effect of positive counter-stereotypical voice on endorsement; Study 1 is an associational field design with statistical controls on recalled voice events.

**Key Findings**
- H1 (for women, agentic voice raises attributed competence) was supported in all three studies: Study 1 B = 0.33 (SE = 0.11), p < .01; Study 2 counter-stereotypical M = 4.21 (SD = 0.68) vs. stereotypical M = 3.78 (SD = 0.93), t(144) = 3.23, p < .01, d = .54; Study 3 M = 4.19 (SD = 0.63) vs. M = 3.73 (SD = 0.79), t(152) = 4.02, p < .01, d = .65. Men's attributed competence showed no significant condition difference in any study (e.g., Study 2 t(151) = 1.10, p = .28; Study 3 t(153) = -1.82, p = .07).
- H2 (for men, communal voice raises attributed benevolence) received only partial support. It was not supported in Study 1 (B = -0.16, SE = 0.10, p = .13) or Study 2 (counter-stereotypical M = 4.32, SD = 0.67 vs. stereotypical M = 4.18, SD = 0.57; t(151) = -1.45, p = .15), but it was supported in Study 3, where male speakers were rated more benevolent under counter-stereotypical voice (M = 4.38, SD = 0.62) than under stereotypical voice (M = 3.96, SD = 0.74), t(153) = -3.74, p < .01, d = -.60. Women's attributed benevolence showed no significant condition difference in any study.
- H3 (competence mediates for women) was supported in all three studies: the indirect effect on endorsement was .21 (SE = 0.07), 95% CI [.06, .36] in Study 1; .29 (0.10), CI [.11, .50] in Study 2; and .35 (0.10), CI [.16, .56] in Study 3. The competence pathway was non-significant for men throughout, and the index of moderated mediation was significant in Study 1 (.30, CI [.08, .55]) and Study 2 (.37, CI [.14, .63]) but not in Study 3 (.19, CI [-.05, .46]).
- H4 (benevolence mediates for men) was likewise partial: not supported in Study 1 (effect = -.04, SE = 0.05, CI [-.17, .01]) or Study 2 (effect = .04, SE = 0.03, CI [-.01, .12]), but supported in Study 3 (effect = .09, SE = 0.04, CI [.02, .19]), where the index of moderated mediation via benevolence was negative and significant (-.10, SE = 0.05, CI [-.21, -.02]).
- Post hoc experiment: for women, negative counter-stereotypical voice produced backlash, lowering benevolence (M = 2.79, SD = 1.04) relative to negative stereotypical voice (M = 3.80, SD = 0.94), t(147) = -6.25, p < .01, d = -1.03, with a negative indirect effect on endorsement via benevolence (-.40, SE = 0.09, CI [-.58, -.24]). For men the prediction failed in the opposite direction: negative stereotypical (aggressive-agentic, self-focused) voice rather than negative counter-stereotypical voice lowered attributed competence (M = 3.20, SD = 1.03 vs. M = 3.54, SD = 0.88), t(143) = 2.11, p = .04, d = .35.
- Taken together, the argument held for women in all three studies and operated through attributed competence, whereas for men the predicted benevolence pathway emerged only in Study 3, once counter-stereotypicality was signaled jointly by voice content and language use.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconceptualizes voice endorsement as jointly contingent on "who" (speaker gender) and "what/how" (idea content and presentation), rather than treating these factors in isolation, creating a framework that maps the two fundamental dimensions of behavioral content—agency and communality—onto gender stereotypes. It identifies a new attributional mechanism—evaluators' inferences about the speaker's competence and benevolence—linking voice to endorsement, and shows the operative dimension differs by gender. It also advances EVT by testing the augmentation process and reconciling gender role theory with EVT by clarifying that positive versus negative violations drive divergent (benefit vs. backlash) outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
Employees should take a holistic approach—considering who they are (especially gender), what they say (content), and how they say it (presentation style) in tandem—to raise the likelihood their idea is endorsed, because identical content or delivery is evaluated differently depending on the speaker's gender. The findings offer "optimism" to women and men who defy gender stereotypes positively: women voicing agentically and men voicing communally may be especially likely to have such counter-stereotypical ideas endorsed.

**Limitations**
Study 1 relied on managers recalling and categorizing a subordinate's voice event (critical incident technique), so the evaluator's perception of content—rather than the subordinate's intended content—is what was captured, and language use could not be controlled in that study. Studies 2 and 3 used vignette experiments, which have been criticized for limited external validity, though this design maximized control over content and language manipulations.

**Future Research**
Future scholars should examine counter-stereotypical voice for other categories of employees (e.g., ethnic minorities, sexual orientations, cultural backgrounds) and intersectional stereotypes such as how women of color are evaluated when voicing agentically. Researchers are also encouraged to study the extent to which an idea positively or negatively violates expectancies (rather than taking valence as given), additional content–language combinations, and the interaction of promotive versus prohibitive voice with agency and communality.
