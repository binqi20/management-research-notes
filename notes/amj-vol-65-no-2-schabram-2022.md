# How Other- and Self-Compassion Reduce Burnout through Resource Replenishment

**Abstract**
The average employee feels burnt out, a multidimensional state of depletion likely to persist without intervention. In this paper, we consider compassion as an agentic action by which employees may replenish their own depleted resources and thereby recover. We draw on conservation of resources theory to examine the resource-generating power of two distinct expressions of compassion (selfand other-directed) on three dimensions of burnout (exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy). Utilizing two complementary designs—a longitudinal field survey of 130 social service providers and an experiential sampling methodology with 100 business students across 10 days—we find a complex pattern of results indicating that both compassion expressions have the potential to generate salutogenic resources (self-control, belonging, self-esteem) that replenish different dimensions of burnout.

**Research Question**
"When can compassionate action reduce an actor's own burnout?" The paper asks whether and how employees can act compassionately toward themselves or others to generate the specific resources depleted by each dimension of burnout (exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy) and thereby recover.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: Past exhaustion is positively related to future exhaustion.
- H1b: Past cynicism is positively related to future cynicism.
- H1c: Past inefficacy is positively related to future inefficacy.
- H2: (a) Self-compassion is negatively related to the exhaustion dimension of burnout, which is (b) mediated by self-control.
- H3: (a) Other-compassion is negatively related to the cynicism dimension of burnout, which is (b) mediated by belonging.
- H4: (a) Self-compassion and (b) other-compassion are negatively related to the inefficacy dimension of burnout, which is (c) mediated by self-esteem.
- Two directional null predictions are argued in the theory but not numbered as hypotheses: that only self-compassion generates self-control to reduce exhaustion, while other-compassion neither reduces nor contributes to exhaustion; and that self-compassion should have no noticeable effect on belonging or subsequent cynicism. The paper tests these as non-hypothesized assumptions (Study 1) and supplemental analyses (Study 2).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Enacted self-compassion; enacted other-compassion (Study 2 also includes directed/randomly-assigned self- vs. other-compassion vs. neutral instructions)
- DV(s): Three burnout dimensions—exhaustion, cynicism, inefficacy (later time point / next day)
- Mediators: Self-control (energy resource), belonging (condition resource), self-esteem (attribute resource)
- Moderators: None hypothesized; controls include gender, age, tenure, union status (Study 1) and prior-day burnout, day, sleep quality (Study 2)

Grounded in conservation of resources theory's primacy-of-resource-loss and resource-investment principles, the paper argues that burnout persists because early depletion erodes the very resources needed to combat further demands, so recovery requires agentic, resource-generating action. Compassion is most restorative when its particular expression generates the resource that fits the depleted dimension: self-compassion builds self-control to remedy exhaustion, other-compassion builds belonging to remedy cynicism, and both can build self-esteem to remedy inefficacy. Across the field survey (direct effects) and the diary study (indirect effects through resources), self-compassion reduced exhaustion and other-compassion reduced cynicism, while effects on inefficacy were more varied, supporting a compassion–burnout "fit" logic rather than a uniform benefit of compassion.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (three-wave field survey at one West Coast social service provider, waves one year apart): 284 responses at Time 1 (83% organizational response rate), 179 at Time 2, and 130 at Time 3; after listwise deletion of anyone who did not participate at all three waves, the retained sample is 130 respondents (mean age 40.75, SD = 11.17; mean tenure 10.02 years; 86% female; 32% unionized). Burnout was measured at Times 1 and 3 with the MBI-GS (Schaufeli et al., 1996) on a 5-point Likert scale — five exhaustion items, five cynicism items, and six reverse-coded inefficacy items (Table A1: exhaustion items 1–4 and 6; cynicism items 8, 9, 13–15; inefficacy items 5, 7, 10–12, 16); alphas ranged .74–.91 across waves. Enacted other-compassion at Time 2 = three of the four kindness-subscale items from Pommier's (2010) compassion inventory, asked as "To what extent did you try to engage in the following behaviors?" on a 1 (never) to 5 (all the time) scale (alpha = .84); enacted self-compassion at Time 2 = the 5-item kindness subscale from Neff's (2003b) self-compassion inventory with the same prompt and scale (alpha = .84). Controls: gender, age, tenure, union status. Estimated as multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8.2 with "Type = Complex" and grand-mean-centered predictors because employees were nested in 45 units; an eight-factor CFA on item parcels fit acceptably (chi-square = 147.22, CFI = .97, RMSEA = .05, SRMR = .05) and beat a Harman single-factor solution. The design is a lagged, same-source survey — no causal-identification strategy is claimed, and mediators were not collected in this study.
- Study 2 (10-day experiential sampling study with a within-person randomized compassion intervention, undergraduate business students at a Pacific Northwest business school during midterms): 100 students recruited (mean age 21.4, SD = 3.22; 69% female; 48% White, 39% Asian/Pacific Islander, 5% Hispanic; 1% employed full-time), all retained because every enrollee supplied at least three full days. Surveys went out twice daily over 10 consecutive business days (mean start times 8:47 a.m. and 8:16 p.m.); students completed an average of 9.48 days (range 3–10), giving 948 full day-level data points (95% response rate), reduced to 757 after dropping responses more than one day apart and to a final sample of 702 after removing 55 responses where participants reported not following that day's instruction or were rated careless — N(level 1) = 702, N(level 2) = 100. Directed compassion = two dummies from the daily instruction (self-compassion, other-compassion, or a neutral "report what you ate" day) assigned by a constrained random matrix so each participant received each instruction three times. Enacted compassion (evening) = the three highest-loading items from Pommier's (2010) other-kindness and Neff's (2003b) self-kindness subscales (alpha_other = .84, alpha_self = .83), validated against two blind coders' ratings of 400 open-ended action descriptions (mean r = .90 self, .85 other; coder–self correlations .19 and .23, p < .001, with no cross-correlation). Burnout was measured each morning using the three highest-loading MBI-GS items per dimension (exhaustion items 2, 3, 6; cynicism items 8, 9, 14; inefficacy items 7, 10, 12; alphas .74–.88). Mediators: self-control = Lanaj et al.'s (2014) 5-item scale collected in the morning because it tracks sleep quality (alpha = .90); belonging = three items from Zadro et al. (2004), evening (alpha = .77); self-esteem = the four highest-loading Rosenberg (1965) items, evening (alpha = .83). Controls: day, prior-day (Day t) burnout, and Day t+1 sleep quality (Scott & Judge, 2006; alpha = .78). Estimated as a single lagged multilevel path model in Mplus 8.2 with group-mean-centered level-1 predictors, random slopes, and covarying mediation random effects; indirect effects tested by parametric bootstrap with a Monte Carlo simulation of 20,000 replications. Randomization applies to the daily instruction (directed compassion) only; enacted compassion, the resources, and burnout are all self-reported, so the mediated paths are associational.

**Key Findings**
- Burnout persistence (H1a–c) held in Study 1 but only partly in Study 2. Study 1: exhaustion b = .47 (SE = 0.07), cynicism b = .48 (SE = 0.07), inefficacy b = .50 (SE = 0.09), all p < .001 — H1a–c supported. Study 2 (neutral days only): exhaustion b = .22 (SE = 0.08, p < .01) and cynicism b = .23 (SE = 0.09, p < .05) supported H1a and H1b, but inefficacy b = .09 (SE = 0.11, ns) did not support H1c; day-level inefficacy fluctuated rather than persisting.
- Self-compassion and exhaustion (H2). Study 1 supported H2a directly: self-compassion at Time 2 was negatively related to Time 3 exhaustion, b = -.21 (SE = 0.09, p < .05). Study 2 found no direct effect of either directed self-compassion (b = .05, SE = 0.04, ns) or enacted self-compassion (b = -.02, SE = 0.04, ns) on next-day exhaustion, but did support the mediation: directed self-compassion raised enacted self-compassion (b = .15, SE = 0.05, p < .01), enacted self-compassion raised self-control (b = .05, SE = 0.03, p < .05), and self-control reduced exhaustion (b = -.62, SE = 0.07, p < .001), with the indirect effect excluding zero (-.03, 95% CI [-.07, -.00]) and the serial indirect effect also excluding zero (-.01, 95% CI [-.01, -.00]). The authors call this complete support for H2b and only partial support for H2a.
- Other-compassion and cynicism (H3). Study 1 supported H3a directly: other-compassion at Time 2 was negatively related to Time 3 cynicism, b = -.24 (SE = 0.11, p < .05). Study 2 again found no direct effect (directed other-compassion b = -.02, SE = 0.03, ns; enacted other-compassion b = .03, SE = 0.04, ns) but supported the belonging path: directed to enacted other-compassion b = .39 (SE = 0.05, p < .001), enacted other-compassion to belonging b = .09 (SE = 0.05, p < .05), belonging to cynicism b = -.09 (SE = 0.04, p < .05), indirect effect -.01 (95% CI [-.03, -.00]) and serial indirect effect -.003 (95% CI [-.01, -.00]) — complete support for H3b, partial support for H3a.
- Inefficacy (H4) is where the two studies diverge most. Study 1 rejected both H4a and H4b: neither self-compassion (b = -.06, SE = 0.05, ns) nor other-compassion (b = -.05, SE = 0.06, ns) at Time 2 predicted Time 3 inefficacy. Study 2 found no direct effects either (all four directed/enacted paths ns), but supported the self-esteem mechanism: enacted self-compassion to self-esteem b = .15 (SE = 0.04, p < .001), enacted other-compassion to self-esteem b = .08 (SE = 0.04, p < .05), and self-esteem to inefficacy b = -.19 (SE = 0.05, p < .001), with indirect effects of -.03 (95% CI [-.05, -.00]) for self-compassion and -.02 (95% CI [-.04, -.00]) for other-compassion — complete support for H4c and only partial support for H4a and H4b.
- The predicted null paths held. In Study 1, other-compassion was unrelated to exhaustion (b = -.02, SE = 0.09, ns) and self-compassion was unrelated to cynicism (b = -.03, SE = 0.07, ns); Study 2's supplemental tests likewise found no significant direct or indirect effect of other-compassion on exhaustion or of self-compassion on cynicism through the hypothesized resource.
- Study 2's supplemental spillover analysis, which did not change the significance of any hypothesized path, identified additional mediated routes: self-esteem mediated both self- and other-compassion to exhaustion and to cynicism, self-control mediated self-compassion to inefficacy, and belonging mediated other-compassion to inefficacy. The paper reports that self-control and self-esteem, but not belonging, could replenish all three dimensions.
- Net pattern across the two studies: compassion's effects on burnout were direct in Study 1 and only indirect in Study 2, and support was not uniform — self-compassion for exhaustion and other-compassion for cynicism replicated in some form in both studies, whereas the inefficacy predictions failed in Study 1 and were supported only indirectly in Study 2, and the persistence of inefficacy itself replicated in Study 1 but not Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances burnout research by treating recovery as an agentic act the sufferer can initiate—shifting focus from what others do to the burnt-out employee to what employees can do for themselves—and by retaining all three burnout dimensions, surfacing robust cynicism effects that exhaustion-only models miss. It imports self-compassion from psychology and distinguishes it from other-compassion, showing their consequences differ by resource generated and dimension remedied. For COR theory, it provides proof-of-concept for under-tested dynamics (resource caravans, equifinality, multifinality) by modeling three resource categories simultaneously.

**Practical Implication**
Compassion can help address the workplace burnout epidemic, but the calculus is not straightforward—not all compassion combats all burnout, and benefits depend on matching the compassion expression to the depleted dimension. Because the studies found only indirect effects when compassion was prompted by instruction, reaping benefits likely requires ongoing investment in assessing burnt-out employees' specific needs and matching appropriate recovery strategies; employers can use the validated self- and other-compassion measures to track fluctuations.

**Limitations**
The generalization-and-extension approach varied multiple elements at once (sample, time frame, voluntary vs. directed compassion), making it difficult to attribute inconsistent direct/indirect findings to result instability versus design choices. Both samples were largely women and of equal status (coworkers or fellow students), and both studies relied on self-reports; in Study 2, a morning measurement of self-control inadvertently time-separated it from the night-time resource measures.

**Future Research**
Future work should replicate inconsistent results by varying only one element at a time, extend to male-dominated professions and to leader–subordinate (unequal-status) interactions, and use controlled experiments with objective or multisource measures. The authors call for incorporating compassion recipients to study interpersonal/dyadic resource crossover, probing whether compassion can serve as a prophylactic, examining order effects across burnout dimensions and partial-burnout profiles, and pursuing field experiments and clinical trials to test COR-based interventions.
