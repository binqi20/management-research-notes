# “I” Am Affirmed, but Are “We”? Social Identity Processes Influencing Refugees’ Work Initiative and Community Embeddedness

**Abstract**
Facilitating refugees’ transitions to host country society is of interest to their host countries and municipalities, employers within those countries, and the refugees themselves. We develop and test a model of how social identity processes, as outlined in selfcategorization theory, influence how perceiving that one is treated as an insider encourages behaviors reflecting social engagement with host country nationals, both within and outside of work. In a sample of 389 Syrian refugee employees in 88 supervisory units, perceived insider status was indirectly related to work initiative and community embeddedness through organizational identification. These indirect effects were moderated by diversity climate and perceived stigmatization of refugees in the broader society. Perceived insider status had its weakest effect on identification, and was not related indirectly to the outcomes when diversity climate was lower and perceived stigma was higher. We discuss the implications for theory development and practice concerning how social identity salience can inhibit personal affirmations at work from encouraging members of marginalized groups to demonstrate a deeper commitment to the organization and society.

**Research Question**
How do social identity processes—specifically the salience of the refugee social identity—shape whether refugee employees' perceptions that they are personally treated as insiders translate, through organizational identification, into work initiative and community embeddedness in the host society? When does personal affirmation at work fail to promote engagement?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Perceived insider status is positively related to organizational identification.
- H2: Diversity climate is positively related to organizational identification.
- H3: Diversity climate moderates the effect of perceived insider status on organizational identification, such that there will be a stronger positive effect of perceived insider status when the diversity climate is higher than when it is lower.
- H4: Perceived societal stigma toward refugees moderates the moderating effect of diversity climate on the relationship between perceived insider status and organizational identification: when perceived societal stigma is relatively high, perceived insider status will have a stronger positive relationship with identification at higher (vs. lower) levels of diversity climate; when perceived societal stigma is relatively low, perceived insider status will be positively related to identification regardless of diversity climate level.
- H5: Organizational identification will be positively related to (a) work initiative and (b) community embeddedness.
- H6: The positive relationships between perceived insider status and (a) work initiative and (b) community embeddedness are partially mediated by organizational identification.
- H7: The positive indirect relationships between perceived insider status and (a) work initiative and (b) community embeddedness through organizational identification will be moderated jointly by diversity climate and perceived societal stigma: when perceived societal stigma toward refugees is relatively high, the indirect relationships will be stronger at higher (vs. lower) levels of diversity climate; when perceived societal stigma is relatively low, the indirect relationships will not differ between higher and lower levels of diversity climate.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived insider status (Time 1)
- DV(s): Work initiative (Time 3), community embeddedness (Time 3)
- Mediators: Organizational identification (Time 2)
- Moderators: Diversity climate (supervisory-unit level, Time 1); perceived societal stigma toward refugees (individual level, Time 1) — modeled as a higher-order (three-way) moderator

Drawing on self-categorization theory, the authors argue that perceiving high insider status affirms refugees' personal identity and thereby builds organizational identification, which serves as a transitional/bridging identity that supplies the security and confidence to take the interpersonal risks involved in work initiative and in embedding within the broader community. However, an unfavorable diversity climate raises the "category fit" between refugees and nonrefugees, rendering the refugee social identity more salient; per the functional antagonism between social and personal identity salience, a salient refugee identity weakens the effect of personal affirmation (insider status) on identification. This salience-amplifying effect of poor diversity climate operates only among refugees who perceive high societal stigmatization of refugees (high category accessibility), so the indirect effects are weakest when diversity climate is low and perceived stigma is high.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and sample (one three-wave field study): a stratified random process drew from a population of approximately 1,000 Syrian refugee employees located in a large country at the nexus of the Asian and European continents, working in manufacturing operations and living in a city with a population of over 500,000. The authors recruited from a pool of 200 managers of departmental units spanning nine manufacturing companies and obtained onsite access from 91 of them (44% of those contacted). A total of 391 refugee employees agreed to the three-administration study; two who answered only a few demographic questions were removed, leaving a final analysis sample of 389 refugee workers in 88 supervisory units — a 93% effective response rate against the 417 refugee employees the 88 participating departments' managers reported. The sample was 67% male, with mean age 38.4 years (SD = 9.55), mean organization tenure 47.5 months (SD = 21.15), and a mean of 58.5 months living in the host country (SD = 17.5); 65.0% held an undergraduate degree or higher.
- Time separation: demographics, perceived insider status, perceived societal stigma, and diversity climate were collected at Time 1; organizational identification at Time 2, three weeks after Time 1; work initiative and community embeddedness at Time 3, five weeks after Time 2. All items were translated from English to Arabic and back following Brislin (1986). All scales used 5-point response formats except perceived societal stigma, which used a 7-point scale.
- Measures: perceived insider status = the six-item Stamper and Masterson (2002) scale (alpha = .80; exemplar item "I feel I am an 'insider' in my work organization"). Diversity climate = the four-item Pugh, Dietz, Brief, and Wiley (2008) scale (alpha = .76), specified as a reference-shift compositional construct and aggregated to the supervisory-unit level (30% of the variance in individual reports resided at the work-unit level, equivalent to ICC1 = .30, F(87, 301) = 2.92, p < .001; mean rwg(j) = .85, median .92). Perceived societal stigma toward refugees = a four-item scale adapted from Schaubroeck, Lam, Lai, Connors, Peng, and Chan (2018), retained at the individual level (alpha = .85). Organizational identification = a shortened four-item version of Mael and Ashforth (1992) (alpha = .83). Work initiative = supervisor ratings of direct reports on an adapted version of the Frese, Fay, Hilburger, Leng, and Tag (1997) instrument, with five of the original seven items retained after a principal components analysis dropped two that did not load with the others (alpha = .84). Community embeddedness = the nine-item Felps, Mitchell, Hekman, Lee, Holtom, and Harman (2009) instrument (alpha = .86).
- Estimation: two-level structural equation models in Mplus version 8.3 with full information maximum likelihood, distinguishing an individual level (Level 1, n = 389) from a supervisory-unit level (Level 2, n = 88), with random intercepts for the mediator and the two outcomes at Level 2 and eight organization dummies modeled as predictors of those intercepts to absorb nesting within the nine companies. Fixed-slope rather than random-slope models were used after slope-variance tests were non-significant. Indirect and conditional indirect effects used the product-of-coefficients approach with Monte Carlo 95% confidence intervals; the three-way simple slopes and slope differences followed Dawson and Richter (2006), and region-of-significance analysis followed Gardner, Harris, Li, Kirkman, and Mathieu (2017).
- Controls and identification: sex, education, time lived in the host country, organization tenure, and a 12-item psychological capital measure (Luthans, Youssef, & Avolio, 2007; alpha = .75) were measured but deliberately excluded from the primary models and used only in supplementary analyses. The design is associational rather than causally identified — the authors state that their findings do not establish causality but instead rely on logic and extant theory to infer causal order, with the multi-week separation of antecedents, mediator, and outcomes offered as the safeguard against priming and consistency artifacts.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: perceived insider status (Time 1) demonstrated a significant positive effect on organizational identification (Time 2) (coefficient = .57, SE = .07, p < .001).
- H2 not supported: the unit-level main effect of diversity climate on organizational identification was not statistically significant (coefficient = .13, SE = .10, p = .22). The authors note that the only other published study to treat diversity climate as a shared group construct likewise found no main effect, and suggest the relatively small number of work units as a possible reason.
- H3 supported: the diversity climate x perceived insider status interaction was positive and significant (coefficient = .44, SE = .11, p < .001), with the insider-status simple slope stronger for individuals in more favorable diversity climates (.79, SE = .09, p < .001) than in less favorable ones (.34, SE = .07, p < .001). The region-of-significance analysis placed the effective region between diversity climate values of 2.99 and 4.55, against an observed range of 2.17 to 4.55.
- H4 supported: the three-way perceived insider status x diversity climate x perceived societal stigma interaction was significant (coefficient = .21, SE = .07, p < .01), and all four simple slopes were positive and significant (p < .01). Under high perceived stigma the slope was .79 (SE = .12, p < .001) at high diversity climate versus .28 (SE = .10, p < .01) at low diversity climate, a significant difference (t = 3.18, p < .001); under low perceived stigma the two slopes (.58, SE = .14 at low and .54, SE = .11 at high diversity climate) did not differ significantly (t = .26, p = .79). The combination of high diversity climate and high perceived societal stigma produced the strongest effect of insider status on identification.
- H5a and H5b supported: organizational identification (Time 2) had a positive time-lagged relationship with both Time 3 outcomes — work initiative (coefficient = .22, SE = .07, p < .01) and community embeddedness (coefficient = .30, SE = .04, p < .001).
- H6a and H6b supported: the Monte Carlo 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects of perceived insider status through organizational identification excluded zero for work initiative (CI [.063, .206]) and for community embeddedness (CI [.120, .232]).
- H7a and H7b supported: when perceived societal stigma was high, the conditional indirect effect was stronger at high than at low diversity climate for community embeddedness (CI [.157, .325] vs. CI [.027, .150]; difference = .15, t = 3.08, p < .01) and for work initiative (CI [.065, .289] vs. CI [.016, .121]; difference = .11, t = 2.26, p < .05); when perceived societal stigma was lower, the two simple indirect effects were significant but did not differ for either outcome.
- Robustness: adding the four demographic controls and psychological capital did not alter any focal inference; an impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable analysis indicated that an omitted variable would need to correlate at .56 or higher with both organizational identification and community embeddedness to reduce their relationship to non-significance; and re-estimating the models on the 356 participants in the 71 units with three or more diversity-climate respondents left both the two-way (.43, SE = .11, p < .001) and three-way (.22, SE = .07, p < .01) interactions significant.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends diversity-climate theory by showing that diversity climate operates as a collective signal that conditions whether individualized, personal-identity-affirming treatment (perceived insider status) builds organizational identification—reframing diversity climate beyond a simple positive perception of the organization toward a determinant of identity salience and collective sensemaking. It also extends work on organizational identification as a bridging/transitional identity (Rogers et al., 2017) from ex-inmates to employed refugees and, potentially, to other stigmatized and marginalized groups, applying self-categorization theory's accessibility-and-fit logic to organizational engagement outcomes.

**Practical Implication**
Employers can harness refugees' human capital by promoting policies that signal fair treatment and inclusion of all employees and by ensuring individuals feel informed and included, which strengthens identification and downstream initiative and community engagement. Because perceived insider status is a scarce, status-based resource that managers cannot grant to everyone, fostering a favorable shared diversity climate—and educating nonrefugee employees against stigmatizing refugees—is an important complementary lever, especially where the broader climate is unfavorable.

**Limitations**
Generalizing beyond Syrian refugees depends on how their sensemaking is shared with other refugee populations, and the sample comprised refugees employed in established businesses working routinely with nonrefugees, distinguishing them from the larger population of unemployed or camp-confined refugees. The design does not establish causality, relying on theory and temporally separated measures (multi-week lags) to infer causal order; the predicted main effect of diversity climate on identification was not supported, possibly owing to the relatively small number of work units.

**Future Research**
Future work could develop theory on how nonrefugee employees' perceptions and behaviors interact with refugees' in ways that support or impede refugee thriving, and compare nonrefugees with refugees to isolate the transitional-identity mechanism. Researchers might examine whether specific personal affirmations (e.g., positive feedback, merit raises, promotions) show weaker identification effects under unfavorable diversity climate and high stigma, and test whether the diversity-climate-by-insider-status process generalizes across different refugee populations and other stigmatized groups.
