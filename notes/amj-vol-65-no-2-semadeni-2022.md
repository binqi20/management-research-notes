# Pumping the Brakes: Examining the Impact of CEO Political Ideology Divergence on Firm Responses

**Abstract**
Research on CEO political ideology has demonstrated the construct’s impact on many firm outcomes. Yet, political ideology does not exist in a vacuum; beyond representing a CEO’s values, political ideology also affects the CEO’s perception of the political environment. It remains unclear how a difference between the national political climate and the CEO’s own political ideology might affect strategic decision-making. Applying upper echelons and threat response perspectives, we propose that divergence between the national political climate and the CEO’s own ideology provokes a threat response, leading CEOs to cut research and development spending and increase retained earnings. We then examine two boundary conditions for this relationship. First, we propose that CEO wealth tied to the firm in the form of vested stock options will increase the perceived threat of ideology divergence, strengthening the threat response. Second, we propose that industry regulation will dampen the threat perceived from divergence, as well as hindering the CEO’s ability to respond to the threat at all, because of greater stability and lower discretion, respectively. We test these predictions on a sample of Standard & Poor’s 500 CEOs from 2004 to 2017 and find support for our hypothesized relationships.

**Research Question**
How does divergence between the national political climate and a CEO's own political ideology — rather than the CEO's ideology in isolation — affect strategic firm decisions, and how do CEO vested stock options and industry regulation condition that effect?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: CEO political ideology divergence is negatively related to firm R&D intensity.
- H2: CEO political ideology divergence is positively related to firm retention of earnings.
- H3: The negative effect of CEO political ideology divergence on R&D intensity is stronger when the value of CEO vested stock options is high.
- H4: The positive effect of CEO political ideology divergence on retention of earnings is stronger when the value of CEO vested stock options is high.
- H5: The negative effect of CEO political ideology divergence on R&D intensity is weaker in more regulated industries.
- H6: The positive effect of CEO political ideology divergence on retention of earnings is weaker in more regulated industries.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO political ideology divergence (absolute difference between CEO liberalism index, from 10-year pre-CEO donation records, and the president-level national political climate)
- DV(s): R&D intensity (R&D spending / sales) and retained earnings
- Mediators: None formally tested; perceived threat is the theorized (unmeasured) intervening mechanism
- Moderators: CEO vested stock options (logged vested option value; amplifies the threat response) and regulated industry (dummy for the 10 most highly regulated industries; dampens the threat response)

Drawing on upper echelons theory and threat response theory, the authors argue that when the national political climate diverges from a CEO's deeply held political ideology, the CEO perceives a threat to their beliefs and responds defensively by retreating to the known and familiar — curtailing forward-looking, uncertain R&D investment (H1) and accumulating internally controllable retained earnings (H2). Because vested stock options tie the CEO's personal wealth to firm outcomes (an "endowment effect"), they heighten sensitivity to the threat and strengthen both responses (H3, H4). Industry regulation, by reducing managerial discretion and providing stability, both lowers the perceived threat and constrains the CEO's ability to respond, weakening the responses (H5, H6). GEE models on S&P 500 firms support five of the six hypotheses; the paper describes Hypothesis 5 as receiving partial support.

**Data & Measures**
Single archival panel study (no multi-study design). Sample construction: CEOs hired at Standard & Poor's 500 firms from 2004 to 2010 inclusive who served at least two years, with the dependent variables observed for each year the CEO remained in office (or through 2017 if still in post), giving a dependent-variable window of 2005-2017; the authors report a final sample of 1,242 firm-year observations, while the table notes report n = 1,244 for the R&D intensity models (Table 2) and n = 1,216 for the retained earnings models (Table 3). Data sources: Compustat for both dependent variables; Federal Election Commission filings, manually checked over the 10 years before each executive became CEO, for donation records; ExecuComp for vested option value; the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) for total annual returns to shareholders; McLaughlin and Sherouse's (2016) categories for regulated industries; and Kinder, Lydenberg, Domini data for CSR profiles, used only as an instrument in the endogeneity test.

- DV — R&D intensity: R&D spending divided by firm sales (Cohen & Levinthal, 1989), with the examination limited to firms with R&D intensity less than 1; missing values were replaced with 0 and a dummy for firms with no reported R&D expenditure was included.
- DV — Retention of earnings: the amount of retained earnings depicted in the firm's annual report.
- IV — CEO political ideology divergence: the absolute difference between the CEO liberalism index and the national political climate. The liberalism index averages four donation-based indicators (the share of donations, dollar amounts, donating years, and distinct recipients going to the Democratic Party, with .1 added to numerators and .2 to denominators) and ranges from 0 (intensely conservative) to 1 (intensely liberal). National political climate is measured at president level only, coded 1 if the president was a Democrat and 0 if not; an alternative measure averaging president, Senate, and House affiliation is used in supplementary analysis.
- Moderator — CEO vested stock options: the vested (unexercised but exercisable) option value, logged to normalize its distribution.
- Moderator — Regulated industry: a dummy coded 1 if the industry is one of the 10 most highly regulated industries.
- Timing: dependent variables are measured at t; independent, moderating, and control variables are lagged at t – 1.
- Controls: three-digit-SIC industry averages and pre-CEO levels of each dependent variable (pre-CEO values averaged over the hire year and the year before); CEO tenure, education, age, duality, and logged stock ownership excluding options; board independence and independent-director stock ownership; TMT age, gender, and functional-background diversity (Blau index over eight functional categories); board nationality mix ratio, directorships, average age, and size; prior-year ROA, total annual returns to shareholders, logged assets, diversification entropy, current ratio, working-capital-to-sales ratio, and revenues from global market; industry competitiveness (1 minus a Herfindahl–Hirschman Index) and industry dynamism; divergence from Congress; and year dummies.

Identification is associational panel estimation rather than a causal design. Models are estimated by generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a Gaussian distribution, an identity link function, and an exchangeable correlation, with robust standard errors and two-tailed tests; Allison's (2009) hybrid method indicated no consistency problems and VIFs ranged from 1 to 3.95. The only identification check is a Durbin–Wu–Hausman test using firm-headquarters location in liberal-controlled states and KLD CSR profiles as instruments (first-stage F = 16.21 against a recommended 11.59; Sargan–Hansen statistic 2.734, p = 0.0982 for the R&D intensity model and 1.332, p = 0.2484 for the retained earnings model), which the authors report gave no evidence of endogeneity.

**Key Findings**
Five hypotheses are supported and H5 receives what the authors themselves call partial support. Coefficients are as reported in the Results narrative, with Model 5 the full model.

- H1 supported: divergence is negatively related to R&D intensity (Model 2: b = -0.39, p = 0.03; Model 5: b = -0.86, p = 0.02). Holding other variables at sample means, a firm at maximum divergence spends $80 million less on R&D than one at minimum divergence.
- H2 supported: divergence is positively related to retention of earnings (Model 2: b = 1.46, p = 0.04; Model 5: b = 3.71, p = 0.02). A firm at maximum divergence has $1.46 billion greater retained earnings than one at minimum divergence.
- H3 supported: the divergence × CEO vested stock options interaction on R&D intensity is negative and significant (Model 3: b = -0.08, p = 0.01; Model 5: b = -0.07, p = 0.03). The maximum-versus-minimum divergence gap in R&D expenditure is $14 million at low vested options (-1 SD) and $143 million at high vested options (+1 SD).
- H4 supported: the same interaction on retained earnings is positive and significant (Model 3: b = 0.22, p = 0.01, which Table 3 reports as 0.21; Model 5: b = 0.15, p = 0.05). The maximum-versus-minimum divergence gap in retained earnings is $561 million at low vested options (-1 SD) and $2.24 billion at high vested options (+1 SD).
- H5 partially supported: the divergence × regulated industry interaction on R&D intensity is positive (Model 4: b = 0.83, p = 0.03; Model 5: b = 0.73, p = 0.05), which the authors describe as providing partial support; Table 2 flags the Model 5 coefficient at p < 0.1 rather than p < 0.05. The maximum-versus-minimum divergence gap in R&D expenditure is $25 million in highly regulated industries versus $195 million in less regulated industries.
- H6 supported: the divergence × regulated industry interaction on retained earnings is negative and significant (Model 4: b = -3.81, p = 0.02; Model 5: b = -3.60, p = 0.03). The maximum-versus-minimum divergence gap in retained earnings is $125 million in highly regulated industries versus $3.93 billion in less regulated industries.

Robustness and additional analyses: results held when the national political climate was measured as the average of president, Senate, and House affiliation, when a Congress dummy or divergence from governor was added as a control, and when the highly correlated industry and pre-CEO R&D intensity controls were dropped individually and together; results were also unchanged when firms without an R&D expenditure report and when utility and financial firms were excluded. Divergence had no significant effect on capital spending, SG&A, several common productivity measures, or firm diversification, and no moderating effects appeared for those outcomes. Replacing divergence with CEO political ideology alone produced no significant results; board political ideology divergence was non-significant in a 50% subsample of 663 firm-years tracking 1,208 directors' donation records; and a conservative-CEO dummy showed no relationship with threat response, with liberal and conservative CEOs responding to divergence in the same pattern.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends the CEO political ideology literature by shifting attention from a CEO's absolute ideology to the divergence between that ideology and the national political climate, thereby capturing the causal-belief (not merely value) dimension of ideology whose effects flip with the political environment. It also extends threat response theory from realized threats (e.g., resource scarcity, competition) to a purely perceived threat experienced only by those discerning the divergence, and identifies CEO wealth and managerial discretion (via regulation) as boundary conditions on when a perceived threat translates into firm action.

**Practical Implication**
Boards should recognize that a CEO may alter firm decisions — cutting R&D and hoarding earnings — when the political climate diverges from the CEO's ideology, creating a potential agency problem, especially where CEO and board ideologies converge. Because vested stock options intensify these responses, boards should weigh this form of compensation carefully, and ensure CEO decisions to cut R&D or build retained earnings are justified to stakeholders who may not perceive the same threat.

**Limitations**
The study is set in the United States' long-established two-party system, so the divergence construct and theory may not generalize directly to multi-party parliamentary systems. The sample comprises large S&P 500 firms with multinational operations, leaving open whether the effects hold for smaller or mid-sized firms embedded in more regional or local political environments. Ideology is characterized along a single liberal–conservative spectrum, whereas political attitudes are increasingly viewed along separate economic and social dimensions.

**Future Research**
Scholars could examine whether convergence between CEO ideology and the national climate generates the opposite of threat — opportunity or (over)optimism — and test for asymmetric effects of convergence versus divergence. Research could extend the theory to other countries with multi-party systems and to regional or local political environments, and decompose divergence along separate social and economic ideological dimensions to see whether effects differ across them.
