# Too Much on the Plate? How Executive Job Demands Harm Firm Innovation and Reduce Share of Exploratory Innovations

**Abstract**
Building on psychological research on job demands and executive job demands theory, we explain why executive job demands negatively influence a firm’s overall innovation and shift the balance of innovative activities toward a larger share of exploitative innovations at the expense of exploratory innovations, leading to a smaller share of innovations that are exploratory. In addition, we explain how variety in executives’ gender, age, and tenure and an innovative climate weaken the negative effects of job demands on both overall innovation and the share of exploratory innovations. Our theory suggests that a controlling climate and employees’ education weaken the negative effect of job demands on overall innovation but exacerbate the negative effect of job demands on the share of exploratory innovations. Using surveys collected on-site from 243 Chinese firms, we find support for five of our 10 hypotheses and marginal support for three additional hypotheses but no support for the two moderating effects of innovative climate. This study shifts innovation scholars’ attention away from executive cognition and characteristics to their job environment attributes. It also develops executive job demands theory by examining its boundaries and applicability to the domain of innovation management.

**Research Question**
How do executive job demands—a fundamental attribute of top executives' objective job environment rather than their cognition or characteristics—influence a firm's overall innovation and its share of exploratory (versus exploitative) innovations, and which features of the executive job environment (TMT variety in gender/age/tenure, controlling climate, innovative climate, employees' education) moderate these effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater the job demands on top executives, the worse the firm's overall innovation.
- H2: The greater the job demands on top executives, the lower the firm's share of innovations that are exploratory.
- H3: The greater the horizontal variety in top executives' gender, age, and tenure, the weaker the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's overall innovation.
- H4: The greater the horizontal variety in top executives' gender, age, and tenure, the weaker the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's share of innovations that are exploratory.
- H5: The stronger a firm's controlling climate, the weaker the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's overall innovation.
- H6: The stronger a firm's controlling climate, the stronger the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's share of innovations that are exploratory.
- H7: The stronger a firm's innovative climate, the weaker the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's overall innovation.
- H8: The stronger a firm's innovative climate, the weaker the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's share of innovations that are exploratory.
- H9: Employees' education weakens the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's overall innovation.
- H10: Employees' education strengthens the negative relationship between executive job demands and the firm's share of innovations that are exploratory.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Executive job demands (measured via causal/formative indicators: task challenges [environmental dynamism, hostile suppliers/buyers, hostile competitors], performance challenge, executive aspirations [CEO need for achievement])
- DV(s): Firm overall innovation (12-item Jansen et al. 2006 scale); firm share of exploratory innovations (exploratory ÷ [exploratory + exploitative], standardized)
- Mediators: None formally tested (the cognitive-resource depletion logic is theorized but not measured as a mediator)
- Moderators: Variety in executives' gender, age, and tenure (TMT variety); controlling climate; innovative climate; employees' education (percentage with at least a bachelor's degree)

Executive job demands consume top executives' limited cognitive and emotional resources, forcing them to attend selectively to familiar information, rely on heuristics (e.g., the availability heuristic), weaken long-term orientation, lower risk-taking propensity, and centralize decision-making—reducing their ability to identify innovation opportunities and integrate knowledge. Because exploratory innovation requires divergent thinking, longer horizons, and greater risk, job demands harm exploration more than exploitation, lowering the exploratory share. TMT variety supplies cognitive diversity that buffers both effects; a controlling climate frees cognitive resources (helping overall innovation) but reinforces exploitation (worsening the exploratory share); educated employees compensate cognitively (helping overall innovation) yet are rewarded for exploitation (worsening the exploratory share).

**Data & Measures**
- Hypothesis-testing sample: one wave of on-site surveys administered to top management teams of Chinese firms in 2015. The frame was firms in 43 districts of Suzhou, Jiangsu Province; roughly 10 representative firms per district were randomly drawn from comprehensive government-supplied lists, giving 412 initial survey targets, of which 404 agreed to participate. Six TMT members per firm were invited (typically the CEO or vice CEO plus the human-resources, administrative, financial, procurement, and production/technological directors), each completing a questionnaire tailored to their area of expertise; all six responded in 70% of firms. Missing values reduced the analytic sample to 243 firms — the n reported in Table 1 and in every model of Tables 2 and 3 — for an effective response rate of 59%. Sampled firms averaged 481 employees and about US$85 million in revenue; almost all were owned by a single owner or family and had no board, with the TMT typically including the primary owners.
- Two additional samples were used only for instrument development and validation and are not the hypothesis-testing population: a pilot study of 160 top executives whose feedback was used to improve the questionnaires, and a separate 2019 criterion-validity survey of 117 top executives enrolled in an executive program at a leading Jiangsu business school, of whom 74 (63%) gave complete responses. In that 2019 sample the formative job-demands index correlated 0.62 (p < .001) with a reflective job-demands index from Gonzalez-Mule and Cockburn (2017) (alphas 0.81-0.89) and -0.13 (p < .01) with job satisfaction (Brayfield & Rothe, 1951), which the authors read as convergent and discriminant validity evidence.
- DV 1, overall innovation: the 12-item Jansen et al. (2006) scale, rated by executives on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); the paper used the simple average of the 12 items (alpha = 0.94). Confirmatory factor analyses (Table A3) fitted both a one-factor and a two-factor solution satisfactorily, supporting use of all 12 items for overall innovation and six items each for the two subtypes (exploratory alpha = 0.91; exploitative alpha = 0.90).
- DV 2, share of exploratory innovations: exploratory innovations divided by the sum of exploratory and exploitative innovations after standardization, following Benner and Tushman (2002) and Uotila et al. (2009). The authors defend the ratio DV by noting the denominator's coefficient of variation is 0.16, far below the level flagged as alarming by Certo et al. (2020).
- IV, executive job demands: no reflective scale was used. Following Hambrick et al. (2005), job demands were measured as the sum of the standardized scores of five causal (formative) indicators, which are treated as proximate antecedents and therefore not expected to intercorrelate. Task challenges: environmental dynamism (3-item Miller 1987 scale, 6-point Likert over the past three years, alpha = 0.84), hostile suppliers and buyers (8-item Meeus, Oerlemans, & Hage 2001 scale, alpha = 0.91), and hostile competitors (3-item Mesquita & Lazzarini 2008 scale, alpha = 0.92), the latter two reverse-coded because higher raw values indicated friendlier environments. Performance challenge: the firm's percentage sales growth from 2013 to 2014 minus the average sales growth of same-industry firms over the same period, reverse-coded so higher values mean weaker relative growth. Executive aspirations: the CEO's need for achievement from the 18-item Achievement Motive Questionnaire (Elizur, 1979; Sagie & Elizur, 1999; alpha = 0.93), reverse-coded before standardizing.
- Moderators, all centered before forming the interaction terms: TMT variety = the sum of standardized variety in gender (Blau's index), age, and tenure (age and tenure each as the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean); controlling climate and innovative climate = four-item scales each from Tsui, Wang, and Xin (2006), alpha = 0.93 and 0.88 respectively; employees' education = the percentage of employees with at least a bachelor's degree.
- Controls (33 in the complete models): ties to government (4-item scale answered by four government officials overseeing each district, alpha = 0.84, ICC1 = 0.32), fear of imitation, industry innovation, firm age, firm size (total assets), R&D expenditure, four ownership dummies, industry dummies, TMT male ratio, TMT mean age, TMT mean tenure, CEO title (whether the CEO or vice CEO answered the innovation questionnaire), and the inverse Mills ratio.
- Estimation and identification: a Heckman two-stage sample-selection correction. The first-stage selection model (n = 7,621) pooled the sampled firms with firms traded on China's "New Third Board" market and satisfied the exclusion restriction with three predictors omitted from the second stage (geographic distance from Jiangsu, provincial GDP growth, provincial market-development index); a likelihood-ratio test indicated selection bias, so the inverse Mills ratio was carried into second-stage OLS regressions with industry and ownership dummies. Hypothesized effects were tested one-tailed and controls two-tailed. Common-source variance was limited by taking the innovation DVs from the CEO or vice CEO and the job-demands indicators from the administrative director, with inter-rater reliability reported in Table A1 and common-method-bias diagnostics in Appendix A. The design is a single-wave cross-sectional survey and the authors state it "limited our ability to make a strong causal inference," so the estimates are associational rather than causally identified.
- Robustness operationalizations: new patents granted to each firm within two years of the survey (Poisson regressions) as an objective proxy for overall innovation; the logged percentage of sales from new versus existing products (OLS) as an objective proxy for the exploratory share; innovation averaged across the CEO and the administrative director; job demands taken from the CEO or vice CEO instead, which raised that specification's sample to 323 firms; and an impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable calculation for omitted variables.

**Key Findings**
- Support was partial, and the paper reports it as such: five of the 10 hypotheses (H1, H4, H5, H6, H10) were supported at p < .05, three (H2, H3, H9) were only marginally supported at p < .10, and the two innovative-climate hypotheses (H7, H8) were not supported. All coefficients below come from model 6, the complete model, in Table 2 (overall innovation) and Table 3 (share of exploratory innovations), with one-tailed tests for hypothesized effects.
- H1 supported: executive job demands are negatively related to overall innovation (b = -0.539, SE = 0.138, p < .001). Holding moderators at their means, moving job demands from one standard deviation below to one standard deviation above the mean decreases overall innovation by 0.51, or 0.55 standard deviations.
- H2 marginally supported only: the effect on the share of exploratory innovations is negative but significant only at p < .10 (b = -0.293, SE = 0.205). The same two-standard-deviation move decreases the exploratory share by 0.28, or 0.21 standard deviations.
- H3 marginally supported: the job demands x TMT variety interaction on overall innovation is positive at p < .10 (b = 0.097, SE = 0.075). Table A4 simple slopes stay negative at every level of TMT variety, weakening from -0.6866 (p = .0005) at low variety to -0.5075 (p = .0002) at the mean and -0.3284 (p = .0882) at high variety.
- H4 supported: the job demands x TMT variety interaction on the exploratory share is positive at p < .05 (b = 0.253, SE = 0.110), and Table A5 shows the moderation is large enough to erase the effect — simple slopes of -0.6776 (p = .0194) at low variety, -0.2124 (p = .2926) at the mean, and +0.2529 (p = .3742) at high variety, i.e., insignificant at the mean and positive though insignificant at high variety.
- H5 supported: the job demands x controlling climate interaction on overall innovation is positive at p < .05 (b = 0.287, SE = 0.168). Table A4 reports the job-demands slope as positive but insignificant at all three levels of controlling climate (0.8546, p = .3087; 1.1505, p = .2555; 1.4465, p = .2221), and the appendix note states the effect becomes positive even at a relatively low level of controlling climate although that positive effect is not significant.
- H6 supported: the job demands x controlling climate interaction on the exploratory share is negative at p < .05 (b = -0.469, SE = 0.248) — a controlling climate exacerbates rather than buffers the shift away from exploration. Table A5 slopes remain negative and significant at every level (-2.5680, p = .0384 at low; -3.0511, p = .0411 at the mean; -3.5342, p = .0433 at high).
- H7 and H8 not supported: the job demands x innovative climate interaction was insignificant in both models (b = -0.183, SE = 0.213 for overall innovation, a point estimate opposite in sign to the predicted buffering direction; b = 0.354, SE = 0.314 for the exploratory share).
- H9 marginally supported: the job demands x employees' education interaction on overall innovation is positive at p < .10 (b = 0.649, SE = 0.436), with Table A4 slopes of -0.4998 (p = .0003) at low education, -0.3181 (p = .0812) at the mean, and -0.1365 (p = .6239) at high education.
- H10 supported: the job demands x employees' education interaction on the exploratory share is negative at p < .05 (b = -1.187, SE = 0.650), so a more educated workforce significantly worsens rather than buffers the erosion of the exploratory share. Table A5 slopes: -0.3645 (p = .0719) at low education, -0.6967 (p = .0114) at the mean, -1.0290 (p = .0147) at high education.
- Model fit: adding the hypothesized terms raised R-squared from 0.152 to 0.223 for overall innovation and from 0.162 to 0.208 for the exploratory share, with a significant increment whenever a significant interaction was added.
- Robustness and additional analyses: the effect of executive job demands was negative and significant on the number of new patents granted (p < .01) and on new-product sales (p < .05); results were consistent when innovation was averaged across two informants and when job demands were taken from the CEO or vice CEO (n = 323). A squared term produced no support for a nonlinear, inverted-U specification. Decomposing the index, task challenge had significantly more negative effects than performance challenge or executive aspirations on both outcomes, which the authors label an ex post speculation rather than a tested prediction. Executive job demands were also negatively and significantly related to divergent organizational change, which the authors read as support for the risk-taking mechanism.
- Caveat the authors flag themselves: in additional analyses without controls for TMT gender mix, age, and tenure, the moderating effect of innovative climate on the exploratory share became marginally significant at p < .10 while the moderating effect of employees' education became insignificant. Findings for the other hypotheses were robust to including or excluding controls, but the paper says these two moderating results should be interpreted with caution.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study shifts innovation research from executives' cognition and individual attributes toward their objective job environment, identifying executive job demands as a novel and important determinant of firm innovation. It advances strategic-leadership/upper-echelons research by examining the previously untested boundary conditions and applicability of executive job demands theory to innovation, and—by showing systematic effects on overall innovation and the exploratory share—offers what the authors describe as the first piece of direct empirical evidence of the validity of executive job demands theory. It also extends the broader job demands literature to the most influential group of organizational members, top executives.

**Practical Implication**
Executives should be acutely aware that high job demands can damage firm innovation, especially exploratory innovation. Firms can counteract these effects through levers in the executive job environment: cultivating TMT variety in gender, age, and tenure to buffer both overall innovation and the exploratory share; recognizing that a controlling climate and a highly educated workforce help overall innovation but can crowd out exploration; and attending to how the design of the executive job environment shapes the exploration-exploitation balance.

**Limitations**
The one-wave (cross-sectional) survey design limits strong causal inference. Evidence on two moderators was inconsistent: the innovative-climate hypotheses (7 and 8) received no support, and the significance of the innovative-climate and employees'-education moderators shifted depending on whether TMT demographic controls were included, so those moderating findings warrant cautious interpretation. The sample comprised small and medium-sized firms in China, constraining generalizability to other firm sizes and national contexts.

**Future Research**
Future studies could systematically examine why different indicators of executive job demands (e.g., task challenge appeared most influential) have differential effects on strategic decisions, and assess generalizability across countries with stronger or weaker institutional environments and differing cultural individualism. Researchers could explore CEO–TMT interface dynamics (e.g., patterns of dependence between the CEO and other executives, harmony-oriented social norms) as conditioning factors, and extend the framework to other strategic consequences of executive job demands such as competitive behavior, mergers and acquisitions, and other risk-taking decisions.
