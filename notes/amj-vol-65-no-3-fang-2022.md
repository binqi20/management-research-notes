# Prosocial Occupations, Work Autonomy, and the Origins of the Social Class Pay Gap

**Abstract**
Despite decades of research on social mobility and wage disparities, it remains a puzzle why people from lower-class families earn less than people from upper-class families even when similar in education and occupational prestige. Taking a sociocultural perspective on social class, we argue that a key contributor to the class pay gap is that people from upper-class origins tend to work in occupations with greater autonomy, whereas their lower-class counterparts tend to work in occupations that are more prosocial. We further propose that autonomous occupations pay better than prosocial occupations. Across two distinct nationally representative samples in the United States, we find that people with upper-class (vs. lower-class) parents are more likely to work in autonomous occupations, but less likely to work in prosocial occupations, even when controlling for education, occupational prestige, and other potential confounds. This pattern of occupational sorting explains a substantial portion of the class pay gap. Our study extends the literatures on social class, occupational segregation, and social mobility, and joins an important scholarly conversation that has, until recently, taken place outside the field of management.

**Research Question**
Why do people from lower-class origins earn less than people from upper-class origins even when they are similar in education and occupational prestige? The paper asks whether class-based sorting into occupations that differ in work autonomy versus prosocial orientation helps explain this residual social class pay gap.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Individuals from upper-class origins work in more autonomous occupations than do individuals from lower-class origins.
- H2: Individuals from lower-class origins work in more prosocial occupations than do individuals from upper-class origins.
- H3: Autonomous occupations pay higher wages than do prosocial occupations.
- H4: Individuals from upper-class origins have higher personal incomes than individuals from lower-class origins in part because the former work in more autonomous occupations, whereas the latter work in more prosocial occupations.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Social class of origin (standardized composite of parental education, occupational prestige, and income; treated as continuous)
- DV(s): Personal income (the class pay gap); occupational work autonomy and prosocial orientation as intermediate occupational outcomes
- Mediators: Work autonomy (positive path to income) and prosocial orientation (negative path to income) of the respondent's occupation — dual-path mediation of the class–income relationship
- Moderators: Exploratory demographic moderators (gender; race — weaker negative class–prosocial link for Black vs. White respondents in GSS)

Drawing on a sociocultural perspective, the authors argue that childhood resource abundance fosters an independent model of self that draws upper-class people toward high-autonomy occupations, while resource scarcity fosters an interdependent model of self that orients lower-class people toward prosocial occupations; this two-sided supply- and demand-side sorting, combined with the wage premium autonomous occupations command over prosocial ones (devaluation of communal work plus willingness to sacrifice pay for meaningful work), reproduces the class pay gap above and beyond education and prestige.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: two separate observational studies — Study 1 draws on 32 independent cross-sectional GSS surveys, Study 2 on the NLSY97, which the paper describes as "a longitudinal study of a nationally representative cohort" — each pairing individual-level survey data with occupation-level characteristics matched from O*NET and the BLS (occupational data collected in 2019 using the most updated available estimates). Estimation is OLS multiple regression; H4 is tested with a dual-path mediation (path analysis) model and bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals from 1,000 random samples. Categorical variables (e.g., region, survey year) are treated as fixed effects and all continuous variables other than personal income are standardized. The design is associational: the paper names no causal identification strategy and addresses alternative accounts (reverse causality, work values, occupational aspirations) with supplementary analyses rather than a design-based identification claim.
- Study 1 data (General Social Survey): 32 independent cross-sectional GSS surveys fielded annually or biennially from 1972 to 2018; after removing respondents under 25 and over 69, the GSS sample is 50,204. Occupations are coded in the 2010 U.S. Census classification, yielding 539 occupations; 366 mapped directly to a 2018 SOC occupation and 112 broader GSS titles were handled by averaging scores across the narrower O*NET/BLS occupations (ICC(1) .58-.99, ICC(2) .82-.99, all p < .001). Regression models run on N = 22,484 after listwise deletion (correlation matrix N = 22,944-43,793).
- Study 1 measures: social class origin = standardized average of parental education (mean years of schooling completed by mother and father), parental occupational prestige (Duncan, 1961 scheme, from 80 for physicians and surgeons to 16 for dining room and cafeteria attendants), and parental income (retrospective family income relative to the average American family at age 16, 1 = far below average to 5 = far above average). Work autonomy = average of three O*NET job-incumbent items on 5-point scales (decision-making freedom without supervision; freedom to determine tasks, priorities, or goals; importance of developing one's own ways of working and depending on oneself), alpha = .80. Prosocial orientation = standardized average of social interest (Holland's RIASEC dimension, rated on a 7-point scale by six trained O*NET analysts) and nonprofit orientation (the BLS-derived proportion of an occupation's employees working for nonprofit organizations), alpha = .84. Personal income = respondent personal income as reported by the GSS, inflation-adjusted to 2010 dollars and divided by 1,000 (reported by 29,332 participants).
- Study 1 controls: sociodemographic (personal education, race, gender, age, immigrant status, marital status, religious orientation, country of family origin, plus parental work autonomy and parental prosocial orientation to address occupational reproduction), geographic (location type, region, interstate and intercity moves), and occupational (occupational prestige, preparation level, verbal and quantitative skill, relationship importance, proportion women, proportion minority).
- Study 2 data (NLSY97): a nationally representative cohort of nearly 9,000 Americans (51% men, 49% women) born between 1980 and 1984, aged 12-16 when first surveyed in 1997 and 32-36 at the most recent survey in 2017 (18 survey periods). Respondents reported their primary occupation in 2017, to which the same O*NET and BLS data were matched. Analyzed sample sizes range from 5,042 to 5,322 due to missing data and attrition (Table 8 income models: N = 5,322 after listwise deletion).
- Study 2 measures and controls: class origin = average parental income from 1997 to 2001 plus the highest year of school completed by mother and by father; parental occupational prestige is unavailable because the NLSY97 does not track parents' occupations, so parental autonomy and prosociality could not be controlled. Personal income = average personal income from 2010 to 2017. Work autonomy and prosocial orientation are measured exactly as in Study 1. The NLSY97 adds human- and social-capital controls the GSS lacks: private school attendance, grit (Duckworth & Quinn, 2009 scale), school grades, whether the respondent contacted friends or relatives during the job search, and whether they used a school or university employment center; race controls cover Asian, Black, Hispanic, Mixed, Native American, White, and "Other."
- Robustness variants reported: seemingly unrelated regressions for H1 and H2, testing with the individual class-origin indicators rather than the composite, matching 2003 O*NET data instead of current estimates, adding organization size as a control, analyzing social interest and nonprofit orientation separately, and controlling for personal income when predicting occupational attainment.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (GSS), H1 supported: class origin positively predicted occupational work autonomy (b = 0.019, p < .001; Table 3, Model 1), an effect as strong as that of parental work autonomy (b = 0.022, p < .001).
- Study 1, H2 supported: class origin negatively predicted prosocial orientation (b = -0.044, p < .001; Table 3, Model 2), roughly twice the magnitude of parental prosocial orientation (b = 0.017, p < .01). The authors read H1 and H2 together as showing the gradational class perspective explains occupational attainment as well as, or better than, the micro-class (occupational reproduction) perspective, with class origin about one third as consequential as personal education.
- Study 1, H3 supported: with the full battery of controls (Table 5, Model 4), work autonomy positively predicted personal income (b = 2.10, p < .001) while prosocial orientation negatively predicted it (b = -7.35, p < .001); prosocial orientation predicted income more strongly than education or occupational prestige. Class origin's own coefficient on income fell from 7.05 (basic demographics only) to 1.20 with the full controls.
- Study 1, H4 supported: in the dual-path mediation model both bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for the indirect effects excluded zero, and work autonomy and prosocial orientation together explained 23% of the class pay gap. Comparing otherwise similar people at the 10th versus the 90th percentile of class origin, the two mediators explained $934 of the pay gap (about $1,202 in 2021 dollars), with the indirect effect via prosocial orientation ($831) much larger than that via work autonomy ($103); across an average 44-year working life this corresponds to $41,096 (nearly $53,000 in 2021 dollars).
- Study 1, how much occupations matter: class origin alone explained 3% of the variance in income; adding all occupations as fixed effects added 26 percentage points of explained variance and absorbed 68% of the class pay gap [(6.32 - 2.01) / 6.32], whereas the two proposed mediators alone added 8 percentage points and 21% of the gap - close to one third of the total occupational-segregation effect (Table 4).
- Study 1, partial support when class origin is disaggregated: using the individual indicators instead of the composite, H1 and H2 held for parental education and parental income but NOT for parental occupational prestige (footnote 7; Online Appendix Tables A2 and A3).
- Study 2 (NLSY97): all four hypotheses were again supported, and the authors describe the NLSY97 results as directionally very similar to the GSS results although the magnitudes differ somewhat. Class origin positively predicted work autonomy (b = .052; Table 6, Model 1 and Figure 2) and negatively predicted prosocial orientation (b = -.043; Table 7, Model 1 and Figure 2); parental income and parental education were each separately significant in the same directions for both outcomes (work autonomy: b = 0.039, p < .01 and b = 0.045, p < .001; prosocial orientation: b = -0.046, p < .001 and b = -0.025, p < .05), so unlike in the GSS every class-origin indicator available in this dataset supported H1 and H2.
- Study 2, H3 and H4: with all controls added, work autonomy was positively related to income (b = 0.96, p < .05) and prosocial orientation negatively related (b = -5.26, p < .001) (Table 8, Model 3). The two mediators explained 7% of the total class pay gap; at the 10th-versus-90th-percentile comparison they explained $710 of the gap (about $914 in 2021 dollars), again with a larger indirect effect via prosocial orientation ($581) than via work autonomy ($128), and both bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals excluded zero.
- Alternative explanation tests: in the GSS Work Orientation module, class origin was unrelated to how important respondents rated "a high income" or "good promotional opportunities," while respondents from lower-class origins were more likely to report that "being able to help other people" and "being useful to society" are very important - evidence that the sorting reflects prosocial work values rather than indifference to income. In the NLSY79, adolescents with upper-class parents were more likely to aspire to autonomous occupations and their lower-class counterparts more likely to aspire to prosocial occupations.
- Reverse-causality checks: work autonomy in finance and analytic occupations remained generally stable over time despite large earnings increases, so the authors find no evidence that higher wages raise work autonomy; and H1 and H2 were still supported among GSS respondents aged 25-29, whose occupations they are unlikely to have reshaped.
- Exploratory demographic moderators: in both datasets the class pay gap was smaller among women than among men, but this was not the result of gender differences in class-based occupational sorting. A race interaction emerged in the GSS only - the negative relationship between class origin and prosocial orientation was weaker for Black than for White respondents, suggesting upper-class Black people are more likely than upper-class White people to work in prosocial occupations. No other notable interactions with region, location type, or generational cohort emerged.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study fuses the sociocultural perspective on social class with research on occupational wage inequality to develop and test a theory of class reproduction through occupational sorting, identifying an unexplored path—differential attainment of autonomous versus prosocial occupations—by which class origin shapes income. It extends research on social class, occupational segregation, and wage inequality by moving class-based autonomy/prosociality differences from non-work behavioral settings into labor markets, examining nearly all occupations in the economy rather than only access to a narrow set of prestigious jobs.

**Practical Implication**
Compensation managers can curb the class pay gap by avoiding overpaying for autonomy and underpaying for prosociality—e.g., using job evaluation of compensable factors and adding accountability and transparency to pay decisions. Managers can limit class-based occupational segregation by prioritizing task performance and structured, job-analysis-based selection. Business schools and collective-voice institutions such as unions are also identified as broader levers.

**Limitations**
Findings may be subject to omitted-variable bias from unmeasured occupational characteristics, and the authors could not directly control for individual cognitive ability. The occupation-level data lack the granularity to capture intra-occupational variation (e.g., where prosocial-titled jobs diverge in practice from their prosocial promise), and the two data sources differ in available controls and sampled populations.

**Future Research**
Future work could establish the extent to which additional variables (e.g., cognitive ability, social capital, personality) explain the class pay gap, and examine whether the proportion of upper- versus lower-class incumbents shapes an occupation's own characteristics over time. The authors also call for studying intra-occupational variation, such as how the gap between an occupation's prosocial promise and its day-to-day realities affects job seekers from different class origins.
