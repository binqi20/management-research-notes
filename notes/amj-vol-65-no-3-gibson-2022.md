# Investing In Communities: Forging New Ground in Corporate Community Codevelopment through Relational and Psychological Pathways

**Abstract**
Within management scholarship and practice, corporate community investments are heralded as an important form of corporate social responsibility. Yet, community development scholars have engaged in discourse and debate regarding corporate community investment, surmising that in some instances it does more harm than good. Where management research has not focused—and can substantially contribute to both theory and practice—is on how to implement corporate community investment for mutual gain. Integrating the fields of management and community development, I provide an evidence-based theory for understanding corporate community codevelopment, addressing relational and psychological pathways for behavioral change. This model emerged from a longitudinal qualitative ethnographic study of corporate-community investment programs, involving 1,176 hours of observations, 63 interviews, as well as narratives and reflections from participants representing 11 large corporations, at-risk remote Indigenous Australian communities, and a nonprofit organization. The 35 programs in focus involved initiatives such as family income management, educational trusts, literacy programs, and nutritional and substance abuse campaigns. The results in competency-building and community development have exceeded the expectations of all parties. Implications for theory, research, policy, and practice in management and community development are included.

**Research Question**
"How does the implementation of CCI contribute to mutual development in corporations and communities?" The study seeks to resolve the tension between the managerial focus on returns that characterizes the corporate community investment (CCI) literature and the capacity-building emphasis advocated by community development scholars.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering conditions: deep, direct, and ongoing corporate involvement enabled by local embeddedness of corporate secondees and the intentional early exit of the nonprofit broker (Bridge) once programs were underway.
- Relational processes: mutual perspective-taking, reciprocating respect, and communal advocacy (the "connective tissue" between corporations and communities).
- Psychological pathways: identity synergy, dignity, and self-determination (autonomy, competence, relatedness) for Indigenous partners; intercultural competence, meaningfulness, and thriving for corporate secondees.
- Behavioral changes (intermediate): technical/professional skills, proactivity, and strategic capability in communities; intercultural adjustment, prosocial behavior, and informed strategic decisions in corporations.
- Emergent outcomes (longer term): community-wide uplift and social impact (economic participation, educational attainment, community safety), plus reputational and productivity gains and feedback into firms' social-impact strategies.

As corporations and communities collaborated over an extended period, three relational processes each commonly cascaded into specific psychological gains and then behavioral changes among individuals, communities, and corporations. For example, communal advocacy fostered self-determination, which enabled strategic capability; mutual perspective-taking facilitated identity synergy and intercultural competence. These pathways exhibited equifinality and sometimes broadened beyond direct participants—a "cascade of mutual transformation"—shifting CCI from an arm's-length influx of funding toward self-sustaining codevelopment.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and inference status: a longitudinal inductive grounded-theory ethnography — "Taking an inductive, theory-building approach, I" iterated between the data and an emerging theoretical understanding of the processes that unfolded as corporations and communities engaged with one another, with a process focus requiring a temporal view of unfolding events, decisions, and actions. The study induces a process model; it claims no causal identification strategy and no statistical inference, and the supporting percentages are proportions of self-reports collated from annual and program reports rather than estimated effects.
- Setting and analytic sample: the 35 corporate–community investment programs underway during the study period, funded by 11 of the largest corporations in Australia in industries such as mining, oil and gas, banking, retail, professional services, and transportation, in remote Indigenous communities across four regions of Australia. The partnerships were launched in 2001; the author and a series of research assistants worked in the communities over three years. The 35 programs were completed in five cohorts of three to nine corporate secondees (CSs) each, with secondments ranging from six weeks to three years. Wider program context, distinct from this analytic sample: over the preceding decade the programs involved over 2,500 CSs from 30 large corporations in support of 115 community programs.
- Participants: 70 primary participants — the primary CS and the primary Indigenous partner (IP) for each of the 35 programs — chosen because the experience of community development was likely to be most salient to those living it daily. Nearly half (49%) were women; the age range was 25–55 (mean 42). All 35 IPs were born and raised in Australia; among the corporate participants, 19 were born in Australia and 16 elsewhere (New Zealand 4, South Africa 4, Canada 2, Scotland 2, Greece 1, Macedonia 1, India 1, Brazil 1), holding positions in corporate management, human resources, finance/accounting/risk, sales and marketing, research/product development/design, and internal consulting. Theoretical sampling added 8 corporate leaders (CLs), 6 Indigenous leaders (ILs), and 8 representatives of the nonprofit broker Bridge (BRs).
- Data corpus (five sources, Table 1): "I collected data from five sources—observations", interviews, narratives, reflection journals, and archival documents. (a) 1,176 hours of participant observation — 762 hours in secondments, 294 in coordination meetings, 60 in secondment debriefings, and 60 in Bridge retreats — with detailed notes and recorded theoretical interpretations shared back with IPs, CSs, and BRs after each cohort. (b) 63 formal and informal interviews of 20–60 minutes (averaging 45), comprising 31 corporate participants, 24 Indigenous participants, and 8 BRs, mostly spontaneous and open-ended on a semistructured evolving protocol (Online Appendix A); most were not recorded because of the physical environment, so notes were taken during and immediately after. (c) 51 informal narratives and conversations of 20–60 minutes (21 corporate, 30 Indigenous), gathered through an in situ "tell the story" approach, frequently on country around a campfire or in a community center. (d) 35 reflection journals of about 10–15 pages each (30 CSs and 5 Indigenous participants), enabling a chronological reading of the processes. (e) Three years of annual reports produced by the 11 corporations, Bridge, and the IP organizations, used as unobtrusive data for triangulation on acknowledged program impacts.
- Coding of constructs: each of the 35 programs was treated as a separate case and all its associated materials coded with a deliberately parsimonious initial list of general codes ("skills," "resources," "actions," "changes") to construct case histories. Open coding then assigned concepts representing core themes to blocks of raw data, staying close to informants' own language at the first-order level; sample concepts include perspective, respect, and advocacy (examples in Online Appendix B), and each piece of supporting data is labeled IL, IP, CL, CS, or BR with an identifying number. The author open-coded an initial set of interviews, narratives, and journals; two research assistants (one of whom had worked directly with CSs and IPs in the remote communities) then independently coded additional data sets to establish a baseline, correspondence was high, and discrepancies were discussed until agreement before the remaining data were coded.
- Model construction: relationships among codes were elaborated and refined and their linkages mapped program by program (Online Appendix C presents one program's example), then compared using the constant comparative method (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) between occurrences and across participants and programs. Several theoretical frames were tried and judged inadequate for the most interesting phenomena in the data — stakeholder theory, CSR, and radical community-development views on maintaining freedom — prompting further rounds of coding and theorizing on relational processes, from which categories such as intercultural competencies emerged.
- Outcome indicators: longer-term community impact was assessed against OECD (2001) indicators — economic participation (e.g., labor force participation, less unemployment), educational attainment (e.g., reaching year 12), and safe and supportive communities (e.g., reductions in homicides, assaults, robberies) — supplemented by debriefing accounts. Community- and corporate-side gains were captured as percentages of self-reports in annual and program reports. No formal financial analysis was undertaken because of an insufficient sample size.

**Key Findings**
- Core finding: as corporations and communities collaborated over an extended period, they progressed to a heightened level of corporate community codevelopment in which relational processes produced psychological gains and subsequent behavioral changes among individuals, communities, and corporations. The relational processes served as the "connective tissue" between corporations and communities, fostering independent capacity-building; this was made possible by the local embeddedness of the CS, direct ongoing involvement with the community, and the quick exit of Bridge once programs were underway. The three relational processes frequently happened simultaneously and were not mutually exclusive, and changes occurred in most (sometimes all) psychological and behavioral elements; three common pathways, one per relational process, were nonetheless identified.
- Pathway 1 — mutual perspective-taking: "mutual perspective-taking frequently facilitated identity synergy" for IPs and intercultural competencies (social flexibility, interpersonal engagement, emotional sensitivity) for CSs, which in turn enabled technical and professional skill development among IPs and intercultural adjustment among CSs. Identity synergy is characterized as identities remaining separate but compatible and operating synergistically, distinct from identity holism and identity enhancement. Supporting self-reports: across annual reports, 86% of Indigenous organizations reported improvement in expertise, skills, and professionalism; 92% of returned CSs reported personal growth, 67% an improvement in their adaptability and flexibility, and 72% substantial increases in their knowledge, understanding, and awareness of Indigenous culture and communities.
- Pathway 2 — reciprocating respect: reciprocating respect created psychological pathways that frequently took the form of dignity for IPs and meaningfulness for CSs, which in turn enabled proactivity among IPs and prosocial behavior among CSs. Dignity was reported as experienced, even regained, within a work relationship, functioning as a form of recognition by which Indigenous participants acknowledged their own self-worth; among CSs, meaningfulness preceded prosocial behavior including autonomy-oriented helping, and in at least two instances CSs left the company to pursue activism or nonprofit roles, which a CL framed as a positive rather than a negative.
- Pathway 3 — communal advocacy: communal advocacy most commonly co-occurred with self-determination (relatedness, autonomy, and competence) for IPs and thriving (learning and vitality) for CSs, which in turn enabled strategic capability among IPs and better-informed strategic decisions among CSs. Across program reports, 76% reported improvements in strategic planning among the IPs. The analysis specifically locates IPs' autonomy and competence needs as met through communal advocacy.
- Equifinality and less common permutations: codevelopment demonstrated equifinality — the same final state was reached from a variety of paths — with three permutations that were less common than the three focal pathways: alternative links from a given relational process to other outcomes (mutual perspective-taking sometimes fostered dignity rather than identity synergy, which could then shift strategic capability rather than proactivity); the same change appearing in both parties (IPs sometimes mentioned increased meaningfulness and several CSs alluded to increased dignity, so neither set of psychological outcomes was exclusive to one side); and intrapersonal clustering (within a single IP, identity synergy, dignity, and self-determination sometimes co-occurred, and within a single CS, intercultural competencies, meaningfulness, and thriving sometimes co-occurred).
- Longer-term community impact, reported as coincident and preliminary rather than causal: across communities, preliminary evidence was obtained that the aggregation of the psychological and behavioral changes coincided with "communitywide uplift and longer-term impact." Asked in debriefings about the overall social impact on their communities, all participants replied that it was "extremely high." Against the OECD indicators, economic and educational outcomes were documented in all regions, while improvements in community safety were realized in three of the four regions — partial rather than uniform on that dimension.
- Corporate-side outcomes: the annual reports indicate that 72% of the corporations increased employee loyalty, 72% increased employee performance, and 59% reported increased customer engagement; all 11 companies described reputational gains during feedback and debriefing sessions; CLs also reported gains in productivity, although formal financial analysis was not undertaken because of an insufficient sample size, so the business-value claims rest on self-report.
- Cascade of mutual transformation beyond direct participants: IPs who had worked closely with CSs began to encourage others in their community to engage in perspective-taking, respectful interaction, and advocacy, and even community members with no direct exposure to the corporate participants began to demonstrate these behaviors; CSs performed the same role with colleagues who had not participated in a secondment. The deep engagement of CSs also informed firms' longer-term strategies for social impact and future contributions, described as a feedback loop not previously identified.
- Self-sustaining continuation: after an initial series of programs, numerous corporations initiated their own programs without Bridge's involvement, and several communities became proactive in approaching previously involved or entirely new corporations for subsequent codevelopment programs — evidence the author treats as "lending at least initial credence" to the emergent model of corporate community codevelopment rather than as a confirmatory test of it.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops an evidence-based theory of corporate community codevelopment that integrates the management literature on CCI with community development and cross-sector partnerships, identifying the relational and psychological mechanisms that connect investment to lasting behavioral change. It extends self-determination theory by tracing how communal advocacy meets autonomy and competence needs, contributes to community development theory by foregrounding identity synergy (distinct from identity holism or enhancement) and dignity, and documents a previously undocumented mechanism by which cross-sector partnerships produce transformative, cascading change across racial and ethnic lines.

**Practical Implication**
Funding alone is an unreliable CCI strategy; meaningful impact requires deep, ongoing relationships that build local control and autonomy. Practitioners can identify the behavioral outcome of greatest interest and work backward through the pathways—e.g., mutual perspective-taking for skill development, reciprocated respect for proactivity, communal advocacy for strategic capability—using participatory processes such as community visioning, storytelling, and action planning that center community ownership.

**Limitations**
Formal financial analysis was not undertaken because of an insufficient sample size, so business-value claims rest on self-reported gains in loyalty, performance, reputation, and productivity. The approach is unlikely to apply to all types of community development—following Ebrahim and Rangan's contingency framework, simpler change theories (e.g., emergency relief) may warrant a focus on activities rather than longer-term impact.

**Future Research**
Future work should capture each element of the model using longitudinal and quasi-experimental designs that compare codevelopment with more traditional CCI modes and varying program durations. Additional priorities include measuring intermediate community impacts (e.g., strategic capabilities, cultural-heritage maintenance) with multifaceted indicators, examining mutuality in inputs and activities (not just relational processes), and exploring how Bridge's initial brokerage role can be transferred to other settings.
