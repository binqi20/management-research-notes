# The Policy–People Gap: Decision-Makers Choose Policies That Favor Different Applicants than They Select When Making Individual Decisions

**Abstract**
This work documents a contemporary organizational problem—a gap between selection policies and individual selection decisions—and suggests one intervention to address it. In college admissions and workplace hiring contexts, we find that decision-makers are more likely to favor disadvantaged applicants over applicants with objectively higher achievements when choosing between selection policies than choosing between individual applicants. We document this policy–people gap among admissions officers, working professionals, and lay people using both within-subject and between-subject designs and across a range of stimuli. We find that the gap is driven in part by shifting standards of fairness across the two types of decisions. When choosing between individuals, compared to choosing between policies, decision-makers are more likely to prioritize what is fair to individuals (a microjustice standard of fairness) over what is fair in the aggregate (a macrojustice standard of fairness). As a result, an intervention that has decision-makers prioritize the same standard of fairness across the decisions mitigates the policy–people gap. This research helps us understand why decision-makers’ choices so frequently violate espoused organizational policies and suggests one way to increase the representation of disadvantaged groups in organizations.

**Research Question**
Why do decision-makers choose selection policies that favor lower-scoring, disadvantaged applicants yet select higher-scoring, advantaged applicants when making the corresponding individual decisions, and how can this "policy–people gap" be mitigated?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Decision-makers favor higher-achieving, advantaged individuals over lower-achieving, disadvantaged individuals more often when making a decision about individuals than when making a decision about policies.
- H2: A greater relative prioritization of microjustice (versus macrojustice) accounts for the relationship between making an individual versus policy decision and the likelihood of favoring higher-achieving, advantaged individuals over lower-achieving, disadvantaged individuals.
- H3: Decision-makers favor higher-achieving, advantaged individuals over lower-achieving, disadvantaged individuals in their individual and policy decisions at more similar rates when they rely on the same standard of fairness (microjustice or macrojustice) across the decisions than when they do not.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Decision type — policy decision vs. individual decision (manipulated between- and within-subjects); macrojustice-priming intervention vs. control (Study 4).
- DV(s): Choice of the higher-scoring, advantaged option vs. the lower-scoring, disadvantaged option; likelihood-of-choice rating; which option is "fairer" to select.
- Mediators: Relative reliance on microjustice vs. macrojustice standards of fairness (validated scales developed in Study 3).
- Moderators: The macrojustice intervention (moderated mediation in Study 4); education emerged as the only demographic moderator across studies.

Policies are construed at a higher (more abstract) level than individual decisions. Drawing on construal-level theory and the microjustice/macrojustice distinction, the authors theorize that the abstractness of policy decisions shifts decision-makers away from a microjustice standard (what is fair to specific individuals, given their merit) toward a macrojustice standard (what is fair in the aggregate, e.g., equality across groups). Because microjustice favors the higher-achieving applicant and macrojustice favors the disadvantaged applicant, the same person makes systematically divergent choices across the two framings—even when consequences are identical. An intervention asking decision-makers to prioritize their institution's macrojustice standard roughly halved the gap by shifting individual decisions, while leaving policy decisions (already macrojustice-motivated) unchanged.

**Data & Measures**
- Common design logic across the four main studies: preregistered vignette experiments in which participants chose either between two specific individuals or between two policies that would favor those individuals, with the information describing the options held constant across the two framings. Decision type (and, in Study 4, the intervention) is randomly assigned, so the policy-versus-individual contrast is experimentally manipulated. The mediation analyses are explicitly framed as assuming rather than establishing the proposed causal model: the authors state that Study 3's results implicate microjustice and macrojustice as potential mediators but do not establish a causal relationship, and treat Study 4's manipulation of the mechanism as the stronger causal evidence.
- Study 1 (college admissions; n = 190): paper surveys distributed over a two-day period at a national conference for college admissions, targeting attendees whose lanyards identified them as university or college admissions officers or as high school college counselors; 159 completed the survey in full and 31 completed only parts of it (59% female, M age = 37.25, SD = 9.83), and 96.5% had at least one year of admissions-department experience (M = 10.03 years, SD = 8.42). Mixed design: every participant made both an individual and a policy admissions decision, order randomized, permitting between- and within-subjects comparisons. Stimulus operationalization: the two applicants (or applicant types) were identical except for SAT score (1200, 81st percentile vs. 1350, 94th percentile) and estimated household income ($60,000, 50th percentile vs. $120,000, 80th percentile). Measures: forced choice between option A and option B; likelihood of choosing either option on a 0-to-10 scale; and which option is fairer to select (forced choice). Ns differ across analyses because some responses were missing or illegible to the transcribers.
- Study 2 (workplace hiring; n = 360): United States- and United Kingdom-based Prolific Academic participants filtered for experience in technology-related fields (68.2% in STEM and technology-related occupations; 34.7% with hiring experience, M = 5.78 years, SD = 6.28; 36.67% female, M age = 35.24, SD = 10.24), randomly assigned between subjects to the individual or the policy decision. Participants acted as head of human resources at a technology start-up choosing whom to interview for a software engineer position; the two options were identical except for coding-assignment score and candidate gender, one being a male candidate scoring 8.1 and the other a female candidate scoring 8.0 on a 0-to-10 assignment where 5 (the average) indicates competence. Measures: forced choice and the fairer-option forced choice. An attention check gated entry to the study, so no with-versus-without-exclusion comparison is possible.
- Study 3 (n = 802): United States-based Amazon Mechanical Turk participants (M age = 41.00, SD = 12.72; 56.36% female), between subjects, using Study 1's information table but framing the policy as affecting only one final pair of applicants that year so that the policy and individual decisions had identical consequences. A target of 800 was set to power the intended mediation analyses; per preregistration, 115 participants (13% of the sample) who failed any of three attention checks were excluded. Measures: forced choice, fairer-option forced choice, likelihood on a 1-to-7 scale, and eight motivation items on a 1 (not at all) to 7 (extremely) scale presented in random order and counterbalanced before versus after the choice — four macrojustice items (a diverse university population, greater equality in opportunities, balancing opportunities between disadvantaged and advantaged applicants, avoiding too few disadvantaged applicants) and four microjustice items (selecting applicants on hard work, qualifications, potential to succeed, accomplishments), developed from Brickman et al. (1981) and the authors' earlier studies. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the two-factor structure (χ²(n = 802, df = 19) = 123.53, p < .001; CFI = .98; RMSEA = .08, CI [.07, .10]; TLI = .97) over a one-factor model (CFI = .64; RMSEA = .32); the macrojustice scale had Cronbach's α = .93 (coefficient H = .95) and the microjustice scale α = .87 (coefficient H = .90), with content validity established separately in Study S9. Social desirability was measured with abridged Marlowe–Crowne and Balanced Inventory of Desirable Responding scales. Mediation used PROCESS Model 4 with both scales as simultaneous mediators and percentile bootstrap (10,000 samples), choice coded 1 = higher-scoring, higher-income option and decision type coded 1 = individual.
- Study 4 (n = 1,346): Prolific Academic participants based in the United States with at least a college degree (M age = 34.39, SD = 12.58; 48.66% female) in a 2 (decision type: individual or policy) × 2 (intervention: control or macrojustice) between-subjects design; the 1,400-participant target came from a power analysis for detecting a one-third attenuation of the gap (power = 0.80, α = .05). All participants read an Office of the University President guidance page describing the micro and macro views of fairness; control participants were told to be conscious of both views and make whatever decision they deemed appropriate, whereas macrojustice participants were told that "at our university, we prioritize the macro view" and that they should make decisions helping accomplish a fair allocation of admission spots. Measures: forced choice, fairer-option forced choice, likelihood on a 1-to-7 scale, and an abbreviated, explicitly unvalidated mechanism scale (1 to 7) comprising two macrojustice items (creating equality; increasing diversity), averaged into a composite (r = .82), and a single microjustice item (rewarding applicants for their accomplishments). Estimation: OLS regression of the binary choice on decision type, intervention, and their interaction, following Gomila (2021), with logistic regression as a robustness check, plus PROCESS Model 8 binary moderated mediation with percentile bootstrap (10,000 samples).
- Supplementary evidence and pooled analysis: 15 supplementary studies (n = 8,477) address alternative explanations and robustness, including two non-preregistered recall studies used as pilots — 35 college admissions officers and 108 management faculty asked whether they had faced the achievement-versus-circumstances trade-off. An internal random-effects meta-analysis of the preregistered studies testing Hypothesis 1 pooled 8,653 participants. Construal level was never manipulated or directly measured; it was assessed only in an exploratory analysis that scored participants' textual explanations (n = 3,725, from Studies 2, S1, S3–S6, S10, S12, and S13) with an online construal scoring tool.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 was supported in all four main studies, in the predicted direction: participants chose the higher-scoring, advantaged option more often when choosing between individuals than when choosing between policies. Study 1 (admissions officers), between subjects: 70.11% vs. 36.56% (χ²(1, 180) = 20.31, p < .001); within subjects: 66.08% vs. 39.18% (McNemar χ²(1, 171) = 31.12, p < .001). Study 2 (technology employees): 64.48% vs. 52.54% (χ²(1, 360) = 5.29, p = .021). Study 3 (policy with identical consequences): 56.59% vs. 45.58% (χ²(1, 802) = 9.64, p = .002). Study 4, control condition: 54.69% vs. 28.92% (χ²(1, 716) = 48.32, p < .001).
- Likelihood ratings and fairness judgments moved in the same direction as choice. Study 1 likelihood: individual M = 5.97 (SD = 2.22) vs. policy M = 5.20 (SD = 2.84), t(183) = 2.04, p = .043 between subjects, and M = 6.19 vs. 5.11, t(179) = 5.54, p < .001 within subjects. Fairer-option judgments: Study 1, 53.75% vs. 35.16% (χ²(1, 171) = 5.97, p = .015); Study 2, 79.23% vs. 56.50% (χ²(1, 360) = 21.40, p < .001); Study 3, 63.41% vs. 38.67% (χ²(1, 802) = 48.70, p < .001). In Study 1, 40% of admissions officers made a second choice that diverged from their own first choice.
- Hypothesis 2 received partial, study-dependent support, and the two mediating paths did not behave alike. In Study 3 only the microjustice path mediated: individual (versus policy) decision-makers were significantly more motivated by microjustice (b = 0.28, p < .001), which predicted choosing the higher-scoring, higher-income option (b = 0.70, p < .001), indirect effect = 0.20, 95% CI [0.08, 0.33]. The macrojustice path did not: decision type affected macrojustice only marginally (b = -0.25, p = .072) and the macrojustice indirect effect's interval included zero (0.28, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.60]), even though macrojustice itself predicted choice (b = -1.15, p < .001). The Study 3 mediation was also order-dependent, appearing when the justice items followed the choice and absent when they preceded it. In Study 4's control condition, unlike Study 3, both paths mediated (microjustice indirect effect = 0.30, 95% CI [0.11, 0.50]; macrojustice indirect effect = 0.70, 95% CI [0.47, 0.99]).
- Hypothesis 3 was supported in Study 4. The macrojustice intervention shifted individual decisions (54.69% to 41.18% choosing the higher-scoring, higher-income applicant) but not policy decisions (28.92% to 26.90%): decision type b = 0.26, p < .001; intervention b = -0.02, p = .597; decision type × intervention b = -0.12, p = .027. Likelihood ratings showed the same pattern (decision type b = 1.13, p < .001; intervention b = -0.00, p = .997; interaction b = -0.46, p = .033). Both indices of moderated mediation excluded zero (macrojustice -0.34, 95% CI [-0.70, -0.01]; microjustice -0.32, 95% CI [-0.62, -0.04]); under the intervention the macrojustice indirect effect shrank but still excluded zero (0.37, 95% CI [0.12, 0.63]) while the microjustice indirect effect no longer did (-0.02, 95% CI [-0.24, 0.20]). The authors read this as the intervention reducing, but not eliminating, the shift in fairness standards, roughly halving the size of the gap.
- Boundary on the intervention: it did not change what decision-makers judged to be fair. The decision type × intervention interaction on the fairer-option judgment was null (b = -0.08, p = .132), with individual-versus-policy fairness gaps of 59.90% vs. 27.11% under control and 53.24% vs. 28.28% under the intervention.
- Pooled effect size: a random-effects internal meta-analysis of the preregistered studies testing Hypothesis 1 (8,653 participants) produced an odds ratio of 2.46, 95% CI [2.02, 3.00], z = 8.99, p < .001, Q(15) = 65.58, p < .001, meaning the odds of favoring the lower-achieving, disadvantaged option were 2.46 times higher when choosing policies than when choosing individuals. Magnitudes were similar for the manuscript studies (odds ratio = 2.27, 95% CI [1.48, 3.46], n = 2,058) and the supplementary studies (odds ratio = 2.54, 95% CI [2.04, 3.17], n = 6,595). Support was not universal: the authors report finding the gap in 15 of 16 preregistered studies, and the forest plot in Figure A1 shows Study S12's odds ratio at 1.37, 95% CI [0.86, 2.17]. The most pared-down policy operationalization, Study 3, produced the smallest main-study gap (odds ratio = 1.56, 95% CI [1.18, 2.06]).
- Alternative explanations tested and not supported: want–should conflict, differing beliefs about the relationship between (dis)advantage and achievement across the decisions, and differences in how zero-sum the decisions were perceived to be. Consistent with the construal argument, the exploratory scoring of participants' textual explanations found that explanations for policy decisions reflected a more abstract construal than explanations for individual decisions.
- Demographic moderation: the gap was not clearly moderated by gender, age, race, income, or political attitudes. Education was the exception — for admissions decisions highlighting socioeconomic advantage and disadvantage, more highly educated decision-makers were more likely to favor the higher-scoring, advantaged person when choosing between individuals, producing a larger policy–people gap.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper identifies and explains a novel, robust "policy–people gap" and offers a fresh account of long-noted principle–implementation gaps: rather than failing to think through implementation or yielding to temptation, decision-makers earnestly pursue fairness in both decisions but rely on different standards of fairness that shift with construal level. It imports the microjustice/macrojustice constructs into organizational selection contexts, develops validated scales to measure them, and links justice scholarship to construal-level theory, preference reversals, and the psychology of policymaking.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations seeking to align espoused selection policies with the individual decisions those policies are meant to guide can deploy a simple, transparent intervention: have decision-makers learn about both standards of fairness and explicitly prioritize the organization's macrojustice standard when making individual choices. This low-touch step reduced the gap by about half and increased selection of disadvantaged applicants, though it did not change decision-makers' underlying beliefs about which choice is fair.

**Limitations**
The findings rest on vignette experiments that maximize experimental control but are low in external validity, and policies were operationalized narrowly (information held constant across policy and individual framings). The studies measured reliance on microjustice/macrojustice but did not directly measure construal level, and they sampled only a subset of possible macrojustice and microjustice principles (e.g., egalitarian and meritocratic ones), leaving open whether the gap generalizes beyond these specific principles.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine the policy–people gap in more naturalistic, higher-external-validity settings, directly test whether construal level is the operative mechanism, and study macrojustice and microjustice across a broader range of contexts (e.g., aid and helping decisions where microjustice may align with need). Researchers are also encouraged to probe how personal characteristics—beyond the education effect observed here—shape the magnitude of the gap.
