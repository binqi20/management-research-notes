# From Sheltered to Included: The Emancipation of Disabled Workers from Benevolent Marginalization

**Abstract**
While there have been several studies on overt forms of marginalization, few have examined benevolent marginalization, where people may unquestioningly participate in their own paternalistic subjugation by following a prescribed identity. How might such individuals end up achieving emancipation from an infantilizing identity? To address this puzzle, we conducted a longitudinal study of a German sheltered workshop, an organization providing employment for disabled people. We observed that workers with disability initially maintained a regulating organizational identity based on paternalistic segregation. However, over time, they constructed their own self-determining identity and coproduced a change in the workshop’s identity from “shelter” to “inclusion.” First, we show that in order to coconstruct their preferred self-concept, benevolently marginalized individuals need to gain the support of those in power, who change their role from a guardian to an ally. Second, while achieving liberation from overt marginalization is likely to involve confrontation with one’s oppressors, we suggest that achieving emancipation from benevolent marginalization is a collaborative process of mutual consciousness raising and sensitization. Third, while changes of identity may occur after exposure to alternative discourses, often involving the contentious performances of external activists, we show how insider activists mobilize collective action for change within a protectionist organization.

**Research Question**
How may benevolently marginalized individuals whose self-concept is tightly regulated by an organizational identity collectively achieve emancipation from that infantilizing identity?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: benevolent marginalization; the "filter of paternalistic benevolence"; a regulating organizational identity ("shelter") vs. an alternative "inclusion" discourse; spoiled/infantilizing identities; the double-edged sword whereby benevolent marginalization makes resistance harder yet is more amenable to non-threatening change.
- Process / phases: (1) Maintaining identity constraints — staff and workers reproduce a paternalistic organizational identity and maintain workers' prescribed "in need of protection" identity; (2) Mobilization of internal activists — placatory measures unexpectedly yield positive lived experiences and "moments of epiphany," an initial group of workers draws on alternative inclusion conceptions to co-construct a self-determining self-concept, then reframes the discourse reassuringly (via "safe spaces" and enabling measures) to overcome remaining workers' resistance; (3) Driving organizational identity change — a critical mass of self-determining workers clashes with the paternalistic identity, prompting both staff and workers to reshape the workshop's identity and structures toward inclusion.
- Triggering conditions: external criticism (UN CRPD activism, media coverage), placatory and enabling measures, confidence-building experiences, redefinition of powerful actors from guardians to allies.
- Emergent outcomes: a self-determining worker identity (~90% of employees) and a reshaped, participative organizational identity that accommodates both inclusion and those who still prefer protection.

Emancipation from benevolent marginalization is theorized as a collaborative, gradual process of "conscientization" (Freire, 1970) rather than confrontation: marginalized actors work *with* rather than against those in power, leveraging differing priorities among powerful actors to recast them as allies through "joint labor." Insider activists animate currents from external emancipatory discourses inside a protectionist organization, frame them as non-threatening identity opportunities, and mobilize collective identity work that ultimately revises the regulating organizational identity.

**Data & Measures**
Single-site, six-year longitudinal process case study of one large German sheltered workshop set up in the 1960s, comprising four divisions (metal processing, industrial assembly, packaging and shipping; catering and cleaning services; gardening and landscaping; supervised apartments outside the city centre); data were collected from all divisions but the analysis focused largely on the first, which employs the most workers. Of the workshop's disabled workers, around 70% have mainly cognitive impairments (e.g., autism, Down syndrome, attention deficit disorder, learning impairments, dyslexia), 26% psychological impairments (e.g., bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, depression), and 4% physical impairments. Real-time data were collected between 2013 and 2018 and temporally bracketed into three phases: maintaining identity constraints (January 2013–May 2014), mobilization of internal activists (June 2014–October 2016), and driving organizational identity change (November 2016–December 2018). Corpus (Table 1): 72 observation days — 15 days of public events, 17 days of internal company events, and 40 days of daily work schedules split across offices (16), production units (15) and service units (9) — with detailed field notes written immediately after each visit; 60 formal semi-structured interviews lasting 20 minutes to two hours (24 disabled workers, 40% of formal interviews), 17 follow-up interviews as reported in Table 1 (the Data Sources text instead states 12), and 88 informal conversations (46 disabled workers, 52%), with repeated conversations covering 62% of individuals; interviews were tape-recorded with permission, transcribed, translated into English and checked by a third person, and one social worker translated deaf workers' sign language; interviewees were reached via key informants in snowball fashion using a role-adapted interview guide (Appendix A). Archival data were narrowed to 200 external documents (website, podcasts, newsletters, brochures, videos, annual reports, plus press and other media coverage) and 150 internal documents (board meeting notes, agendas and minutes, internal presentations, communications and policies), supplemented by an internal proprietary database accessed at six points in time. Constructs were not measured but inductively coded in four steps: (1) a thick longitudinal case description used as a "data organization device" to build a chronology of key events; (2) identification of critical junctures to bracket phases of continuity and discontinuity using visual maps; (3) coding of the meanings individuals attached to the workshop, the disparity in their level of power, and how they saw themselves into first-order categories (Miles & Huberman, 1994) and then more abstract second-order themes, refined until descriptively dense; and (4) aggregation of second-order themes into the three theoretical dimensions and a process model, combined with grounded theorizing. Emerging coding ideas were discussed to consensus at each step and five member checks were performed. The design is interpretive and process-oriented; no causal identification strategy is claimed and no effects are estimated.

**Key Findings**
The study induces a three-phase process model of emancipation from benevolent marginalization (Figure 4). (1) *Maintaining identity constraints* (January 2013–May 2014): staff reproduced a paternalistic organizational identity through a "filter of paternalistic benevolence" that made the workshop impermeable to the external inclusion discourse, while disabled workers enacted the prescribed "in need of protection" identity without contestation — the authors report observing no tensions in this phase, since all members were socialized into the protectionist organization. (2) *Mobilization of internal activists* (June 2014–October 2016): a small group of mostly recently hired staff introduced placatory measures — short-term job rotations inside and, from September 2014, outside the workshop, social media training, and regular discussions with parents — intended to defuse external criticism and expected by seasoned staff to fail or to reaffirm the need for segregation. These measures instead proved revelatory, producing "eureka"/"aha" moments for workers and positive feedback from outside employers, so that around 30 disabled workers, a dozen staff and some parents formed a self-identified internal activist group that coconstructed a self-determining self-concept for the workers. The activists then reframed inclusion reassuringly rather than confrontationally — personalized confidence-building experiences plus informal and formal "safe spaces", including a newly created disability board — to overcome resistance from workers who still wanted protection. (3) *Driving organizational identity change* (November 2016–December 2018): around 90% of both workers and staff came to support a self-determining worker identity, and the resulting mismatch with the "shelter" identity triggered joint organizational identity work in which staff recast themselves from guardians, caregivers and protectors into allies and "inclusion assistants"; the workshop's documents, structures and practices were changed accordingly (from the end of 2016 long-term transition programs and staff training firms to accommodate workers rather than "only adapting workers to firms"; in 2017 a production unit relocated to the city centre; the workshop redescribed as a "participative space"). The paper reports the process as collaborative rather than confrontational — confidence building, mutual consciousness raising (Freire, 1970) and sensitization achieved by working with rather than against those in power — and reports support as uneven rather than uniform: some staff were initially skeptical or feared for their jobs and doubted that workers needing more support could cope, and in the member checks a very few workers said the new self-concept was not critical to them. The revised organizational identity does not preclude protection: workers who explicitly preferred to stay (e.g., a mathematically gifted man with autism who "never wanted to return to this brutal job market", and a deaf worker who preferred the deaf community) were accommodated, so inclusion came to mean offering options that workers decide between. No hypotheses were tested and no effect estimates are reported; all findings are interpretive and grounded in the case corpus.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to the organizational identity literature on three fronts. First, it shows how benevolently marginalized individuals can remove or loosen identity constraints by collaborating with powerful actors to change "who we are," offering a more tempered view of regulating identities and a form of emancipation between micro- and macro-emancipation. Second, it illuminates benevolent (rather than overt) marginalization, where subjugation occurs subtly and in solidarity with those in power, so that emancipation is a collaborative process of confidence building, mutual consciousness raising, and sensitization. Third, it foregrounds insider activists who let currents from external discourses permeate a paternalistic organization by reframing those discourses as identity opportunities.

**Practical Implication**
Benevolent marginalization is harder to tackle through legislation than overt discrimination, so practitioners should dismantle protectionist assumptions, shift from "telling" to "listening," and have those in power act as allies offering nondidactic support. Organizations should create enabling mechanisms and "safe spaces," tailor inclusion experiences to individuals' differing impairments and preferences (including the preference to be protected), and educate employers to view disabled workers as valuable employees—"no decision should be made about them without them."

**Limitations**
The study examined one German sheltered workshop, and the authors note it would be useful to examine other forms of benevolent marginalization to reveal the obstacles faced by those seeking emancipation from a regulating identity. The boundary between internal and external activists is not always distinct, and dividing respondents into nondisabled staff and disabled workers is somewhat simplistic given that a few staff also had disabilities, pointing to fluid disability boundaries.

**Future Research**
Examine other forms of benevolent marginalization (e.g., the infantilizing treatment of older adults in care homes exposed by Covid-19 lockdowns) to surface obstacles to emancipation; study activists who are simultaneously insiders and outsiders; and adopt an intersectionality lens, since people define themselves not only through disability but also ethnicity, caste, gender, or age, all of which may compound oppression.
