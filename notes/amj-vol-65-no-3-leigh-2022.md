# “Am I Next?” The Spillover Effects of Mega-Threats on Avoidant Behaviors at Work

**Abstract**
“Mega-threats”—negative, identity-relevant societal events that receive significant media attention—are frequent occurrences in society, yet the influence of these events on employees remains unclear. We draw on the theory of racialized organizations to explain the process whereby exposure to mega-threats leads to heightened avoidant work behaviors for racial minority employees. We theorize and find—across two studies centered upon various mega-threats, including a mass shooting targeting Asian Americans and police killings of Black civilians—that event observers who share identities with mega-threat victims become vicarious victims, which triggers an experience of “embodied threat,” an appraisal of the increased likelihood of personally encountering identity-based harm. The experience of embodied threat coupled with the racialized nature of organizational structures, which limits the agency of racial minorities, then compels employees to engage in threat suppression. Furthermore, we find that threat suppression consumes psychological resources, leading to heightened avoidant work behaviors, or higher work withdrawal and lower social engagement, but, when the psychological safety of identity-based discussions is high, it attenuates this effect. Altogether, our paper advances research on mega-threats and race in organizations, and yields practical insights that can assist managers in reducing the detrimental effects of mega-threats on employees.

**Research Question**
How and why does exposure to "mega-threats"—negative, identity-relevant, heavily publicized societal events—influence the work behaviors of racial-minority employees who share an identity with the events' victims, and under what organizational conditions are those effects attenuated?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: When a mega-threat occurs, individuals who are members of the harmed identity group will experience higher levels of embodied threat than those who are not members of this group.
- H2: When a mega-threat occurs, individuals who share identity group membership with victims of the event will engage in increased threat suppression in the workplace, which is mediated by embodied threat.
- H3a: When a mega-threat occurs, being a member of the harmed identity group has a positive indirect effect on work withdrawal, which is mediated first by embodied threat and second by threat suppression.
- H3b: When a mega-threat occurs, being a member of the harmed identity group has a negative indirect effect on social engagement, which is mediated first by embodied threat and second by threat suppression.
- H4: When a mega-threat occurs, if the psychological safety of identity-based discussions is high, then the effect of embodied threat on threat suppression will be smaller, reducing the positive indirect effect of being a member of the harmed identity group on work withdrawal. The paper states a parallel prediction, also numbered "Hypothesis 4," for the negative indirect effect on social engagement.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Shared identity with mega-threat victim(s) (operationalized as race; 0 = White, 1 = Asian/Black)
- DV(s): Avoidant work behaviors — work withdrawal (higher) and social engagement (lower)
- Mediators: Embodied threat (Mediator 1) → threat suppression (Mediator 2), in serial sequence
- Moderators: Psychological safety of identity-based discussions (attenuates the embodied-threat → threat-suppression link)

When a mega-threat harms a member of one's identity group, observers become "vicarious victims" and, through embodied cognition, experience "embodied threat"—a heightened appraisal that they could personally encounter identity-based harm ("I could be next"). Because organizations are racialized structures that constrain the agency and emotional expression of racial minorities, this threat is suppressed at work rather than expressed. Threat suppression is effortful and depletes psychological resources, so (per conservation of resources theory) employees enact protective avoidance—withdrawing from tasks and disengaging from colleagues. When employees perceive that identity-based discussions are psychologically safe, the need to suppress is reduced, interrupting the deleterious chain.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and inference status: two associational field-survey studies of employees exposed to real mega-threats. Study 1 is a pre-registered cross-sectional online field study; Study 2 is a three-phase pre/post panel that re-surveys the same baseline participants after two later mega-threats and uses Phase 1 levels of the work variables as controls. Exposure is not randomized and the paper names no experimental or quasi-experimental identification strategy — the Phase 1 baselines are used to rule out pre-existing racial-group differences in threat suppression, work withdrawal, and social engagement, not to establish causal identification. Estimation uses bootstrapped mediation analysis and bootstrapped SEM path analysis in R with the lavaan package, 1,000 resamples, plus moderated regression with simple-slopes and Johnson–Neyman probing for the Phase 2 moderator.
- Study 1 sample (Atlanta-area spa shootings, March 2021): 332 participants recruited through Cloud Research and Prolific, who had to live in the United States and be employed at least part-time; quota sampling produced a relatively equal number self-identifying as Asian (N = 187; 64% East Asian, 20% South Asian, 16% South East Asian) or White (N = 145), mean age 37.3 years (SD = 10.43). Participants first viewed a short news excerpt about the shootings; 97% reported already being aware of the event, and the 11 who were not were dropped, leaving a final analytic sample of 321 (181 Asian, 140 White).
- Study 1 measures: race = dummy variable (0 = White, 1 = Asian). Embodied threat = the authors' new 4-item scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree; α = .96). Threat suppression = the authors' new 11-item cognitive and emotional suppression scale (1 = not at all to 7 = a great deal; α = .92). Both new scales were developed in separate measurement-development studies reported in Appendix A and summarized in Table 1 (content validation n = 115; EFA n = 151; CFA n = 400). Work withdrawal = 3 items adapted from Lehman and Simpson's (1992) 8-item scale, with items related to other constructs (e.g., turnover) or not general across occupations eliminated a priori (α = .73). Social engagement = 3 items adapted from Soane et al.'s (2012) scale (α = .91). Study 1 collected no moderator data; a previously validated task-related psychological safety measure (Liang et al., 2012) was collected and did not moderate the embodied-threat → threat-suppression link.
- Study 2, Phase 1 (May 2019 baseline, during a relatively quiet news cycle): 710 employees recruited through Cloud Research who lived in the United States, worked at least 20 hours per week, and belonged to one of three marginalized identity groups or the comparison group — 201 Black participants, 109 U.S. immigrants, 191 White women, and 209 White men; mean age 37 years (SD = 10.68). "Resting" baseline threat suppression (α = .98), work withdrawal (α = .70; ω = .72), and social engagement (α = .91) were measured with the same items and Likert scales as Study 1; the SEM tables report Phase 1 n = 403.
- Study 2, Phase 2 (October 2019; the publicized trial over the fatal shooting of Botham Jean and the police killing of Atatiana Jefferson): 201 Black and 200 White Phase 1 participants were re-surveyed, with a 51% response rate (102 Black, 101 White); 83% had heard of the events beforehand, and excluding the unaware left a final Phase 2 sample of 170 (88 Black, 82 White). Race = dummy variable (0 = White, 1 = Black). Measures repeated Study 1: embodied threat (4 items, α = .92), threat suppression (11 items, α = .98), work withdrawal (3 items, α = .67; ω = .71), social engagement (3 items, α = .91). The moderator — psychological safety of identity-based discussions — was captured from an open-ended item ("Have you felt comfortable discussing the event with your coworkers? Why or why not?") coded by three trained student coders on a scale from 1 (extremely safe for mega-threat discussions) to 5 (extremely unsafe), ICC(2) = .89, with observed values spanning the full 1–5 range (M = 1.96, SD = 1.20).
- Study 2, Phase 3 (May 2020, murder of George Floyd): the 203 Black and 201 White Phase 1 participants were followed up again; 64 Black and 57 White participants responded, a 30% response rate from baseline that the authors attribute to the more-than-one-year gap since Phase 1. Ninety-three percent had heard of the event, giving a final sample of 112 (61 Black, 51 White), mean age 39 years (SD = 11.19); 9 participants were unemployed because of the COVID pandemic and were not shown the work-variable items. Measures were as in Phase 2 — embodied threat (4 items, α = .94), threat suppression (11 items, α = .99), work withdrawal (3 items, α = .64; ω = .70), social engagement (3 items, α = .86) — but the safety of identity discussions was not assessed in this phase because the pandemic had drastically changed workplace interaction.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 321; Atlanta-area spa shootings), H1 supported: Asian participants experienced significantly higher embodied threat (M = 5.53, SD = 1.55) than White participants (M = 2.12, SD = 1.45), F(1, 319) = 405.0, p < .001.
- Study 1, H2 supported: the indirect effect of race on threat suppression through embodied threat was positive and significant (indirect effect = 0.83, SE = 0.14, 95% CI [0.56, 1.11]), and the hypothesized SEM fit the data relatively well (χ²(5) = 17.23, p = .004; CFI = .97, RMSEA = .087, SRMR = .04).
- Study 1, H3a and H3b supported: in the days following the event the mega-threat led Asian employees to engage in higher work withdrawal (overall indirect effect = 0.18, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.09, 0.29]) and lower social engagement (overall indirect effect = −0.14, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.26, −0.05]). H4 was not tested in Study 1, which collected no moderator data.
- Study 2, Phase 2 (n = 170; Botham Jean and Atatiana Jefferson), H1 and H2 supported: Black participants reported higher embodied threat (M = 4.75, SD = 1.73) than White participants (M = 2.36, SD = 1.36), F(1, 168) = 100.2, p < .001; the indirect effect of race on threat suppression through embodied threat was positive and significant (0.84, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [0.50, 1.18]) and remained significant after controlling for Phase 1 threat suppression (0.62, SE = 0.14, 95% CI [0.35, 0.89]). Black employees suppressed significantly more than White employees in Phase 2 (t(168) = 2.57, p = .01) but did not differ from them at the Phase 1 baseline (t(168) = 1.62, p = .11), which addresses the pre-existing-difference alternative explanation.
- Study 2, Phase 2, H3a and H3b supported: the serial mediated indirect effect of race was positive for work withdrawal (0.17, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [0.07, 0.30]) and negative for social engagement (−0.16, SE = 0.08, 95% CI [−0.37, −0.03]); both remained significant after controlling for Phase 1 threat suppression, work withdrawal, and social engagement (work withdrawal 0.07, 95% CI [0.001, 0.16]; social engagement −0.13, 95% CI [−0.25, −0.03]).
- Study 2, Phase 2, H4 supported — the only test of the moderator in the paper: a positive interaction between embodied threat and the safety of identity discussions on threat suppression (b = 0.10, SE = 0.04, p = .04). Because the moderator is scored 1 (extremely safe) to 5 (extremely unsafe), the embodied-threat → suppression slope became steeper as discussions became more unsafe, and simple slopes were significant at every observed moderator value. At +1 SD (unsafe) the positive indirect effect of race on work withdrawal (0.11, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.02, 0.25]) and the negative indirect effect on social engagement (−0.12, SE = 0.07, 95% CI [−0.31, −0.03]) remained significant; at −1 SD (safe) neither was significant (work withdrawal 0.01, 95% CI [−0.15, 0.17]; social engagement −0.01, 95% CI [−0.21, 0.16]).
- Study 2, Phase 3 (n = 112; murder of George Floyd), H1, H2, and H3a supported: Black participants reported higher embodied threat (M = 4.57, SD = 1.98) than White participants (M = 1.99, SD = 1.21), F(1, 110) = 65.74, p < .001; the indirect effect of race on threat suppression through embodied threat was positive and significant (0.63, SE = 0.24, 95% CI [0.18, 1.10]); and the serial mediated indirect effect of race on work withdrawal was positive and significant (0.12, SE = 0.08, 95% CI [0.01, 0.32]), remaining significant after controlling for Phase 1 threat suppression and work withdrawal.
- Study 2, Phase 3, H3b supported only in part: the serial mediated indirect effect of race on social engagement was negative and significant in the uncontrolled model (−0.10, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.28, −0.01]) but was no longer significant at the 95% CI level once baseline threat suppression and social engagement were controlled — a non-significant result the authors attribute to the COVID pandemic limiting workers' ability to connect socially with colleagues. H4 was not tested in Phase 3.
- Support pattern across the two studies: H1, H2, and H3a held in all three tests (Study 1, Phase 2, Phase 3), including after baseline controls in Study 2. H3b held in Study 1 and in Phase 2 (with and without baseline controls) and in the uncontrolled Phase 3 model, but not in Phase 3 with baselines controlled, so support for the social-engagement path is partial rather than uniform. H4 was tested only in Study 2, Phase 2, where it was supported.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper centers race in organizational theory by extending Ray's (2019) theory of racialized organizations to explain how macro societal events spill across organizational boundaries to shape individual behavior. It introduces and validates the construct of "embodied threat," distinct from identity threat and stereotype threat in that it concerns the fear of personally encountering identity-based physical harm, including death. It also advances work at the intersection of diversity and psychological safety by identifying the psychological safety of identity-based discussions as a distinct, identity-relevant form of safety.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that mega-threats can cause multitudes of minority employees to suppress threat and withdraw at once, and should increase the psychological safety of identity-based discussions (e.g., through open, honest manager–subordinate relationships and sustained—not merely reactionary—diversity commitments). Organizations can scrutinize racialized norms and routines, and offer flexibility to step away from work after a group-relevant mega-threat so employees can replenish depleted resources.

**Limitations**
The studies rely on online research samples and self-reported measures, raising concerns about common method variance and external validity, and required newly developed scales. All studies centered on mega-threats involving racial-minority victims in the United States, and were constrained to the specific events that occurred during the research period; the Phase 3 (George Floyd) social-engagement effect lost significance after controls, possibly due to COVID-era disruption of workplace interaction.

**Future Research**
Scholars could use experience-sampling or experimental methods to trace the duration and causal structure of mega-threat effects, and examine mega-threats involving other identities (e.g., religious or organizational) and contexts outside the United States. Further work could clarify how mega-threats vary in severity, reach, and cumulative effect, how employees form beliefs about the safety of identity-based discussions, and how mega-threats implicating dominant-group identities differ in their effects.
