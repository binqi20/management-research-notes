# “Invisible” Discrimination: Divergent Outcomes for the Nonprototypicality of Black Women

**Abstract**
By integrating the intersectional invisibility hypothesis with the behaviors from intergroup affect and stereotypes map framework, we examine the extent to which Black women’s dual-subordinated identities render them nonprototypical victims of discrimination, relative to White women and Black men, and the corresponding consequences. We predicted that Black women’s categorical nonprototypicality would reduce the believability of their discrimination claims, but that their nonprototypical attributes would lead to divergent treatment, depending on the type of discrimination alleged. Our predictions were supported across six experimental studies (Studies 1–4b). Specifically, Black women’s gender and racial discrimination claims were believed less compared to those made by White women and Black men, respectively. Moreover, after they alleged discrimination, Black women received less financial remedy versus White women, but more financial remedy versus Black men. Mediation testing revealed that the mechanisms underlying the believability and treatment of Black women were their nonprototypical categorization and attributes. Using discrimination data from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, Studies 5a and 5b replicated the effects observed on believability and financial remedy. By focusing on nonprototypicality at both categorical and attribute levels, we thus help to disentangle when Black women’s intersectional invisibility may result in either intersectional advantages or disadvantages.

**Research Question**
Why do people tend to exclude Black women from considerations of gender and racial discrimination, and what are the positive and negative implications of this relative "invisibility" for the believability of their discrimination claims and the financial remedy they are awarded?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Black women are perceived as less prototypical victims of gender discrimination than are White women.
- Hypothesis 2: Black women are perceived as less prototypical victims of racial discrimination than are Black men.
- Hypothesis 3a: Black women who allege gender discrimination are believed less than are White women.
- Hypothesis 3b: The difference between the believability of Black women and White women is mediated by ratings of their prototypicality.
- Hypothesis 4a: Black women who allege racial discrimination are believed less than are Black men.
- Hypothesis 4b: The difference between the believability of Black women and Black men is mediated by ratings of their prototypicality.
- Hypothesis 5a: Black women elicit less pity than do White women, which is mediated by lower ratings of their warmth.
- Hypothesis 6a: Black women elicit less contempt than do Black men, which is mediated by higher ratings of their warmth.
- Hypothesis 5b: Black women who allege gender discrimination are awarded less financial remedy than are White women.
- Hypothesis 5c: The difference between the financial remedy awarded to Black women and White women is serially mediated by ratings of their warmth and their elicitation of pity.
- Hypothesis 6b: Black women who allege racial discrimination are awarded more financial remedy than are Black men.
- Hypothesis 6c: The difference between the financial remedy awarded to Black women and Black men is serially mediated by ratings of their warmth and their elicitation of contempt.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Race of female claimant (White vs. Black) and gender of Black claimant (man vs. woman); type of discrimination alleged (gender vs. racial)
- DV(s): Categorical prototypicality, believability of discrimination claims, and financial remedy awarded
- Mediators: Categorical (non)prototypicality for believability; prototypical warmth and its elicitation of pity (gender cases) or contempt (racial cases), tested as serial mediators of financial remedy
- Moderators: Axis of discrimination (gender vs. racial) functions as the contingency that flips the direction of the nonprototypicality effect on financial remedy

Black women's dual-subordinated identities make them categorically nonprototypical of both "women" (prototyped by White women) and "Black people" (prototyped by Black men), which depresses the believability of their gender and racial discrimination claims. At the attribute level, the BIAS map predicts that warmth governs helping behavior: because gender-discrimination victims are stereotyped as warm and racial-discrimination victims as cold, Black women's intermediate warmth (lower than White women, higher than Black men) elicits less pity than White women but less contempt than Black men. These divergent emotional reactions translate into less financial remedy than White women in gender cases but more financial remedy than Black men in racial cases — so the same nonprototypicality is simultaneously a disadvantage and an advantage depending on the outcome and the axis of discrimination.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and preregistration: eight studies — six vignette experiments (Studies 1-4b) plus two archival analyses of EEOC discrimination charges (Studies 5a, 5b). Hypotheses, sample sizes, and analysis plans for Studies 1-4b were preregistered on the Open Science Framework; Studies 5a and 5b analyze publicly available, non-individually-identifiable datasets. Claimant race and gender are randomly assigned in the experiments, but the mediators (prototypicality, warmth, pity, contempt) are measured rather than manipulated, so the mediation evidence is associational; the two archival studies are correlational regressions with covariates and claim no causal identification strategy.
- Study 1 (N = 402 Prolific participants, paid $0.75): Princeton-trilogy attribute-checklist method. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of five categories — Black women, White women, Black men, people who experience gender discrimination, or people who experience racial discrimination — and selected the 10 attributes most stereotypic of that category from a 99-attribute list compiled from prior work. Categorical prototypicality was operationalized as attribute overlap: attributes chosen by at least 15% of participants in at least one condition were compared with chi-square tests (Yates's correction), and relative overlap was tested with Z-tests for proportions.
- Study 2a (301 recruited from Prolific at $0.50; 16 excluded for failing a memory check; final N = 285): between-subjects manipulation of claimant race in a fictitious New York Times article about an EEOC lawsuit against a large U.S.-based company ("Delsom Corp."), with race and gender signaled by a written description plus validated first and last names; the article did not state which type of discrimination was alleged. Prototypicality was measured indirectly (to avoid alerting participants to typicality) as the rated likelihood, on a 7-point scale, that the claim alleged gender discrimination; believability was a three-item 7-point measure of the likelihood a jury would believe, determine, and rule that the claimant was discriminated against because of gender (M = 4.81, SD = 1.38, alpha = .96). Analyses: t-tests plus SPSS PROCESS Model 4 bootstrapped mediation (10,000 resamples).
- Study 2b (301 recruited from Prolific at $0.50; 4 excluded for failing a memory check; final N = 297): parallel article manipulating the gender of a Black claimant (woman vs. man); prototypicality was the rated likelihood that the claim alleged racial discrimination; believability used three items parallel to Study 2a for the racial-discrimination claim (M = 5.12, SD = 1.31, alpha = .95); same t-test and PROCESS Model 4 analyses.
- Study 3 (two separate Prolific samples paid $0.85, both rating the same 99 attributes used in Study 1): the first sample (n = 80) rated the warmth of each attribute (1 = not at all warm, 7 = very warm), with warmth defined as an accommodating orientation that profits others more than the self; the second sample (n = 161) was randomly assigned to rate how much each attribute would elicit "pity and sympathy" or "contempt and hostility" (1 = not at all, 7 = very much). These attribute-level ratings were then imputed into the five group attribute lists generated in Study 1 and compared with pairwise contrasts and PROCESS Model 4 mediation.
- Study 4a (N = 201 from CloudResearch's approved-participant list, paid $0.50): within-subjects design in which participants imagined a jury evaluating two class-action gender-discrimination lawsuits with closely matched facts, one brought by a group of Black women and one by a group of White women (plaintiff race varied within subject, order counterbalanced). Warmth was three items on whether a jury would view the plaintiffs as warm, good natured, and pleasant (alpha > .96); pity was a single item on whether a jury would feel pity toward each group; financial remedy was "How much money in financial remedy do you think a jury would award the [White female/Black female] plaintiffs?" on a 13-point scale (1 = $0 to 13 = $600,000). Analyses: repeated-measures ANOVA and MEMORE two-condition within-participants serial mediation (10,000 bootstraps).
- Study 4b (201 recruited from CloudResearch at $0.50; 10 excluded for failing a memory check; final N = 191): parallel within-subjects design with two class-action racial-discrimination lawsuits, one brought by a group of Black women and one by a group of Black men; warmth used the same three items (alpha > .95), contempt replaced pity, and financial remedy used the same 13-point scale; same ANOVA and MEMORE serial-mediation analyses.
- Study 5a (archival, gender-discrimination charges): two public EEOC datasets merged — claimant race from BuzzFeed News (2017) and closure plus financial-remedy outcomes from the Center for Public Integrity (2019). After control variables were entered, 3,566 charges filed with the EEOC between 2011 and 2016 had known closure outcomes (1,300 Black women, 2,266 White women) and 707 contained financial-remedy information (223 Black women, 484 White women); because the BuzzFeed file contained only sexual-harassment claims, every gender-discrimination charge analyzed also included a sexual-harassment allegation. Believability was operationalized as the closure outcome (1 = "no reasonable cause," 0 = other closure outcome) and financial remedy as the monetary benefits in dollars received by complainants. The independent variable was claimant race (0 = White, 1 = Black), with four covariates: number of issues alleged, employer size (0 = under 500 employees, 1 = 500 or more), private company, and masculinity of industry (1 = strongly feminine to 7 = strongly masculine, rated by independent coders). Estimation: binary logistic regression for closure, linear regression for remedy, with no-controls and multiple-imputation robustness checks.
- Study 5b (archival, racial-discrimination charges): Center for Public Integrity (2019) data on charges filed between 2010 and 2017. After control variables were entered, 99,533 charges had known closure outcomes (50,552 Black women, 48,981 Black men) and 11,597 contained monetary-benefit information (5,916 Black women, 5,681 Black men). The two dependent variables were operationalized exactly as in Study 5a and the same four covariates were entered; the independent variable was claimant gender (0 = man, 1 = woman); same logistic and linear estimation with the same robustness checks.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supported Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2. The prototypical gender-discrimination victim shared a greater proportion of attributes with White women than with Black women (Z = 2.62, p = .009), and the prototypical racial-discrimination victim shared a greater proportion with Black men than with Black women (Z = 4.22, p < .001). Only 17% of the compared attributes were selected at the same frequency for Black women and gender-discrimination victims, versus 52% for White women; likewise 17% for Black women and racial-discrimination victims, versus 81% for Black men.
- Study 2a supported Hypothesis 3a and Hypothesis 3b. The Black claimant was rated less likely than the White claimant to have alleged gender discrimination (M = 4.45 vs. 5.76; t(1, 283) = 7.18, p < .001, d = .85), her gender-discrimination claim was believed less (M = 4.58 vs. 5.07; t(1, 283) = 3.03, p = .003, d = .36), and the race effect on believability ran through perceived gender-discrimination likelihood (indirect effect b = .43, SE = .10, 95% CI [.25, .65]), with a nonsignificant direct effect (95% CI [-.26, .38]).
- Study 2b supported Hypothesis 4a and Hypothesis 4b. The Black woman's racial-discrimination claim was believed less than the Black man's (M = 4.90 vs. 5.34; t(1, 295) = 2.91, p = .004, d = .34), mediated by the perceived likelihood of alleging racial discrimination (indirect effect b = .14, SE = .06, 95% CI [.04, .27]); the direct effect did not reach significance (95% CI [-.002, .59]).
- Study 3 supported Hypothesis 5a and Hypothesis 6a. Black women's prototypical attributes were rated less warm than White women's (M = 2.92 vs. 3.56; p < .001, d = .86) and than gender-discrimination victims' (M = 3.17; p = .036, d = .33), but warmer than Black men's (M = 2.65; p = .019, d = .38) and racial-discrimination victims' (M = 2.60; p = .005, d = .43). Warmth mediated Black women's lower elicitation of pity relative to White women (indirect effect b = .24, SE = .05, 95% CI [.15, .33]) and their lower elicitation of contempt relative to Black men (indirect effect b = .27, SE = .11, 95% CI [.05, .48]).
- Study 4a supported Hypothesis 5b and Hypothesis 5c. Black female plaintiffs were rated less warm (M = 4.55 vs. 5.37; F(1, 200) = 68.15, p < .001, f = .58), elicited less pity (M = 4.88 vs. 5.32; F(1, 200) = 12.52, p = .001, f = .25), and were awarded significantly less financial remedy than White female plaintiffs (M = 6.19 vs. 7.42 on the 13-point scale; F(1, 200) = 27.19, p < .001, f = .37). The serial indirect effect through warmth and then pity was significant (b = -.89, SE = .16, 95% CI [-1.18, -.57]) while the direct effect was not.
- Study 4b supported Hypothesis 6b and Hypothesis 6c — the sign reverses here relative to Study 4a. Black female plaintiffs were viewed as warmer than Black male plaintiffs (M = 4.45 vs. 3.92; F(1, 190) = 26.53, p < .001, f = .43), elicited less contempt (M = 3.41 vs. 3.74; F(1, 190) = 13.38, p < .001, f = .27), and were awarded more financial remedy (M = 6.10 vs. 5.47; F(1, 190) = 14.98, p < .001, f = .28), with a significant serial indirect effect through warmth and contempt (b = .14, SE = .07, 95% CI [.03, .29]) and a nonsignificant direct effect.
- Study 5a replicated the gender-axis effects in the archival EEOC data. Charges filed by Black women were significantly more likely than those filed by White women to be closed with a "no reasonable cause" finding, i.e., believed less (b = .23, SE = .07, Wald = 12.01, p = .001, OR = 1.26), and Black female complainants who received remedy were awarded less money on average than White women ($13,008 vs. $28,982; standardized B = -0.13, p = .001).
- Study 5b replicated the racial-axis effects. Charges filed by Black women were significantly more likely than those filed by Black men to be closed with "no reasonable cause" (b = .05, SE = .02, Wald = 11.02, p = .001, OR = 1.05), and Black female complainants who received remedy were awarded more money on average than Black men ($18,878 vs. $17,888; standardized B = 0.02, p = .026). This is the weakest test in the set: in the same data analyzed without the four covariates, the gender difference in monetary benefits was nonsignificant and slightly negative (B = -.01, p = .288), although the multiply imputed pooled results were significant both with and without controls.
- Overall pattern: every one of the twelve hypotheses was supported in the six experiments, and the four focal archival tests pointed the same way, so the believability disadvantage held on both the gender and the racial axis, while the financial-remedy effect reversed sign across axes — less remedy than White women in gender-discrimination cases, more remedy than Black men in racial-discrimination cases.

**Theoretical Contribution**
By integrating the intersectional invisibility hypothesis with the BIAS map, the paper distinguishes categorical prototypicality (which drives recognition-based outcomes like believability) from prototypical attributes (which drive support-based outcomes like financial remedy), showing that victim recognition does not necessarily translate into victim support. This integration explains when multiply subordinated individuals experience double jeopardy versus intersectional advantage, reconciling two perspectives prior research has tended to pit against one another and extending intersectional theory into organizationally relevant discrimination contexts.

**Practical Implication**
Initiatives to combat discrimination may inadvertently exclude Black women because their claims are categorically less believable; allies (particularly White allies), accountability-based diversity programs (e.g., diversity committees, mentoring), and tailored efforts addressing intersectional experiences are needed. The authors further argue for structural and legal solutions — recognizing intersectional discrimination claims (per the 2006 EEOC guidance) and amending Title VII to cover combinations of protected bases — so Black women are not forced into single-axis comparisons to White women or Black men.

**Limitations**
Experimental samples were largely homogeneous online convenience samples relying on vignettes, limiting diversity of populations and stimuli. The archival EEOC data lacked variables such as claimants' organizational position or salary that likely affect financial remedy, and the correlational mediation models cannot provide an explicit causal test of categorical prototypicality versus prototypical attributes or rule out alternative explanations such as broader racism and sexism.

**Future Research**
Future work should manipulate the proposed mediators and test the framework among more diverse populations and with a wider array of stimuli, and examine factors such as status and salary that may shape financial remedy. The principles should also be tested in other multiply subordinated groups (e.g., Asian women and men), whose distinct warmth stereotypes may yield different prototypicality and treatment patterns.
