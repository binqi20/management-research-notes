# Living Up to the Hype: How New Ventures Manage the Resource and Liability of Future-Oriented Visions within the Nascent Market of Impact Investing

**Abstract**
Hype is a collective vision and promise of a possible future, around which attention, excitement, and expectations increase over time. Within nascent markets, hype can thus serve as a cultural resource by which entrepreneurs might encourage greater early stakeholder support and resources. Yet, as hype-driven support couples with mounting temporal and categorical expectations, this can also limit ventures' flexibility during the entrepreneurial process. Drawing on an inductive, longitudinal, and comparative study of three new ventures within the much-hyped nascent market of impact investing, we develop an emergent theory of hype management, illustrating the field organizing practices that give rise to different forms of social proof, thereby allowing new ventures sufficient flexibility to convert hype into a sustained entrepreneurial opportunity. Our account contributes directly to contemporary public conversations of hype by revealing how entrepreneurs might engage with, and indeed live up to, the hype surrounding nascent markets without succumbing to the deceit and disappointment typically associated with hype. Beyond this, our findings extend existing scholarship on entrepreneurship within nascent markets, the sociology of expectations, and the realization of distant futures.

**Research Question**
How do entrepreneurs in nascent markets engage with and manage hype so as to retain temporal and categorical flexibility? The study asks how new ventures convert the short-term cultural resource of hype into sustained opportunity while managing its long-term relational liabilities.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper — the study is inductive, longitudinal, and comparative, and it states no hypotheses and no formally numbered propositions anywhere in the text. The authors use an inductive theory-building approach suited to exploratory studies where theory is lacking, developing an emergent model of hype management from cross-case analysis rather than testing a priori predictions.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Hype as a short-term unbounded cultural resource versus a long-term relational liability; temporal flexibility versus categorical flexibility.
- Field-organizing practices: Cultural practices (motivating future state, locating the opportunity, role claiming) and relational practices (configuring boundaries, smoothing exchange, local network brokerage).
- Emergent mechanism — social proof: Practices generate third-party endorsement (varying in salience) and engagement (varying in proximity), yielding salient vs. proximate social proof.
- Boundary conditions / outcomes: Salient social proof increases categorical flexibility but reduces temporal flexibility; proximate social proof increases temporal flexibility but reduces categorical flexibility.

Across the three SSEs (London's SSX, Singapore's IIX, Toronto's SVX), the ventures' differing cultural and relational field-organizing efforts produced distinct forms of social proof. That social proof, rather than scarce material proof (e.g., listings or share-price metrics), is what allowed stakeholders' disappointment to be deferred and shaped the categorical and temporal flexibility each venture retained while attempting to "live up to the hype." The model thus theorizes how entrepreneurs actively stimulate social proof rather than passively receiving it through herding.

**Data & Measures**
- Case selection and observation window: the three cases are the first three SSEs founded, launched independently and yet within three months of each other in 2013 — London (Social Stock Exchange, SSX), Singapore (Impact Investment Exchange, IIX), and Toronto (Social Venture Connection, SVX). Each founder had attended the 2007 Rockefeller Foundation meeting at which the term "impact investing" was allegedly coined, and each venture initially launched and promoted itself as an SSE, giving a consistent starting point relative to the global hype. The sources together provide more than a decade-long observation period, from the first claims of the need for an SSE in 2007, through early white papers and the 2013 launches, to the ventures' first five years of trading. Table 1 records each case's regulatory environment, pre-launch and at-launch business model, governance, philanthropic funding, impact-measurement approach, and business model at the end of the observation period.
- Data corpus, per case (Table 2): 42 interviews, approximately 64 hours in total — 14 for SSX, 7 for IIX, 21 for SVX — comprising interviews with the ventures conducted each year plus interviews with other key staff and stakeholders. 5 site visits over 14 days (SSX 2015 and 2017; IIX 2016; SVX 2015 and 2017). 12 local events attended, yielding 41 hours of observation (SSX 3, IIX 5, SVX 4). More than 1,000 pages of company and website documentation (issuer, member, and impact reports; investor research books, manuals, and surveys; public slide decks; a listing guide; annual accounts; archived website; details of trust transfer). 159 public listserv email updates (SSX 33 across 2016–2018; IIX 102 across 2015–2019; SVX 24 across 2017–2019). Plus media coverage of each platform. Tables 3 and 4 label the data types behind each case as I = Interview, O = Observation, D = Archival documentation, M = Media: SSX and SVX draw on all four, IIX on interviews, archival documentation, and media.
- Building the comparison: chronologies of events were written for each venture from interviews, email announcements, company documents, company web data, and newspaper and media reports, noting when funding or regulatory approvals were secured, partnerships announced, programs or services initiated, staff and board appointments made, events hosted or attended, deals or platform activity listed, reports published, and achievements noted. The timelines were then plotted visually to expose differences in resource acquisition and expectation growth over time. A second researcher reviewed the timelines and raised the rival contextual explanations one might expect to generate the variation — initial funding requirements and the ventures' resulting resource dependency, the regulatory environment, and possible cultural differences moderating global hype effects — each of which the analysis and discussion then ruled out.
- Coding field organizing: practices were compared systematically across the three cases using replication logic (Yin, 2003) and combined into two broader sets — cultural practices (motivating future state, locating the opportunity, role claiming), which address the abstractness of hype, and relational practices (configuring boundaries, smoothing exchange, local network brokerage), which address its diffuseness. Tables 3 and 4 give each practice a definition, a rationale, and a per-case coding.
- Coding the focal construct, social proof: the analysis returned to the case chronologies and separated third-party endorsement (e.g., media mentions, high-profile praise) from engagement (e.g., partnerships, strategic involvement, investment). Endorsement was coded for salience — whether the respective stakeholder endorsement "was evaluated as attention-worthy" — on three levels: unlikely to increase awareness of the venture, likely to increase local awareness, and likely to increase both local and global awareness. Engagement was coded for proximity — whether it was deeply involved in or "socially distant" from operationalization of the vision (MacKenzie, 1998) — also on three levels: limited to no involvement, involvement in strategic discussions of the venture opportunity, and deeper involvement in the cocreation of the venture opportunity.
- Coding the outcomes: relational liabilities were captured from stakeholder interviews about mounting expectations or concerns about venture performance and about resources withheld after the ventures violated expectations. Temporal and categorical flexibility were captured from venture interview data describing historic or anticipated resource flows and stakeholder support, together with press releases and other secondary data indicating delays in impact reports or shifts in the business models. Tables 3 and 4 define both flexibilities as the degree of discretion stakeholders afford entrepreneurs — over the urgency of demonstrated progress (temporal) and over the capacity to pivot (categorical).
- Identification: none claimed. There are no measured variables, no estimation model, and no statistical identification strategy. The paper does write that differences in social proof caused differences in the relational liabilities imposed by stakeholders, but that inference rests on cross-case replication logic plus the analytic exclusion of the contextual alternatives above, not on a design that isolates an effect; the ventures' outcomes were unknown when data collection began, and the diversity of sources is offered as triangulation rather than as identification.

**Key Findings**
- The emergent model (Figure 1): hype operates at the societal level as an unbounded short-term cultural resource and a long-term relational liability; ventures address its abstractness through cultural practices and its diffuseness through relational practices; those field-organizing practices generate third-party endorsement that varies in salience and engagement that varies in proximity; and the resulting social proof shapes the categorical and temporal flexibility a venture retains. Social proof was almost always the form the evidence took, relied upon in lieu of material proof such as the number of listings, average share-price performance, and average impact performance, which stayed scarce in all three cases. Its provision gave stakeholders a basis on which disappointment could be deferred or even displaced despite meagre and incremental results, absolving entrepreneurs of the need for revised storytelling.
- The two central relationships run in opposite directions, so the finding is a trade-off rather than a uniform benefit: salient social proof increased categorical flexibility for ventures but reduced temporal flexibility, whereas proximate social proof increased temporal flexibility but reduced categorical flexibility. The three cases do not share one outcome, and the pattern must be read per case.
- SSX (London) — unsustained salient social proof, reducing both temporal and categorical flexibility. Its cultural practices (impact investing as a financial revolution with the United Kingdom as financial leader; the SSE as both a familiar solution and a world first; role claiming as the ideal global platform) won highly salient early endorsement: the then Prime Minister's recognition at the launch, office space in the London Stock Exchange building, and Big Society Capital as first large investor. Its relational practices, however, did little to demarcate field boundaries, decentralized impact authentication to participants, and offered only brief tertius iungens; stakeholders were left confused about the venture's role, expertise, and market boundaries, financial intermediaries came to see it as a competitor, and information asymmetries persisted — Table 4 records the social proof generated by these practices as NA. Celebrity endorsement produced no lasting salient proof and engagement never became proximate, so stakeholders demanded material proof points, few planned deals materialized, and a vicious cycle further shortened temporal flexibility while continuous pivoting was read as failure to meet expectations, cutting categorical flexibility too. Five years after launch SSX lacked all forms of social or material proof, grant applications and university partnerships fell through, and in 2018 it quietly shut its doors; its entrepreneurial opportunity to create an SSE had collapsed.
- IIX (Singapore) — sustained salient social proof, increasing categorical flexibility but reducing temporal flexibility. It amplified the hype, claimed the role of expert global leader (initially describing itself as the "world's first regulated social stock exchange"), policed field boundaries against dilution, authenticated impact through its own proprietary framework, and brokered as tertius gaudens to become the central coordinating actor in East Asian impact investing. Despite no progress on securing listings on the Mauritius exchange, sustained endorsement of the founders' renown and expertise supplied salient social proof, and stakeholders afforded the venture categorical flexibility: IIX strayed from the original SSE business model into social enterprise consulting, private investment advisory services, educational training, crowdfunding, and foreign aid delivery. The same salient proof raised expectations about the speed of delivery, reducing temporal flexibility — the venture moved to almost weekly stakeholder emails and by 2018 was publicly quantifying impact (lives impacted, private-sector capital unlocked, enterprises supported, carbon emissions avoided).
- SVX (Toronto) — proximate social proof, increasing temporal flexibility but reducing categorical flexibility. It discursively moderated the hype, located the SSE as one piece of a broader impact investing market requiring long-term local collaboration, broadened rather than policed boundaries, outsourced impact authentication to B Lab's B-Corporation certification, and sustained a tertius iungens orientation, cocreating with mainstream exchanges, regulators, government departments, and philanthropists. Deep partner involvement generated proximate social proof that stakeholders accepted as a proxy for material proof: in 2015 the platform had facilitated only about $4 million in transactions and some listed enterprises went elsewhere for growth capital, yet stakeholders pushed on this point said they did not require anything more. That bought time to deliver, and regulatory approvals were sustained and extended to operating as an "exempt market dealer" able to complete transactions. But the same proof deepened perceived collective commitment to the original exchange vision and brought regulatory scrutiny of the business model, narrowing categorical flexibility: instead of diversifying financial products as IIX did, SVX scaled the existing model globally by white-labeling and licensing its proprietary platform to entrepreneurs elsewhere.
- Two pathways follow for entrepreneurs navigating hype's long-term relational liabilities: develop salient social proof to gain the categorical flexibility to pivot the business model toward more feasible and less distant possibilities (IIX), or produce proximate social proof to gain the temporal flexibility to make incremental progress toward the original, more distant vision (SVX). SSX, which sustained neither form, is the negative case.
- The findings also reverse the directionality implied by prior social proof research: rather than proof emerging only from imitative or herding behaviour among evaluators, entrepreneurs' own efforts to engage with and manage hype can directly stimulate it, at the venture level, through attention-grabbing endorsements and deepened stakeholder engagement. Salience and proximity are established as distinct attributes of social proof with opposite consequences for flexibility.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops an emergent theory of hype management, theorizing hype at the collective level as a vision of a possible future that is simultaneously a short-term cultural resource and a long-term relational liability, distinct from venture-level promises. It challenges and extends research on social proof by showing that entrepreneurs' own field-organizing efforts can directly generate social proof (rather than it merely emerging from imitative or herding behavior), and by distinguishing salient from proximate social proof and their differential effects on categorical and temporal flexibility. It contributes to scholarship on entrepreneurship in nascent markets, the sociology of expectations, and the realization of distant futures.

**Practical Implication**
For entrepreneurs in hyped nascent markets, the study reveals at least two pathways to navigate hype's relational liabilities: developing salient social proof to gain the categorical flexibility to pivot toward more feasible, less distant possibilities (as with IIX), or producing proximate social proof through deep stakeholder engagement to gain the temporal flexibility to make incremental progress toward the original distant vision (as with SVX). Managing hype through field organizing, rather than only revised storytelling, lengthens and broadens the runway available to deliver on collective visions.

**Limitations**
Not reported in paper

**Future Research**
Future research could investigate how ventures attempt to produce different types of social proof at the same time or over time—oscillating between salient and proximate forms—as a way to gain different forms of required flexibility along the entrepreneurial journey, and how such proof types interact alongside material proof as complements rather than substitutes.
