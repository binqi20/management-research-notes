# Remaking Capitalism: The Strength of Weak Legislation in Mobilizing B Corporation Certification

**Abstract**
Myriad cross-sector initiatives seek to remake capitalism into a more just, sustainable, and inclusive system. But how do these distributed efforts—which often vary in strength—interact? To answer this question, we attend to the interaction between weak and strong governance reforms. Drawing on longstanding research on organizational values and the sociology of law, we theorize how the enactment of weak and broad sustainability legislation is likely to increase pressure on values-driven businesses to pursue both values authentication and material authentication by way of strong third-party certification. We test our conceptual model by examining the effects of the frequently criticized benefit corporation legislation passed in 36 U.S. jurisdictions on the related B Corporation certification. We find that new certifications and recertifications both increase in jurisdictions with such legislation and these effects are amplified or attenuated depending on corporate sustainability norms in the region. Taken together, our findings contribute to the intensifying societal conversation regarding the prospects for remaking capitalism, illustrating how even weak legislation can contribute to systems change not only by encouraging incremental sustainability reforms within a field but also by triggering an authentication imperative that mobilizes values-driven businesses to pursue rigorous certifications.

**Research Question**
How does the enactment of weak sustainability legislation (benefit corporation legislation) affect companies' adoption and retention of related, albeit voluntary, B Corporation certification, and under what regional institutional conditions are these effects amplified or attenuated?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The enactment of weak sustainability legislation increases the imperative for values authentication, thereby increasing initial certification within the jurisdiction.
- H2: In regions with weak corporate sustainability norms, the imperative for values authentication introduced by weak sustainability legislation is amplified, further increasing initial certification within the jurisdiction.
- H3: The scope of newly enacted sustainability legislation increases the imperative for values-driven businesses to materially authenticate their compliant practices, thereby increasing recertification within the jurisdiction.
- H4: In regions with strong corporate sustainability norms, the imperative for material authentication introduced by broad sustainability legislation is amplified, further increasing recertification within the jurisdiction.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Enactment of benefit corporation legislation (BCL); legislative weakness (Model-style vs. weaker Delaware-style BCL); legislative scope (continuous count of 24 statutory criteria addressed)
- DV(s): Initial B Corp certifications (state-year counts); B Corp recertification / decertification (firm-level survival)
- Mediators: Implied "authentication imperative" — values authentication (initial certification) and material authentication (recertification); not separately estimated
- Moderators: Regional corporate sustainability norms (reversed, normalized count of ESG concerns by U.S. Census division)

The authors argue that weak, broad sustainability legislation paradoxically strengthens demand for rigorous voluntary certification. Weak legislation enables low-cost, low-scrutiny affiliation (e.g., the benefit corporation legal form), heightening the risk that non-sustainable firms counterfeit field membership; genuinely values-driven firms respond by seeking strong certification for values authentication. Separately, as the scope of legislation grows, it expands future enforcement opportunities and noncompliance risk, prompting already-certified firms to recertify for material authentication. Regional sustainability norms condition these effects: the values-authentication effect is amplified where norms are weak ("dirty"), whereas the material-authentication effect is amplified where norms are strong ("clean").

**Data & Measures**
Both datasets derive from B Lab's publicly available B Impact Assessment (BIA) dataset (version #93ba3637, dated November 2019), restricted to U.S. observations and supplemented by the authors' own data-validation effort, which added 430 observations for 326 unique companies. The state-level analysis that tests H1-H2 is an annual panel of the 50 states and the District of Columbia from 2008 through 2018; its DV, initial B Corp certifications, is a count of newly certified B Corps per state-year after assigning each B Corp to its home state, and the estimating samples are 564 state-years across 47 states (Table 2 Models 1, 2, 4) and 528 state-years across 44 states (Models 3, 5-7), with three states dropped for having no B Corps. Benefit corporation legislation (BCL) is coded from public legislative records as an indicator equal to 1 in years when BCL was in effect in a state, and is separately split into Model-style BCL (28 states) and the weaker Delaware-style BCL (7 states). The firm-level analysis that tests H3-H4 is a separate survival panel of U.S.-based B Corporations covering certification and decertification events from May 2007 through November 6, 2019, with survival time coded as "certification spells" to accommodate changing and inconsistently enforced recertification timelines; its estimating samples are 3,327 spell-observations on 1,821 firms with 746 decertifications (Table 4 Models 1, 2, 4) and 3,232 observations on 1,756 firms with 719 decertifications (Models 3, 5), and 551 companies (30%) form the untreated baseline. BCL scope is a continuous count of how many of 24 statutory criteria - compiled from legal reviews of BCL and hand-coded from each state's statute by two of the authors - a state's legislation addresses. The moderator, corporate sustainability norms, is the reversed, normalized count of MSCI ESG KLD "concerns" for publicly traded firms from 2005 to 2018, aggregated to each of nine U.S. Census divisions by headquarters location, so higher scores indicate "cleaner" regional norms. State controls include B Local chapter presence, B Corp employment, B Lab employees, Google Trends search intensity, establishments, tax-exempt organizations, population, LEED buildings, other social enterprise legislation, Democrat-controlled state government, LCV score, and benefit corporation counts; firm controls include logged BIA score, Best for the World recognition, impact variety (Blau index across the five impact areas), employee size range, sector, BIA version, and certification-cycle dummies. Identification rests on a "staggered adoption design" exploiting variation in the timing of BCL across states, which the authors describe as a special case of difference-in-differences while acknowledging that it "involves making some strong assumptions (e.g., random policy timing)"; certifications are estimated with two-way fixed-effects Poisson regression (robust standard errors clustered by state, firm count lagged two years as the exposure term) and decertification with discrete-time complementary log-log hazard models (robust standard errors clustered by firm).

**Key Findings**
H1 is supported: BCL is positively related to initial B Corp certifications (Model 2 IRR = 1.479, p < .01; Model 3 IRR = 1.467, p < .01), implying roughly 48% more certifications per year than in states without BCL. Consistent with the theorized weakness mechanism, the effect is larger for the weakest statute - Delaware-style BCL (Model 5 IRR = 2.336, p < .001) versus Model-style BCL (Model 5 IRR = 1.316, p < .10) - and a Wald test rejects equality of the two coefficients (4.89, p < .05). H2 is supported for the pooled measure: the BCL x corporate sustainability norms interaction is negative and significant (Model 6 IRR = 0.764, p < .05), so BCL's positive effect is largest in the "dirtiest" regions and fully dissipates in the cleanest. Support is only partial once BCL is disaggregated, because the Model-style interaction falls short of significance (Model 7 IRR = 0.824, p = .12) while the Delaware-style interaction is much stronger (IRR = 0.304, p < .001; Wald test of the difference 12.48, p < .001). H3 is affirmed: broader BCL scope significantly lowers the hazard of decertification in both models (HR = 0.986, p < .05), meaning firms in states with broader BCL are more likely to recertify, with predicted six-cycle retention rising from 13% (no BCL) to 16% (narrowest BCL) and 21% (broadest BCL). H4 is affirmed: the BCL scope x corporate sustainability norms interaction is negative and significant (Model 4 HR = 0.981, p < .05; Model 5 HR = 0.979, p < .01), and the retention benefit is concentrated in the cleanest regions (six-cycle survival of 29% for broadest BCL versus 18% for narrowest and 13% for no BCL) while being effectively absent in dirty regions (12-14% across all three regimes); the direct BCL scope coefficient is no longer statistically significant once this interaction is included (HR = 0.995 and 0.996). Among non-hypothesized results, other social enterprise legislation is negatively related to initial certifications, and a larger benefit corporation population is associated with lower decertification (HR = 0.837).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces a systemic, governance-strength perspective on intersecting reforms, parsing legislation into distinct dimensions of strength (unambiguous requirements and strict enforcement) and scope (breadth of regulative requirements) rather than treating legislation as uniformly strong and mandatory. It shows that institutional incompatibility between distributed, imperfectly aligned governance efforts can be generative—even weak legislation can mobilize systems change by triggering an authentication imperative—thereby challenging the assumption that reform efforts must be strong, robust, and aligned to drive change.

**Practical Implication**
For advocates seeking to remake capitalism, "weak" legislation is not necessarily a failure: even ambiguous, lax benefit corporation statutes can catalyze adoption and retention of rigorous certifications like B Corp. Movement organizations such as B Lab can treat legislation and certification as complementary levers, recognizing that legal scaffolding—however imperfect—can mobilize values-driven firms while regional sustainability norms shape where each lever works best.

**Limitations**
The staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design relies on strong assumptions (e.g., quasi-random policy timing), and the proposed values- and material-authentication mechanisms are inferred from differential effects rather than directly measured. The setting is confined to U.S. B Corps and benefit corporation legislation, and some firm-level timing (e.g., exact decertification dates and variable recertification timelines) is imperfectly observed, requiring "certification spell" coding choices.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine intersecting weak and strong reforms in other empirical contexts and governance domains to assess the generalizability of the authentication mechanisms. Researchers might also explore how additional forms of distributed, overlapping governance interact—reinforcing or undermining broader systems-change efforts—beyond the legislation–certification pairing studied here.
