# Upward Mobility, the Cleft Habitus, and Speaking Up: How Class Transitions Relate to Individual and Organizational Antecedents of Voice

**Abstract**
This research explores the relationship between upward mobility and voice. We build hypotheses and find evidence that rather than being imprinted with a lower sense of efficacy, the upwardly mobile possess a high internal sense of efficacy and are likely to speak up. However, this positive pathway to voice for the upwardly mobile is offset by managers being more inclined to solicit voice from those who come from, and have remained in, higher social class positions. We test our hypotheses in three studies: a field survey, a preregistered analysis of an archival dataset, and a preregistered experiment. This work provides evidence that the internal self-views long associated with those from lower social class backgrounds may not adequately describe the upwardly mobile. Contrary to having a persistent low sense of their abilities, we find that the upwardly mobile espouse high efficacy and do speak up but that managers appear less likely to provide them with equal opportunities for voice, instead seeking it from employees from more elite backgrounds. This work also extends theories of employee voice by showing how managers' decisions about whose input to solicit are influenced by employees' socially significant characteristics in ways that could lead to systematic disadvantages.

**Research Question**
Do the prior lower-class experiences of the upwardly mobile imprint them with a reduced sense of their own abilities and result in less voice, or does upward transition correspond to a high general sense of efficacy that facilitates voice? And do managers view the upwardly mobile as equally valuable sources for input as employees from higher-class backgrounds, or do they provide more opportunities for input to those with elite pedigrees?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Upward mobility is positively related to self-efficacy.
- H2: Self-efficacy positively mediates the relationship between upward mobility and voice.
- H3: Upward mobility is negatively related to managerial voice solicitation.
- H4: Managerial voice solicitation negatively mediates the relationship between upward mobility and employee voice.

The hypotheses are distributed across the three studies rather than tested in all of them: Study 1 tests the full theorized model (all four hypotheses) in a single organization, Study 2 is described as providing evidence for Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2 in a larger sample with decade-scale lags, and Study 3 is described as providing causal evidence for Hypothesis 3 plus evidence on the mechanisms (perceived competence and cultural fit) behind it.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Upward mobility / social class background (operationalized via parental and own occupational prestige and education; in Study 3, manipulated class trajectory: upwardly mobile vs. stable middle-class)
- DV(s): Employee voice (Studies 1-2); managerial voice solicitation (Studies 1, 3)
- Mediators: General self-efficacy (individual pathway); managerial voice solicitation (organizational pathway); in Study 3, perceived competence and perceived cultural fit
- Moderators: Evaluator's own social class position (Study 3 additional analysis — competence association strengthens at higher evaluator class)

The paper integrates the "cleft habitus" idea (that class transitions partially rewrite, rather than fully imprint, one's dispositions) with the dual-pathways model of voice. Along the individual pathway, upward mobility coincides with a high internal sense of self-efficacy that motivates the upwardly mobile to overcome fears and speak up. Along the organizational pathway, lingering signals of a lower-class origin lead managers to perceive the upwardly mobile as less competent and worse cultural fits, so managers solicit their input less often. These two pathways operate in opposing directions, such that the upwardly mobile jump over barriers to voice while managers lower the barriers for those from more privileged backgrounds.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (field study), sample and design: 490 adults employed in the products division of a large and prestigious technology company headquartered in the United States; a three-wave lagged survey with each wave separated by one week and question order randomized within surveys to reduce common method variance; 229 of the 490 employees completed all three waves (47% response rate). Average respondent age was 37 years; 64% were male and 80% were White. Because 96% of respondents held a bachelor's degree or higher and occupational prestige was largely equivalent, class mobility was operationalized as a function of class background, with lower-class backgrounds corresponding to a greater class distance travelled to a largely shared destination. Respondents and nonrespondents did not differ in age, but fewer women were respondents (M = .65, SD = .48) than nonrespondents (M = .55, SD = .50, F = 4.10, p = .04).
- Study 1 measures: social class background (Time 1) = parental education (highest level attained by mother and father) plus parental occupational prestige (each parent's stated occupation converted to a U.S. Census occupation code, then to a Duncan socioeconomic index prestige score using the Center for Demography and Ecology crosswalk), standardized and summed taking the highest value indicated for each parent; self-efficacy (Time 2) = Chen and colleagues' (2001) four-item Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale (α = .82), 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); supervisor solicitation of voice (Time 2) = four items (α = .83) from Fast et al. (2014) rating how frequently the boss engaged in each behaviour, 1 (never) to 5 (always); employee voice (Time 3) = three self-reported items (α = .91) adapted from prior voice research, 1 (never) to 5 (always). Controls were employee gender, race, and psychological safety (five items, α = .87, from Liang, Farh, and Farh, 2012). Analysis: confirmatory factor analysis of the four-factor measurement model, path analysis testing all hypothesized relationships simultaneously, and mediation assessed with 5,000 bootstrap samples with replacement. The design is lagged but associational; no causal identification strategy is claimed, and the authors treat the survey as correlational.
- Study 2 (archival study), data: a preregistered analysis of the Wisconsin Longitudinal Survey, a long-term study of a cohort of 10,317 men and women from the state of Wisconsin who graduated high school in 1957 and were followed into their early-to-mid sixties. The total sample available for analysis (those who completed all items of interest) was 4,166, of which 54% were men; respondent race is not publicly available.
- Study 2 measures: social class origin (1957) = head-of-household occupational prestige (Duncan SEI) plus head-of-household education, standardized and summed; class destination (1975) = post-1957 educational attainment plus most recent employment prestige (Duncan SEI), standardized and summed, with the 1975 rather than the 1992 class measures chosen to be conservative about common method issues because the mediator and dependent variable come from 1992. Efficacy (1992) and voice (1992) were proxy items drawn from the WLS psychological well-being battery. A separate, non-preregistered item-validation study (239 employed Prolific Academic participants, average age 31, 68% women) found the proxy composites unreliable (α = .41 for efficacy, .54 for voice), so the single most theoretically representative item was used for each construct: efficacy = agreement that "you gave up trying to make big improvements or changes in your life a long time ago" (reverse-scored), voice = agreement that "you are not afraid to voice your opinions, even when they are in opposition to the opinions of most people." Respondent gender was the only control. Analysis: the efficacy measure was regressed on class position in 1957, class position in 1975, and their interaction, following prior work on class trajectories, with conditional indirect effects computed at ±1 SD of class destination. The design is archival and correlational, with origin, destination, and outcomes separated by 18 and 17 years respectively.
- Study 3 (vignette experiment), sample and manipulation: a preregistered two-condition, between-subjects scenario experiment. Anticipating a medium effect size (at least 172 participants for power above .9), the authors recruited 400 individuals through Prolific; 49 failed one or both attention checks and were removed, leaving a final sample of 351. Average participant age was 33 years; 71% had a college degree or higher, 82% were White, and 50% were women. In the "Brain Trust Task" participants played the lead architect choosing two of three fellow architects for their go-to group. The manipulated (always second) resume was either an upwardly mobile target, Jimmy Clark (University of Oklahoma undergraduate degree, MIT master's, interests in country music and fishing, prior work as a landscaper and in an auto parts store), or a stable middle-class target, James Cabot (MIT undergraduate and master's, interests in classical music and sailing, internships at an architecture and a law firm), with years in architecture (10) and years with the firm (4) held constant; the two filler resumes were identical across conditions. Class cues followed Rivera and Tilcsik's (2016) audit-study design and were reinforced with audio clips from the International Dialects of English Archive (a college-educated Oklahoman with a regional accent for Clark, a college-educated, neutrally accented Syracuse voice for Cabot) reading the same text. A non-preregistered pretest with 267 Prolific participants confirmed the class manipulation: Clark was more often perceived as upwardly mobile (62%) than Cabot (32%; χ2 = 73.09, p < .01), while Cabot was more often rated stable (63%) than Clark (31%; χ2 = 75.13, p < .01).
- Study 3 measures: perceived cultural fit = four items (α = .93) from Rivera and Tilcsik (2016), 1 (not at all well) to 5 (extremely well); perceived competence = six items (α = .93) from Fiske and colleagues' (2002) measure (competent, capable, confident, efficient, intelligent, skillful), 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); voice solicitation measured two ways, as espoused intent to solicit (three items adapted from Fast et al., 2014, α = .96, 1 to 7) and as the binary behavioural choice of whom to select for the brain trust. Warmth (α = .94) and organizational commitment (α = .89) were also preregistered as alternative mechanisms. Participants reported their race, gender, and current social class position using the same education and occupational-prestige measures as the prior studies. Analysis: t-tests, a binary logistic regression for the selection outcome, path analysis with multiple mediators for each solicitation measure using 5,000 bootstrapped samples, and post hoc conditional process analysis with participant class as a moderator. Because condition was randomly assigned, the authors treat this study as providing causal evidence for the solicitation pathway.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 229), H1 supported: the class-background composite was negatively and significantly related to self-efficacy (B = -.06, SE = .03, p = .04), which the paper reads as indicating that those from lower social class backgrounds report having higher efficacy; in this near-uniform-destination setting a lower-class background indexes greater upward mobility, and Table 2 labels this row "Upward Mobility."
- Study 1, H3 supported: social class background was positively related to managerial solicitation of voice (B = .08, SE = .04, p = .04), indicating that workers from higher social class origins are more likely to report having managers ask them for input. Both mediators were positively related to voice: self-efficacy (B = .17, SE = .08, p = .02) and managerial voice solicitation (B = .29, SE = .05, p < .01).
- Study 1, H2 and H4 supported: the bootstrapped indirect effect of class background on voice via self-efficacy excluded zero (effect = -.01, SE = .01, 95% CI [-.03, -.001]) and the indirect effect via managerial solicitation excluded zero (effect = .02, SE = .01, 95% CI [.002, .05]). The two mediation tests were introduced as those posited in Hypothesis 2 and Hypothesis 4, although the sentence reporting the efficacy path labels its conclusion "Hypothesis 3."
- Study 1, no net effect: the direct effect of class background on voice was not significant and the total effect was also nonsignificant (effect = .03, SE = .04, 95% CI [-.04, .10]), which the authors interpret as the individual and organizational mediations being offsetting in this organization, adding that it is unclear whether this would be the case in other contexts. A model excluding all controls produced a largely similar pattern, with the class-efficacy relationship becoming marginally significant (B = -.05, SE = .03, p = .08). Explained variance was modest (R2 = .02 for efficacy, .02 for solicitation, .18 for voice).
- Study 2 (n = 4,166) tested only H1 and H2, and both were supported. The class origin (1957) × class destination (1975) interaction on efficacy was significant and negative (B = -.01, SE = .01, p = .02), indicating a weakening relationship between class origin and efficacy at higher levels of class destination, and the plotted form of the interaction indicates that upward class transitions are associated with high levels of efficacy. Class position in 1975 was positively related to efficacy (B = .06, SE = .01) while class origin in 1957 was only marginal (B = .02, SE = .01, p < .10), which the authors read as efficacy being more a function of where one ends up than where one begins.
- Study 2, mediation: efficacy was positively related to voice behaviours (B = .11, SE = .02, p < .01), and the indirect effect of class origin on voice via efficacy was positive with a confidence interval excluding zero when class destination was low (effect = .004, 95% CI [.001, .01]) but negative with a confidence interval including zero when class destination was high (effect = .000, 95% CI [-.003, .002]); the total effect was significant (effect = .04, SE = .01, 95% CI [.02, .06]). The authors describe the effect sizes of this study as modest (R2 = .01 for efficacy, .02 for voice).
- Study 3 (n = 351) tested H3 and its mechanisms, and supported H3 on both operationalizations: espoused intention to solicit voice was higher for the stable high-class target Cabot (M = 5.70, SD = 1.15) than for the upwardly mobile Clark (M = 5.07, SD = 1.50; t = 4.41, p < .01), and Cabot was selected for the brain trust more often (61%) than Clark (50%; Wald χ2(1) = 3.90, p = .05).
- Study 3 mechanisms: Cabot was seen as more competent (M = 5.94, SD = .85) than Clark (M = 5.44, SD = 1.20; t = 4.51, p < .01) and as a better cultural fit (M = 3.85, SD = .86) than Clark (M = 3.39, SD = 1.00; t = 4.67, p < .01). Condition predicted cultural fit (B = .45, SE = .10, p < .01) and perceived competence (B = .49, SE = .11, p < .01). Mediation depended on how solicitation was measured: for the intent scale both cultural fit (effect = .33, SE = .08, 95% CI [.19, .51]) and competence (effect = .27, SE = .07, 95% CI [.15, .42]) were significant indirect paths and the total effect was significant (effect = .62, SE = .15, 95% CI [.33, .89]); for actual brain-trust selection only cultural fit mediated (effect = .10, SE = .03, 95% CI [.05, .17]), the competence path was not significant (95% CI [-.004, .07]), and the total effect was not significant (effect = .10, SE = .05, 95% CI [-.01, .20]). Perceived cultural fit was the mechanism most consistently predictive of who was chosen.
- Study 3 post hoc analyses: the difference was driven by the upwardly mobile target being solicited less rather than the stable target being solicited more. Participants in the stable high-class condition were significantly more likely to pick Cabot than not to pick him (χ2(1) = 7.82, p < .01), whereas in the upwardly mobile condition they were equally likely to pick Clark as not to (χ2(1) = .00, ns), and Clark was chosen less often (26.3%) than either filler target (36.5% and 37.1%). Evaluator class mattered for competence: there was a significant positive interaction between respondents' own class and condition on perceived competence (B = .15, SE = .06, p = .02) but not on cultural fit (B = .09, SE = .06, ns), and the conditional indirect effect of condition on solicitation intent via competence was significant when respondents' class was high (effect = .42, SE = .11, 95% CI [.23, .65]) but not when it was low (effect = .13, SE = .08, 95% CI [-.03, .30]); no significant indirect effects via competence emerged when solicitation was operationalized as brain-trust selection.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The work changes how management theory accounts for class background: rather than imprinting an enduring low sense of initiative, the self-views of the upwardly mobile better reflect their current class position, evidencing high self-efficacy. It extends voice research by shifting attention from how managers respond to or recognize voice toward how they actively *solicit* it, showing that managers use socially significant cues like class signals when deciding whose input to seek — a previously unexamined source of inequity. It also demonstrates that the individual and organizational antecedents of voice, rarely studied jointly, can compete rather than complement one another.

**Practical Implication**
Firms should treat evidence of an upward trajectory as a positive signal of efficacy and other desirable qualities when screening employees or composing teams. Managers should consciously counteract the tendency to rely on elite cultural signals as proxies for quality and make a deliberate effort to solicit input from people who reached the same destination from less privileged origins, helping rebalance disparities in the opportunity to contribute.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 used correlational designs and cannot establish causal habitus or efficacy change; efficacy spirals leave open whether high-efficacy lower-class individuals are simply the ones who become upwardly mobile. Voice in Studies 1-2 was self-reported, and Study 3's vignette experiment, while causally informative, sacrifices ecological validity. The Study 1 offsetting competing mediations (yielding a nonsignificant total effect) occurred within a single prestigious organization and may not generalize across contexts.

**Future Research**
Future work could disentangle the causal direction between upward mobility and efficacy via performance/efficacy spirals, examine downward mobility, and test whether the competing-pathways dynamic extends to diversity dimensions beyond social class. Researchers could also study what topics people from different class backgrounds speak up about, use more direct (non-self-report) voice measures, and assess managerial solicitation in more ecologically valid settings and in contexts beyond the workplace.
