# When Conscientious Employees Meet Intelligent Machines: An Integrative Approach Inspired by Complementarity Theory and Role Theory

**Abstract**
Over the past century, conscientiousness has become seen as the preeminent trait for predicting performance. This consensus is due in part to these employees’ ability to work with traditional 20th-century technology. Such pairings balance the systematic nature of conscientious employees with the technology’s need for user input and direction to perform tasks—resulting in a complementary match. However, the 21st century has seen the incorporation of intelligent machines (e.g., artificial intelligence, robots, and algorithms) into employees’ jobs. Unlike traditional technology, these new machines are equipped with the capability to make decisions autonomously. Thus, their nature overlaps with the orderliness subdimension of conscientious employees—resulting in a non-complementary mismatch. This calls into question whether the consensus about conscientious employees’ effectiveness with 20th-century technology applies to 21st-century jobs. Integrating complementarity and role theory, we refine this consensus. Across three studies using distinct samples (an experience sampling study, a field experiment, and an online experiment from working adults in Malaysia, Taiwan, and the United States), each focused on a different type of intelligent machine, we show not only that using intelligent machines has benefits and consequences, but, importantly, that conscientious (i.e., orderly) employees are less likely to benefit from working with them.

**Research Question**
Does the long-standing consensus that conscientious (specifically, orderly) employees are most effective with workplace technology still hold when employees work with autonomous intelligent machines, and for whom do the benefits versus costs of intelligent machines accrue?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Conscientiousness (orderliness) moderates the positive relationship between the usage of intelligent machines at work and role breadth self-efficacy, such that this positive relationship is stronger at lower levels of conscientiousness (orderliness) and weaker at higher levels.
- H2: Conscientiousness (orderliness) moderates the positive relationship between the usage of intelligent machines at work and role ambiguity, such that this positive relationship is stronger at higher levels of conscientiousness (orderliness) and weaker at lower levels.
- H3: The indirect effect of usage of intelligent machines on task performance via role breadth self-efficacy is moderated by conscientiousness (orderliness), such that the indirect effect is stronger when conscientiousness (orderliness) is lower compared to higher.
- H4: The indirect effect of usage of intelligent machines on task performance via role ambiguity is moderated by conscientiousness (orderliness). The printed hypothesis statement reads "stronger when conscientiousness (orderliness) is lower compared to higher," but all three results sections restate and test the prediction as stronger at higher, compared to lower, levels of conscientiousness.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Usage of intelligent machines at work (daily AI usage in Study 1; robotic-work condition in Study 2; "smart" algorithm condition in Study 3)
- DV(s): Task/service performance (coworker report in Study 1, supervisor report in Study 2, independent-judge report in Study 3)
- Mediators: Role breadth self-efficacy; role ambiguity (role conflict modeled as a controlled alternative mechanism)
- Moderators: Conscientiousness (orderliness subdimension)

Drawing on complementarity theory, the authors argue that because intelligent machines autonomously create order and structure workflows, they form a non-complementary mismatch with orderly employees, who prefer to direct work themselves and are therefore less likely to view these machines as credible. Through a role-theory lens, this mismatch weakens orderly employees' role breadth self-efficacy and heightens their role ambiguity, which in turn shape task performance. The negative indirect effect via role ambiguity was supported across all three studies (stronger at higher orderliness), whereas the role breadth self-efficacy path was supported only in Study 3.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (10-workday experience-sampling field study): an announcement was emailed to 122 analysts at a Malaysian technology company that provides screening and regulatory-compliance monitoring services for governmental authorities; 114 participated and provided 776 day-level observations (68% response rate), receiving three surveys per day (two to the employee, one to a coworker who rated only that employee). Conscientiousness: four adjective items from Saucier (1994) in the sign-up survey (α = .74). Daily artificial intelligence usage: three items adapted from Medcof (1996) in the mid-day survey (average α = .92); shortened scales were validated in a separate Prolific sample of 178 individuals (short-form/long-form rs = .78 to .92), and definitional correspondence (.89) and distinctiveness from 20th-century technology (.60) were assessed in a further separate sample of 130 U.S. Prolific employees. Daily role breadth self-efficacy: three items adapted from Parker (1998) (average α = .81). Daily role ambiguity: six items adapted from Rizzo et al. (1970) (average α = .90). Daily task performance: coworker-rated, four items from Turnley et al. (2003) (average α = .91). Daily role conflict (three Rizzo et al. items, average α = .77) was modeled as an alternative mechanism, with controls for study day, day of week plus its sine and cosine, lagged and prior-day endogenous variables, and tenure with intelligent machines. Multilevel path analysis in Mplus 7.4 with conditional paths evaluated at ±1 SD of the grand-mean-centered moderator and 95% bias-corrected Monte Carlo CIs (20,000 replications); daily usage was measured rather than manipulated, so this study is associational.
- Study 2 (pre-registered field experiment): all 162 of the 168 briefed service employees at a contemporary Taiwanese hotel took part (55.6% female; average age 32.13, SD = 6.82), randomly assigned by service section (clubhouse, reception, concierge, lounge) either to work with intelligent service robots as much as possible or not to use service robots at all when serving customers for three consecutive days, with a hypothesis-blind research assistant per section verifying compliance. Measures were back-translated into Taiwanese and used Study 1's anchors: conscientiousness (orderliness) from the DeYoung et al. (2007) orderliness subscale at baseline (α = .92); role breadth self-efficacy from Parker's (1998) 10-item scale adapted to the service context, measured at baseline and post-manipulation (α = .97 and .99); role ambiguity from the six-item Rizzo et al. (1970) scale (α = .92 and .97); and service performance rated by each employee's immediate supervisor with the three-item Salanova, Agut, and Peiró (2005) scale at baseline and post-manipulation (α = .83 and .97). Role conflict (eight Rizzo et al. items, α = .89 and .94) served as the alternative mechanism, with baseline values of endogenous variables and tenure with intelligent machines as primary controls and industriousness, job autonomy, and job demands retained only in robustness models. The manipulation check (α = .98) was confirmed by ANOVA (robotic M = 4.88, SD = 0.96 vs. control M = 1.58, SD = 1.12; t(160) = 20.12, p < .001, d = 3.16). Path analysis in Mplus used the COMPLEX sandwich estimator because supervisors rated multiple employees.
- Study 3 (pre-registered online experiment): 415 full-time U.S. employees recruited through Prolific completed the study and, after 10 exclusions, the final sample was 405 participants (82.0% Caucasian; 58.3% female; average age 33.33, SD = 6.84) holding job titles such as accountant, computer engineer, financial advisor, pharmacist, and teacher; they were randomly assigned to an algorithm condition (199), in which they worked with what they believed was a "smart" algorithm assistant named "Ai" (conversational animation voiced by the Amazon Polly text-to-voice service), or a control condition (206), in which they worked alone, across a four-round lemonade-stand business-consulting simulation adapted from Ederer and Manso (2013). Conscientiousness (orderliness) was measured with the DeYoung et al. (2007) subscale before the experiment (α = .89); role breadth self-efficacy (α = .99) and role ambiguity (α = .94) used the Study 2 scales at the end of round three. Performance was the round-four business plan written for a new client, evaluated with the consensual assessment technique by 16 independent judges (eight beverage business owners and eight business graduate students) on comprehensiveness, concreteness, likelihood of being profitable, novelty, and feasibility (average α = .86; ICC(A, 8) = .95 for owners and .88 for students; the two groups' ratings correlated r = .62, p < .001, and were averaged). Role conflict (α = .92) was the alternative mechanism, with industriousness (α = .98), job autonomy (α = .88), job demands (α = .91), and tenure with intelligent machines as controls; baseline measures were not collected because participants had no prior knowledge of the scenario. The manipulation check (α = .93) was confirmed by ANOVA (algorithm M = 5.77, SD = 1.22 vs. control M = 3.81, SD = 1.81; t(403) = 12.71, p < .001, d = 1.27), and path analysis in Mplus followed Study 2's mediation and moderated-mediation procedures.

**Key Findings**
- H1 (orderliness attenuates the machine-usage to role breadth self-efficacy link) received partial support. Study 1 failed to support it: daily artificial intelligence usage did raise role breadth self-efficacy (γ = .29, SE = 0.03, p < .01), but conscientiousness did not significantly moderate that relationship (γ = .05, SE = 0.04, p = .20). Study 2 supported it (robotic condition raised role breadth self-efficacy, B = 2.05, SE = 0.24, p < .01; moderation B = -0.66, SE = 0.18, p < .01, with the condition effect stronger at lower orderliness, B = 2.80, than at higher, B = 1.31, both p < .01), as did Study 3 (algorithm condition B = 2.21, SE = 0.16, p < .01; moderation B = -0.50, SE = 0.15, p < .01; effect B = 2.75 at lower vs. B = 1.67 at higher orderliness, both p < .01).
- H2 (orderliness amplifies the machine-usage to role ambiguity link) was supported in all three studies. Machine usage raised role ambiguity in each (Study 1 γ = .14, SE = 0.03; Study 2 B = 0.79, SE = 0.27; Study 3 B = 0.62, SE = 0.12; all p < .01), and orderliness significantly moderated the relationship in each (Study 1 γ = .07, SE = 0.03, p = .04, with simple slopes of .19 at higher vs. .09 at lower conscientiousness; Study 2 B = 0.67, SE = 0.26, p < .01, slopes 1.55, p < .01, at higher vs. 0.03, p = .90, at lower; Study 3 B = 0.80, SE = 0.12, p < .01, slopes 1.47, p < .01, at higher vs. -0.26, p = .147, at lower).
- H3 (moderated indirect effect via role breadth self-efficacy) was supported only in Study 3. In Study 1 role breadth self-efficacy predicted task performance (γ = .40, SE = 0.04, p < .01), but because the first-stage moderation was absent the difference between the conditional indirect effects at higher (.129, 95% CI [.091, .177]) and lower (.103, 95% CI [.067, .146]) conscientiousness was nonsignificant (95% CI [-.014, .071]). In Study 2 role breadth self-efficacy did not predict service performance (B = 0.13, SE = 0.08, p = .12), neither conditional indirect effect was significant (.170, 95% CI [-.051, .478]; .363, 95% CI [-.133, .859]), and their difference was nonsignificant (95% CI [-.224, .025]). In Study 3 role breadth self-efficacy predicted performance (B = 0.06, SE = 0.02, p < .01) and the difference between the conditional indirect effects at higher (.107, 95% CI [.030, .198]) and lower (.176, 95% CI [.045, .306]) orderliness was significant (95% CI [-.069, -.007]).
- H4 (moderated indirect effect via role ambiguity) was supported in all three studies. Role ambiguity was negatively related to performance throughout (Study 1 γ = -.29, SE = 0.04; Study 2 B = -0.34, SE = 0.08; Study 3 B = -0.18, SE = 0.03; all p < .01), and the negative conditional indirect effect of machine usage on performance was significant at higher orderliness and weaker or nonsignificant at lower: Study 1 -.053 (95% CI [-.078, -.033]) vs. -.026 (95% CI [-.049, -.007]), difference 95% CI [-.058, -.002]; Study 2 -.522 (95% CI [-.904, -.193]) vs. a nonsignificant -.011 (95% CI [-.163, .145]), difference 95% CI [-.412, -.094]; Study 3 -.267 (95% CI [-.372, -.161]) vs. a nonsignificant .046 (95% CI [-.012, .115]), difference 95% CI [-.209, -.084].
- The support pattern was therefore partial and heterogeneous rather than uniform across studies: Study 3 supported all four hypotheses, Study 2 supported H1, H2, and H4 but not H3, and Study 1 supported only H2 and H4.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper refines the established consensus on conscientiousness by showing it requires contextualization to the type of technology employed: orderly employees may experience reduced efficacy and heightened ambiguity with intelligent machines. It identifies orderliness as the moderator answering "for whom" intelligent machines help versus hurt, extends complementarity theory beyond human-to-human interactions to human-machine interactions, and returns role theory to the study of how modern technology reshapes role perceptions.

**Practical Implication**
Managers may wish not to emphasize conscientiousness when recruiting for jobs involving intelligent machines, and to pair such machines with employees lower in conscientiousness (orderliness) where possible, since these employees may be better positioned to benefit. The authors also suggest including work-sample tasks pairing candidates with intelligent machines during selection.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 each drew on a single organization in Eastern cultural contexts, limiting generalizability, and Study 3 relied on an online sample. Study 1 used shortened scales and coworker performance ratings, and several hypotheses (the role breadth self-efficacy path) went unsupported in Studies 1 and 2, with the authors noting moderating effects are harder to detect in field settings.

**Future Research**
Future work should identify boundary conditions such as the nature of the job and whether intelligent machines offer high-quality or user-friendly output, develop ways to classify the richness of the user experience with intelligent machines, and examine how the employee-machine working relationship varies across machine types, jobs, and industries, including via a trait-activation lens.
