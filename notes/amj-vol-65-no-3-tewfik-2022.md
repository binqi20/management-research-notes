# The Impostor Phenomenon Revisited: Examining the Relationship between Workplace Impostor Thoughts and Interpersonal Effectiveness at Work

**Abstract**
Prevailing wisdom paints the impostor phenomenon as detrimental. I seek to rebalance the existing conversation around this phenomenon by highlighting that it may also have interpersonal benefits. To identify these benefits, I revisit seminal theorizing to advance the construct of workplace impostor thoughts which I define as the belief that others overestimate one's competence at work. Incorporating theory on contingencies of self-worth, I present an integrative model that outlines why such thoughts may be positively associated with other-perceived interpersonal effectiveness and why they may not be. I test my theory across four studies (n 5 3,603) that feature survey, video, and pre-registered experimental data. I find that employees who more frequently have such thoughts are evaluated as more interpersonally effective because they adopt a more other-focused orientation. I do not find that this interpersonal benefit comes at the expense of competence-related outcomes (i.e., performance, selection)—a point I revisit in my future directions. When examining my theorized competing pathway, I find that, whereas workplace impostor thoughts do encourage those who have them to self-handicap—consistent with prevailing wisdom—such thoughts do not operate through self-handicapping to harm other-perceived interpersonal effectiveness. I conclude by situating my findings alongside prior work.

**Research Question**
Are workplace impostor thoughts — the belief that others overestimate one's competence at work — associated with greater (not just lesser) other-perceived interpersonal effectiveness, and if so, through what mechanism, and does that interpersonal benefit come at the expense of competence-related outcomes?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Having workplace impostor thoughts is positively associated with other-rated interpersonal effectiveness at work (i.e., there is a positive total effect).
- H2: There is a positive indirect effect of workplace impostor thoughts on other-rated interpersonal effectiveness through the adoption of a more other-focused orientation.
- H3a: There is a positive indirect effect of workplace impostor thoughts on the adoption of a more other-focused orientation through self-esteem.
- H3b: There is a positive indirect effect of workplace impostor thoughts on other-rated interpersonal effectiveness at work serially through self-esteem and the adoption of an other-focused orientation.
- H4: There is a negative indirect effect of workplace impostor thoughts on other-rated interpersonal effectiveness at work through self-handicapping.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Workplace impostor thoughts (measured via a validated five-item self-report scale; manipulated via recall in the two pre-registered experiments).
- DV(s): Other-perceived interpersonal effectiveness (rated by supervisors, standardized patients, or yoked evaluators with hiring/supervisory experience).
- Mediators: Other-focused orientation (the proposed primary defensive response; measured via video coding and behavioral question-asking counts); self-esteem and other-directed positive affect (serial micro-mechanism upstream of other-focused orientation).
- Moderators: None formally tested; self-handicapping is modeled as a competing (alternative) mediator rather than a moderator, and self-presentational concerns, self-monitoring, self-promotion, and self-denigration are examined as alternative explanations / mechanisms.

Drawing on contingencies-of-self-worth theory, the paper argues that workplace impostor thoughts threaten the self-esteem of people who stake their self-worth on competence, prompting a "domain-switching" response: rather than turning inward, they shift effort toward the interpersonal domain by adopting a more other-focused orientation (attending to, asking questions of, and showing care for others). Because perceivers reward these verbal, paraverbal, and nonverbal behaviors, the other-focused orientation translates into higher other-rated interpersonal effectiveness. A competing self-handicapping pathway is theorized but is not found to harm interpersonal effectiveness. The positive indirect effect through other-focused orientation held in all three studies that measured the mediator (Studies 2, 3A, and 3B; Study 1 did not measure other-focused orientation), even though a significant total effect appeared only in the two field studies.

**Data & Measures**
Because existing impostor phenomenon scales do not capture the sociocognitive conceptualization, the author first developed and validated a five-item self-report workplace impostor thoughts measure across seven lab and field samples (n = 1,059), following Hinkin (1998) and DeVellis (1991); respondents answered on a 6-point frequency scale (1 = never to 6 = very frequently).

- Study 1 (155 employees remaining after listwise deletion at an investment advisory firm in the mid-Atlantic United States; two waves about two months apart): IV = the five-item workplace impostor thoughts scale, self-reported at Time 1 (α = .93); DV = supervisor-rated interpersonal effectiveness at Time 2, three items from Van Scotter, Motowidlo, and Cross (2000) (α = .74); controls = self-presentational concerns (four items, Heatherton & Polivy, 1991; α = .88), the organization's own baseline performance rating ("1," "2," or "3"), race, gender, and role tenure; supplementary competence outcome = supervisor-rated subjective performance on a 10-point scale (four items, Pearce & Porter, 1986; α = .93). Other-focused orientation was not measured in this study. Estimated with hierarchical linear modeling (maximum likelihood) because employees were nested under different supervisors.
- Study 2 (70 physicians-in-training at a medical school in the mid-Atlantic United States, each interacting with one of 13 standardized-patient actors across two scripted cases; only Case 1 interactions recorded successfully, giving 45 coded videos, 64.3% of the sample): IV = the same five-item scale with "at work" reworded to "in medical school," collected about one week before the encounter (α = .91); DV = standardized-patient ratings on the medical school's interpersonal skills inventory (eliciting information, listening, expressing empathy) on a 4-point scale from 1 (poor) to 4 (very good) (α = .60); mediator = other-focused orientation coded from the 45 videos by two research assistants blind to hypotheses using verbal, nonverbal, and paraverbal indicators (e.g., follow-ups to patient statements, eye gaze, nodding, receptive tone), rated every minute from 1 (very slight or not at all) to 5 (very much) (α = .87); controls = self-monitoring (eight items, Lennox & Wolfe, 1984; α = .81), race, and gender; supplementary competence outcome = objective performance, whether the correct diagnosis was reached (1 = correct, 0 = incorrect). Estimated with HLM (maximum likelihood) for the total effect and multilevel structural equation modeling with a Bayes estimator for the mediation test; logistic regression for diagnosis accuracy.
- Study 3A (pre-registered yoked-design online experiment; 308 U.S. full-time employees recruited via Prolific, 45 excluded for failed attention checks leaving a final sample of 264, each yoked to one of 264 second-sample participants with hiring experience, 528 in total): workplace impostor thoughts manipulated by recall — recall a work incident in which others overestimated one's talent or abilities versus a control recall in which others thought one's abilities matched one's own view — followed by an imagined coffee chat before a promotion interview; mediator = other-focused orientation measured behaviorally as the count of ask-rather-than-answer choices across 10 question pairs (range 1–8); DV = the yoked evaluator's interpersonal effectiveness rating, eight items from Bartram (2005) on a scale from 1 (not at all likely) to 6 (extremely likely) (α = .94); manipulation check used four scale items (α = .85).
- Study 3B (second pre-registered yoked-design experiment; 701 U.S. full-time employees via Prolific, 104 excluded for failed pre-registered attention checks leaving a final sample of 597, plus 597 yoked evaluators with supervisory experience and a third set of 597 rendering selection judgments, 1,791 participants in total): the same recall manipulation against a lunch-recall active control; state self-esteem = four reverse-scored items (Heatherton & Polivy, 1991; α = .91); other-focused orientation = count of questions chosen to ask out of eight (range 0–8); self-handicapping, self-promotion, and self-denigration = behavioral counts of the specific questions chosen to answer, each out of two (range 0–2), with the construct assignment of the questions validated by two blind organizational behavior scholars and 10 naive coders; DV = the yoked evaluator's interpersonal effectiveness rating, six items from Bartram (2005) on the same 1–6 scale (α = .94); further alternative micro-mechanisms = belonging, other-directed positive affect, and anxiety; supplementary competence outcome = the third evaluator's yes/no decision on whether to invite the candidate to interview. Hypotheses were tested simultaneously in a structural equation model; logistic regression for the selection decision.

Identification: the two field studies are associational (measured, time-lagged, multisource data with statistical controls for rival explanations), while Studies 3A and 3B randomly assign the recall manipulation, which the paper presents as strengthening causal inference; both experiments were pre-registered and used a yoked design so that each employee's choices were rated by a different, independent evaluator.

**Key Findings**
The support pattern is heterogeneous: the indirect (mediated) pathway replicated wherever the mediator was measured, but the predicted total effect emerged only in the two field studies.

- H1 partially supported. Study 1: workplace impostor thoughts were significantly positively associated with supervisor-rated interpersonal effectiveness (estimate = 0.14, SE = 0.07, p = .048), holding with self-presentational concerns and baseline performance controlled (estimate = 0.18, SE = 0.07, p = .009) and with race, gender, and role tenure added (estimate = 0.19, SE = 0.07, p = .006). Study 2: estimate = 0.18, SE = 0.05, p = .001, holding with self-monitoring, gender, and race controlled (estimate = 0.18, SE = 0.06, p = .002), with a significant total effect in the multilevel SEM (estimate = 0.21, SE = 0.12, 95% CI [0.010, 0.460]). Study 3A: not supported (impostor-thoughts condition M = 4.00, SD = 0.90 vs. control M = 3.93, SD = 0.90; t(262) = 0.64, p = .53, d = 0.08). Study 3B: not supported (M = 4.03, SD = 0.95 vs. M = 3.95, SD = 0.90; t(595) = 1.08, p = .28, d = 0.09; total effect = 0.08, SE = 0.08, p = .28, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.23]).
- H2 supported in all three studies that measured the mediator. Study 2: indirect effect = 0.17, SE = 0.09, 95% CI [0.036, 0.374] (impostor thoughts to other-focused orientation, estimate = 0.23, SE = 0.06, p < .001; other-focused orientation to interpersonal effectiveness, estimate = 0.73, SE = 0.32, p = .004). Study 3A: indirect effect = 0.13, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.04, 0.22], with the condition raising other-focused orientation (M = 5.12, SD = 2.49 vs. M = 4.35, SD = 2.04; t(262) = 2.74, p = .007, d = 0.34). Study 3B: indirect effect = 0.05, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.01, 0.11], again with higher other-focused orientation in the impostor-thoughts condition (M = 4.64, SD = 1.26 vs. M = 4.39, SD = 1.05; t(595) = 2.62, p = .009, d = 0.21) and a positive path to interpersonal effectiveness (estimate = 0.19, SE = 0.03, p < .001). H2 was not tested in Study 1, which did not measure other-focused orientation.
- H3a not supported (Study 3B). The manipulation sharply lowered state self-esteem (M = 3.76, SD = 1.35 vs. control M = 5.15, SD = 1.30; p < .001, d = -1.05), but self-esteem was not significantly related to other-focused orientation (estimate = 0.03, SE = 0.04, p = .43), so the indirect effect was null (indirect effect = -0.04, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [-0.14, 0.06]).
- H3b not supported (Study 3B): serial indirect effect through self-esteem and other-focused orientation = -0.01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.03, 0.01].
- H4 not supported (Study 3B). Workplace impostor thoughts raised self-handicapping only at p < .10 (M = 0.36, SD = 0.58 vs. control M = 0.28, SD = 0.52; t(595) = 1.84, p = .067, d = 0.15) — directionally consistent with prevailing wisdom — but self-handicapping was unrelated to interpersonal effectiveness in the full model (estimate = 0.00, SE = 0.08, p = .99), so the hypothesized negative indirect effect was null (indirect effect = 0.00, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [-0.02, 0.02]).
- Supplementary mechanism analyses (Study 3B): a serial path from workplace impostor thoughts through lower self-esteem to other-directed positive affect did predict other-focused orientation (indirect effect = 0.04, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.004, 0.07]) and extended to interpersonal effectiveness (indirect effect = 0.01, SE = 0.003, 95% CI [0.001, 0.02]); self-promotion emerged as a noncompeting alternative mechanism only at 90% confidence (indirect effect = 0.01, SE = 0.01, 90% CI [0.002, 0.04]); no evidence was found for self-denigration. Across the eight additional supplementary models, support for the indirect effect through other-focused orientation persisted.
- Competence-related spillover analyses found no significant harm: subjective performance in Study 1 (estimate = 0.18, SE = 0.14, p = .193, with a positive indirect effect through interpersonal effectiveness of 0.26, SE = 0.13, 95% CI [0.015, 0.509]), diagnosis accuracy in Study 2 (estimate = -0.46, SE = 0.30, p = .129; indirect effect through other-focused orientation = 0.13, SE = 0.22, 95% CI [-0.268, 0.613]), and interview selection in Study 3B (estimate = 0.29, SE = 0.23, p = .207; indirect effect = 0.04, SE = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.028, 0.125]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconceptualizes the impostor phenomenon — typically treated as an affective, uniformly harmful experience — as a sociocognitive construct ("workplace impostor thoughts"), recovering a "forgotten" conceptualization and supplying a validated measure. It identifies other-focused orientation as the previously missing mechanism that explains why such thoughts can have an interpersonal upside, integrating contingencies-of-self-worth theory with impostor-phenomenon theory to show that threatened self-esteem can prompt people to turn outward rather than inward. In doing so it resolves a latent tension in prior literature between assertions that the phenomenon damages relationships and passing hints that impostors are seen as socially adept and likable.

**Practical Implication**
The findings caution against blanket practitioner advice to "overcome" or "get rid of" impostor feelings: because the same thoughts can foster an other-focused orientation that others reward, guidance should be more nuanced and attend to the workplace context. Interventions such as cognitive reappraisal may help individuals down-regulate the negative emotional undertones while preserving potential interpersonal benefits, but managers should weigh the phenomenon's net effect — especially the degree to which a role is socially embedded — before deciding whether "overcoming" advice is appropriate.

**Limitations**
The supplementary competence-related null effects (no significant harm to performance or selection) should not be overgeneralized, and the pre-interview chat may be a particularly strong competence-forward situation that made it harder to find significant differences in ultimate interpersonal evaluations (no significant total effect in Studies 3A and 3B). Self-esteem did not significantly mediate the path to other-focused orientation in Study 3B (only a serial path through other-directed positive affect did).

**Future Research**
Future work could map boundary conditions on the interpersonal upside — for example, whether power attenuates it (greater power increases self-focus) and whether the degree of a job's social embeddedness amplifies or constrains domain-switching. Scholars could examine gender as a moderator of effects rather than as an antecedent, search for additional alternative or competing mechanisms beyond those measured (self-handicapping, self-denigration, self-promotion), and consider the phenomenon's simultaneous "net" effects across interpersonal, competence-related, and well-being outcomes.
