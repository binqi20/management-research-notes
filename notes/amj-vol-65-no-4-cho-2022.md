# How Work Orientation Impacts Objective Career Outcomes via Managerial (Mis)perceptions

**Abstract**
Views differ on whether individuals with a calling orientation toward work (i.e., seeing work as personally fulfilling and contributing to a better world) enjoy more favorable objective career outcomes, such as higher income and chance of promotion, versus those with a job orientation (i.e., seeing work as a means to a financial end). We suggest that the impasse is partially due to prior research's exclusive focus on how work orientation affects one's effort and subsequent job performance. Drawing on theories of signaling, cognitive biases, and reciprocity, we propose that calling-oriented employees enjoy better objective career outcomes than job-oriented employees via an external pathway: managers misperceive employees' calling orientation as evidence of better performance and stronger organizational commitment. In Study 1—analyses of the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study—we find support for the main effect, and in Study 2—an online experiment—we constructively replicate this effect and find evidence for our predicted explanatory mechanisms. Furthermore, observing a calling-oriented employee prompts managers to perceive them more favorably in other domains, creating a halo effect. Our research sheds light on how individuals' subjective view of the meaning of work influences their objective career success, highlighting workplace signals and managerial perceptions as important mechanisms.

**Research Question**
How does an employee's work orientation (calling vs. job) relate to their objective career outcomes (income, raises, bonuses, promotion), and does this relationship operate through an external pathway in which managers (mis)perceive calling-oriented employees as higher performing and more organizationally committed?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1: Calling-oriented employees (a) earn a higher level of income and (b) receive stronger support for promotion than job-oriented employees.
Hypothesis 2: The positive relationships between calling orientation and income and between calling orientation and promotion are mediated by managers' (mis)perception that calling-oriented employees are better performers and are more committed to their organization than job-oriented employees.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Employee work orientation (calling vs. job orientation; control condition added in Study 2)
- DV(s): Objective career outcomes — total wages/income (Study 1); bonus, raise percentage, and promotion support (Study 2)
- Mediators: Managers' perceived employee job performance; managers' perceived organizational commitment
- Moderators: None formally tested (paper instead specifies boundary conditions, e.g., contexts dominated by one orientation, purely objective-metric reward systems, expert vs. nonexpert evaluators)

Because calling- and job-oriented employees emit different observable workplace signals, managers draw inferences (per signaling theory) and, through common cognitive biases (e.g., availability bias, fallacy of association), misperceive calling-oriented employees as better performers and more committed to the organization—even when objective performance and commitment are held constant. Per the theory of reciprocity, managers then repay these favorable perceptions with greater financial rewards and stronger promotion endorsement. Study 1 shows calling-oriented WLS respondents earn higher wages; Study 2 experimentally demonstrates the perceptual mediation and a halo effect (calling-oriented "Sam" is also seen as more passionate, intrinsically motivated, and positive despite expert-validated objective equivalence).

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (archival, Wisconsin Longitudinal Study): the WLS is a long-term data collection effort tracking a random sample of Wisconsin high school graduates in 1957. Of the entire WLS population, 3,155 responded to the 2004 survey question about work orientation; restricting to respondents who answered that question and had complete control-variable data left 1,077 observations for the main model (378, or 35%, job oriented; 171, or 16%, career oriented; 528, or 49%, calling oriented). IV: work orientation, measured in the 2004 survey module with the Wrzesniewski et al. (1997) questionnaire — respondents read three short descriptions of people with a job, career, or calling orientation (the labels themselves were not shown) and chose the one closest to their own view; the three categories were dummy coded with job orientation as the reference. DV: total wages, the natural logarithm of self-reported yearly earnings from the item "In the last 12 months, how much did you receive in wages, salaries, commissions, and tips?" for 2004, and the same item in the 2011 wave for the lagged specification. Controls: hours worked per week (2004 and 2011 waves), gender (1 if male), years of education, parents' socioeconomic status score, the Nakao-Treas occupational prestige score, industry (13 categories), and logged 1992 total wages as a prior-earnings control. Robustness: coarsened exact matching (Iacus, King, & Porro, 2012) on gender, parental SES quartiles, education (three buckets), the Nakao-Treas score (nine buckets), 1992 wages, and industry, yielding matches for 148 job-oriented and 149 calling-oriented individuals (CEM sample size 297); a Heckman sample-selection correction with the inverse Mills ratio for attrition into the 2011 wave; additional controls for number of employers, job satisfaction, and financial satisfaction; and coefficient stability analyses. The design is associational: work orientation was measured only in 2004, and the authors state they were unable to fully rule out the possibility of a reverse causal effect.
- Study 2 (preregistered between-subjects online experiment): the manipulations were built from an inductive prestudy of 51 Prolific participants (61% male, 84% White, 10.29 years of work experience on average) who described the observable behaviors of calling- versus job-oriented colleagues, which surfaced three behavioral indicators; a literature cross-check added a fourth. Scripts for job, calling, and control dialogues between the focal subordinate "Sam" and coworker "Taylor" were written by a professional scriptwriter, filmed with experienced actors as recorded Zoom meetings, validated by five domain experts who publish on the meaning of work or callings, and pretested on nonoverlapping Amazon Mechanical Turk and Prolific samples. For the main experiment the study, sample size, exclusion criteria, and analysis plan were preregistered; a power analysis targeting a small-to-medium effect (partial eta-squared = .03) with 1 - beta > .80 set a recruitment target of 375 Prolific participants screened as full-time employees whose work requires regular interaction with other employees. Three participants failed the attention check, yielding a final sample of 372 (average 12.40 years of work experience, SD = 7.98; 62% men; 76% White; 94% with at least some college education). Participants took the role of manager "Alex" and were randomly assigned to the job, calling, or control video condition. Measures used a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) unless noted: perceived job performance was a single rating of Sam's job performance from 1 (far below average) to 7 (far above average); perceived organizational commitment combined two items from Galperin et al. (2020) with Allen and Meyer's (1990) five-question scale (alpha = .96); perceived passion adapted the six-item perceived entrepreneurial passion scale of Li et al. (2017) (alpha = .94); perceived intrinsic motivation used three statements adapted from Grant (2008) (alpha = .90); perceived work meaningfulness combined three Bunderson and Thompson (2009) items with three from the Work as Meaning Inventory (alpha = .97); perceived positive valence rated Sam as "Positive," "Pleasant," or "Enjoyable" from 1 (very slightly or not at all) to 5 (extremely) (alpha = .92). Outcomes: bonus was the dollar amount allocated to Sam from a limited $1,000 pool shared with Taylor; raise was the salary increase percentage given to Sam within a 0 to 5% range; promotion was 7-point agreement that Sam should be promoted. Each reward also had an alternative "increment" measure (the difference between what was given to Sam and to Taylor). The manipulation check used the Wrzesniewski et al. (1997) calling orientation scale, seven statements rated from 1 (not at all) to 4 (a lot) (alpha = .87). Analysis used ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests and the PROCESS macro (Model 4; Hayes, 2017) with 5,000 bootstrap samples and 95% bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals, coding condition as two dummies with the job condition as the reference group. Random assignment gives Study 2 experimental identification of the effect of the displayed work-orientation signal.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 tested only Hypothesis 1(a) (income); the promotion half of Hypothesis 1(b) and all of Hypothesis 2 were tested in Study 2. Hypothesis 1(a) was supported: after controlling for demographic, socioeconomic, and employment-related characteristics, the paper found a significant positive association between calling orientation (relative to the job-orientation reference category) and logged total wages in 2004 (b = .18, p = .01; Model 2, 1,077 observations). Career orientation was not significantly different from job orientation in that model (b = .00).
- Study 1 robustness ran in the same positive direction. Lagging the dependent variable to the 2011 wave still showed a significant positive association for calling orientation (b = .33, p = .03; Model 8), as did the Heckman selection-corrected estimate (b = .33, p = .03). In the coarsened-exact-matching sample (n = 297) the estimated coefficient for calling orientation was positive and significant. The positive effect also remained significant for both 2004 and 2011 after adding controls for number of employers, job satisfaction, and financial satisfaction. Reverse causality was not supported: 1992 wages did not predict whether respondents later reported a job or a calling orientation (Models 4 and 5).
- Study 1 exploratory contrasts (footnote 7) indicated a calling-orientation premium over both other categories in 2004: with career orientation as the reference, calling orientation was positively associated with 2004 total wages (b = .17, p < .05) while job orientation was not (b = -.00, p = .98); with calling orientation as the reference, both job orientation (b = -.18, p = .01) and career orientation (b = -.17, p < .05) were significantly negative. In the lagged 2011 model, however, the premium for calling over career orientation was not significant (b = -.20, p = .24), while job orientation was associated with significantly lower 2011 wages than both career orientation (b = -.53, p = .01) and calling orientation (b = -.33, p = .03) — which the authors read as evidence of a possible job-orientation penalty.
- Study 2 (n = 372) supported Hypothesis 1 on all three rewards. The manipulation check worked, F(2, 369) = 216.79, p < .001. Bonus differed by condition, F(2, 369) = 27.26, p < .001, with the calling condition allocating more to Sam (M = 675.00, SD = 128.19) than the job condition (M = 629.98, SD = 134.10, p = .02) and the control condition (M = 555.50, SD = 123.36, p < .001). Raise differed, F(2, 369) = 28.46, p < .001 (calling M = 4.36, SD = .85 vs. job M = 3.95, SD = 1.11, p < .001). Promotion support differed, F(2, 369) = 12.24, p < .001 (calling M = 6.12, SD = 1.32 vs. job M = 5.74, SD = 1.29, p = .05). Job-oriented Sam was also rewarded more than control Sam on all three outcomes.
- Study 2 supported Hypothesis 2, though the organizational-commitment path was not uniform across outcomes. In the theorized two-mediator model (calling vs. job), the indirect effect on Sam's bonus was significant via both perceived organizational commitment (b = 28.51, SE = 8.87, 95% CI [11.77, 46.12]) and perceived performance (b = 25.80, SE = 6.90, 95% CI [13.44, 40.39]), and both paths also excluded zero for promotion support (commitment b = .19, SE = .07, 95% CI [.05, .34]; performance b = .25, SE = .08, 95% CI [.11, .42]). For the raise given to Sam, the perceived-organizational-commitment path contained zero (b = .03, SE = .08, 95% CI [-.12, .18]) while the perceived-performance path did not (b = .26, SE = .06, 95% CI [.16, .39]); when the outcome was instead the raise increment given to Sam versus Taylor, both paths excluded zero (commitment b = .25, 95% CI [.08, .41]; performance b = .25, 95% CI [.14, .39]). The paper concludes that Study 2 supported Hypotheses 1 and 2.
- Study 2 exploratory analysis adding perceived passion, intrinsic motivation, work meaningfulness, and positive valence as parallel mediators showed that perceived performance remained a significant mediator in all cases, whereas perceived organizational commitment was significant only for bonus (b = 26.72, SE = 13.37, 95% CI [.71, 53.37]) and no longer significant for raise or promotion. The four additional constructs were nonsignificant mediators, with one exception: perceived positive valence for the raise outcome in the calling versus job comparison (b = .07, SE = .05, 95% CI [.00, .18]).
- Study 2 also documented a halo effect. Five experts rated the calling-oriented and job-oriented Sam as not significantly different on passion for work (p = .06), intrinsic motivation (p = .21), positive valence (p = 1.00), organizational commitment (p = .24), or job performance (p = 1.00), while rating the calling-oriented Sam higher on calling orientation (M = 3.45, SD = .43 vs. M = 2.00, SD = .34; p = .01) and on work meaningfulness (p = .01). Nonexpert participants nonetheless perceived the calling-oriented Sam as more passionate, more intrinsically motivated, viewing work as more meaningful, more positive, better performing, and more committed to the organization; the manipulation's effect on these perceptions was fully mediated by the change in perceived calling orientation (partially mediated for passion).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper redirects the meaning-of-work and work-orientation literatures away from their exclusive focus on the internal pathway (how orientation shapes one's own effort and performance) by theorizing and demonstrating an external, organizational pathway operating through managers' misperceptions of performance and commitment. It also conceptually and empirically differentiates calling orientation from passion, and uncovers a novel way passion itself may be (mis)perceived when physical manifestations of passion are held constant.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should recognize that their judgments of subordinates rest partly on employees' verbal expressions and visible behaviors and may be inconsistent with employees' actual psychological and behavioral states, yet still drive consequential outcomes like income and promotion. Job-oriented employees, even when objectively comparable in performance and commitment, may earn less and receive weaker promotion support than calling-oriented peers.

**Limitations**
Both studies have empirical limits: Study 1's WLS categorical work-orientation measure cannot capture intensity, and the data lack precise measures of job performance or organizational commitment, while work orientation was measured only in 2004 (limiting causal claims about reverse causality). Study 2's manipulation bundled four behavioral indicators at once (precluding isolation of any single indicator), may not have fully held passion constant, relied on short one-off video vignettes rather than repeated real-life interactions, and used a control condition that may have under-supplied performance/passion cues.

**Future Research**
Future work should collect richer data on performance and commitment, isolate which behavioral signals drive perceptions, and disentangle a possible "calling orientation premium" from a "job orientation penalty." Scholars are encouraged to study why the bias-to-reward asymmetry arises, why organizational commitment mediates bonuses but not raises/promotions, the antecedents/consequences of interpersonal congruence of passion, the neglected career orientation, and whether rising income reshapes work orientation over time (including crowding-out effects).
