# Who Will Stay When Crisis Strikes? Venture Capitalist Threat-Defiant Learning Response to Ventures in Crisis

**Abstract**
Firms often depend on relationships with other firms to access important resources. When a crisis strikes a firm, the continuation of those relationships can be jeopardized, as many indirectly affected firms will leave the relationship. This abandonment can be particularly harmful if the crisis-struck firm is dependent on the relationship for valuable resources. However, some firms choose to continue their relationship with the crisis-struck firm. We consider what makes those firms resist the temptation to respond rigidly and abandon the crisis-struck firm. We contend that firms that defy such indirect threats do so as a result of threat-defiant learning, which stems from confidence gained from their prior relational experiences. We test this concept in the context of venture capital firms' (VCs) investments after venture crises in the form of venture involvement in a lawsuit. Studying 435 VCs' decisions on whether to continue to invest in 105 ventures experiencing lawsuits, we find support for threat-defiant learning. This study contributes to the literature on crisis, threat rigidity, and entrepreneurship by showing how firms can resist threat rigidity in interfirm relationships, specifically how VCs learn from experience to defy threats posed by venture lawsuits and make post-lawsuit follow-on investments.

**Research Question**
How does learning from prior experience affect a firm's decision whether to continue an interfirm relationship when the other firm faces a crisis? Empirically: what forms of VC experience make a VC threat defiant and likely to make a follow-on investment in a venture facing the crisis of a lawsuit, rather than responding rigidly and abandoning it?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1 (positive): A VC firm's overall investment experience is positively related to the likelihood of the VC firm making continued investment following the filing of a venture lawsuit.
- Hypothesis 2 (positive): A VC firm's greater investment experience in the focal venture is positively related to the likelihood of the VC firm making continued investment following the filing of a venture lawsuit.
- Hypothesis 3 (positive): A VC firm's own prior lawsuit experience is positively related to the likelihood of the VC firm making continued investment following the filing of a venture lawsuit.
- Hypothesis 4 (negative moderation): A VC firm's prior lawsuit experience as a defendant decreases the positive relationship between the VC's lawsuit experience and the likelihood of the VC firm making continued investment following the filing of a venture lawsuit.
- Hypothesis 5 (positive moderation): A VC firm's prior prejudgment lawsuit settlement experience increases the positive relationship between VC lawsuit experience and the likelihood of the VC firm making a continued investment following the filing of a venture lawsuit.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): VC overall (general) investment experience; VC focal-venture experience (rounds invested in the focal venture); VC lawsuit experience (depreciated count of own prior lawsuits)
- DV(s): VC investment after lawsuit (dichotomous — whether the VC made a follow-on investment in the first round after the venture's lawsuit was filed)
- Mediators: Threat-defiant learning / confidence in own abilities, the focal venture, and crisis-response capacity (theorized, not directly measured)
- Moderators: VC defendant experience (more negative own-crisis experience, weakens the lawsuit-experience effect); VC settlement experience (less negative own-crisis experience, strengthens it)

Crises act as indirect threats that normally provoke threat-rigidity (conservative, standardized responses and relationship abandonment). The authors theorize "threat-defiant learning": through experience with three relational systems — similar interfirm relationships, the focal venture, and the VC's own prior lawsuits — VCs gain confidence in their ability to control and withstand the crisis, enabling them to dismiss, challenge, or attack the threat (Oliver's defiance) and continue investing. The negativity of the VC's own crisis experience is a boundary condition: defendant experience erodes identification and confidence, whereas settlement experience builds it.

**Data & Measures**
Two distinct data collections. The archival VC firms and the seven interviewed venture capitalists are separate populations and are not pooled.

*Quantitative study (archival).* Ventures that received first-round funding between 2006 and 2009 (inclusive) from more than one traditional VC investor were drawn from the SDC VentureXpert Platinum database (n = 2,366). The authors then manually searched the Federal Judicial Center's Integrated Database of civil lawsuits for venture lawsuits during the 2006-2018 period following the first round of funding (n = 329), identifying 181 ventures that had a lawsuit and more than one investor prior to the lawsuit. Lawsuit and investment data were collected for each VC firm's own lawsuits in the five years before the venture lawsuit. Of the 181 ventures, 11 had all of their existing VCs make follow-on investments and 52 had none, leaving 118 ventures with within-venture variation; after accounting for missing data, the analyzed sample is 435 VC investment decisions in 105 ventures. The unit of analysis is a VC's decision whether to invest in a venture following a lawsuit.
- DV, VC investment after lawsuit: dichotomous, coded one if the VC firm invested in the first round following the filing of the first venture lawsuit and zero if it did not.
- IV for H1, VC overall investment experience: number of overall investments the VC firm made in the five years prior to the round following the filing of the venture lawsuit.
- IV for H2, VC focal venture experience: count of the rounds a particular VC firm invested in a particular venture before the round following the filing of the venture lawsuit.
- IV for H3, VC lawsuit experience: the number of the VC firm's own lawsuits in each of the five years preceding the venture lawsuit, depreciated at 15% per year (a rate borrowed from the patent literature) and then summed.
- H4 term, VC defendant experience: contrast coded 0.5 if the VC firm had defendant experience within five years of the filing of the venture lawsuit and -0.5 if it did not.
- H5 term, VC settlement experience: contrast coded 0.5 if the VC firm settled a lawsuit within five years of the filing and -0.5 if it did not.
- Controls, drawn from VentureXpert: VC invest round before lawsuit; VC lead investor round before lawsuit; VC number IPOs (a reputation proxy); VC venture capital under management (natural log plus 1); VC age (natural log plus 1); VC-venture geographic distance (natural log plus 1); VC early-stage preference; VC late-stage preference. Three further controls are built from the Federal Judicial Center lawsuit data rather than VentureXpert: VC IP-contract lawsuit experience, VC-venture lawsuit party match, and VC-venture lawsuit type match. Continuous variables are mean centered and dichotomous variables contrast coded.

Estimation in the paper's own terms: conditional fixed-effects logit regression using the clogit command in Stata to fix the venture and its lawsuit characteristics, so that all the variance explained stems from VC firm-related attributes, with robust standard errors. The H4 and H5 terms, although theorized as moderations, are entered as additive effects of specific subsets of lawsuit-experience types and are reported directly rather than as multiplicative interaction effects. The design is associational: the fixed effects absorb unobserved venture and lawsuit heterogeneity, but the paper claims no experimental or quasi-experimental identification, and learning itself is not measured, only its behavioral effects.

*Qualitative component (post hoc).* Seven open-ended, semi-structured interviews with experienced venture capitalists, selected on two criteria: they had worked in the VC sector during at least a portion of the time period of the quantitative study, and they had at least five years of VC investment experience. Three of the seven had over 20 years of experience as a venture capitalist. They were predominantly partners owning their own small and medium-sized firms with less than $100 million in capital under management, although some had experience with larger firms. Interviews lasted 30 minutes to one hour. None of the interviewed VCs had been sued themselves, though they had experienced other forms of crises and venture lawsuits. The interviews were used to add depth to the theory rather than to code constructs.

**Key Findings**
Quantitative results (Table 2; n = 435 in all nine conditional fixed-effects logit models).
- Hypothesis 1 supported. VC overall investment experience is positively related to post-lawsuit follow-on investment, b = 0.01 (p = .00) in Model 2 and again b = 0.01 (p = .00) in the full Model 9. A one-standard-deviation increase moves the log odds of a follow-on investment from a sample mean of 0.724:1 to 2.713:1, that is, from slightly less than 50% to well over 50% probability. The authors caution against over-interpreting these effect sizes because marginal effects vary greatly in logistic regression.
- Hypothesis 2 supported. VC focal venture experience is positively related to continued investment, b = 0.26 (p = .01) in Model 3 and b = 0.25 (p = .03) in Model 9.
- Hypothesis 3 supported only in the full specification, not uniformly across models. In Model 4, which adds VC lawsuit experience to the nonlawsuit controls on its own, there is no statistical evidence of a relationship (b = -0.19) and H3 is not supported. Model 7 provides what the authors call some limited support (b = 3.60, p = .04), and Model 8 likewise limited support (b = 1.48, p = .05). Only the full Model 9 yields clear support (b = 5.84, p = .02). The authors read this as evidence that the defendant and settlement effects must be teased out before the true VC lawsuit-experience effect becomes visible.
- Hypothesis 4 supported in the full model. VC defendant experience is negatively related to continued investment, b = -4.40 (p = .02) in Model 9. In Model 7, where it enters without the settlement term, the coefficient is negative but does not reach the 0.05 threshold (b = -2.99, p = .07).
- Hypothesis 5 supported in the full model. VC settlement experience is positively related to continued investment, b = 4.66 (p = .00) in Model 9. In Model 8, where it enters without the defendant term, it is positive but above the 0.05 threshold (b = 3.02, p = .09).
- Controls: VC invest round before lawsuit and VC capital under management are positively related to post-lawsuit investment (b = 1.27, p = .00 and b = 0.28, p = .00 in Model 1). Mean VIF was under 2 with no individual variable over 4.
- Robustness: adding the amount invested gives generally consistent full-model results (VC overall investment experience b = 0.01, p = .02; VC focal venture experience b = 0.23, p = .07; VC lawsuit experience b = 5.59, p = .04; VC defendant experience b = -4.60, p = .01; VC settlement experience b = 4.64, p = .01) but reduces the sample to 381 observations, so the full sample is retained for the main results. Seven- and 10-year experience windows reproduce H1 and H2 but yield no statistically significant lawsuit-variable effects. Depreciation rates of 0% and 30% are weaker than the 15% rate used. Counts of defendant and settlement experiences, and controlling for VC plaintiff experience, leave the results unchanged.

Post hoc analyses. Splitting by whether the venture was the plaintiff (133 observations) or the defendant (302 observations), the positive effects of VC overall investment experience, VC focal venture experience, and VC lawsuit experience are generally consistent across both subsamples, and VC defendant experience is negative in both (b = -8.00, p = .04 for plaintiff ventures; b = -8.90, p = .00 for defendant ventures). VC settlement experience diverges: it is positive and significant in the defendant-venture subsample (b = 7.03, p = .00) but negative in the plaintiff-venture subsample (b = -9.61, p = .01), the opposite of the full-sample result; the authors suggest VCs may believe the costs of bringing a lawsuit are not worth the amount the venture will likely obtain in a settlement. Rounds averaged 327 days apart when no lawsuit was present and 666 days apart when one was, consistent with VCs taking longer to decide but not always waiting for resolution. Descriptively, 16% of ventures involved in a lawsuit went public and another firm acquired them in 21% of cases.

Qualitative evidence (the secondary method) converges with the quantitative account on the learning mechanism. VCs described accumulating "scar tissue" from past experience, said that due diligence in screening and evaluating a venture decreases over time and becomes more confirmatory in nature, and one noted that after living through a first venture lawsuit "you realize that anyone can sue anyone for any reason, so [you] don't necessarily panic for any lawsuit." The interviews diverge on the baseline threat: most interviewees still treated venture lawsuits as serious deterrents to investing in follow-on rounds, with one saying that even a lawsuit without merit will "scare off 75% of people from coinvesting."

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops and labels the concept of threat-defiant learning, integrating the threat-rigidity hypothesis with the organizational-learning-from-crisis and relational (post-traumatic growth) literatures to explain how firms resist rigidity in the face of indirect crises affecting a partner firm. It extends learning-from-crisis theory beyond a firm's own future crisis management to firms' responses to external, indirect threats, and contributes to entrepreneurship by showing how distinct forms of VC experience — and the negativity of that experience — shape follow-on investment in ventures in crisis.

**Practical Implication**
VCs should recognize whether their decision to continue or abandon a ventureship in crisis reflects a rigid versus defiant response, since poor management of a relationship in crisis can impair a firm's ability to attract future partners. Entrepreneurs are alerted to investigate VCs' crisis and lawsuit histories and to build deeper relationships with experienced VCs, prioritizing those less likely to abandon the venture when crisis strikes.

**Limitations**
The authors could not assess every aspect of VC experience and lawsuit characteristics and did not model possible interactions between VC experience and venture/lawsuit attributes. Learning was not measured directly; only its behavioral effects were captured. The qualitative post hoc analysis was limited, and the study relied on federal (not state) lawsuit data due to data-availability constraints.

**Future Research**
Future work could analyze additional types of VC experience, specific lawsuit outcomes and amounts at issue, and their interactions; replicate the findings in state courts as recordkeeping improves; use richer qualitative designs to find direct evidence of the theorized learning; study other venture crises beyond lawsuits; and examine the conditions under which a defiance response outperforms a rigid one, including for direct crises and how rigidity/defiance affects ventures' subsequent success.
