# Forming Entrepreneurial Teams: Mixing Business and Friendship to Create Transactive Memory Systems for Enhanced Success

**Abstract**
Successfully navigating critical uncertainties during the incipient stages of new ventures requires these ventures to develop learning systems, and building the right team may be key in this process. Drawing on prior work indicating that entrepreneurial teams form using either an interpersonal-attraction strategy (relationships with similar others in a close network) or a resource-seeking strategy (instrumental focus on complementary skills), we theorize that a dual formation strategy, although challenging to execute, is critical for early performance. Using dual formation strategies from the onset fosters the development of stronger transactive memory systems, because close relationships facilitate smooth coordination among founders specializing in complementary tasks. Transactive memory systems thus mediate the relationship between formation strategies and early entrepreneurial success. Findings from two field observational studies and a field intervention study support our theory: teams formed based on a dual strategy raised greater seed funding on Kickstarter—a leading crowdfunding platform—(Study 1), were more successful in a prestigious entrepreneurial competition (Study 2), and gained more profits from selling their initial products (Study 3). Our research advances knowledge on entrepreneurial team formation and offers practical recommendations to facilitate this process at such nascent, but critical, stages.

**Research Question**
How and why do the initial strategies that founders use to form new venture teams—interpersonal attraction, resource-seeking, or a dual combination of both—shape early entrepreneurial success, and what learning mechanism explains this effect?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Founding teams that utilize a dual formation strategy from the onset reach higher early entrepreneurial success relative to founding teams that engage in either an interpersonal-attraction or a resource-seeking strategy alone (or do not engage in either).
- H2: Founding teams that utilize a dual formation strategy from the onset reach stronger transactive memory systems relative to founding teams that engage in either an interpersonal-attraction or a resource-seeking strategy alone (or do not engage in either).
- H3: Transactive memory systems positively influence early entrepreneurial success.
- H4: Transactive memory systems mediate the effect of formation strategies on early entrepreneurial success.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Initial team formation strategy (dual strategy vs. interpersonal-attraction-alone vs. resource-seeking-alone); in Study 3, fully crossed manipulations of high/low interpersonal attraction and high/low resource-seeking.
- DV(s): Early entrepreneurial success (Study 1: Kickstarter seed funding amount and number of backers; Study 2: entering and surviving in the competition; Study 3: team profits from initial product sales).
- Mediators: Transactive memory systems (the shared structural and process system for encoding, storing, and retrieving "who knows what" on the team).
- Moderators: In Study 3, interpersonal attraction and resource-seeking moderate each other (transactive memory systems strengthen only when both are high).

Forming a team with attention to both relational closeness and complementary expertise from the onset lets founders leverage each strategy's selection criteria in their earliest interactions, so that familiar cofounders who also bring complementary knowledge can quickly establish role structure, trust, and coordination. This accelerates the emergence of stronger transactive memory systems, which in turn enable the team to match members to tasks, integrate diverse expertise, and navigate critical early milestones. Because the dual strategy is valuable, rare, and difficult to imitate (if not adopted at the onset), it provides a source of early competitive advantage, with transactive memory systems mediating the link between formation strategy and success.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (Kickstarter; cross-sectional observational). All technology-category ventures that launched and raised money on Kickstarter between April and December 2014 were scraped with Python's Urllib and bs4 packages. Of 875 initial projects, 175 solo-founder projects were removed; of the remaining 700, the 206 teams (29.43%) that disclosed team-formation information on their campaign form the analysed sample (collectively raising over $25 million, averaging 830.72 backers and a $123.192 K pledge per venture). IV: two blind undergraduate judges coded the disclosed basis of cofounder-relation initiation as interpersonal attraction (friendship, family, romantic relations), resource-seeking (professional, entrepreneurial, educational background), or dual (a combination of more than one type across strategies), Cohen's kappa = .91, entered as two dummies with interpersonal attraction as the omitted referent. DV: campaign funding amount in USD (non-USD amounts converted at 2014 average exchange rates; reported in $1,000 units), with number of backers and funding raised above the team's stated goal as robustness DVs. Controls: campaign duration, U.S.-based, Kickstarter "Staff Pick" promotion, prior Kickstarter experience, prior entrepreneurial experience, and venture-type dummies (technology omitted); team size was reported for only 114 teams and used in a supplementary model. Analysis: one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc tests, OLS regression, and Poisson regression for backer counts. The design is associational, and the authors treat Study 1 as observational rather than causal.
- Study 2 (Israeli technology entrepreneurship competition; time-lagged observational). Sample: 631 entrepreneurs in 242 preseed venture teams enrolled in the 2016 and 2017 competition cycles, after excluding 18 solo-founder ventures. IV: the same three-category formation-strategy coding, applied by two blind undergraduate judges to the teams' application forms (Cohen's kappa = .87), dummy-coded with interpersonal attraction as the referent. Mediator: transactive memory systems reported in the last week of the e-school phase using 12 items from Lewis's (2003) 7-point scale (alpha = .93), aggregated to the team level (median rwg(j) = .95; ICC(1) = .43, ICC(2) = .60); only 37 of 60 e-school teams returned the measure (60.7% response rate), so the mediation analyses were run on those 37 teams without control variables and against liberal p < .10 benchmarks to preserve power. DVs: entry into the competition (dummy for entering the e-school phase) and survival, measured as the number of months a team remained in the competition. Controls: team size, proportion of women, competition cycle, and venture-type dummies (social omitted). Analyses: chi-square test of independence, logistic regression, Mantel-Cox (log-rank) test, Cox proportional-hazard regression, one-way ANOVA with least-significant-difference post hoc tests, OLS, and Monte Carlo parametric-bootstrap mediation with 20,000 replications; entrepreneurial passion (Cardon et al., 2013 items; data available for 96 teams) was tested as an alternative mechanism. The paper states that reliance on observational data limits the causal inferences it can draw from Studies 1 and 2.
- Study 3 (multisection entrepreneurship course, Eastern U.S. university; preregistered field experiment). Sample: 287 participants in 94 teams, drawn from undergraduate (138 students, 44 teams; mean age 21.29, SD 2.36, 29.7% women) and online-MBA (155 students, 50 teams; mean age 32.79, SD 7.09, 45.8% women) entrepreneurship courses, across 19 sections (7 undergraduate, 12 online MBA) taught by four instructors in the fall and spring semesters of 2018-2019; preregistered on AsPredicted (#26932). Design: a fully crossed 2 (interpersonal attraction: high vs. low) x 2 (resource-seeking: high vs. low) between-subject randomized controlled trial with double-blind randomization, in which the first author assembled three-member teams. Interpersonal-attraction manipulation: a network-assessment task based on Burt's (1984) network items, in which students rated closeness to each classmate from 1 = "very distant" to 7 = "very close"; members were paired with peers they had a strong relationship with (high) or whom they did not know (low). Resource-seeking manipulation: two blind coders rated each student's resume on four skill domains (general management, marketing and sales, finance, technical) on a 7-point scale (ICC(1) .78-.97; ICC(2) .88-.98), and members were paired to hold different dominant skills (high) or the same dominant skill (low). Mediator: transactive memory systems measured after three weeks of teamwork with the same 12-item Lewis (2003) scale (alpha = .92), aggregated to the team level (median rwg(j) = .96; ICC(1) = .25, ICC(2) = .49). DV: team profits in USD from selling a minimum viable product over a predefined period (96.8% of teams earned profits, averaging $783.83). Controls: proportion of women, team size, mean age, proportion of members with prior venture-founding experience, and dummies for course type, academic semester, and instructor. Analyses: two-way ANOVA and the PROCESS macro (model 7) with 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals, with hierarchical linear modelling (lme4 in R) as a robustness check; equity shares coded from archived founder-agreement forms (87 of 94 teams) tested power structure as an alternative mechanism.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (tests H1 only; H1 supported). Mean funding differed significantly across formation strategies (F(2,203) = 6.82, p < .01, eta-squared = .26). Tukey post hoc tests showed dual-strategy teams raised more (M = $232.16 K, SD = 383.76) than resource-seeking-alone teams (M = $121.50 K, SD = 217.96; t(162) = 2.49, p < .05) and interpersonal-attraction-alone teams (M = $34.96 K, SD = 33.21; t(76) = 3.70, p < .01); resource-seeking-alone teams outperformed interpersonal-attraction-alone teams only marginally (p = .10). In OLS with the full control set, the dual strategy predicted greater funding relative to interpersonal attraction alone (b = 148.05, SE = 55.72, p < .01), while resource-seeking alone did not. Dual-strategy teams also drew more backers than interpersonal-attraction (Exp(b) = 2.18, p < .001) or resource-seeking (Exp(b) = 1.58, p < .001) teams. As predicted, the dual strategy was the rarest (17.5% of teams; t(205) = 12.26, p < .01).
- Study 2 (tests H1-H4; all four supported). Entry: 69.6% of dual-strategy teams were selected for the competition versus 23.2% of resource-seeking and 19.7% of interpersonal-attraction teams (chi-square(2) = 24.21, p < .001); logistic regression confirmed the dual effect (b = 2.11, p < .01, OR = 8.26) while resource-seeking alone was null (b = .14, p = .74, OR = 1.16). Survival: dual-strategy teams faced a lower hazard of elimination (b = -.67, Wald(1) = 4.65, p < .05; log-rank chi-square(2) = 22.62, p < .001). Both results are in line with H1. H2: dual-strategy teams developed stronger transactive memory systems (M = 6.35, SD = .41) than resource-seeking-alone (M = 5.31, SD = .86; t(17) = 3.09, p < .01) or interpersonal-attraction-alone teams (M = 5.59, SD = .99; t(25) = 2.01, p = .05), with OLS b = .76, SE = .38, p = .05; resource-seeking alone was not associated with stronger systems. H3: stronger transactive memory systems raised the likelihood of passing the semifinals (b = 1.07, p < .05, OR = 2.92). H4: the indirect effect of the dual strategy on passing the semifinals through transactive memory systems was positive at 0.82 (90% CI [.03, 1.93]). These mediation tests rest on 37 teams, no controls, and p < .10 benchmarks. Entrepreneurial passion was unrelated to the dual strategy, to transactive memory systems, and to the outcomes, and its indirect effects were nonsignificant.
- Study 3 (tests H1-H4; H1 NOT supported, H2-H4 supported). Unlike in Studies 1 and 2, the interpersonal-attraction x resource-seeking interaction did not directly predict team profits (F(1,93) = 1.02, p = .32), failing to support H1. H2 was supported: the interaction significantly predicted transactive memory systems (F(1,93) = 5.64, p < .05, eta-squared = .06). Higher interpersonal attraction raised transactive memory systems (M = 5.84, SD = .53 vs. M = 5.24, SD = .79; t(50) = 3.31, p < .01) only when resource-seeking was also high, with no significant difference when resource-seeking was low (t(37) = 0.51, p = .61); symmetrically, higher resource-seeking raised them (M = 5.84, SD = .53 vs. M = 5.27, SD = .65; t(38) = 3.01, p < .01) only when interpersonal attraction was also high (t(52) = 0.63, p = .53 when it was low). H3 was supported: transactive memory systems positively predicted team profits (b = 363.33, SE = 194.28, p = .06 with controls; b = 428.64, SE = 174.90, p < .05 without controls). H4 was supported: transactive memory systems mediated the interactive effect of formation strategies on team profits (b = 243.58, SE = 177.20, 95% CI [18.11, 772.04]), with the conditional indirect effect positive and significant when resource-seeking was high (b = 218.65, SE = 129.87, 95% CI [48.24, 608.59]) but nonsignificant when it was low (b = -24.93, SE = 96.81, 95% CI [-257.79, 149.76]). Variance in equity splits did not carry the effect.
- Support pattern across studies: H1 is supported in Studies 1 and 2 but not in Study 3, where the dual strategy influenced success only indirectly through transactive memory systems; H2, H3, and H4 are supported in Studies 2 and 3, and were not tested in Study 1. The authors attribute the Study 3 divergence to the more constrained variance and lower statistical power created by experimental assignment relative to naturally formed teams.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates the macro entrepreneurship literature on team formation with the micro teams-and-learning literature to identify transactive memory systems as a novel mechanism through which initial formation strategies drive early entrepreneurial success. It extends entrepreneurial team formation research by theorizing and testing the value, rarity, and inimitability of a dual strategy, and contributes to the team-learning literature by reconciling mixed findings on prior familiarity—showing that familiarity aids transactive memory systems only when members also bring different expertise.

**Practical Implication**
Aspiring founders should attend to team assembly early, deliberately overcoming the tendency to search only relationally or only instrumentally, and proactively seek cofounders who offer both close relations and complementary skills. Entrepreneurial education programs, accelerators, and cofounder-matching platforms should teach partner-selection processes alongside business-model training, and investors should prioritize teams that combine close relationships with diverse knowledge.

**Limitations**
In Study 3, the dual strategy affected success only indirectly through transactive memory systems (not directly as in Studies 1 and 2), possibly because experimental assignment constrained variance relative to naturally formed teams. Study 1's sample could only include teams that voluntarily disclosed their formation strategy on their campaign, raising potential selection bias and the possibility that disclosed strategies differ from actually employed ones.

**Future Research**
Future work could disentangle self- versus facilitated selection and separate selection from training effects in entrepreneurial programs; examine other team processes (e.g., shared mental models) and external-stakeholder dynamics alongside transactive memory systems; and test whether dual strategies applied within each member versus across different members differentially accelerate transactive memory system emergence. Researchers might also explore the antecedents of formation strategies and their long-term effects at later growth stages.
