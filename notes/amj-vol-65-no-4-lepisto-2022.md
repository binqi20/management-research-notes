# Ritual Work and the Formation of a Shared Sense of Meaningfulness

**Abstract**
Drawing from a 21-month field study of an athletic footwear and apparel company, I build theory on how a shared sense of meaningfulness forms through interaction ritual and emotion. I find that a shared sense of meaningfulness is composed of a shared feeling and a shared representation that are constructed sequentially. Leaders trigger and propel formation through a set of actions I refer to as ritual work that proliferates emotional energy across the population of members. Members subsequently share their feelings of emotional energy through talk and gesture to develop a shared feeling of meaningfulness. Leaders then articulate words and connect these words to shared feelings to produce a shared representation of meaningfulness. I integrate these findings into a three-step process model. This model contributes to our understanding of the triggers, cultural mechanisms, and role of emotions in the formation of meaningfulness in organizations.

**Research Question**
How does a shared sense of meaningfulness toward an organization's work and activities emerge and form from members' shared experiences, and what actions, actors, and resources trigger and propel that formation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition (Step 1 — Proliferating emotional energy): leaders perform *ritual work* — comprising *material production* (creating space, buying objects, hiring instructors), *mobilizing assembly*, and *inclusion practices* — that creates and scales an *organizational interaction ritual* ("Liven," a communal workout) with barriers to outsiders, shared attention, and shared mood.
- Process / phases (Step 2 — Sharing emotional energy): members display and talk about emotional energy through talk and gesture, growing participation and producing a *shared feeling of meaningfulness*.
- Emergent outcome (Step 3 — Representing shared feeling): leaders articulate significance and connect words to the shared feeling, yielding a *shared representation of meaningfulness*.
- Boundary conditions: physical coassembly is possible; leaders do not initially trigger formation through deliberate top–down communication; the focal activity resonates with broader cultural scripts and is conducive to rhythmic synchronization.

Effective interaction rituals "pump participants up with emotional energy"; ritual work assembles members and propels the flow of bodies through these situations, so that personal emotional energy, once shared, constitutes a collective feeling that leaders subsequently endow with symbolic representation. The model recasts leaders as "gatherers" rather than only rhetoricians, and treats meaningfulness as emerging from felt experience before it is cognitively articulated.

**Data & Measures**
Single inductive field study of one organization — "FitCo" (pseudonym), a global athletic footwear and apparel company with revenues of over $2 billion annually — across 21 months of fieldwork; the paper reports one integrated case corpus, not separate studies. Table 1 summarizes three data sources: semistructured interviews ("Total Semistructured Interviews 5 147" in the table's "= renders as 5" typesetting), archival documents ("Total Documents 5 121"), and participant and nonparticipant observation ("Total Time 5 2–3 days per week for 21 months"; the narrative reports going to FitCo "anywhere between two to four times a week, depending on what I was observing"). The 147 interviews covered the current CEO, the previous two CEOs, senior leaders, members, a highly regarded industry analyst, and a member of FitCo's advertising agency, and excluded the many informal conversations held during observation; Table 2 lists the informant codes and roles reported in the study. Interviews typically lasted one hour, were conducted face-to-face, and were transcribed verbatim. Sampling began purposefully with leaders responsible for the recent fitness initiatives, then expanded through theoretical sampling to other members as themes emerged. The 121 archival documents included iterations of the internal brand book, popular press reports, external videos, annual reports, internal brand videos, a commissioned company history book, and a newly drafted internal document entitled "We Believe." Observation included fitness classes and communal areas, participation in fitness and regular classes (among them an introductory Liven series that met twice a week over a five-week period, plus scheduled Liven classes), an observed presentation for key accounts, a tour led for visitors from an outside company, shadowing, and all-employee events. Constructs were operationalized by coding rather than measurement. Analysis co-occurred with data collection in line with constant comparison: the author memoed and open-coded close to informants' language (in vivo codes such as "hiring instructors," "feeling a buzz," and "contagious conversations"), assembled a date-ordered timeline of events in a single Microsoft Word document, and ran five member checks with senior leaders and longer-tenured members to confirm the sequence of events. Coding then moved "from in vivo codes to theoretical concepts based on existing literature and my emergent findings," producing second-order themes — material production, mobilizing assembly, inclusion practices, organizational interaction ritual, emotional energy, displaying emotional energy, talking about emotional energy, shared feeling of meaningfulness, articulating significance, connecting words, and shared representation of meaningfulness (representative data in Table 3) — which were aggregated into the theoretical categories of proliferating emotional energy, sharing emotional energy, and representing shared feeling. Theorizing became abductive once Collins's interaction ritual chain theory was adopted as a sensitizing lens, with sensegiving/sensemaking used to provide "alternative casting." The design is interpretive and theory-building: a single case study chosen because a how/why question with unfolding events outside the researcher's control suits that design (Yin, 2003) and because meaningfulness was extreme or "transparently observable" at FitCo. No causal identification strategy is claimed and no effects are estimated.

**Key Findings**
The study induces a three-step process model (Figure 1) rather than testing hypotheses. Headline finding: a shared sense of meaningfulness is composed of two elements — a shared feeling of meaningfulness and a shared representation of meaningfulness — and these form sequentially, with the shared feeling preceding the shared representation, through leaders' top–down actions and members' bottom–up interactions. Step 1, proliferating emotional energy: leaders' ritual work occurred in a specific order — material production first (clearing out an old storage building, buying new workout equipment, hiring part-time instructors to launch the communal workout "Liven"), then mobilizing assembly (internal communications promoting participation, senior-leader role modeling, and reversal of the previous CEO's norm discouraging workouts during work hours) and inclusion practices (more class times, "scaling" exercises to any fitness level, and the "check your ego at the door" norm), followed by further material production to accommodate growth. Liven operated as an effective organizational interaction ritual with barriers to outsiders (a converted storage building about a five-minute walk from headquarters, changing into workout attire, walking over in pairs or groups), shared attention (10 to 20 participants, a sequenced warm-up, rising music volume, a "3, 2, 1, Go!" countdown), and shared mood (all-out exertion producing a shared emotion of "suffering" and camaraderie). Formation was not designed: the author found that "forming a shared sense of meaningfulness was not an initial motivation for leaders," informants called it "totally unintentional," and he "did not find that leaders engaged in lofty rhetoric" at this early stage — the paper states as a key empirical finding that leaders' rhetoric and communication are not a necessary condition to trigger formation. Participation grew from "only a small fragment of the population" to 130 regular participants within a month of a strongly encouraged all-employee event, and at its peak "nearly 75% of members participated in Liven" (the discussion states upwards of 75% participated and 25% did not); leaders responded with more ritual work, hiring four new instructors who became full-time employees, buying more equipment, and "investing nearly $500,000 to expand the space from 7,000 square feet to 12,000 square feet." Step 2, sharing emotional energy: members displayed emotional energy through posture and gesture and talked about it in lunchrooms and cubes, which produced two feedback effects — attracting non-participants, who "would just start flocking over" to Liven, and signaling leaders to undertake further ritual work — and converted personal emotional energy into a group-shared emotion the author labels a shared feeling of meaningfulness. That shared feeling ran ahead of members' capacity to express it; members and outsiders alike described it as difficult to articulate, with one saying they "just didn't know how to express it." Step 3, representing shared feeling: leaders closed the gap by articulating significance (an introspective, therapy-like interrogation of why FitCo's fitness activities mattered, yielding "We inspire people to be their absolute best—in mind, body, and community") and connecting words (30 employee testimonials posted at the heavily trafficked elevator bays, later replaced by posters drawn from the "We Believe" document, an all-employee event where the CEO presented that document, a new logo, and the CEO's tattoo of it), producing a shared representation of meaningfulness that members then used to express their shared feeling. Because members had already shared the experience of Liven, leaders' articulations were received as authentic, genuine, and accurate rather than opportunistic; the shared feeling justified the shared representation and gave it emotional force. Directionally, the paper reverses the prior assumption that energy comes from meaningfulness (Dutton & Heaphy, 2003), finding instead that emotional energy, when shared, can generate a shared feeling of meaningfulness. No hypotheses were tested and no effect estimates are reported; all findings are interpretive and grounded in the single FitCo case.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper expands understanding of the *triggers* of meaningfulness by showing formation can be triggered unintentionally through ritual work, in contrast to prior theory emphasizing deliberate top–down rhetoric and visioning. It identifies new *cultural mechanisms* residing at the level of situations — materiality and inclusion practices that assemble members and facilitate their flow through interaction rituals. It advances the *role of emotions*, offering a correction to predominantly cognitive views by showing meaningfulness has an emotional component (a shared feeling) that can precede and justify its cognitive representation.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations seeking to cultivate a shared sense that "what we do" is meaningful can do so by investing in ritual work — providing spaces, objects, and people, and using mobilizing assembly and inclusion practices to draw the maximum number of members into a recurring, activity-centered interaction ritual — rather than relying first on leaders' communication. Starting with ritual (rather than cheap words) may make later claims of purpose feel authentic rather than opportunistic, though it carries financial and opportunity costs.

**Limitations**
As a single deviant case at "FitCo," the theory generalizes to organizational settings with similar features (e.g., leaders who do not initially communicate to trigger formation; members able to physically assemble) and may hold less relevance for distributed or virtual organizations where physical coassembly cannot occur frequently. The author cannot definitively rule out that the emotional energy observed was simply the hormonal effect of intense exercise, though other research finds non-exercise rituals also produce emotional energy.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine whether and how a shared sense of meaningfulness forms in virtual/distributed settings; whether exercise versus other activities is more or less effective at synchronizing members; how other forms of energy (e.g., adrenaline, caffeine) boost or inhibit ritual outcomes; the tradeoffs of starting with communication versus ritual work; and the conditions under which forming meaningfulness through ritual work generates feelings of exclusion or marginalization.
