# The Push and Pull of Attaining CEO Celebrity: A Media Routines Perspective

**Abstract**
Why do some CEOs become celebrities, while others with seemingly equal accomplishments do not? After two decades of research, far more is known about the substantial consequences of CEO celebrity than about its determinants. Drawing on the media routines literature, we develop and test a “push–pull theory” of CEO celebrity attainment, arguing and finding that (a) journalists pull certain CEOs into the limelight, particularly those whose firms are nonconformists within their industries and who themselves are demographically atypical; (b) CEOs, through various self-promotion tactics, can push themselves and their stories into public view; and (c) these push tactics are particularly beneficial for helping atypical CEOs achieve celebrity. We conceptualize CEO celebrity as an ordinal construct with discrete gradations, which we refer to as noncelebrities, B-list celebrities, and A-list celebrities. We test our theory using a longitudinal sample of CEOs, and develop a novel, ordinal measure of CEO celebrity that encompasses a broad array of media (newspaper, broadcast, magazine, and online), allowing us to set forth and test a more nuanced theory about different levels of celebrity attainment.

**Research Question**
Why do some CEOs become celebrities while others with seemingly equal accomplishments do not? Specifically, what "pull" factors (firm and CEO distinctiveness) and "push" tactics (CEO self-promotion) — operating through journalists' media routines — drive a CEO's degree of celebrity attainment (B-list and, beyond that, A-list)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: A firm's strategic nonconformity (relative to its industry) will be positively associated with its CEO's degree of celebrity attainment, specifically B-list attainment and, beyond that, A-list attainment (positive).
- H2: A CEO's demographic atypicality (relative to most CEOs) will be positively associated with their degree of celebrity attainment, specifically B-list attainment and, beyond that, A-list attainment (positive).
- H3: A CEO's use of self-promotion tactics (i.e., push tactics) will be positively associated with their degree of celebrity attainment, specifically B-list attainment and, beyond that, A-list attainment (positive).
- H4: The greater a CEO's use of self-promotion tactics (i.e., push tactics), the stronger the positive association between strategic nonconformity and the CEO's likelihood of achieving the highest degree of celebrity attainment (becoming an A-list versus a B-list celebrity).
- H5: The greater a CEO's use of self-promotion tactics (i.e., push tactics), the stronger the positive association between the CEO's demographic atypicality and their likelihood of achieving the highest degree of celebrity attainment (becoming an A-list versus a B-list celebrity).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm strategic nonconformity (standardized absolute deviation of eight resource-allocation indicators from industry means); CEO demographic atypicality (dummy = 1 if CEO is non-Caucasian or female); CEO self-promotion / push tactics (standardized sum of CEO mentions in firm press releases and tweets).
- DV(s): CEO celebrity, an ordinal three-category construct (noncelebrity = 0, B-list = 1, A-list = 2), built from 11 volume-of-attention indicators multiplied by a positive-valence (emotional resonance) score and binned annually by quartile/decile cutoffs.
- Mediators: None modeled (media routines are the theorized but unmeasured channel translating push and pull into coverage).
- Moderators: CEO self-promotion (push tactics) is hypothesized to amplify the effects of strategic nonconformity (H4) and demographic atypicality (H5) on reaching the A-list.
- Controls: Firm size, firm performance (industry-adjusted ROA, TSR), firm/CEO presuccession visibility, total press releases, total tweets, CEO tenure, year and industry dummies.

Theorizing from media routines, the authors argue journalists — facing time, information, and audience constraints — "pull" distinctive CEOs (nonconformist firms, demographically atypical leaders) into heroic narratives because they make compelling, newsworthy stories, while CEOs can "push" themselves into coverage by supplying information subsidies and self-promotion that ease journalists' production. Ordered probit results support H1 (strategic nonconformity), H2 (demographic atypicality), and H3 (self-promotion) as positive predictors of celebrity; H5 is supported (self-promotion amplifies atypicality's effect, especially for the A-list) but H4 (self-promotion × nonconformity) is not.

**Data & Measures**
Single-study longitudinal panel. Data sources: Execucomp for CEO appointments, gender, and ethnicity (supplemented by firm websites and Bloomberg.com); FACTIVA for newspaper, magazine, and broadcast coverage and the underlying article and transcript text; Wikipedia administrative data for page views and page edits; Compustat for resource-allocation and financial indicators; and firm press releases and Twitter accounts. Sampling: the initial frame was 734 CEOs of S&P 1500 firms who started their positions between 2006 and 2010. Because celebrity is rare, the authors used a modification of state-based sampling — 83 of the 734 CEOs met the threshold of being mentioned in two or more Wall Street Journal articles in any year within the timeframe and were all included, plus 166 CEOs randomly drawn from the remaining 651, giving a combined 249. After several inappropriate cases were dropped (delisted firms, bankruptcies, subsidiary CEOs), the analysed sample is 244 distinct CEOs — 81 from the WSJ-coverage group and 163 from the subsequent random sample — each observed for every year in office excluding the appointment year, through 2014, yielding 1,450 CEO-year observations. Within the sample the base rate of women was 5% and of people of color 11%, with only three individuals having both attributes; control and independent variables span from two years prior to each CEO's start through 2014 or the end of tenure.
- DV: CEO celebrity, an annual ordinal three-category variable coded 2 (A-list), 1 (B-list), 0 (noncelebrity). Built in steps: 11 volume-of-attention indicators (newspaper total, front-page, and title articles; magazine total, title, and feature articles plus manually counted cover appearances in Forbes, Bloomberg Businessweek, and Fortune; broadcast total and feature stories; Wikipedia page views and page edits), each standardized by year and summed into a volume score; a positive-valence score computed with the Linguistic Inquiry Word Count positive- and negative-affect dictionaries as each item's positive affective content divided by its total affective content, averaged across a CEO's coverage in a year and standardized by year (Wikipedia excluded from valence); both scores rescaled to be positive and nonzero, multiplied to give a volume x valence score, then normalized within year on a 100-point scale. Cutoffs: top 10% of a year's scores = A-list, next 15% = B-list, remainder = noncelebrity (the top quartile is the initial celebrity/noncelebrity cutoff).
- IV — firm strategic nonconformity: sum of the standardized absolute differences between the firm's score and its 2-digit GICS industry mean, each year, on eight Compustat resource-allocation indicators: advertising intensity, R&D intensity, overhead efficiency, capital intensity, plant and equipment newness, financial leverage, acquisition intensity, and foreign income intensity. Firm-year variables were windsorized at the 1% level and missing data replaced with the industry mean.
- IV — CEO demographic atypicality: dummy variable coded 1 if the CEO was non-Caucasian or female and 0 otherwise. Gender and ethnicity were combined because neither indicator alone had sufficient representation to estimate effects separately.
- IV / moderator — push tactics (self-promotion): the standardized annual count of firm press releases mentioning the CEO plus the standardized annual count of tweets mentioning the CEO (from company Twitter accounts, and from CEO personal accounts in the few cases where these existed), the two standardized measures added together; CEOs scored 0 in years when neither the firm nor the CEO had a Twitter account.
- Controls (one-year lag unless otherwise noted): firm size (log of sales); firm performance as industry-adjusted ROA and industry-adjusted total shareholder return (TSR1), each adjusted by subtracting the industry median across Compustat firms in the 2-digit GICS industry; firm presuccession visibility and CEO presuccession visibility (newspaper, magazine, and broadcast mentions over the two years before the CEO's start; because the two were correlated at r = .67, CEO visibility enters as the residual after a first-stage regression on firm visibility); the company's total number of press releases and total number of tweets in the focal year; CEO year of tenure; and calendar-year and 2-digit GICS industry dummies.

Estimation in the paper's own terms: panel-based ordered probit regression (the xtoprobit command) with robust standard errors, all data clustered by CEO, in Stata 16.1. Because the coefficients of a nonlinear ordinal model are not sufficient for interpretation, the authors use marginal-effects analyses (margins and lincom in the spost13 package) to assess sign and significance at each celebrity level and to depict the interactions. The design is observational and associational: endogeneity is addressed only by the modified state-based sampling procedure, an extensive control set, and an impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable (ITCV) analysis, not by an experiment, instrument, or exogenous shock. Because the modified state-based sample likely influenced effect sizes, the authors interpret only the sign and significance of the marginal effects and explicitly do not interpret the magnitude of any significant relationship.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. Across all models, firm strategic nonconformity was significantly related to CEO celebrity (b = 0.18, p < .01). In marginal-effects analyses comparing one standard deviation above and below the mean, high strategic nonconformity was positively related to attaining B-list celebrity (b = 0.09, p < .01) and positively related to attaining A-list celebrity (b = 0.02, p < .05) within the sample.
- H2 supported. CEO demographic atypicality was significantly related to CEO celebrity in all models (b = 0.60, p < .05). Atypicality was positively related to attaining B-list celebrity (b = 0.09, p < .05) and positively related to attaining A-list celebrity (b = 0.02, p < .10); the authors note the A-list effect was only marginally significant.
- H3 supported, with the qualification the authors state. Across all models, CEO self-promotion was significantly related to CEO celebrity (b = 0.11, p < .05). High self-promotion was positively related to attaining B-list celebrity (b = 0.03, p < .10) and positively related to attaining A-list celebrity (b = 0.01, p < .10); the authors report support for Hypothesis 3 "although the marginal effect analyses for self-promotion were only marginally significant."
- H4 not supported. The strategic nonconformity x push tactics interaction terms were nonsignificant in Models 3 and 5, so no marginal-effects examination followed.
- H5 supported. The CEO atypicality x push tactics interaction was positive and significant in both Models 4 and 5 (b = 0.31, p = .05). Marginal effects showed no significant interaction relationship at low self-promotion (-1 SD) for either celebrity category. The relationship between atypicality and attaining B-list celebrity was positive and significant at mean self-promotion (b = 0.05, p < .05) and at high (+1 SD) self-promotion (b = 0.08, p < .01), and the relationship between atypicality and attaining A-list celebrity was likewise positive and significant at mean (b = 0.05, p < .05) and high levels (b = 0.08, p < .01). Figure 1 shows increments of self-promotion by atypical CEOs raising the probability of B-list and A-list attainment roughly equally up to a point, beyond which the patterns diverge: rising self-promotion greatly elevated atypical CEOs' chances of A-list celebrity while reducing their chances of B-list celebrity.
- Controls: firm size, total number of press releases, firm presuccession visibility, and CEO presuccession visibility were consistently associated with attaining CEO celebrity. Firm performance was not significantly related to celebrity attainment.
- Robustness and post hoc: t-tests confirmed that mean celebrity scores differed significantly across noncelebrity, B-list, and A-list categories (all p < .01). Restricting the A-list to the top 5% of each year left the pattern of all significant relationships unaffected. A four-category specification (top 5% A-list, next 5% B-list, next 15% C-list, rest noncelebrity) produced consistent results but showed significant marginal-effect differences between noncelebrities and C-list and between C-list and both B-list and A-list (p < .05), and not between B-list and A-list, so the two-level celebrity construct was retained. Re-estimating celebrity as a binary variable (top 25% cutoff) in a logit reproduced the main effects, but the atypicality x self-promotion interaction was not significant under that specification. The ITCV analysis indicated that 38.04% of the strategic nonconformity estimate, 16.60% of the atypicality estimate, and 19.71% of the self-promotion estimate would have to be biased to invalidate the inferences.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts CEO celebrity research from consequences to antecedents, introducing a media-routines-grounded "push–pull theory" that distinguishes who CEOs are (demographic atypicality) from what they do (strategic nonconformity and self-promotion). It reconceptualizes celebrity as an ordinal construct (noncelebrity, B-list, A-list) rather than a binary, and contributes a comprehensive multimedia, valence-weighted ordinal measure spanning newspaper, magazine, broadcast, and online sources.

**Practical Implication**
For CEOs aspiring to celebrity, distinctive personal attributes matter more than self-promotion or strategic actions alone, and combining high self-promotion with demographic atypicality is especially potent for reaching the A-list. Boards should calibrate vigilance to a CEO's self-promotion and distinctiveness, given celebrity's documented risks (complacency, hubris, backlash) for firms.

**Limitations**
The sample is restricted to newly appointed CEOs, excluding longer-serving incumbents who were already celebrities. Modified state-based sampling (oversampling WSJ-covered CEOs) means the sample may differ from a random one, biasing effect magnitudes, so the authors interpret only sign and significance, not effect sizes. The celebrity measure equally weights newspaper, magazine, broadcast, and Internet sources, and the nonconformity measure treats actions exceeding or lagging industry norms equivalently.

**Future Research**
Investigate the motivational mechanisms (e.g., narcissism versus altruistic or firm-promoting motives) driving CEO self-promotion, and how self-promotion shapes susceptibility to backlash when strategic efforts fail. Use the ordinal measure to study trajectories or "arcs" of celebrity — whether levels are attained gradually or abruptly and how celebrity persists or decays. Examine whether more specialized or business-oriented media, or directional (aggressive vs. conservative) nonconformity, yield different interactive effects.
