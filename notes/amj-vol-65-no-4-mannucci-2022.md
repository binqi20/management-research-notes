# “Who Are You Going to Call?” Network Activation in Creative Idea Generation and Elaboration

**Abstract**
Considering creativity as a journey beyond idea generation, scholars have theorized that different ties are beneficial in different phases. As individuals usually possess different types of ties, selecting the optimal ties in each phase and changing ties as needed are central activities for creative success. We identify the types of ties (weak or strong) that are helpful in idea generation and idea elaboration and, given this understanding, whether individuals activate ties in each phase accordingly. In an experimental study of individuals conversing with their ties, we provide evidence of the causal effects of weak and strong ties on idea generation and idea elaboration. We also find that individuals do not always activate ties optimally and identify network size and risk as barriers. Our results in a series of studies reveal that individuals with large networks, despite providing more opportunity to activate both strong and weak ties, activate fewer weak ties and are less likely to switch ties across phases than individuals with smaller networks, particularly when creativity is perceived as a high-risk endeavor. Finally, we find that activating the wrong ties leads to either dropping creative ideas or pursuing uncreative ones.

**Research Question**
Which types of ties (weak or strong) are helpful in different phases of the idea journey (generation vs. elaboration), and—given this—what helps or hinders individuals from activating the appropriate ties in each phase and switching ties between phases?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1(a/b): Interacting with weak (strong) ties during idea generation (elaboration) will lead to higher creativity than interacting with strong (weak) ties (weak ties positive in generation; strong ties positive in elaboration).
- H2: Network size negatively affects the proportion of weak ties individuals activate in idea generation and idea elaboration (negative).
- H3: Network size negatively affects the likelihood of switching ties from idea generation to idea elaboration (negative).
- H4: The effect of network size on the likelihood of activating weak ties is negative when perceived risk is high, and this effect is weaker when perceived risk is low (negative under high risk, attenuated under low risk).
- H5: The effect of network size on the likelihood of switching ties is negative when perceived risk is high, and this effect is weaker when perceived risk is low (negative under high risk, attenuated under low risk).
- H6: For creators who interact with strong ties during idea generation, sticking to strong ties during idea elaboration will increase the likelihood of false positives — pursuing uncreative ideas — compared with creators who switch to weak ties (positive).
- H7: For creators who interact with weak ties during idea generation, sticking to weak ties during idea elaboration will increase the likelihood of false negatives — dropping creative ideas — compared with creators who switch to strong ties (positive).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Tie strength activated (weak vs. strong); network size (small vs. large); idea-journey phase (generation vs. elaboration); tie switching vs. not switching across phases.
- DV(s): Creativity of the elaborated idea; proportion of weak ties activated; likelihood of switching ties; likelihood of dropping/pursuing the idea (false negatives vs. false positives).
- Mediators: Conversation behaviors of alters—"sharing" new ideas vs. "building" on the creator's idea (moderated mediation of the tie-strength → creativity link, differing by phase).
- Moderators: Perceived risk (high vs. low) conditions the effect of network size on weak-tie activation and on tie switching.

Weak ties supply non-redundant knowledge and cognitive flexibility that aid idea generation, whereas strong ties supply constructive feedback and support ("building," yes-anding) that aid idea elaboration. Because networks are cognitive representations subject to limited mental capacity, large networks induce cognitive narrowing and anchoring: creators disproportionately call to mind salient strong ties and stick to prior choices, so they activate fewer weak ties and switch less—especially when creativity feels risky. Failing to switch produces two errors: over-relying on strong ties yields false positives (pursuing uncreative ideas), and sticking to weak ties yields false negatives (abandoning creative ideas).

**Data & Measures**
Four data collections, each with random assignment to condition: Study 1, a preliminary field experiment and a main laboratory experiment reported together as Study 2, and Study 3. All constructs were measured on the study's own participants; no archival data were used.
- Study 1 (93 individuals; M_age = 27.27, SD = 9.89; 70% female; an active participant pool at the behavioral lab in a European business school): 2 (weak vs. strong ties) × 2 (interaction during idea generation vs. idea elaboration) + 1 (control, no interaction) between-subjects randomized experiment run in two parts about one week apart. The available network was captured by a name generator (up to eight people with whom the participant discusses important matters, plus up to five additional names, the second prompt added to surface weak contacts), with emotional closeness rated 1 (distant) to 4 (very close) and alter-alter acquaintance coded 1/0; contacts rated 3 or 4 were classified as strong. In part two, participants held two real, videotaped five-minute Skype conversations in the lab, either with two of their own strong ties or with two weak ties drawn from the lab subject pool, or none in the control condition. The creativity task, adapted from Gino et al. (2010), asked for ideas for a decorative object that could be sold in the university shop. DV: creativity of the final elaborated idea, rated 1-7 by two expert judges blind to condition using the consensual assessment technique (average weighted κ = 0.69; rwg = 0.86; the two ratings averaged). Mediators: counts of alter "sharing ideas" and "building on ideas", coded from the videotapes by two blind raters (ICC = 0.92 and 0.77). Controls: openness to experience (Goldberg's IPIP scale, α = 0.75), creative self-efficacy (Tierney & Farmer, 2002, α = 0.84), and network size and density computed in UCINET. Analysis: the five conditions were converted to a one-way ANOVA design and tested with planned contrasts; mediation was tested with Hayes's PROCESS macro (Model 8) on 10,000 bootstrap samples.
- Study 2, preliminary field experiment (74 members of GRV Italia, the largest and longest-operating live-action role-playing (LARP) organization in Italy; M_age = 31.53, SD = 5.85; 34% female; mean network size 42.89, SD = 29.59): the available network was captured by a roster method listing all members who took part in the most recent LARP event, with closeness (1 = distant, 4 = very close) and interaction frequency (1 = less than once a month, 5 = every day). Two weeks later participants were randomly assigned to a phase manipulation (generate vs. elaborate ideas for the background of the character they would play at an upcoming GRV Italia event) and then answered a name-generator question capturing activation. Measures: strength of the activated network (average strength of all activated ties); proportion of activated strong ties, with ties above the sample mean strength of 2.09 (SD = 0.49) classified strong and the rest weak; and activated inner-circle ties. Network size was measured as degree centrality in the participant's actual available network. Analysis: comparison of activated versus available network strength; a Poisson regression predicting the number of weak ties in the available network; and OLS regressions of the proportion of activated weak ties and of inner-circle ties on network size, controlling for phase condition, age, gender, years in the field, self-rated experience, and ties' experience. Because network size here is measured rather than manipulated, the authors report this as an exploratory analysis giving preliminary support, not a causal test.
- Study 2, main laboratory experiment (221 individuals; M_age = 33.48, SD = 13.35; 67% female; an active participant pool at the behavioral lab in a European business school): 2 (small vs. large available network size) × 2 (low vs. high perceived risk) between-subjects randomized experiment. Network size was manipulated by showing an ego-network diagram of eight contacts (large) or four contacts (small), each condition half strong ties (thick lines, friends) and half weak ties (thin lines, acquaintances); a pretested memory procedure produced 98% accuracy in remembering the network. Perceived risk was manipulated with a scenario about a fictional company, I Love Italy, in which creativity and idea sharing were either discouraged (high risk) or encouraged (low risk); the manipulation check was a six-item 1-7 scale (α = 0.84). The creative task was one problem from Shalley's (1991) complex heuristic in-basket exercise, with participants listing whom they would consult before each phase. DVs: proportion of weak ties activated in a phase = activated weak ties ÷ total ties activated in that phase; tie switching = ties activated before elaboration that had not been activated before generation ÷ total ties activated before elaboration (a more conservative alternative requiring the new tie also to differ in strength yielded identical results). Analysis: t-tests comparing size conditions within each phase and risk condition, tests on the equality of proportions against a 0.5 benchmark, and a 2 × 2 ANOVA for post hoc risk effects.
- Study 3 (217 individuals; M_age = 33.20, SD = 12.90; 59% female; an active participant pool at the behavioral lab in a European business school): 2 (weak vs. strong ties in idea generation) × 2 (switching vs. not switching ties in elaboration) between-subjects randomized scenario experiment set at a fictional design company, Idea Peach, where a designer's reputation is said to depend on their creativity. Idea creativity was held constant by design: participants in the weak-tie generation condition received the five ideas rated most creative in Study 1 (average rating = 5.20), and those in the strong-tie condition the five rated least creative (average rating = 1.00). Alters' elaboration feedback was scripted from the Study 1 conversation coding — strong ties built on the chosen idea, weak ties shared alternative new ideas of comparably high creativity; the two "sharing" forms used were selected via a pretest on 194 Prolific participants. DV: likelihood of dropping the idea, a self-developed three-item scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) with one item reverse-coded (α = 0.90). Manipulation checks: tie closeness (two items from Perry-Smith, 2014; α = 0.84), perceived sharing (α = 0.70), and perceived building (α = 0.87). Risk was not manipulated in this study. Analysis: planned contrasts.
Identification in the paper's own terms: randomized laboratory and field experiments, which the authors say allowed them to isolate causality and rule out alternative explanations. The exception is the network-size analysis inside the preliminary field study, which uses participants' measured networks and is reported as exploratory and associational. The cognitive processes the theory rests on (cognitive narrowing, anchoring) are inferred from activation behaviour rather than measured directly.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (N = 93) — H1(a) supported: during idea generation, participants who interacted with weak ties produced more creative final ideas than participants who interacted with strong ties (M_weak = 3.87, SD = 0.95; M_strong = 2.62, SD = 0.99; t(88) = 3.15, p = 0.022) and than the no-interaction control (M_control = 2.87, SD = 1.15; t(88) = 2.89, p = 0.048). H1(b) not supported: strong ties outperformed weak ties in elaboration in the predicted direction, but the difference was not statistically significant (M_weak = 2.72, SD = 1.22; M_strong = 3.27, SD = 1.08; t(88) = -1.39, p = 0.841). The authors describe this as partial support for Hypothesis 1.
- Study 1, exploratory conversation-content analysis (PROCESS Model 8): neither mechanism mediated during generation — sharing (B = 0.08, SE = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.159, 0.413]) and building (B = -0.16, SE = 0.14, 95% CI [-0.504, 0.067]) both bracketed zero. Both mediated during elaboration — sharing (B = -0.18, SE = 0.11, 95% CI [-0.495, -0.028]) and building (B = -0.60, SE = 0.23, 95% CI [-1.135, -0.224]) — with significant indices of moderated mediation (B = 0.26, SE = 0.17, 95% CI [0.001, 0.687]; B = 0.44, SE = 0.26, 95% CI [0.006, 1.030]). Weak ties kept sharing new ideas and built less on the creator's idea during elaboration, which reduced creativity in that phase. Participants nevertheless rated strong ties as more useful than weak ties (M_strong = 6.24, SD = 0.71; M_weak = 5.54, SD = 0.86; F(3,62) = 11.80, p = 0.001), despite strong ties being less effective in generation.
- Study 2, preliminary field experiment (74 GRV Italia members): the activated network was significantly stronger than the available network (M_activated = 3.31, SD = 0.73; M_available = 2.09, SD = 0.49; t(73) = 13.59, p = 0.000), in generation (t(36) = 9.07, p = 0.000) and in elaboration (t(36) = 10.21, p = 0.000) alike, and participants activated a lower proportion of weak ties in elaboration than in generation (M = 0.14, SD = 0.19 vs. M = 0.27, SD = 0.28; t(72) = -2.18, p = 0.032, d = -0.51) and more inner-circle ties (M = 3.26 vs. M = 2.78; t(72) = 2.00, p = 0.049, d = 0.47). Exploratory regressions gave preliminary support for H2: network size had a negative effect on the proportion of activated weak ties (p = 0.002) and a positive effect on activated inner-circle ties (p = 0.003), even though participants with larger networks had more weak ties available (p = 0.000).
- Study 2, main experiment (N = 221) — H2 supported: participants in the large-network condition activated a lower proportion of weak ties than those in the small-network condition, both in generation (M_small = 0.17, SD = 0.28; M_large = 0.10, SD = 0.21; t(219) = -2.26, p = 0.02, d = -0.30) and in elaboration (M_small = 0.20, SD = 0.28; M_large = 0.13, SD = 0.24; t(219) = -2.03, p = 0.04, d = -0.27). H3 not supported: tie switching did not differ significantly between the small- and large-network conditions (M_small = 0.15, SD = 0.27; M_large = 0.11, SD = 0.24; t(219) = -1.05, p = 0.30).
- Study 2, main experiment — H4 partially supported and H5 partially supported: the negative network-size effect appeared only in the high-risk condition (activation: t(111) = -2.23, p = 0.03, d = -0.42 in generation and t(111) = -2.83, p = 0.01, d = -0.53 in elaboration; switching: t(111) = -2.33, p < 0.02, d = -0.44) and was non-significant in the low-risk condition (activation t(106) = -1.30, p = 0.20 and t(106) = -0.48, p = 0.63; switching t(106) = 0.04, p = 0.99), but the difference between the two risk conditions was itself not significant (activation F(1,217) = 0.00, p = 0.96 and F(1,217) = 1.56, p = 0.21; switching F(1,217) = 1.14, p = 0.29), which is why the authors record partial rather than full support. Post hoc, perceived risk had significant main negative effects on weak-tie activation in generation (F(2,218) = 7.02, p = 0.01, η² = 0.03) and elaboration (F(2,218) = 7.32, p = 0.01, η² = 0.03) and on tie switching (F(2,218) = 11.63, p = 0.00, η² = 0.05), with low-risk participants switching more (M = 0.19, SD = 0.32) than high-risk participants (M = 0.07, SD = 0.17); the authors describe perceived risk, rather than size, as the dominant factor undermining activation switching.
- Study 2, main experiment, baselines: the proportion of activated weak ties was significantly below the 0.5 available proportion in both generation (M = 0.13, SD = 0.25; z = -10.88, p = 0.00) and elaboration (M = 0.17, SD = 0.27; z = -9.86, p = 0.00), and the proportion of switched ties was significantly below the 0.5 random benchmark (M = 0.13, SD = 0.26; z = -10.99, p = 0.00) — participants over-activated strong ties and rarely switched.
- Study 3 (N = 217) — H6 supported: holding idea creativity constant, participants who started with strong ties and did not switch were significantly less likely to drop their uncreative idea than participants who switched to weak ties (M_not-switch = 2.05, SD = 0.74; M_switch = 2.86, SD = 1.41; t(108) = -3.92, p = 0.00, d = 0.72), i.e., more false positives. H7 supported: participants who started with weak ties and did not switch were significantly more likely to drop their creative idea than participants who switched to strong ties (M_not-switch = 2.59, SD = 1.16; M_switch = 2.01, SD = 0.91; t(105) = -2.72, p = 0.04, d = -0.55), i.e., more false negatives.
- The support pattern across the four data collections is heterogeneous, not uniform: H1(a), H2, H6 and H7 supported; H1(b) and H3 not supported; H4 and H5 partially supported. The switching prediction failed as a main effect of network size (H3) and held only within the high-risk condition, and even there the size × risk contrast was not significant.
- Note on the paper's own wording: the Discussion of Study 3 restates the two Study 3 results with the false-positive and false-negative labels transposed relative to the paper's own definitions and to Hypotheses 6 and 7. The directions recorded above follow the definitions the paper states (false positives = pursuing uncreative ideas; false negatives = dropping creative ideas), the hypothesis statements, and the reported hypothesis tests.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops and experimentally tests a theory of network *activation* across phases of the idea journey, shifting the creativity-and-networks debate from the static configuration of ties to which ties creators cognitively call to mind and whether they switch ties as phases change. It supplies a causal test distinguishing weak- versus strong-tie benefits by phase, and explains the long-standing mixed findings on network size by showing that large networks paradoxically impede optimal activation through cognitive narrowing amplified by perceived risk.

**Practical Implication**
The study implies that tie selection is phase-specific: creators benefit from activating weak ties during idea generation and switching to strong ties during elaboration, and failing to switch leaves them either advancing uncreative ideas or abandoning creative ones. The paper notes that successful creative companies promote behaviors consistent with building on others' ideas — Pixar's practice of "plussing," which parallels the improvisational notion of "yes-anding."

**Limitations**
The experimental design isolates causality but cannot reproduce the complexity, duration, and richer social dynamics of real organizational creative projects, and it captured only a single, clear-cut generation-to-elaboration transition rather than repeated iterations. The studies inferred but did not directly measure the underlying cognitive processes, used multiple studies rather than direct replication, and drew on subject pools predominantly composed of women.

**Future Research**
Future work could use neuroimaging or other methods to directly capture the cognitive mechanisms of tie activation, adopt longitudinal designs to study repeated iterations and blurred phase boundaries, and examine how alter characteristics (e.g., status), indirect ties, and the broader network of contacts shape activation. Scholars are also encouraged to test whether the effects differ in predominantly male settings and to use qualitative methods to further probe conversation dynamics across the idea journey.
