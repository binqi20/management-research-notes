# Paradox Peers: A Relational Approach to Navigating a Business–Society Paradox

**Abstract**
Despite advancements in our understanding of organizational paradox, we know little about how individuals navigate paradox when tensions are core to one's occupational role but not a dominant concern for senior leaders. This study explores how grantmakers working in corporate philanthropy navigate a business–society paradox when their leaders prioritize business over social objectives at the organization level. By analyzing interview data from grantmakers, alongside observations of their group meetings, I induce a peer-based model of navigating paradox. The findings reveal the important supportive role of relationships with "paradox peers"—ongoing, cooperative connections to individuals external to one's organization but facing similar paradoxical challenges. I detail three relational mechanisms for engaging paradox that together facilitate the ability of individuals to navigate paradox in their home organizations: connecting with others to relieve tension, collectively protecting the paradox, and collaboratively brainstorming responses. My findings shift paradox scholarship toward a more relational understanding of navigating paradoxes, exploring how engaging tensions outside of one's organizational boundaries can support the navigation of paradox internally.

**Research Question**
How do individuals navigate paradox that is core to their occupational role but not a dominant organizational concern for senior leaders? The study examines this through corporate grantmakers who must jointly pursue business and social objectives even when firm leaders prioritize economic goals and treat social impact as peripheral.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: A business–society paradox (persistent, interdependent yet contradictory social and economic objectives) that is core to grantmakers' occupational mandate but dismissed or minimized by profit-focused leaders.
- Triggering condition: Grantmakers occupy a relatively low-power, internally isolated position lacking interactional partners and resource-rich leaders who appreciate the paradox.
- Enabling relationship: "Paradox peers"—ongoing, cooperative, noncompetitive ties to grantmakers at other organizations facing a similar paradox, built and strengthened through repeated interactions (e.g., monthly meetings).
- Relational mechanisms (process): (1) connecting with one another to relieve tension; (2) collectively protecting the paradox (keeping both elements legitimate rather than letting one dominate); (3) collaboratively brainstorming responses.
- Emergent outcome: Working through paradox by recalling peer interactions to remain energized, motivated, and committed to both social and business objectives back at the home organization.

Drawing on an inductive grounded-theory analysis, the study theorizes a peer-based model in which relationships with paradox peers, operating through the three relational mechanisms, sustain individuals' ability to work through a business–society paradox internally. The process is fundamentally relational and collective: lower-power individuals build a sense of efficacy and resilience through cross-organizational peer interaction even without leader support, reinforcing both the relationships and continued engagement with paradox in a positive cycle.

**Data & Measures**
Single inductive, grounded-theory qualitative study conducted in one major U.S. metropolitan area; there are no separate sub-studies or comparison samples. Setting and recruitment: starting from Fortune 500 companies, the author identified approximately 50 large companies in the metro area and invited every corporate grantmaker with publicly available contact information; five responded to the initial email, after which the sample grew primarily by referral chains until saturation (subsequent interviews revealed very few new ideas or themes). Interview corpus: 40 informants from 32 different large firms, with two grantmakers from the same company interviewed on eight occasions; 33 women and 7 men (reported as 82.5% and 12.5%); 28 (70%) had begun their careers in other corporate functions and 12 (30%) in the nonprofit sector, 7 of the latter transitioning from corporate fundraising. Interviews were conducted in person at a location of the informant's choosing, except two by phone; most took place between late 2014 and the end of 2016, lasted 45 to 120 minutes and averaged 75 minutes, and covered day-to-day activities, funding practices, the purpose of the grantmaking function, how informants thought about success, and reporting practices. Four follow-up phone interviews in 2020 also served as member checks. Observational corpus: 12 group meetings observed from 2015 to 2016, typically attended by about a dozen grantmakers, with roughly 90-minute presentations plus about 15 minutes of informal conversation before and after; the author documented question-and-answer responses and informal conversation as fieldnotes. Operationalization is coding rather than measurement: the data were organized and coded in ATLAS.ti following an inductive grounded-theory approach, presented as three steps. Step 1 used open coding to produce first-order empirical codes (e.g., "grantmakers perceived that leaders prioritize business objectives and minimize social objectives"; "peer relationships experienced as noncompetitive"). Step 2 used axial coding to group first-order codes into second-order conceptual themes (e.g., "connections to peers situated inside the paradox and outside the focal organization"). Step 3 synthesized the themes into four aggregate theoretical dimensions, shown as the data structure in Figure 1. Validity procedures: triangulation of interview and observational data, in-depth shadowing of one organization (whose data are not analyzed in the paper), recurring meetings with external colleagues to reduce single-coder bias, and member checks in which all informants found the model to accurately represent their experience. The design is interpretive and theory-building; the paper specifies no variables, hypothesis tests, or causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
The paper reports four main findings corresponding to the four aggregate dimensions that emerged from the analysis; it reports no hypothesis tests, effect sizes, or significance levels. (1) Experiencing paradox in one's occupational role: grantmakers recognized both contradiction and interdependence between business and social objectives, with one remarking that "you can never really align business impact and social impact fully," while perceiving that firm leaders prioritized economic goals and minimized social impact; this left them with little internal guidance on pursuing competing demands and they described the work as hard, complicated, and hurtful. A footnote records that three grantmakers instead described leaders as deeply supportive of social impact, so the account presents the majority experience. (2) Paradox peer relationships: grantmakers held ongoing, cooperative connections with grantmakers at other organizations who were insiders to the paradox but outsiders to the focal organization; these ties were built and strengthened over repeated interactions at local peer meetings, lunches, and happy hours, and were experienced as noncompetitive and collaborative even across firms that competed in the market. (3) Three relational mechanisms of engaging paradox, presented as overlapping and mutually reinforcing rather than sequential: connecting with one another to relieve tension (fostering feelings of connection and understanding, and offering opportunities to commiserate about shared tensions); collectively protecting the paradox (alternating the focus of meetings between social impact and business impact, and encouraging each other to use business language to provide room to pursue social impact goals); and collaboratively brainstorming responses to paradox (exploring new ways to pursue business and social impact, and seeking advice and feedback from peers). (4) Navigating paradox in one's occupational role: back at their home organizations, grantmakers worked through paradox by recalling peer interactions, both to remain energized and motivated and to remain committed to social impact when leaders dismissed it, as when Olivia continued to use an impact matrix her leaders had called "not necessary"; this working through in turn reinforced the peer relationships and the relational engagement of paradox, forming a positive cycle. The single deviant case, Gerry, reached by cold email rather than a referral chain, largely worked alone, lacked strong peer relationships, and directed donations only to plant communities in ways aimed at building reputational capital with local decision makers; the paper reads him as a counterexample that highlights the value of peer relationships for motivating grantmakers to work through competing business–social demands.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances a more relational approach to paradox theory, unpacking how and why interactions with external peers enable individuals to navigate paradox—shifting the field beyond individualized cognitive/behavioral accounts and beyond intra-organizational dynamics. It extends the paradox-mindset construct to include a relational, collective dimension; foregrounds the role of lower-power relational partners (rather than high-power leaders); and bridges paradox theory with research on occupations by showing that cross-organizational occupational communities supply relational resources for living with persistent tensions.

**Practical Implication**
Firms that staff CSR, philanthropy, ethics, sustainability, or diversity roles may shift business–society paradoxes from the organizational level to the occupational level, leaving individuals to absorb persistent tensions; without organizational engagement this can stall or reverse social impact. The findings suggest that cross-organizational peer networks and professional associations are valuable supports that help such individuals sustain commitment to social objectives, and that organizations should attend to these positions and relationships.

**Limitations**
The referral-chain sampling in one geographic location with an active peer group likely over-sampled connected grantmakers rather than isolated ones, and all informants worked for large Fortune 500 firms, so the model may not transfer to smaller organizations with scarce philanthropic resources. The study did not capture whether some peer connections matter more than others, nor negative/destructive peer dynamics, and generalizability beyond corporate philanthropy to other competing-demand occupations remains to be tested.

**Future Research**
Researchers could examine variation in how strongly individuals connect with paradox peers (including isolated individuals), whether mature versus still-professionalizing occupations display different relational mechanisms, and the factors that prevent destructive cycles while enabling positive relational dynamics. Further work could explore how occupational groups move social issues from the periphery to the core of the firm, and whether actors lacking a clear occupational community can form paradox-peer ties.
