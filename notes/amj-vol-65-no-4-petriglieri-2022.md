# Stranger Leaders: A Theory of Marginal Leaders’ Conception of Learning in Organizations

**Abstract**
Not all marginal leaders took the same trajectory toward firm ground for their identities. Some left the margins to lead, embracing either an instrumental or a humanistic view of their function. Others learned to lead from the margins, casting that duality as a paradox. Taking a systems psychodynamic approach to examine marginal leaders’ trajectories through a defining duality, this study reveals the interplay between existential and strategic layers of leader identity construction. Theorizing the conception of learning as the process through which leadership comes to life and becomes organized, the study expands and bridges the literatures on leader identity and on the management of dualities.

**Research Question**
How do marginal leaders navigate defining dualities in organizations? The authors study senior L&D executives as an "extreme case" of marginal leaders who hold a formal leadership role yet lack an established template for their leader identity and face conflicting (instrumental vs. humanistic) prescriptions for their function.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper — this is an inductive, grounded-theory study with no a priori hypotheses or formally numbered propositions. The paper states a research question ("How do marginal leaders navigate defining dualities in organizations?") that the authors describe as having been settled on iteratively, as their focus "sharpened over time" while moving between data and literature, and it develops a theoretical model post hoc rather than testing stated predictions.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: the "defining duality" of learning (instrumental vs. humanistic views of the function); leadership conceived as an identity rather than a position; marginality / "stranger" status (insiders with loose ties, caught between principles).
- Process / phases: the "conception of learning" — finding a place in relation to significant counterparts, taking a stance on learning, and building learning spaces — through which marginal leaders craft a leader identity.
- Trajectories (emergent leader identities): custodian (instrumental view, leave the margins), challenger (humanistic view, leave the margins), and connector (holds the duality as a paradox, leads from the margins).
- Systems psychodynamic underpinnings: splitting and projection (projecting the instrumental facet onto established leaders and the humanistic facet onto learners) drive the custodian/challenger bifurcation; integration and relatedness underpin the connector trajectory.

The conception of learning is the process through which leadership "comes to life and becomes organized": by relating to significant counterparts and taking a stance on why and what learning counts, marginal leaders settle existential angst into a viable leader identity and translate it into a strategy for organizing learning spaces. Custodians and challengers reduce ambiguity by splitting the duality and grounding identity in a single pole, whereas connectors resist splitting and integrate both poles, treating the duality as a paradox to be held rather than a conflict to be resolved.

**Data & Measures**
- Data corpus: individual semistructured interviews with 69 senior L&D executives in 69 multinational companies, recorded with permission and professionally transcribed; informants are identified in the text by progressive number rather than by name (pseudonyms are used for the three exemplar informants). Access was obtained by phrasing the request broadly, asking to interview "the person in charge of learning and development" at each company; 14 of those executives reported directly to the CEO, 40 to the Chief Human Resources Officer, and the remaining 15 were heads of learning for a large region or unit. Informants were based in 19 countries; 24 were men and 45 were women; 59 had worked in more than one organization.
- Interview protocol (Appendix A): four blocks — background and experience; role; developing talent and learning initiatives; and the future of learning. Questions began broad (background, career journey, activities) and, as the study progressed, focused on informants' experiences of marginality as leaders and on the views of learning that oriented their work; the protocol was no longer revised after 55 interviews. Italicized protocol items mark the questions the authors introduced, emphasized, or followed up on more over time.
- Supplementary data: interviewed informants were invited to group discussions during the first part of data collection and analysis, which served as a source of member check (Lincoln & Guba, 1985).
- Stopping rule: no new themes emerged after 64 interviews, which the authors read as theoretical saturation (Glaser & Strauss, 1967); five further informants confirmed that impression before data collection stopped.
- How constructs were coded (descriptive mode): the two authors coded each interview line by line, first separately and then together, assigning one or more codes to sections of text providing evidence for a phenomenon of interest — in-vivo codes drawn from participants' language where possible (e.g., "confused," "gatekeeper") and descriptive codes otherwise. Axial codes were then deployed to capture broader themes (Locke, 2001). A preliminary coding scheme emerged after 20 hours of conversation about 12 interviews and was then used to code the whole sample, with regular meetings to compare codes and resolve discrepancies.
- How constructs were coded (theoretical mode): constant comparison (Strauss & Corbin, 1998) condensed the axial codes into theoretical categories. Systematic differences in informants' relations to the L&D function, to their company's established leaders, and to its employees as learners — identified as axial codes — were used to group transcripts; grouped this way, transcripts also reported similar views on whether learning should primarily serve the company, its members, or both. Informants were then grouped by the leader identity that transpired in their interviews; three cases remained uncertain and were resolved in discussion between the authors.
- Interpretive layer: a systems psychodynamic reading (Berg & Smith, 1985; Petriglieri & Petriglieri, 2020) attended to associations in the text (which themes heated up or cooled down the emotional intensity of the conversation) and associations in the field (between informant and others), and applied the concepts of splitting and projection (Klein, 1959) and relatedness (Bion, 1961) to infer why leaders took different trajectories.
- Identification: none claimed. The design is inductive, interpretive, and cross-sectional, and the paper makes no causal or estimation claims; the authors state that not every individual fit perfectly in one trajectory and that the model is "a theoretical extrapolation of patterns we inferred across the sample." No counts are reported for how many informants fell into each of the three trajectories.

**Key Findings**
- The defining duality (Table 1): informants agreed that learning mattered, but their explanations of why it mattered, what kind mattered, and who should benefit "diverged systematically" into two poles. The instrumental pole treats learning as a tool to keep people and the organization performing and as a means to turn talent into performance, with the organization as primary beneficiary; its practices foster incorporation through contextualized and standardized activities, with formal assessment a key element. The humanistic pole treats learning as a right to keep growing and as "a weapon against oppression" by the status quo, with the individual as primary beneficiary; its practices foster individuation through personalized activities, with assessment seen as a hindrance.
- The marginal predicament (Table 2): despite senior roles and sizeable resources, informants still felt somewhat marginal. Two conditions gave them few anchors for a leader identity — loose professional standards (no established profession, little role-specific training, no L&D community) and ambiguous organizational authorization (no clearly specified mandate, insufficient or fluctuating budgets, struggling for others' attention and time).
- That predicament evoked two identity dilemmas: an existential one ("Why am I a leader?") and a strategic one ("How do I organize?"). The authors report that the existential dilemma ignited the learning of leadership and the strategic one the leadership of learning, and that both had to be tackled to define one's leadership.
- The core emergent process — the conception of learning — has three steps and is theorized as recursive, not linear, since relatedness, ideology, and practice inform and reinforce each other: (a) finding a place in relation to the function, established leaders, and other organizational members; (b) taking a stance on one's function as an enterprise through which people are made to fit or freed up; and (c) organizing spaces that facilitate incorporation or individuation.
- Three trajectories and resulting leader identities (Figure 1): custodian — place insider, stance instrumental, space incorporation; connector — place marginal, stance paradoxical, space inclusion; challenger — place outsider, stance humanistic, space individuation. Custodians related to established leaders as insiders seeking support and to learners as gatekeepers; challengers related to established leaders as outsiders demanding redemption and to learners as liberators; connectors related to established leaders as marginals struggling to broaden them and to learners as hosts.
- Distribution across trajectories: the majority of informants took two of the three trajectories — custodian or challenger — and the rest resisted splitting and became connectors. The authors note a "sizeable fraction" took the custodian trajectory, which they suggest "might reflect the enduring prevalence of an instrumental view of leadership and learning," while explicitly cautioning that this "should be taken with a large grain of salt." Separately, the connector trajectory "seemed to include some of the most seasoned executives in the sample," consistent with the earlier observation that informants who remained marginal were among the most experienced in the role.
- Inferred systems psychodynamic mechanism: custodians and challengers both minimized ambiguity by grounding their leader identity in a single view of the function. The authors infer that splitting the duality of learning and then projecting its instrumental facet onto established leaders and its humanistic facet onto learners "is the origin of the bifurcation between those trajectories" — custodians embracing the instrumental pole and moving toward established leaders while distancing themselves from learners, challengers embracing the humanistic pole and moving toward learners while distancing themselves from (and seeking to redeem) established leaders. Connectors instead resisted splitting and attempted integration, reframing the duality as a paradox to be held; the authors describe this balancing act as costly but, for connectors, worth it.
- A cross-cutting finding: all three categories of leaders cast learners as lacking precisely what the leaders themselves had struggled with — specific competencies for their new roles, enough power to remain different, or the opportunity to stay in difficult yet creative dialogues — and designed learning spaces accordingly. The authors offer this as a speculation, based on that finding, that learning spaces may in part serve to soothe and amplify marginal leaders' own vulnerabilities.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study describes the predicament of marginal leaders — a boundary region between emerging and established leadership — and surfaces existential and strategic layers of leader identity work that lie beneath and beyond the interpersonal/social layer scholars know best. It theorizes the "conception of learning" as the link between leaders' becoming and organizing, and argues the instrumental–humanistic duality may sit at the core of all leaders' identities, thereby bridging the leader-identity literature to the literature on the management of paradoxes.

**Practical Implication**
The work informs the management-learning conversation on the purpose, value, and practice of learning, and the relationship between L&D executives and academic providers of executive education. It advises the proliferating cadre of marginal leaders (heads of ethics, diversity, well-being, learning, etc.) to recast marginality as a positive, creative experience — keeping a healthy distance from the organization while sustaining connections with diverse internal and external groups — since holding the instrumental–humanistic duality requires embracing rather than escaping marginality.

**Limitations**
The sample, though spanning many industries and locations, is a theoretical (not representative) one, so generalizability must be treated cautiously. The cross-sectional design precludes inferences about how leaders' views shift over time, and the authors did not collect data on personality differences or stages of adult development. Interviews better mirror informants' internalized reality than their social acts, limiting insight into the tactics leaders use to influence others and build consensus.

**Future Research**
Further research is needed to determine the conditions under which the theorizing is most informative and the prevalence of different trajectories across leadership functions. Longitudinal research could examine whether the same person takes different trajectories at different times, organizations, or in relation to different populations, and ethnographic methods within a smaller subset of organizations could add nuance about how these interpretations are enacted or denied.
