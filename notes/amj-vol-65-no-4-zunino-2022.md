# How Do Investors Evaluate Past Entrepreneurial Failure? Unpacking Failure Due to Lack of Skill versus Bad Luck

**Abstract**
Research has shown that most ventures fail, yet there has been limited work on investors' views of entrepreneurs who have failed in the past. We address this gap and call attention to an innate asymmetry between past failure and success. This asymmetry arises because success requires skill and luck jointly, whereas failure materializes due to either lack of skill (mistakes) or bad luck (misfortune). We ask: Are investors "failure-averse" and discount a failed entrepreneur even in the presence of additional information about entrepreneurial skill? Or do they make "rational inferences" in light of the additional skill information and proceed to fund the new startup? To test whether investors are failure-averse or engage in rational inference, we use experiments in the context of equity crowdfunding. The results suggest that prospective crowdfunding investors rationally integrate informational cues regarding past outcomes and entrepreneurial skill.

**Research Question**
How do investors evaluate entrepreneurs who have previously experienced failure, and does information about entrepreneurial skill moderate the negative evaluation of past failure? Specifically, are investors "failure averse" (categorically discounting anyone who failed) or do they make "rational inferences," looking beyond past outcomes to incorporate credible skill cues?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1 (cue of previous entrepreneurial outcome): Absent cues about skill, investors are less likely to fund a venture proposed by an entrepreneur who has experienced past failure, compared to one who has experienced past success. Predicted negative; tested as bF < 0.
- Hypothesis 2 (rational inference): The positive effect of an additional skill cue on investors' funding a venture is larger for entrepreneurs who have experienced past failure than for entrepreneurs who have experienced past success. Predicted positive interaction; tested as bFSk > 0.
- Hypothesis 3 (failure aversion): In the presence of skill cues, investors are less likely to fund venture proposals by entrepreneurs who have experienced past failure than venture proposals by entrepreneurs with past success. Predicted negative; tested as bF + bFSk < 0.

Hypotheses 2 and 3 are deliberately competing tests of two alternative views of investors, so the paper states that by design it expects to find support for one view and lack of support for the other, rather than support for all three hypotheses. Investors are said to make "rational inferences" if the patterns support Hypothesis 2 and do not support Hypothesis 3, and to exhibit failure aversion if the reverse holds.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Past venture outcome (Failure = 1 if previous venture failed, 0 if succeeded); Skill cue availability (Skill = 1 if a credible skill cue was presented); No Experience (first-time vs. serial entrepreneur).
- DV(s): Investment Attractiveness (5- or 7-point Likert), Amount Invested (winsorized at 95th percentile), and Expected Investment (their product).
- Mediators: None formally tested.
- Moderators: The Failure × Skill cue interaction (bFSk) — the credible skill cue moderates (attenuates) the failure discount.

The paper builds on a skill-and-luck framework: success requires both skill and luck jointly, whereas failure can arise from lack of skill (mistakes), bad luck (misfortune), or both. Past failure is therefore a *noisy* rather than a *negative* cue of skill. Absent additional information, investors discount failed entrepreneurs (Hypothesis 1). A credible, third-party skill cue (inclusion in Forbes's "30 Under 30" list) resolves the ambiguity about the root cause of failure, allowing Bayesian-like inference of high skill and mitigating the failure discount (Hypothesis 2, supported for investment amount and expected investment). The alternative view that investors are behaviorally "failure averse" and ignore skill cues (Hypothesis 3) finds no support. Study 3 shows that a credible *luck* cue (an E. coli outbreak) does not similarly restore funding, because luck is inherently less diagnostic of skill.

**Data & Measures**
- Design and stimulus (all three studies): online framed-field experiments (Harrison & List, 2004) built on one real, anonymized equity-crowdfunding project seeking £350,000 for a 20% equity stake, shown as a three-section proposal (business idea, founding team, investor Q&A) rendered as .png graphics. Treatments were randomly assigned in a between-subjects design; the paper argues that random assignment allows for causal analysis because it rules out endogeneity problems and removes the need for assumptions about how cues become available.
- Study 1 (Amazon Mechanical Turk; respondents prescreened for prior investment experience): five treatments — first-time entrepreneur, plus a 2 × 2 of previous outcome (failure, success) × skill cue (available, unavailable). 627 respondents were recruited; 6% failed the instructional manipulation check, and approximately 15% and 40% failed the manipulation and sanity checks; the final sample consisted of 269 respondents (265 in models that add respondent attributes). Online Appendix D reports a separate U.K. replication run on Prolific, with an average investment amount of £1,304.
- Study 2 (Prolific; respondents prescreened for employment and investment experience; a London indicator is among the controls): a 2 × 2 randomized between-subjects design of previous outcome (failure, success) × cue type, where the benchmark is a *nonskill* cue rather than no information. 29.2% and 20.6% of respondents were excluded for failing sanity and manipulation checks, retaining 140 respondents (136 in models with controls).
- Study 3 (Prolific; same screening): a 2 × 2 randomized between-subjects design of previous outcome × luck cue, benchmarked against the same nonskill-cue baseline. After sanity- and manipulation-check exclusions the sample is 130 respondents (126 in models with controls).
- Dependent variables: Investment Attractiveness — a Likert item, described in Study 1 as a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (definitely unattractive) to 5 (definitely attractive), with Studies 2 and 3 reporting it on a 1–7 range (Tables 7 and 10); Amount Invested — the investment amount reported by respondents, winsorized at the 95th percentile (£10,000); Expected Investment — the product of the two, adjusted in tranches of 20%.
- Treatment variables (dummies): Failure = 1 where the previous venture failed and 0 if it succeeded; Skill = 1 if the respondent saw a treatment indicating entrepreneurial skill; No Experience = 1 for the first-time-entrepreneur treatment (Study 1 only). The skill cue was inclusion in Forbes's "30 Under 30" list, disclosed in the Q&A section (47 words); the Study 2 nonskill cue was a 44-word statement of self-proclaimed passion and commitment; the Study 3 luck cue was the 2016 U.K. E. coli O157 outbreak, framed as a sudden and unexpected negative (or positive) impact on the previous venture's operations.
- Controls: age, college education or higher, risk propensity (a nonincentivized multiple price list, Holt & Laury, 2002), homeownership as a wealth proxy, and male; Studies 2 and 3 add a London location indicator.
- Estimation: OLS regressions with robust standard errors. Hypotheses are tested as one-tailed inequality tests on the coefficients bF (Hypothesis 1), bFSk (Hypothesis 2), and bF + bFSk (Hypothesis 3).

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 269; Table 6): Hypothesis 1 is supported on all three outcomes — the Failure coefficient is negative and significant for Investment Attractiveness (-0.34; one-tailed p = .033; a drop of about 0.35, roughly 9% below the baseline), Amount Invested (-459.5; p = .019; a discount of £460, 61% of the baseline), and Expected Amount (-376.0; p = .018; a discount of £376, 61% of the baseline). Hypothesis 2 receives only partial support: the Failure × Skill cue interaction is positive but not statistically significant for Investment Attractiveness (0.17; p = .23), and positive and significant for Amount Invested (+828.6; p = .013, an increase of £828) and Expected Amount (+702.1; p = .013, an increase of £702). Hypothesis 3 is not supported — bF + bFSk is negative but insignificant for attractiveness (p = .16) and positive, contrary to the hypothesis, and insignificant for the two monetary outcomes (p = .78 and p = .79). The No Experience coefficient (first-time entrepreneur versus the previously successful serial baseline) is negative but not statistically different from zero (p = .20, .25, .21), so serial entrepreneurs command no premium on average. Adding respondent attributes (Models 6.4–6.6) leaves signs and significance intact: Hypothesis 1 fully supported, Hypothesis 2 supported for Investment Amount and Expected Amount, Hypothesis 3 not supported.
- Study 2 (n = 140; Table 9; nonskill-cue benchmark): Hypothesis 1 is supported on all three outcomes — Investment Attractiveness -0.91 (p = .001; a drop of about 0.90 out of 7, roughly 17% of the baseline), Amount Invested -1,364.5 (p = .036; a £1,364.50 discount, 63% of the baseline), Expected Amount -1,210.0 (p = .028; a £1,210 discount, 66% of the baseline). Hypothesis 2 is supported on all three — attractiveness +0.79 (p = .038; 0.79 out of 7 points, 14.5% of the baseline), Amount Invested +1,760.6 (p = .040; £1,760.60, 81% of the baseline), Expected Amount +1,467.5 (p = .038; £1,467.50, 80% of the baseline). Hypothesis 3 is again not supported: the sum is negative but insignificant for attractiveness (p = .35) and positive with no statistical support for the two monetary outcomes. Because every Study 2 scenario carried some cue about the entrepreneur, this rules out the "info-about-the-entrepreneur" explanation in favour of the "info-about-skills" explanation.
- Study 3 (n = 130; Table 11; luck cue in place of the skill cue): the table reports no formal hypothesis tests, because the hypotheses concern skill cues rather than luck cues. The Failure coefficient is negative and, the authors report, statistically significant across all models, replicating the failure discount. The Luck Cue coefficient is statistically insignificant across the six models, as is the Failure × Luck Cue interaction; investment patterns are not sensitive to the availability of a credible luck cue.
- Taken together, the studies support the rational-inference view and not the failure-aversion view: investors discount past failure, but a credible third-party skill cue offsets that discount for the monetary outcomes, whereas a credible luck cue does not, because even a documented misfortune leaves the entrepreneur's skill uncertain.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study challenges the implicit symmetry assumption in the entrepreneurial resource-acquisition literature — that if success signals skill, failure signals its absence — by recasting past failure as a noisy (not negative) cue whose informational value depends on the cues available at the time of investment. It contributes to the crowdfunding literature (crowders make rational inferences rather than being failure averse), foregrounds the under-studied role of misfortune/luck in resource acquisition, and informs the experimental-capitalism literature by identifying a micro-level mechanism (non-aversion to failed founders) that sustains continued entrepreneurial experimentation.

**Practical Implication**
At the seed stage, entrepreneurs may be reluctant to disclose past failures, but the findings show disclosure is not detrimental when paired with an adequate, credible cue of skill. Crowdfunding platform owners have an opportunity to increase transaction volumes by including serial entrepreneurs and introducing mechanisms for reporting credible skill cues.

**Limitations**
The study adopts a binary view of entrepreneurial skill (above/below a threshold) and operationalizes the skill cue with a single proxy (Forbes's "30 Under 30"); moderate-credibility cues may not undo the failure effect. The context is equity crowdfunding with prospective (not always actual) investors, and the experiments focus on two facets — past outcomes and skill cues — among many that matter to serial entrepreneurs.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine whether the interplay between previous-outcome cues and skill cues decays as a venture matures and clearer performance cues emerge; study other sources of negative sentiment (e.g., founder gender or race) and whether credible skill cues dissipate them; test generalizability to other investor types (e.g., venture capitalists); and revisit the role of learning and luck, alternative skill-cue credibility levels, and additional facets such as venture longevity or number of entrepreneurial attempts.
