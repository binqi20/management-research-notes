# Surging Underdogs and Slumping Favorites: How Recent Streaks and Future Expectations Drive Competitive Transgressions

**Abstract**
Any single competition is rarely a "one-off" event and instead is often part of a larger sequence of related competitions. Thus, we contend that in order to better understand people's competitive experiences, we must take a more holistic view, where their experiences and behaviors in the present are a function of their past and expected future outcomes. This research expands the temporal lens of competition by examining how past outcomes (i.e., winning vs. losing streak) and future expectations (i.e., underdog vs. favorite standing) collectively influence an actor's cognitive and affective reactions to a competition, with implications for their willingness to transgress. Studies 1 (fantasy football managers) and 2 (the English Premiere League teams) show that streaks and underdog vs. favorite standing interact to predict competitive transgressions: winning streaks increase transgressions for underdogs, and losing streaks increase transgressions for favorites. Studies 3 (public defenders) and 4 (Democrats and Republicans) experimentally manipulate streaks and standing and unpack the cognitive (i.e., outcome uncertainty) and affective (i.e., excitement for underdogs, and anxiety for favorites) mechanisms that precipitate these transgressions. Theoretical implications for the competition literature, as well as managerial insights, are discussed.

**Research Question**
How do an actor's recent performance trajectory (winning vs. losing streak) and future performance expectations (underdog vs. favorite standing) jointly shape the cognitive and affective reactions that drive competitive transgressions? The paper asks when and why competitors who are embedded in a sequence of related contests are willing to depart from accepted rules of conduct.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1: Underdog vs. favorite standing will moderate the effect of streaks on competitive transgressions, such that being on a winning streak will prompt transgressions for underdogs, and being on a losing streak will prompt transgressions for favorites.
Hypothesis 2: The interactive effect of streaks and underdog versus favorite standing on competitive transgressions will be mediated by outcome uncertainty.
Hypothesis 3a: For underdogs, the effect of winning streak on transgressive behaviors will be serially mediated by outcome uncertainty and excitement.
Hypothesis 3b: For favorites, the effect of losing streak on transgressive behaviors will be serially mediated by outcome uncertainty and anxiety.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Winning vs. losing streak (past outcomes); underdog vs. favorite standing (future expectations); their interaction.
- DV(s): Competitive transgressions (intentions in Studies 1 and 3; behavioral measures — yellow cards in Study 2, misreported quiz scores in Study 4).
- Mediators: Outcome uncertainty (first-stage); excitement (for underdogs) and anxiety (for favorites) as serial second-stage affective mediators.
- Moderators: Underdog vs. favorite standing moderates the streak-to-transgression relationship.

The paper integrates research on underdogs/favorites with work on winning/losing streaks through a temporally embedded model grounded in mental time travel and event segmentation theory (EST). Streaks create continuous "cognitive dots" that let competitors mentally traverse from past to projected future, generating psychological momentum. When these streak-based projections clash with one's standing (a winning streak as an underdog, or a losing streak as a favorite), the mismatch raises outcome uncertainty. This uncertainty makes alternative futures vivid and elicits high-arousal emotions — excitement for underdogs anticipating an unexpected win, anxiety for favorites fearing an unexpected loss — which crowd out moral considerations and precipitate transgressions.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (field survey, fantasy football managers): 1,133 managers were recruited through a Facebook advertisement targeting people over 18 living in the United States, run during week six of the 2018-2019 NFL season; after listwise deletion for missing data (n = 19) and exclusion of those who failed two NFL knowledge questions or an attention check (n = 55), 1,059 were analysed (87.8% male; Mage = 34.52, SD = 9.67). Winning and losing streaks were two separate counts of consecutive wins and consecutive losses prior to week six (range 0-5), computed from participants' reported season results. Standing was self-reported on a Likert scale from 1 (definitely the underdog) to 6 (definitely the favorite). Competitive transgressions were self-reported likelihoods (1 = definitely not to 7 = definitely yes) for four behaviors drawn from ESPN's fair play and conduct guidelines, aggregated as a three-item formative index after a separate validation survey of 123 Reddit fantasy managers rated the fourth item (picking up and dropping players to block an opponent) permissible. Controls: years of playing, number of leagues, league award, overall record, age, gender. OLS regression with interaction terms and simple slopes; all variables came from the same rater at the same time, so the design is cross-sectional and associational.
- Study 2 (archival, English Premier League): 18 consecutive seasons (2000-2001 to 2017-2018), 380 contests per season and 6,840 contests in total, entered as two rows per contest (focal team and opponent), giving 12,694 team-game observations after dropping each season's first two contests, 81 matches with missing odds, and the .08% of matches where the two teams' odds were equal. Streaks were counts of consecutive games the focal team had won or lost immediately preceding the current contest within that season. Standing was the aggregated implied probability of winning derived from Interwetten.com and Ladbrokes.com betting odds (the inverse of each quoted odds; r = .99 between the two books), a continuous variable on which high values indicate a favorite. Competitive transgressions were the number of yellow cards the focal team received during a match. Controls: home/away, competitive crowding below and above, potential to fall or rise in rank, first- and last-rank dummies, and current rank. Poisson regression with year, team, and week fixed effects and clustered standard errors; the fixed-effect specification addresses unmeasured team-level heterogeneity, but the data are archival and the estimates associational. Robustness checks used negative binomial models, a three-level multilevel model, a binary standing variable, yellow-plus-red cards, outlier trimming, and prior-season rank and streak controls.
- Study 3 (preregistered vignette experiment, public defenders): 387 public defenders participated after recruitment through a Facebook advertisement that ran for 30 days; after excluding those who had never taken a case to trial, answered attention checks incorrectly, or finished in under 60 seconds, 332 were analysed (59% female, 40% male, 1% not reported; Mage = 44.50, SD = 11.82). Participants were randomly assigned in a 2 (winning vs. losing streak) x 2 (underdog vs. favorite) between-participants design built around a vignette about an upcoming trial. Outcome uncertainty was four items written from the construct's conceptual definition, 1-7 (alpha = .87). Excitement and anxiety were four items each from Van Katwyk et al. (2000), 1-5 (alpha = .92 and .88). Competitive transgressions were three items on the likelihood of "coaching" the key witness, 1-5 (alpha = .97); a separate survey of 32 public defenders found 87.5% rated coaching a gray-area or clearly unethical behavior. Alternative mechanisms measured included the desire to prove others wrong, the desire to avoid embarrassment, goal commitment, self- and opponent-performance expectations, and standing legitimacy. Analysis used two-way ANOVA plus PROCESS Model 7 and custom serial-mediation syntax with 5,000 bootstrap resamples.
- Study 4 (preregistered behavioral experiment, partisan trivia competition): 430 working adults who identified as Democrats or Republicans were recruited through TurkPrime four months before the 2020 US presidential election; after excluding non-US residents and those failing two attention checks, plus listwise deletion on the dependent variables, 372 were analysed (52% female, 48% male; Mage = 35.64, SD = 11.62). The same 2 x 2 between-participants design was staged as the 16th weekly round of an ostensible "American Political History Competition" in which each participant's own record was held constant (9 of 15 wins, ranked 21st of 100); the streak manipulation displayed five consecutive win or loss boxes, and the standing manipulation set the opponent's rank at 13th (making the participant the underdog) or 29th (favorite). Competitive transgressions were behavioral and binary: coded 1 when the score a participant submitted exceeded the score the survey had automatically computed from their answers to the 15-question quiz, and 0 otherwise. Mediators used the Study 3 items. Analysis used binary logistic regression plus PROCESS Model 7 and custom serial-mediation syntax.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (OLS, n = 1,059) supports Hypothesis 1. There were no main effects of winning streak (b = .04, SE = .03, p = .205), losing streak (b = .05, SE = .05, p = .282), or standing (b = -.03, SE = .03, p = .267). Standing moderated the winning-streak effect (b = -.05, SE = .02, p < .001): for underdogs (-1 SD) winning streaks were positively related to transgressions (b = .14, SE = .04, p < .001), while for favorites (+1 SD) the effect was non-significant (b = -.02, SE = .03, p = .621). Standing also moderated the losing-streak effect (b = .08, SE = .02, p < .001): positive for favorites (b = .22, SE = .06, p < .001) and non-significant for underdogs (b = -.01, SE = .05, p = .857). Each additional win is estimated to raise an underdog's intention to transgress by 4.8%, and each additional loss to raise a favorite's by 7.7%.
- Study 2 (Poisson, n = 12,694) replicates Hypothesis 1 with yellow cards. There was no main effect of winning streaks (b = .00, SE = .01, p = .710) or losing streaks (b = -.00, SE = .01, p = .724), but a significant main effect of standing under which underdogs were more likely to transgress (b = -.78, SE = .13, p < .001). Winning streak x standing was significant (b = -.07, SE = .03, p = .008): positive for underdogs (b = .02, SE = .01, p = .039) and null for favorites (b = -.00, SE = .01, p = .692). Losing streak x standing was also significant (b = .13, SE = .05, p = .014): positive for favorites (b = .04, SE = .02, p = .020) and null for underdogs (b = -.01, SE = .01, p = .173). One additional win raised an underdog team's likelihood of a yellow card by about 4%; one additional loss raised a favorite's by about 5%.
- Study 3 (n = 332) does not support Hypothesis 1 at the conventional threshold, but supports the mediation hypotheses. The streak x standing interaction on willingness to coach the witness was only marginally significant (F[1, 328] = 3.17, p = .076, eta-squared = .01), and the paper states that Hypothesis 1 was not supported using the traditional threshold of p < .05; neither planned contrast reached significance (underdogs: winning M = 2.65, SD = 1.45 vs. losing M = 2.31, SD = 1.20, t[328] = 1.61, p = .108; favorites: losing M = 2.72, SD = 1.26 vs. winning M = 2.53, SD = 1.48, t[328] = .90, p = .367), and the interaction crossed p < .05 when covariates were added (standing legitimacy, or the desire to prove others wrong and to avoid embarrassment) and also in the supplemental analysis that fully followed the preregistered exclusion plan (F[1, 311] = 4.03, p = .046). Hypothesis 2 was supported: streaks and standing interacted on outcome uncertainty (F[1, 328] = 16.35, p < .001), with a positive indirect effect for underdogs (effect = .07, SE = .04, 95% CI [.003, .164]) and a negative one for favorites (effect = -.08, SE = .05, 95% CI [-.186, -.004]); index of moderated mediation = -.15, SE = .08, 95% CI [-.325, -.011]. Hypothesis 3a was supported (underdogs, serially via outcome uncertainty and excitement: effect = .02, SE = .01, 95% CI [.001, .056]) and Hypothesis 3b was supported (favorites, serially via outcome uncertainty and anxiety: effect = -.04, SE = .02, 95% CI [-.087, -.008]). With winning streak coded 1 and favorite coded 1, the negative favorite-path coefficients mean the losing-streak condition raised transgressions.
- Study 4 (n = 372) supports Hypotheses 1, 2, and 3b but not 3a. The streak x standing interaction on the behavioral misreporting measure was significant (b = -2.24, SE = .72, p = .002): the effect of streaks was positive for underdogs, whose winning-streak condition transgressed more (M = .14, SD = .35 vs. M = .04, SD = .20; b = .47, SE = .23, p = .040), and negative for favorites, whose losing-streak condition transgressed more (M = .24, SD = .43 vs. M = .11, SD = .32; b = -.60, SE = .23, p = .008). Moderated mediation through outcome uncertainty held (index = -.45, SE = .20, 95% CI [-.907, -.110]) even though the underdog indirect effect was non-significant (effect = .12, SE = .12, 95% CI [-.091, .374]) while the favorite one was significant (effect = -.33, SE = .15, 95% CI [-.657, -.083]). Hypothesis 3b was supported for favorites (effect = -.25, SE = .12, 95% CI [-.520, -.066]); Hypothesis 3a was not supported for underdogs (effect = .03, SE = .03, 95% CI [-.019, .105]), although it was directionally consistent. Standing also had a significant main effect here, with favorites transgressing more than underdogs (M = .18, SD = .39 vs. M = .09, SD = .29; b = .76, SE = .32, p = .017) — the opposite sign to the standing main effect in Study 2.
- Support is therefore uneven across the four studies: Hypothesis 1 was supported in Studies 1, 2, and 4 but only marginal in Study 3; Hypothesis 2 was supported in Studies 3 and 4; Hypothesis 3a (the underdog excitement path) was supported only in Study 3; Hypothesis 3b (the favorite anxiety path) was supported in Studies 3 and 4.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops a temporally embedded model of competition that connects past competitive outcomes and future expectations, which prior work largely studied in isolation, showing that any single contest is encoded within a larger "event schemata." It deepens understanding of underdog/favorite psychology by shifting attention from motivation to cognitive (outcome uncertainty) and affective (excitement, anxiety) mechanisms, and identifies two previously undocumented competitive conditions — favorites on losing streaks and underdogs on winning streaks — that generate enough uncertainty to trigger transgressive behavior.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should be cautious about introducing or sustaining workplace competitions, because under certain configurations of past performance and standing their deleterious effects can outweigh motivational benefits. Common exhortations to "put the past behind them" or "not get ahead of ourselves" are not equally useful in all situations; managers should attend to the interplay between an actor's competitive past and future expectations, intervening especially when situations arouse uncertainty, excitement, or anxiety.

**Limitations**
Study 1 is subject to common-method bias because all variables were assessed by the same rater at the same time. The theory operationalizes competitive standing as binary (one underdog, one favorite), which does not capture competitions with many players or varying degrees of underdog/favorite status. The focus on streaks as the sole element of the competitive past does not fully represent the broader claim that "the past matters," since non-coherent past outcomes may not shape mental projections in the same way.

**Future Research**
Future work should move beyond the binary conceptualization of standing to examine whether the psychology and behavior of underdogs and favorites differ by the magnitude of standing or the number of competitors. Researchers could explore other factors beyond streaks — such as rivalry-based wins/losses or carryover from prior seasons — that contribute to momentum and mental projection. Scholars should also study conditions that promote good (rather than bad) competitive behavior, and collect multi-wave, multi-source data to address common-method concerns.
