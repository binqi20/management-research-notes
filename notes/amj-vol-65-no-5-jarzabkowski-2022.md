# Enabling Rapid Financial Response to Disasters: Knotting and Reknotting Multiple Paradoxes in Interorganizational Systems

**Abstract**
While market-based solutions are increasingly being proposed to address major societal and development issues, they are also often considered antithetical to issues such as climate change, poverty alleviation, and disaster response. In particular, the interorganizational systems involved in such market solutions give rise to multiple contradictory tensions, known as paradoxes. We, therefore, adopt a paradox lens to explain the dynamics through which different actors within these systems navigate the contradictions that are generated. Drawing on a global qualitative study of multi-country risk pools that provide rapid capital in the immediate aftermath of a disaster, we advance paradox theory by showing how organizational actors' interactions (a) maintain equilibrium by generating mutually reinforcing balance among paradoxes, while (b) the clustering of poles from different paradoxes generates disequilibrium, and (c) the reknotting of poles from different paradoxes restores equilibrium. As our process framework shows, these dynamics form an iterative cycle between equilibrium and disequilibrium that is essential in enhancing the promise of market-based solutions to address development issues, which in our study refers to increasing the rapid availability of capital to respond to disasters such as hurricanes, earthquakes, and droughts.

**Research Question**
How does the knotting of multiple paradoxes enable, or undermine, dynamic equilibrium within interorganizational systems? The authors ask how coexisting paradoxes are entangled, shape each other, and are managed by actors interacting within an interorganizational system.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: three knotted paradoxes — market–development (Smith & Besharov, 2019), part–whole (Poole & Van de Ven, 1989), and temporal short–long-term (Slawinski & Bansal, 2015) — co-occurring as an inseparably entangled "paradox knot."
- Process / phases: an iterative cycle between equilibrium (paradoxes balanced and knotted) and disequilibrium (knot unravels), interrupted by "critical incidents" (annual renewals; disasters).
- Knotting practices: four interactions (training on the solution, reviewing/refining the solution, collaborating across stakeholders, communicating the solution) that maintain mutually reinforcing balance.
- Triggering conditions: "clustering" of the short-term and part poles from two different paradoxes, which de-emphasizes the long-term and whole poles and unravels the knot, generating disequilibrium.
- Reknotting practices: six interactions binding the market pole, and separately the development pole, to the clustered short-term and part poles (e.g., running scenarios, developing new products, funding countries' projects, working around product triggers).
- Emergent outcomes: reknotting indirectly restores balance across all three paradoxes, reinstates equilibrium, and strengthens the interorganizational solution over time.

The central process is that organizational actors knot multiple paradoxes into a mutually reinforcing balance to keep the interorganizational system viable; when a critical incident causes specific poles to cluster, the knot unravels into disequilibrium; rather than reasserting the neglected pole, actors reknot the dominant poles to a third paradox's poles, which indirectly rebalances the original paradoxes. Iterating between knotting and reknotting is a generative dynamic that not only restores equilibrium but refines and embeds new initiatives that strengthen the solution.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: a longitudinal, multi-sited global qualitative study that treats the three main multi-country risk pools then in existence as the focal interorganizational system, following the disaster-response solution as it manifested across multiple sites; all authors were immersed in the field, and fieldwork was both retrospective (processes and incidents preceding 2016) and real-time as the pools kept evolving over the four years of data collection (2016-2020).
- Data corpus (triangulated across actors and sources): 135 audiotaped and transcribed interviews with 160 individuals — 34 across the three NAOs, 21 with low-income-country actors (ministers, senior government officials, country development liaison officials), 41 with development actors (development banks and donor countries), and 39 with market actors (reinsurers, brokers, modelers); observations of 31 field-configuring events (conferences, workshops, meetings) each lasting from an hour to three days, audiotaped with time-marked fieldnotes, plus attendance at a World Bank and donor-country disaster risk financing course for 35 government officials from 24 different countries; and 285 documents (development reports, country reports, annual reports, pool communications) used to assemble pool histories, check facts, and validate the retrospective parts of the interview data.
- How the constructs were operationalized (six coding steps): (1) detailed Excel chronologies mapping disasters, payouts, countries joining and leaving at each annual renewal, new initiatives, and arising issues, written up as thick chronological descriptions; (2) open coding in NVivo, combining an emic reading across the data set with participants' own etic accounts of tension, which surfaced three paradoxes labeled short- versus long-term, part versus whole, and market versus development, and identified their entanglement as a "paradox knot"; (3) identification of "critical incidents" (annual product renewals; disasters) and of the iterative equilibrium-disequilibrium cycle — segmenting the data into pool "cases" and into chronological phases "did not offer any explanatory variation," so the cycle became the analytic frame; (4) coding of specific interactions between interorganizational actors, grouped into 10 broader interactions (first- and second-order codes, Table 2); (5) thematic interpretation splitting those 10 into 4 knotting interactions (A1-A4) and 6 reknotting interactions (B1-B3 binding the market pole and C1-C3 binding the development pole to the clustered poles); (6) analysis of the implications of knotting and reknotting for strengthening the solution.
- Identification strategy: none in the causal-inference sense. This is an inductive, interpretive process study with no variables, measures, or statistical tests; the claims are interpretive pattern claims about processes, iterated between data and paradox theory, not estimated effects.
- Analytical quality checks: validating the emerging account with participants through an industry report presented at industry events; team devil's-advocate discussion and memo sharing; involving a research assistant in the NVivo coding who did not know the context, did not collect the data, and was not a paradox theorist; using multiple data sources; and returning to the field after the initial analysis to test whether the emerging patterns held across the data set and whether alternative patterns or explanations were at play.

**Key Findings**
- Three entangled paradoxes are surfaced in the risk-pool system — market-development, part-whole, and temporal (short-long-term) — and the paper finds they cannot be balanced in isolation: continuous interactions that knot the three individually balanced paradoxes are what maintain the pool's viability.
- Maintaining equilibrium: four knotting interactions generate a mutually reinforcing balance across the three paradoxes — training on the interorganizational solution (A1), reviewing and refining elements of the existing solution (A2), collaborating across stakeholders (A3), and communicating the solution (A4). Equilibrium is never secure; knotting operates against an ever-present threat of disequilibrium, because the annual renewal means countries can easily leave.
- Generating disequilibrium: at critical incidents (annual renewals and disasters) the short-term and part poles "cluster" — they become dominant and tightly entwined while the long-term and whole poles are de-emphasized. Clustering unbalances the temporal and part-whole paradoxes and unravels their knot with the market-development paradox, which also becomes unbalanced as countries read varied payouts as a failure to deliver on their development needs and threaten to leave. In the Hurricane Harper vignette, Country A's product paid out several million U.S. dollars, Country B received a higher payout despite apparently less severe effects, and three other severely affected countries received none — all contractually consistent with the cover each had bought, and reassuring to market actors, but a trigger for country consternation.
- Restoring equilibrium: actors do not reassert the neglected whole or long-term poles. They reknot the market pole and, separately, the development pole to the already-dominant short-term and part poles, further emphasizing those poles — running different scenarios (B1), agreeing to develop new elements to the solution (B2), and expanding the solution's appeal to new interorganizational actors (B3) for the market pole; funding interorganizational actors' projects (C1), working around the solution's conditions to provide benefits (C2), and supporting interorganizational actors' membership in the solution (C3) for the development pole.
- Pattern of occurrence across critical incidents: all six reknotting interactions were found across the set of critical incidents, and at least one market-pole and one development-pole reknotting interaction occurred in every critical incident — but the authors state explicitly that not all six unfolded in response to every critical incident, so the pattern is partial rather than uniform.
- The rebalancing is indirect: because reknotting addresses the demands that made the clustered poles dominant, those poles cease to cluster, which regenerates balance within the part-whole and temporal paradoxes and thereby restores the mutually reinforcing knotting of all three paradoxes and equilibrium.
- Disequilibrium is characterized concretely by three features: imbalance within individual paradoxes inside the knot (short-term and part dominating at the expense of long-term and whole); unraveling across the knot as the dominant poles impede mutually reinforcing knotting; and a specific form of chaos, namely a threat to the viability of the interorganizational system, with vital stakeholders questioning their ongoing participation.
- The iteration is generative, not merely restorative: initiatives raised during reknotting are carried forward and embedded into the routine knotting interactions, refining and strengthening the solution over time — new products (excess rainfall, drought, agricultural and fisheries), improved and new models for evaluating and planning for different disaster risks, more resilient infrastructure, and improved data and data sharing across regions. After Hurricane Harper, running modeled scenarios led some countries to stay in the pool at the next renewal with the same cover because it met their needs, while others renewed and increased their cover — an NAO informant estimated Country C went up "closer to 15%" and Country A "around 5-10%"; funding specific disaster risk management projects (a seawall in Country C, plus grants to two more countries) met immediate development needs without drawing on the insurance products.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the concept of paradox knots (Sheep et al., 2017) from discursive framing to the interactional realm by specifying the social-interaction processes through which knotting and reknotting occur. It introduces "clustering" of individual poles as an explanatory mechanism for how paradox knots unravel into disequilibrium, and theorizes "reknotting across specific poles" as a fundamentally new response to paradox (distinct from reasserting the suppressed pole). It further characterizes disequilibrium concretely and positions the iterative cycle of equilibrium–disequilibrium as a generative dynamic for sustaining interorganizational systems addressing grand challenges.

**Practical Implication**
The study shows that governments of different countries can work together, and with development organizations, to find disaster-response solutions that satisfy both their own partial interests and wider regional ones. Inevitable tensions and lapses into disequilibrium need not destroy such interorganizational solutions; rather, solutions can be incrementally refined and strengthened through constant interactions to knot and reknot paradoxes as actors learn more about the challenges and how their shared purpose can evolve.

**Limitations**
The longitudinal study, based on intensive personal engagement, documented the dynamics of paradox knotting and disequilibrium only over the relatively short period of the authors' engagement. Not all evolutionary paths may be as benign as those observed, and the study examined a context with three specific entangled paradoxes (market–development, part–whole, temporal), so findings may not generalize to other configurations of paradox multiplicity.

**Future Research**
Future research might examine whether the specific clustering of short-term and partial interests is a feature of interorganizational systems where actors can privilege immediate over collective long-term interests, and study paradox knotting within varied types of interorganizational systems (e.g., networks around a central organization, project-based alliances, supply chains) that may exhibit different dynamics. Longer-term historical studies could reveal how these dynamics expand, shift, or reverse, and how variation in paradox multiplicity shapes knotting and its relationship with (dis)equilibrium, including within (not only across) organizations.
