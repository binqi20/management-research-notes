# Going Viral or Growing Like an Oak Tree? Towards Sustainable Local Development Through Entrepreneurship

**Abstract**
Nurturing venture capital–backed, high-growth entrepreneurship has been strongly promoted as an effective means to achieve local development in impoverished places. Yet, growing evidence has suggested that, despite its notable successes in resource-rich regions, this approach creates limited impact in economically challenged locales. We address this conundrum by calling into question the crux of high-growth entrepreneurship—the pursuit of quick scaling through venture capital financing. Our field research in two entrepreneurship-nurturing organizations in Detroit reveals important heterogeneity in resourcing modes and venture growth in time and space. Specifically, we find that ventures developed through different modes of resourcing (financing vs. local bricolage) grow at different spatiotemporal scales (scaling up toward fast geographical expansion vs. scaling deep toward locally anchored endurance), and consequently generate distinctive yet complementary contributions to their depleted place of origin. Unlike scaling-up ventures whose local impact was explosive yet short-lived, scaling-deep ventures created jobs, products or services, and spillover effects that stayed local and addressed specific local problems. Building on these findings, we challenge the exclusive pursuit of high-growth entrepreneurship for poverty alleviation and suggest that entrepreneurship-driven local development requires cohabitation of ventures growing at varying scales.

**Research Question**
How are different resourcing modes and the scale of venture growth related to the sustainable development of impoverished locales? The study problematizes the prevalent assumption that venture-capital-backed, high-growth entrepreneurship is the most viable path to poverty alleviation, asking what it is about ventures that "deviate from the successful template" on resourcing and scale that makes them more promising in economically challenged places.

**Mechanism Process**
- Modes of resourcing (the primary driver): *Financing* (securing external venture capital/equity) at ACCEL vs. *local bricolage* (making do by recombining locally available resources) at GREEN.
- Spatiotemporal scale of venture growth (the focal construct): *Scaling up* — "the process whereby an organization pursues fast expansion" (quick, broad geographic coverage) vs. *scaling deep* — "a process whereby an organization pursues enduring growth anchored to its original location" (slow, locally rooted).
- Process / phases (idea-development ethnography): Open coding → identifying "time–space idea codes" → analyzing key interaction scenes that infuse spatiotemporal orientations → tracing actual venture growth and contributions via archival data.
- Triggering conditions: At ACCEL the imperative to win investor funding bred a "pressure for speed" (the time value of money / "speed trap"), which translated into a "spatial mandate for expansion"; at GREEN the focus on repurposing local assets deepened ventures' spatial anchors to Detroit and extended their temporal horizon.
- Emergent outcomes (contributions): Scaling-up ventures produced explosive but impermanent local impact (jobs and value that left Detroit through relocation/acquisition); scaling-deep ventures produced enduring, locally customized contributions to Detroit's economic, social, and environmental problems, with complementarity between the two types.

The paper theorizes a self-reinforcing coupling between resourcing and scale: financing places a venture in the rigid timeframe of venture capital, where speed begets a mandate for geographic expansion and pivoting toward universal, readily exploitable demands, which in turn requires still more financing—locking the venture onto a scaling-up trajectory. Local bricolage, by contrast, confines a venture's spatial scope to a particular place and requires slow accumulation of social capital and deep relationships, locking it onto a scaling-deep trajectory whose growth is "inextricably tied to the region's restoration." Because sustainable local development requires contributions that stay local across generations, the authors argue that vigorous revitalization requires the cohabitation of ventures growing at diverse spatiotemporal scales.

**Data & Measures**
Two-stage inductive design on a single comparative field site (Detroit); the study is a processual ethnography plus archival tracing, and the two organizations are compared as contrasting cases — there is no statistical identification strategy, so the link from resourcing to growth is established interpretively rather than estimated. Stage 1, ethnography of four ventures: the first author observed idea development at ACCEL and GREEN for 22 months (July 2012–April 2014), travelling to Detroit at least two days per week and observing full-time for seven of those months (September 2013–March 2014). He followed all idea-development meetings for four ventures chosen "to ensure comparability across contexts" — Medicine Pocket and Dog Pound at GREEN, Wait Less and Boutique Buy at ACCEL — yielding 148 one- to five-hour meetings or events observed and 67 one- to two-hour interviews with founders, mentors, and community participants (Table 1). Audio-recording of idea-development meetings was not permitted, so meetings were captured as pseudo-transcript field notes; interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed. The second author joined in 2015 and took part in follow-up visits, interviews, and archival collection through 2020. Stage 2, archival tracing of all 27 ventures: coverage was expanded to every venture developed in the two organizations during the fieldwork window (19 at ACCEL, 8 at GREEN), built on 620 news articles (about 23 per venture) retrieved by company name from Factiva and LexisNexis and supplemented by company websites and social media, collected until June 2020. Constructs were coded rather than measured. Phase 1 open-coded all field notes and interview transcripts into two families — "approach codes" capturing each organization's idea-development approach and "idea codes" capturing elements of the venture idea (on average 84 idea codes per venture) — and produced one spreadsheet timeline per venture (average 558 rows) recording when each idea code was added, retained, or discarded. Phase 2 recategorized idea codes to isolate "time–space idea codes" and traced "idea code chains" back to the codes that generated them. Phase 3 turned field notes into vignettes and process narratives of the interaction scenes in which spatiotemporal orientations were infused. Phase 4 built, for each of the 27 ventures, a timeline of key growth events (founding, fundraising, launch or expansion of products or services, recognition, relocation, closure or acquisition), summarized in Table 4 (time to go beyond Detroit; time to close, relocate, or be acquired) and Table 5 (jobs at peak, local jobs as of 2020, global impact, local impact). Pseudonyms are used for all organizations and individuals.

**Key Findings**
The emergent model (Figure 4) links resourcing mode to the spatiotemporal scale of growth and then to local contributions: financing at ACCEL produced scaling up and explosive but short-lived local impact, while local bricolage at GREEN produced scaling deep and locally focused, enduring impact. In the ethnography of four ventures, both ACCEL ventures moved away from locally specific ideas — anticipated investor preference led Robin to relax her exclusive focus on brick-and-mortar retailers, then drop inventory management and finally the "hold-in-store" feature, and Jack pivoted Wait Less from Detroit restaurants and bars to national sports and entertainment venues — and both revised models required more investment, so financing and fast/broad expansion reinforced each other. The two GREEN ventures moved the opposite way: ecosystem mapping and "deep root" reflection thickened their Detroit anchors and lengthened their time horizons (Amanda added a two-year certification with no revenue; Dog Pound's final model comprised six service types for local clients; Medicine Pocket became a design-process facilitator on local eldercare issues). In the archival tracing of all 27 ventures, most ACCEL ventures began serving customers beyond the local market within 13 months on average (range 0–36 months), and only 4 of the 19 ACCEL ventures were still operating in Detroit as of 2020; four were acquired after less than four years on average, all but one by acquirers based outside Detroit. Of GREEN's eight ventures, none grew operations beyond Detroit or raised venture capital investment, and six were still operating in Detroit in 2020 — 75% versus 16% for ACCEL. Contributions ran in the same direction. ACCEL ventures created 125 jobs at peak but only 24% remained in Detroit six years out (30 local jobs in 2020); GREEN ventures created fewer jobs (18) and more gradually, but 83% of them survived the first six years (15 local jobs in 2020), and their indirect effect exceeds the direct count — Good Food Network's member businesses collectively created more than 250 jobs in the city. ACCEL products and services served broad geographies (Wait Less: 16 million users from 924 events; GroupOrder: more than 10,000 orders from 7,300 users across 250 cities in 32 states; Solar Design: 32 U.S. states and six countries) while leaving many Detroit-specific needs unaddressed, whereas GREEN ventures addressed Detroit-specific problems in locally customized ways (DetroiTires removed more than 12,000 waste tires between 2013 and 2020; Healthy Corner sold 110,000 servings of healthy food, $263,000 in sales, through 87 partner locations; Dog Pound campaigned successfully for a Detroit anti-tethering ordinance). Spillovers followed the same scale logic: ACCEL knowledge spillover was hardly confined to Detroit (ProVideo's technology was absorbed into a Seattle-based platform; Rentals Online's cofounders carried four years of accumulated knowledge to a San Francisco–based former competitor), while GREEN's local bricolage developed the productive capabilities of local partners such as urban farmers, the farmers' market, and churches. COVID-19 responses reproduced the contrast — Healthy Corner repurposed its delivery capacity for Detroit's frontline medical professionals, while Wait Less built crowd-management solutions for large venues everywhere. The difference was not prior local commitment: Boutique Buy (ACCEL) started with a strong commitment to Detroit brick-and-mortar boutiques and Medicine Pocket (GREEN) started with an idea that could have scaled globally quickly. The authors label the evidence of complementarity preliminary — Healthy Corner catered to scaling-up ventures in downtown Detroit and used the proceeds to subsidize its operations in poverty-stricken neighborhoods — raising the possibility that scaling-deep ventures can capture and redistribute some of the short-lived local wealth scaling-up ventures create.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study's central innovation is to bring the time–space (scale) lens into entrepreneurship, reconceptualizing scaling not as rapid growth in size but as growth that "unfolds at a particular spatiotemporal scale," thereby distinguishing scaling up from a previously undertheorized scaling deep. It extends entrepreneurial bricolage from the individual to the place-based community level ("local bricolage"), showing bricolage is a complementary alternative—not a marginal substitute—to venture capital, and it advances venture-growth research by foregrounding "how" (not just "how much") ventures grow. It also surfaces an inward, identity-based microfoundation of bricolage: founders' deep reflection on their own "deep root" enables them to perceive what local resources can be repurposed.

**Practical Implication**
The findings caution policymakers and ecosystem builders that the prevalent focus on infusing venture capital can obscure a more important need—nurturing entrepreneurial imagination to unlock the latent value of existing local assets in seemingly "barren" landscapes. Scaling-deep ventures can do the critical work of lifting impoverished locales toward self-reliance and can capture and redistribute wealth created by scaling-up ventures, so revitalization strategies should support both types of ventures rather than betting exclusively on high-growth "gazelles."

**Limitations**
The authors acknowledge it is reasonable to ask whether the scaling-up dynamics they observe stem from the peculiarity of venture capital specifically, rather than from financing as a general mode of resourcing (they argue financing's risk-return logic generally intensifies pressure for quick growth). To preserve focus, they also chose not to examine other salient patterns in the data—most notably the dynamics between incubating contexts and participating entrepreneurs (the relative roles of treatment vs. selection effects).

**Future Research**
The authors call for research into alternative venture-financing vehicles—such as "patient capital" and "slow money"—and how they shape invested ventures' scale of growth and local impact; into how individual entrepreneurs' varied responses to incubating-context influence imprint different growth patterns; and into how ventures growing at different scales build connections and create synergies, and what kinds of local ecosystems facilitate such complementary collaborations.
