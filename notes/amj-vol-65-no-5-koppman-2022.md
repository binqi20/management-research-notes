# Overcoming Conflict Between Symmetric Occupations: How “Creatives” and “Suits” Use Gender Ordering in Advertising

**Abstract**
In knowledge-based organizations, conflict among interdependent occupations can be exacerbated by the absence of a clear hierarchical ordering of these occupations within the organization. Moreover, given women’s inroads into some traditionally maledominated occupations but not others, these workplaces are increasingly horizontally gender segregated. In this paper, we study how members of these symmetric and segregated occupations manage conflict in U.S. advertising agencies through the case of relationships between “creatives” (copywriters, designers, and creative directors) and “suits” or account practitioners (account executives, strategists, and managers). Creatives and suits are at the same organizational level in their agencies. While creatives are primarily men, suits, traditionally also men, are now primarily women. Drawing on participant observation in five different U.S. advertising agencies and over 100 interviews, we show how creatives and account practitioners use gender ordering to overcome jurisdictional conflict. These practices are grounded in enacting essentialist gender differences that transform symmetric occupational relationships into hierarchical ones by embedding the gender hierarchy. We find that, while gender ordering helps women and men in cross-occupational pairs get work done, it also reinforces women’s disadvantage, because, for women, it involves low-status and emotionally taxing scut work that it does not involve for men.

**Research Question**
How do members of horizontally gender-segregated, symmetric (same-level) occupations manage jurisdictional conflict when relative rank is ambiguous and no formal hierarchy orders them? Specifically, how do female-dominated account practitioners and male-dominated creatives in U.S. advertising agencies overcome occupational conflict?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: "gender ordering" (enacting essentialist gender roles/stereotypes that import an informal gender status hierarchy into cross-occupational relationships); jurisdictional conflict over legitimacy, authority, and knowledge claims between symmetric occupations; "scut work" (low-status, menial, emotionally taxing work that does not draw on occupational knowledge).
- Sites of conflict: agency–client interactions (legitimacy), revisions (authority), and concept development (knowledge).
- Practices (female account–male creative pairs): protecting (mediating, buffering) to resolve legitimacy conflict; nurturing (conceptual, physical) to resolve authority conflict; counseling/preparing to resolve knowledge conflict.
- Comparison conditions: minority cross-gender pairs (male account–female creatives) use gender ordering via "salesman"/"men are good at math" framings but without performing scut work; same-gender pairs use "occupational entrenchment" (dominance or reasoning), which usually fails to resolve conflict.
- Emergent outcome: creatives acquiesce; women secure cooperation but cede status.

Through gender ordering, cross-gender pairs convert competition between similarly ranked occupations into a hierarchical relationship by enacting taken-for-granted gender status beliefs (Ridgeway, 2011), so women cede status to men in exchange for cooperation. Because gender carries shared expectations agreed upon by both groups, it supplies an alternative informal hierarchy that resolves disagreements over whose work is more valuable, who may direct work, and whose knowledge is most relevant. For women, however, eliciting this cooperation requires performing emotionally taxing scut work (mothering, buffering, bringing food) that men in the same occupation do not perform, reproducing the gender hierarchy within the everyday functioning of the organization.

**Data & Measures**
One inductive, interpretive study that pools two independently conducted qualitative projects on U.S. advertising agencies (Table 1); the two projects are described separately here because their sites, samples, and years differ. Project 1 (first author) was a mixed methods study focused on hiring creative people and creative work: one field site, Quality Solutions Co., a mid-sized (11–50 employees) Southwest agency, with four months of participant observation at three days per week in 2011, plus 52 semi-structured interviews recruited through a survey sent to a random sample of U.S. agencies (42 agencies total: 31% small, 45% mid-sized, 24% large), using a schedule modeled on protocols for studying boundaries between scientific disciplines and advertising agency departments; 51 of 52 interviewees were white, 49 of 52 college educated, 29 of 52 male, average interview length 30 minutes, data collection years 2012–2014. Project 2 (third author) was a multi-sited ethnography focused on meaning-making practices in creative organizations: four field sites — Pioneer (Northeast, mid-sized, three months at one day per week, 2013), Alexander & Sons (Midwest, mid-sized, three months at five days per week, 2013), CultureShock-Mountain (Mountain region, large, over 50 employees, three months at five days per week, 2014), and CultureShock-Pacific (West Coast, mid-sized, three months at five days per week, 2017) — plus 66 interviews recruited from those field sites (12, 23, 24, and 7 respectively) using an open-ended schedule covering day-to-day experiences, work history, and the industry; 63 of 66 interviewees were white, 62 of 66 college educated with the remaining four enrolled in college, 34 of 66 male, average interview length 70 minutes, data collection years 2013–2017. Pooled, the two projects yield 16 months of participant observation at five U.S. agencies and 116 interviews, supplemented by proposals, training materials, and advertising materials from the field sites; all interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Constructs were operationalized through inductive multi-stage coding (Corbin & Strauss, 1990; Miles & Huberman, 1994). All examples of cross-occupational disagreement observed or referenced in interviews were labeled "occupational conflict" and coded on four dimensions: cross-occupational type (four gender pairings — male creative/female account, male/male, female creative/male account, female/female), the type of conflict, the way conflict was managed, and the resolution, i.e., which occupation acquiesced. 129 instances had sufficient data to code on all four criteria; instances where the management practice could not be clearly and systematically identified were eliminated, leaving 113 analysed instances from which three primary areas of conflict and three broad management practices were derived. Acquiescence was operationalized as ceding "manifest authority" — the capacity to successfully order another's actions (Wrong, 1979: 35) — requiring one party to acknowledge, accept, or obey the other in practice. One supplementary quantitative comparison drew on survey data the first author collected from creatives and account practitioners (n = 196) at a probability sample of U.S. agencies, controlling for agency rank, size, and prestige as well as extent of client interaction and education. The design is inductive and interpretive; the paper makes no causal identification claim, and the frequency tables report descriptive counts of coded instances rather than statistical tests.

**Key Findings**
Jurisdictional conflict arose mainly in three areas of the advertising development process that neither occupation fully controlled: agency–client interactions (competing legitimacy claims about what counts as "good" advertising), revisions (competing authority claims about who may direct work), and concept development (competing knowledge claims about whose knowledge is most relevant). Across the 113 coded instances, creatives acquiesced in 75 conflicts, account practitioners acquiesced in 22, and neither acquiesced in 16. Table 3 shows gender ordering as the modal practice (69 of 113 instances), concentrated in cross-gender pairs — 50 in female account–male creative pairs and 12 in male account–female creative pairs, against 5 in both-male and 2 in both-female pairs — while occupational entrenchment through dominance (25 instances) clustered in both-male pairs (17) and entrenchment through reasoning (19 instances) clustered in both-female pairs (13). In the same table, creatives acquiesced in 62 of the 69 gender-ordering instances, compared with 5 of 25 dominance instances and 6 of 19 reasoning instances; these are descriptive counts, not statistical tests.

In majority cross-gender pairs (female account practitioners with male creatives), gender ordering took three forms mapped onto the three conflict sites (Table 5): protecting through mediating and buffering resolved legitimacy conflict, with male creatives then acting more professionally during client interactions and letting these women handle the agency–client relationship; conceptual and physical nurturing resolved authority conflict, with male creatives then considering account practitioners' directions during revisions; and counseling through "preparing" resolved knowledge conflict, with male creatives then integrating account practitioners' suggestions into concepts. In each form the women performed scut work — low-status, menial, or emotionally taxing work that does not draw on occupational knowledge — cloaked in essentialist feminine roles (mother, wife, babysitter, cheerleader); this secured cooperation but ceded status and did not advance their occupational claims. In minority cross-gender pairs (male account practitioners with female creatives) gender ordering was similarly effective at eliciting cooperation but worked differently: these men invoked "salesman" and leader roles and the "men are good at math" stereotype, which reinforced rather than ceded their occupational claims and did not involve scut work. Table 4 records 61 female account–male creative examples of which 56 involved scut work, against 17 male account–female creative examples of which only 1 did. Same-gender pairs relied on occupational entrenchment, which usually left conflict simmering or ended with account practitioners acquiescing.

The supplementary survey analysis (n = 196), controlling for agency rank, size, and prestige as well as extent of client interaction and education, found male account practitioners significantly more likely than female ones to report finding their job emotionally fulfilling; the paper states these results are available upon request rather than tabulating them. The authors also state that, given the nature of their data, they cannot establish whether men's use of gender stereotypes is explicit, unconscious, statistical, or institutional discrimination.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends the literature on managing occupational conflict beyond asymmetric occupations to show how symmetric, horizontally gender-segregated occupations resolve jurisdictional conflict through gender ordering. It reconceptualizes the role of demography in occupational conflict: whereas race-, nationality-, or age-based differences aligned with occupational differences exacerbate conflict, shared status beliefs about gender can instead facilitate cross-occupational cooperation. It also identifies cross-occupational relationships as a previously unrecognized source of women’s disadvantage, since gender ordering embeds the gender hierarchy through women’s scut work.

**Practical Implication**
The findings surface a hidden cost borne by women in mixed-gender, interdependent professional work: securing cooperation often requires low-status, emotionally taxing scut work that does not advance their occupational claims or status. The authors frame this as a double-edged sword: enacting gender differences promotes cross-occupational cooperation in cross-gender pairs, but does so by importing the gender hierarchy, which gives an edge to men and their occupations — so what appears to be an inroad for women does not guarantee equal footing.

**Limitations**
Most coded examples are drawn from interviews rather than direct observation of paired interactions, and the study lacks dyadic-level data with both members of each dyad, limiting triangulation of situations and dynamics. The studied occupations are uniformly white, college educated, and U.S. born, which constrains the ability to observe demographic similarity-based mechanisms and limits generalizability.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine gender ordering in other symmetric, gender-segregated occupational contexts (such as engineering or architecture) to assess how it operates elsewhere, and should ideally collect dyadic-level data with interviews from both members of a dyad to triangulate descriptions of situations and dynamics.
