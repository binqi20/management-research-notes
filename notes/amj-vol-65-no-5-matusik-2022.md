# The Highs and Lows of Hierarchy in Multiteam Systems

**Abstract**
Multiteam system research operates under the assumption that multiteam systems should be structured hierarchically, or consist of component teams nested under a formal, centralized leadership team that oversees and orchestrates system activities. Although multiteam system research has certainly provided evidence suggestive of the merits of more hierarchical (vs. egalitarian) structures, we take a more balanced view and argue that hierarchy both facilitates and impedes multiteam system success. Using an experimental study and a sample of 76 multiteam systems observed over three performance episodes, we provide evidence that structural hierarchy (a) facilitates multiteam system learning by mitigating cognitive depletion among members, but (b) undermines multiteam system learning via its deleterious effects on horizontal coordination. Critically, however, we find that the benefits of hierarchy (i.e., reduced cognitive depletion) wane over performance episodes. This investigation underscores the important role that different hierarchical arrangements play in multiteam system functioning. Although a majority of multiteam system researchers have utilized hierarchical structures—which indeed have their virtues—we illustrate that egalitarian structures have key advantages as well.

**Research Question**
How, why, and when do different hierarchical arrangements (hierarchy vs. egalitarianism) affect multiteam system operations? The paper asks whether hierarchy is simply beneficial or detrimental, or whether it simultaneously facilitates and impedes multiteam system success through distinct mechanisms (horizontal coordination and member cognitive depletion) that evolve over performance episodes.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: Hierarchical multiteam systems are characterized by lower levels of horizontal coordination than egalitarian multiteam systems.
H2: Horizontal coordination (a) relates positively to multiteam system learning and (b) mediates the negative indirect effect of hierarchy (vs. egalitarianism) on multiteam system learning.
H3: Hierarchical multiteam systems are characterized by lower levels of cognitive depletion among members than egalitarian multiteam systems.
H4: Cognitive depletion (a) relates negatively to multiteam system learning and (b) mediates the positive indirect effect of hierarchy (vs. egalitarianism) on multiteam system learning.
H5: Hierarchical arrangement interacts with performance episode, such that differences in horizontal coordination between hierarchical and egalitarian multiteam systems increase over episodes.
H6: Hierarchical arrangement interacts with performance episode, such that differences in cognitive depletion between hierarchical and egalitarian multiteam systems decrease over episodes.
H7: Multiteam system learning (a) relates positively to multiteam system performance, (b) mediates the negative indirect effect of hierarchy on multiteam system performance (via horizontal coordination), and (c) mediates the positive indirect effect of hierarchy on multiteam system performance (via cognitive depletion).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Hierarchical arrangement (1 = hierarchical, 0 = egalitarian), manipulated at the multiteam-system level.
- DV(s): Multiteam system learning (proximal) and multiteam system performance (distal).
- Mediators: Horizontal coordination (first-stage; negative path from hierarchy) and member cognitive depletion (first-stage; negative path from hierarchy), with multiteam system learning serving as a second-stage mediator linking the two first-stage mediators to performance.
- Moderators: Performance episode (T1-T3), moderating the hierarchy→coordination and hierarchy→cognitive-depletion relationships.

Hierarchy elicits competition, power struggles, and conflict, leading component teams to focus resources and information inward, which lowers horizontal coordination and thereby impedes learning (a coordination "barrier"). Simultaneously, hierarchy provides structure, order, predictability, and cognitive fluency, reducing the mental effort of navigating the complex multiteam-system environment, which lowers cognitive depletion and thereby facilitates learning (a cognitive "boon"). Because routines become self-reinforcing while members build schemas and mental models over time, the authors predicted that the coordination disadvantage of hierarchy would increase across episodes (H5) whereas its cognitive-depletion advantage would wane once members no longer need hierarchy as a cognitive "crutch" (H6). Only the second prediction held: the coordination gap remained significant at every episode but did not widen, so H5 was not supported.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from a single laboratory experiment embedded in a broader multiteam-system research program, using the Leadership Development Exercise (LDX), a 10-round military-like simulation in which teams place offensive, defensive, and intelligence-collecting assets on a 16 x 16 grid to find and destroy hidden targets. Participants were 1,060 students fulfilling requirements for two sequential entry-level business courses at a U.S. university (average age 18.06; 56.8% identified as male, 68.2% as Caucasian), randomly assigned to 228 four- or five-person teams (average 4.65 students per team) and later assigned to 76 twelve- to fifteen-person multiteam systems of three component teams each. Data collection used a cohort research design (Cook & Campbell, 1979; Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002) spanning five years, and yielded 224 usable multiteam-system performance episodes rather than the 228 implied by 76 systems across three episodes, due to technical and logistical issues. IV (the only Level 2 variable): hierarchical arrangement, coded 1 = hierarchical (44 systems) and 0 = egalitarian (32 systems), manipulated through visual organizational charts, verbal instructions repeated at T1-T3, and LDX design elements — in the hierarchical condition the leadership team had final control over asset placement, whereas in the egalitarian condition the liaison team could only make recommendations; all participants had to pass a role-comprehension check before the simulation launched, and component-team roles were assigned by prior standalone-team performance in both conditions. Horizontal coordination: the total number of "integrated missions" (a point-team asset sent to a grid location that also contains a support-team asset) the system engaged in over the full 10-round simulation, captured in each episode. Cognitive depletion: five items (a 5 .88) from Johnson, Lanaj, and Barnes (2014) on a 5-point Likert-type scale (example item, "Right now, my mental energy is running low"), measured after in-lab instructions but before task execution in each episode and aggregated to the system level by averaging as an additive construct (ICC(1) = .12, ICC(2) = .62). Multiteam system learning: the number of times a system sent support-team assets to their high-accuracy zones within an episode, zones that were unknown at the outset and changed from episode to episode. Multiteam system performance: an objective function of points gained from destroying enemy targets minus points lost to enemy attacks, measured per episode. Moderator: performance episode number, coded 2, 3, or 4 for T1, T2, and T3. Controls: the total number of support-team assets deployed per episode, and the number of times two different support-team assets were sent to the same grid location. The authors describe the design as an experimental study using a cohort research design and attribute the unequal cell sizes (44 hierarchical vs. 32 egalitarian) to it, since enrollment in a given year could not be controlled; no separate causal-identification strategy is invoked beyond the manipulation, and effects are estimated by multilevel path analysis.

**Key Findings**
This is a single experimental study, and the support pattern is mixed. H1 supported: horizontal coordination was significantly lower in hierarchical than in egalitarian multiteam systems (B = -32.90, SE = 9.29, p < .01). H2a and H2b supported: horizontal coordination had a positive effect on multiteam system learning (B = 0.41, SE = 0.15, p < .01) and mediated the negative indirect effect of hierarchy (vs. egalitarianism) on learning (IE = -13.39, 95% CI [-25.34, -3.27]). H3 supported: cognitive depletion was significantly lower in hierarchical than in egalitarian systems (B = -0.54, SE = 0.15, p < .01). H4a and H4b supported: cognitive depletion had a negative effect on learning (B = -23.57, SE = 7.63, p < .01) and mediated the positive indirect effect of hierarchy on learning (IE = 12.73, 95% CI [4.53, 21.25]). H5 not supported: the hierarchical-arrangement-by-episode interaction on horizontal coordination was not significant (B = 5.80, SE = 3.03, p = .06) and the index of moderated mediation included zero (index = 2.36, 95% CI [-0.03, 7.08]); coordination was significantly lower under hierarchy at T1 (difference = 21.71, p < .01), T2 (difference = 14.91, p < .01), and T3 (difference = 10.30, p < .05), but the gap did not widen over episodes. H6 supported: the interaction on cognitive depletion was significant (B = 0.13, SE = 0.05, p < .05) and the index of moderated mediation excluded zero (index = -2.99, 95% CI [-6.36, -0.58]); depletion was significantly lower under hierarchy at T1 (difference = 0.29, p < .01) but the difference was smaller and non-significant at T2 (0.17, p = .07) and T3 (0.06, p = .49). H7a supported: multiteam system learning related positively to multiteam system performance (B = 0.63, SE = 0.16, p < .01). H7b not supported: the serial indirect effect of hierarchy on performance through horizontal coordination and learning included zero (IE = -8.48, 95% CI [-17.78, 0.29]). H7c supported: the serial indirect effect through cognitive depletion and learning excluded zero (IE = 8.06, 95% CI [0.92, 16.73]). Hierarchical arrangement had no direct effect on multiteam system learning (B = 6.680, p = .479) or performance (B = 6.367, p = .633) once indirect effects were accounted for.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study qualifies the dominant assumption in the multiteam-system literature that hierarchy (a strong, centralized leadership team) is superior, by importing the intra-team insight that hierarchy is both functional and dysfunctional and showing it operates at the inter-team level. It elucidates *why* hierarchy matters—egalitarianism aids horizontal coordination while hierarchy attenuates cognitive depletion—and, through a longitudinal design, *when* these opposing effects hold, demonstrating that the cognitive benefit decays while the coordination cost endures over performance episodes.

**Practical Implication**
Because organizations are gravitating toward interdependent systems of teams and more egalitarian structures, managers should match the hierarchical arrangement to task demands: egalitarian designs may be preferable when component-team process interdependence is high (e.g., obstetric labor-and-delivery systems), even if initially more depleting, whereas the cognitive benefits of hierarchy may outweigh egalitarianism's coordination advantage when interdependence is lower (pooled or sequential) or teams are temporally limited (e.g., ad hoc teams).

**Limitations**
The authors examined only power-based hierarchies, not status hierarchies, so generalization to status arrangements is untested. The research used undergraduate students in a laboratory military-like simulation, raising external-validity concerns, and cognitive depletion was measured before (not during) task execution. The multiteam systems also had relatively stable membership and tasks with multi-week gaps between episodes, which may not represent more fluid or back-to-back real-world multiteam systems.

**Future Research**
Future work should investigate status hierarchies in multiteam systems to test whether the present theorizing generalizes beyond power. Researchers should also manipulate the level of task interdependence among component teams—rarely varied despite its theoretical importance—and study multiteam systems with more fluid membership, more variable tasks, and shorter (e.g., back-to-back) intervals between performance episodes to assess boundary conditions on the coordination and cognitive-depletion effects.
