# How Leaders with Divergent Visions Generate Novel Strategy: Navigating the Paradox of Preservation and Modernization in Swiss Watchmaking

**Abstract**
How do leaders with divergent visions for their organization come together to create a novel strategy? This paper employs paradox as a lens to investigate how leader dyads can integrate opposing strategies to produce a new, generative approach. Drawing on a qualitative historical case study of Switzerland's largest watch company during the quartz crisis in Swiss watchmaking, we induce a process model from the activities of two leaders whose relationship embodied the tensions and strategic contradictions of preserving the past and modernizing for the future. The model specifies a set of individual, relational, and structural mechanisms by which leaders productively engage with a "preservation–modernization paradox" to facilitate novel strategy in the wake of a discontinuity. We interpret our findings in terms of the demands of navigating the management and outcomes of strategic paradoxes. While tracing the theoretical and practical implications of our model and our findings, we address leadership conundrums characteristic of organizations confronting paradox.

**Research Question**
How do leaders with divergent visions for their organization come together to create a novel strategy? Specifically, the paper unpacks how and why a leader dyad integrates seemingly inconsistent strategies—preservation versus modernization—to produce a new, generative approach in the wake of a technological discontinuity.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: a technological discontinuity (the quartz crisis) to which two leaders respond with divergent visions—industrial modernization (Hayek) and craft preservation (Biver).
- Core tension: a "preservation–modernization paradox" surfaced when SMH acquired Blancpain, manifest as organizational inconsistencies in core technologies, economic models, and time orientation of practices.
- Individual practices: redefining personal goals and subjugating egos (willingness to moderate narcissistic/self-absorbed behavior) as precursors to joint engagement.
- Relational exchanges: cultivating affective trust and interacting frequently (daily private phone calls before teams could weigh in).
- Structural boundaries: carving out shared territory (Hayek inviting Biver onto SMH's board and onto Breguet's management committee; Biver inviting Hayek to Omega brand events) and synthesizing skills (each acquiring capabilities initially associated with the other).
- Emergent outcome: a sustained paradox that generated a novel "both/and" strategy and ongoing strategic reorientation at SMH.

The authors induce a recursive process model in which two leaders with opposing visions locate the paradox in their *dyadic relationship* rather than in a single leader or the whole top team. Individual-level practices (goal redefinition, ego subjugation) enable relational exchanges (affective trust, frequent interaction), which are enacted through structural boundaries (shared territory, skill synthesis). These mutually reinforcing mechanisms allow the dyad to embrace and sustain contradictory preservation and modernization demands, productively generating new strategy and continual organizational renewal rather than the destructive infighting prior work would predict.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: qualitative historical case study employing a "nested case study design (Yin, 2008)" of two organizations and their senior leaders, extending a larger research project initiated by the first author on the reemergence of the Swiss watch industry. The analytic approach is abductive, iterating between the data and established theoretical constructs so that theory could "emerge, change, and grow"; the study induces a process model and claims no causal identification strategy and no statistical inference.
- Setting and observation window: Swiss watchmaking during and after the quartz crisis, 1983–2001, temporally bracketed into two periods — pre-acquisition (1983–1991), when Hayek led SMH and Biver led Blancpain independently, and post-acquisition (1992–2001), when the two worked together at SMH after Hayek acquired Blancpain.
- Interviews: 147 semi-structured interviews with the two focal leaders (Hayek and Biver), senior executives, watchmakers, journalists and industry experts, retailers, government officials, trade association representatives, horological archivists and administrators, company historians, academics, and collectors/auction house executives. Average interview length 95 minutes; conducted between 2010 and 2020. Sampling was theoretical rather than random (Glaser & Strauss, 1999), with candidate informants drawn from lists supplied by the National Association of Watch and Clock Collectors (NAWCC) and the Federation of the Swiss Watch Industry. Some informants were interviewed repeatedly, Biver annually for nine consecutive years.
- Archival interviews: 92 articles featuring interviews with Hayek and Biver published in Modern Jeweler, WatchTime, and American Time (with editors supplying interview transcripts and reporters' handwritten notes), plus 27 TimeZone interviews with other Swiss watch company CEOs about industry trends.
- Field observations: one week inside a Biver watch factory with unrestricted access to Biver, his team, and production processes; eight days at the Baselworld trade show (10–12 hours per day); one day of watch- and clock-making classes at the NAWCC School of Horology; nine private factory tours in Switzerland and the United States; plus supplemental tours of five watch museums and three horological archives.
- Other archival documents: nine biographies and historical books about Hayek and/or Biver; 58 company-specific books on Swiss watchmaking; annual reports and press releases for Blancpain, SMH, and related brands for all years 1983–2001; Swiss watch production, employment, and company-count series and global trade/competition data for 1970–2001; and annual macroeconomic indicators (1970–2001) for Switzerland and all major watch-exporting countries. Most archival material was hand-collected from archives in Europe and the United States.
- Coding and construct development (four phases). Phase 1 developed "descriptive codes using a content analysis" package to identify key events, actors, and critical choice moments, then recoded all interview and archival data around Hayek's and Biver's decisions and actions, yielding a detailed longitudinal case history. Phase 2 grouped first-order codes into broader themes through axial coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1998), producing the two focal constructs — an industrial modernization vision (Hayek) and a craft preservation vision (Biver). Phase 3 applied temporal bracketing to track how the organizational inconsistencies between the two visions evolved across the pre- and post-acquisition periods and how the leaders engaged them. Phase 4 built the process model and returned to the field for follow-up interviews with Biver, employees of both leaders, reporters, and industry analysts as "member checks" (Maxwell, 2004: 259) to surface validity threats and researcher bias.
- Definitions used to code the model's mechanisms: subjugating egos is defined as "willingness to moderate narcissistic and self-absorbed behavior"; interacting frequently is "conceptualized as repeated interactions that promote the free exchange of ideas and joint decision-making"; affective trust follows McAllister's (1995: 25) affect-based trust "grounded in reciprocated interpersonal care and concern," distinguished from cognition-based trust; synthesizing skills is the "amalgamation of the skills between senior leaders."
- Trustworthiness: archival data allowed triangulation to identify commonalities and differences across sources (Creswell, 2003); periodical pieces published contemporaneously with the events helped guard against recollection bias in interviews conducted decades later. Table 3 reports supplemental quotes organized by mechanism and by period.

**Key Findings**
- Period 1 (1983–1991) produced two divergent but independently viable responses to the same discontinuity. Hayek's modernization vision yielded the Swatch, whose novel design and quartz movement cut production costs by 80% and required 55% fewer parts; launched in 1983 at CHF75 ($35), SMH produced and sold 50 million Swatches within five years. Biver's preservation vision relaunched Blancpain, whose rights he bought for CHF16,000 ($9,000), pledging that it would make only mechanical watches; Blancpain sold 97 watches for $75,000 of revenue in its first year and within five years sold 3,000 watches a year for $9.4 million in annual revenue. Marquee prices diverged accordingly: CHF990 ($450) for a mechanical Blancpain versus CHF75 ($35) for a quartz Swatch.
- The 1992 acquisition of Blancpain (bought by Biver for $9,000 and sold to SMH for $43 million) made three organizational inconsistencies salient inside one firm: different core technologies (retooling for quartz versus exclusively mechanical production), different economic models (high volume/low price versus low volume/high price), and different time orientations of practice (modernizing production and largely abandoning traditional techniques versus preserving the history and practices of Swiss mechanical watchmaking).
- Contrary to prior work predicting that such inconsistencies fuel conflict, ambivalence, or poor decision-making — and contrary to observers' expectation of "sparks" between two large egos — the dyad did not devolve into turf battles. Three interrelated and mutually reinforcing classes of mechanism account for this.
- Individual practices came first. Both leaders had recently redefined personal goals (Hayek abandoning his goal of turning SMH into an electronics company after the Louis Brand launch flopped, and seeking entry into the "exclusive club" of prestige mechanical-watch makers; Biver revisiting his goal of working for himself after selling Blancpain and regretting it) and both subjugated their egos. The paper positions ego subjugation as "an initial proviso for collaboration, rather than rivalry," and individual-level practices as precursors for and key enablers of the other two mechanism classes.
- Relational exchanges built on those practices. Hayek and Biver cultivated affective trust that exceeded the contractual terms of the acquisition and interacted frequently — speaking by telephone daily, in private, often before work hours and before their teams could weigh in. Interaction frequency is identified as an antecedent to affective trust cultivation, and frequent interaction in the absence of mediators or observers appeared to help avoid destructive infighting, a departure from prior work that attributes duo breakups to the absence of a mediator.
- Structural boundaries channelled the relationship. Carving out shared territory (Hayek gave Biver a seat on SMH's board and the Omega turnaround; Biver made room for Hayek at Omega brand events; Biver sat on Breguet's management committee) and synthesizing skills (Biver learned Hayek's factory automation techniques for Omega; Hayek learned brand repositioning from Biver and later applied it at Breguet) let each leader enter the other's domain without formally abdicating power. Concrete "both/and" settlements followed: Omega's mid-range women's Constellation line housed a non-exclusive modern quartz movement, while Hayek bought the patent for the coaxial escapement, a mechanical component reserved for Omega's highest-grade watches.
- Strategic outcomes were positive and growing, not defensive. After Biver took the helm at Omega the brand experienced a near-threefold increase in revenue, with sales rising from $350 million to nearly $1 billion between 1995 and 1999. Within eighteen months of Hayek personally taking over Breguet (acquired in 1999 for a reported CHF100 million), orders rose from 4,000 to 12,000 watches annually, later reaching nearly 30,000 watches a year with regular double-digit revenue growth. By 2001 SMH was the world's largest watch company — 18 brands, more than 20,000 employees, and over one-third of Switzerland's watchmakers — reporting annual revenue of CHF4.2 billion ($2.5 billion), with net-income margin rising from 9.3% to 13.2% over the preceding decade, against Hayek's 1980s estimate of the group's value at CHF328 million ($180 million).
- The paradox was sustained rather than resolved. By 2001 SMH's core technologies mixed quartz and mechanical variants by brand (Blancpain and Breguet hand-made mechanical, Swatch all-quartz, Omega both); its economic model combined high volume/low price (Swatch), low volume/high price (Breguet, Blancpain), and both (Omega); and Swiss watchmaking heritage was preserved in specific models and marketing while modernized production techniques were introduced into both quartz and mechanical manufacturing. The induced model is explicitly recursive — ongoing tensions continue to fuel the preservation–modernization paradox, which in turn initiates ongoing strategic-reorientation efforts.
- Against theory predicting that organizations facing a technological threat decline or wind down to survive, the study found "neither decline nor retrenchment, but organizational vitality and growth," leading the authors to argue that sustaining the preservation–modernization paradox may itself be profoundly generative. As inductive single-case findings, these are interpretive and process-based claims about one leader dyad, not estimated effects.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study relocates the "locus of paradox" from the singular senior leader or top management team (leader-centric and team-centric models) to the *relationship within a leader dyad*, providing an empirical illustration of a previously largely theoretical "relational" conception of paradox. It specifies three mutually reinforcing classes of leader-dyad mechanisms (individual practices, relational exchanges, structural boundaries) and identifies, defines, and elaborates a generative and generalizable category—the preservation–modernization paradox—reframing the strategic question from "preserve or modernize" to "which elements to preserve while modernizing."

**Practical Implication**
Senior teams whose members hold opposing visions can generate novel strategy if leaders redefine personal goals, subjugate their egos, build affective trust through frequent private interaction, and carve out shared territory that lets them swap positions and synthesize skills. Leaders facing a technological discontinuity need not commit to a single either/or response; sustaining the tension between heritage and modernization can itself be profoundly generative.

**Limitations**
As case-based qualitative research, the findings face scope conditions tied to the empirical setting: the preservation perspective stems from Swiss watchmaking's rich history and a craft orientation characteristic of creative and artistic industries, so the paradox may be less salient where tradition is less bound up in the technology itself. The discontinuity studied was technological, and applicability to other forms of change (e.g., radical environmental change) is uncertain. The study period ended in 2001.

**Future Research**
Researchers could seek other antecedent conditions that enable a convergence of visions (e.g., dyadic psychological safety) and probe the interdependence of affective trust and frequent interaction. Promising directions include mapping how leaders with paradoxical visions reshape industries and fields over time, testing the model in settings where tradition is less entangled with the technology, and examining how confronting and sustaining paradox operates when discontinuity is precipitated by radical environmental change (e.g., COVID-19) rather than a new technology.
