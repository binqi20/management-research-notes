# Intersectionality in Intractable Dirty Work: How Mumbai Ragpickers Make Meaning of Their Work and Lives

**Abstract**
Recent dirty work research has begun to explore intersectionality, attending to how meaning is made at the intersection of multiple sources of taint. This research has shown that individuals often construct both positive and negative meanings, which can be challenging to manage because the meanings people construct require a certain coherence to provide a foundation for action. This challenge is intensified when dirty work is intractable—when it is difficult, if not impossible, for a person to avoid doing this work. Our study of meaning-making in the face of intractable dirty work examines ragpickers in Mumbai, India, who handle and dispose of garbage, and are further tainted by belonging to the lowest caste in Indian society, and living in slums. These ragpickers constructed both an overarching sense of helplessness rooted in the intractability of their situation, and a set of positive meanings—survival, destiny, and hope—rooted in specific facets of their lives and enacted through distinct temporal frames. By holding and combining these disparate meanings, they achieved “functional ambivalence”—the simultaneous experience of opposing orientations toward their work and lives that facilitated both acceptance and a sense of agency, and enabled them to carry on in their lives.

**Research Question**
How do people engaged in intractable dirty work at the intersection of multiple sources of taint make and manage meanings of their work and lives?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Intersecting sources of taint (dirty work, tainted caste, dirty place); the tension between negative meaning (helplessness) and positive meanings (survival, destiny, hope); "functional ambivalence" as the simultaneous holding of opposing orientations.
- Triggering conditions: Intractable dirty work arising from long-term individual and familial embeddedness in a community, such that the work is a structurally determined life course rather than a choice.
- Process / phases: Constructing opportunities for meaning-making from intersecting sources of taint → multifocal meaning-making that pairs a specific (vs. global) focus with distinct temporal frames (present = survival, past = destiny, future = hope; global/enduring = helplessness) → holding functional ambivalence.
- Mechanisms sustaining the outcome: Acceptance (flowing from the global meaning of helplessness) and agency (flowing from the specific positive meanings) mutually support and constrain each other, so acceptance does not become passivity and agency does not become frustration.
- Emergent outcome: A self-reinforcing constellation of negative and positive meanings that enables workers to carry on with their work and tainted lives.

The central process is multifocal meaning-making: by adopting a global focus the ragpickers constructed negative meaning (helplessness rooted in intractability), while by focusing on specific facets of their lives through distinct temporal frames they constructed positive, enabling meanings (survival, destiny, hope). Holding these disparate meanings simultaneously produced functional ambivalence, in which the resulting acceptance and sense of agency sustained one another and grounded the workers' capacity to keep going.

**Data & Measures**
This is a single inductive case study, so no variables were measured. The data corpus has three parts. (1) 73 semistructured interviews conducted December 2016 through February 2017: 46 ragpickers and garbage removers (one interview each — 15 who collected scrap from slums, 15 who collected scrap from residents outside slums, and 16 who collected garbage for the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation), 15 industry customers of the ragpickers (10 sorters who bought garbage from ragpickers and resold it to other businesses, plus five middle managers who oversaw remanufacturing of recycled materials), and 12 NGO workers interviewed for contextual information. Access was brokered by six named NGOs and an NGO representative was typically present at the ragpicker interviews; ragpicker interviews took place in the slums, often at worksites or homes, while customer interviews took place in their offices and three NGO interviews were conducted by phone. Interviews averaged 25 minutes, were carried out in the participants' local language and then translated into English (except four NGO interviews conducted in English), and were all recorded and transcribed. Children were not interviewed, and only seven women were included. (2) Observational field notes taken before, during, and after each interview at the ragpickers' work or homes and written up within 24 hours of each observation, amounting to 50 pages of single-spaced text. (3) Secondary sources restricted to the last 10 years (2009–2019): Indian newspaper coverage, blogs, activist reports, documentary videos, and printed NGO and government material. Constructs were coded rather than measured, using an inductive social-constructivist approach: open coding of the ragpickers' statements into first-order codes labeled largely in the ragpickers' own language, integration of those into second-order themes that represent categories of themes, and combination of themes into aggregate dimensions, which produced the four meanings of survival, destiny, hope, and helplessness; statements were then additionally coded for focus (specific or global) and temporal frame (present, past, or future). Coding was cross-checked through an insider–outsider approach in which a cultural insider and a data insider checked each other and three outsiders on the research team questioned the insiders, offered alternative explanations, and sent the data insider back to recode. The design is interpretive and associational, not causal: the paper studies an extreme case and claims naturalistic rather than statistical generalizability.

**Key Findings**
The analysis produced four sets of findings; no hypotheses were tested and no statistical estimates are reported. First, the ragpickers experienced three intersecting sources of taint — dirty work (onerous, dangerous, and repugnant to others, with all the workers describing working between 14 and 16 hours per day), tainted caste (a commonly used self-description was Dalit, or "untouchable"), and dirty place (living in and around the slums of Mumbai) — and experienced them both as a tightly coupled intersection and as partially separable dimensions. Second, when they adopted a global focus, they constructed an overarching negative meaning of themselves as helpless both to avoid the work and to escape it, grounded in little money, few career alternatives, little market power, a minimal support network, and an experience trap in which ragpicking generated no transferable skills. Third, when they focused on specific facets of their lives paired with distinct temporal frames, the ragpickers made three positive meanings: focusing on the work in a present frame constructed the work as survival; focusing on the intersection of work and caste in a past frame constructed it as destiny; and focusing on the intersection of work and place in a future frame constructed it as hope for their children. Each positive meaning was enacted in concrete action — selling each day's collection for immediate cash, taking up inherited "preferential treatment" municipal posts, and educating children to keep them out of ragpicking. Fourth, the negative and positive meanings were held simultaneously and reinforced rather than undermined each other, producing "functional ambivalence": acceptance, flowing from the global meaning of helplessness, and agency, flowing from the specific positive meanings, support and constrain each other such that acceptance does not lead to passivity and agency does not lead to frustration, which allowed the workers to carry on. These findings are abstracted into a model of multifocal meaning-making that runs from intersecting sources of taint, through meaning-making that pairs a scope of focus with a temporal frame, to functional ambivalence.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to dirty work and intersectionality scholarship by identifying "functional ambivalence" as an outcome of meaning-making, showing that the relationship between negative and positive meanings can be constructive and empowering rather than only a tension to be managed. It extends intersectionality research by demonstrating that workers can experience intersecting sources of taint both as tightly coupled and as separable dimensions, and it identifies the process of "multifocal meaning-making"—pairing a specific focus with a specific temporal frame—through which people in intractable dirty work construct a functionally ambivalent constellation of meanings.

**Practical Implication**
The study foregrounds the embodied wisdom of marginalized workers whose voices are rarely heard, showing the constructive potential of combining negative and positive meanings to ground and empower individuals engaged in intractable dirty work at the intersection of multiple sources of taint: the ragpickers combined a sense of helplessness with survival, destiny, and hope to achieve a "functional ambivalence" that facilitated their ability to carry on.

**Limitations**
As research rooted in an extreme case (the tightly coupled occupation, caste, and living conditions of Mumbai ragpickers), the findings offer naturalistic rather than statistical generalizability, and this extreme intractability likely shaped results such as the prominence of helplessness. The study focused on common experiences and did not examine within-group differences; it excluded child ragpickers and included only seven women, so age and gender differences could not be systematically examined.

**Future Research**
Future dirty work research should broaden the lenses used to study coping by attending to the embeddedness and relationality of dirty work in social and familial ties across past and future. Scholars should also examine the longer-term consequences of such coping—whether the constellation of survival, destiny, hope, and helplessness "locks in" workers—by studying populations with greater variance in their work and lives and following them longitudinally, and by attending to age and gender differences.
