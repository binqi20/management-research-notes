# When and Why Bias Suppression Is Difficult to Sustain: The Asymmetric Effect of Intermittent Accountability

**Abstract**
The justice literature has suggested that adhering to justice rules should be selfreinforcing. Yet, the literature on bias suppression (a facet of procedural justice) has indicated that the effects of debiasing initiatives are transient. We aim to explain why sustained bias suppression is intrinsically challenging under the condition of intermittent accountability. We theorize that bias suppression with high accountability induces counterfactual thinking and regret and, ultimately, with subsequent low accountability, decision reversal. However, this process does not occur when accountability is initially low and subsequently high. Thus, intermittent accountability has an asymmetric effect on bias suppression over time—bias suppression is reversed (and biased decisions sustained). We initially provide proof of concept using Standard & Poor's 500 Firms' CEOs. We then conduct a field study of prominent decision-makers: U.S. Supreme Court judges (Study 1). We find that bias suppression is negatively associated with consistency when accountability is initially high but later reduced, as seemingly mediated through counterfactual thinking. Next, we test our full model in two experiments using recall and a self-focused vignette (Studies 2 and 3). Finally, we explore why bias yields consistency even when accountability increases. Our work highlights the power of cognitive and emotional processing relative to external control.

**Research Question**
When and why is sustaining bias suppression (adherence to the procedural-justice bias suppression rule) from one decision to the next intrinsically difficult, and how does the sequence of accountability across decisions shape whether bias suppression is reversed?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: Bias suppression is positively associated with counterfactual thinking when accountability is high (versus low).
H2: Counterfactual thinking is positively associated with regret.
H3: Regret is negatively associated with consistency when accountability is low (versus high).
H4: Bias suppression has a negative indirect effect on consistency that is mediated by counterfactual thinking and regret and moderated by accountability at times 1 and 2 (T1 and T2), such that the negative indirect effect is strongest when accountability is high at T1 and low at T2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Bias suppression at T1 (a decision contravening one's own bias/ideological attitude in favor of the organization's neutral principles)
- DV(s): Consistency at T2 (sustaining versus reversing the prior decision when facing a similar choice)
- Mediators: Counterfactual thinking ("would/could/should have" cognition), then regret (a self-focused, self-reactive counterfactual emotion); pride mediates the biased-decision-to-consistency path in a supplemental analysis
- Moderators: Accountability at T1 (high amplifies bias-suppression -> counterfactual thinking) and accountability at T2 (low strengthens regret -> inconsistency); the indirect effect is strongest under high-T1 / low-T2 accountability

Drawing on the social cognitive theory of self-regulation, the authors argue that suppressing a bias under high accountability leaves an unresolved internal tension between self-interest and the organization's principles, triggering self-reflective counterfactual thinking and self-reactive regret. When subsequent accountability is low, regret supplies the impetus to reverse the earlier just decision; when accountability instead remains high the counterfactual thinking and regret still arise but do not translate into reversal, and when accountability is initially low and subsequently high the process does not occur at all. The effect is therefore asymmetric: intermittent accountability undermines the bias-suppression-consistency link only when accountability falls from high to low.

**Data & Measures**
Four datasets, reported separately by study.

Proof of concept (S&P 500 CEOs). Archival data supplied by Chin and Semadeni (2017) on S&P's 500 firms' CEOs appointed between 2007 and 2010 who served at least three years; n = 106 CEOs after listwise deletion (12 observations dropped from 118; the authors report results are robust to including all 118), with each CEO appearing only once. CEO liberalism was Chin and Semadeni's (2017) CEO-conservatism measure inverted, derived from 10 years of political-contribution data, continuous from 0 to 1. Bias suppression = each CEO's standardized liberalism score multiplied by the initial change in pay arrangements among non-CEO executives in the CEO's first year (liberal changes = negative values, conservative changes = positive values), then standardized; it captures the degree to which the initial change contravened versus aligned with the CEO's ideological attitudes. Consistency = the degree of liberal (conservative) change in pay equality in the CEO's second year for CEOs who had moved pay arrangements in a liberal (conservative) direction in the first year, standardized, so any reversal of the prior year's decision was coded as reduced consistency; pay equality is the inverse of the coefficient of variation in non-CEO executives' total pay. Covariates replicate Chin and Semadeni (2017). Estimation was OLS on between-individual variation. The design is archival and associational; the authors present it only as proof of concept and state they could not test the theoretical causal chain with these data.

Study 1 (U.S. Supreme Court judges). Archival data on each judge's decision in each case decided and revisited during the 1986-2012 terms if the judge was still serving. Revisited cases came from McMillion and Vance (2017), who identify cases the Court followed, criticized, limited, questioned, or overruled; of the 2,696 cases decided in the window, 651 were revisited, and only the first revisited case was used when a case was revisited more than once. Judges n = 18; judge-decisions n = 4,182; the unit of analysis was the initial judge-decision. Judges' liberalism was measured with Segal and Cover's (1989) ideology scale, based on evaluations in opinion editorials published before they joined the Court; the ideological direction of each decision came from the Supreme Court Database. Bias suppression was coded 1 if a judge above (below) the mean in liberalism made a conservative (liberal) initial decision and 0 otherwise, omitting cases where the ideological direction was unclear. Counterfactual thinking was a dichotomous archival proxy coded 1 if the judge wrote a separate opinion in the initial case (separate opinions were filed in 19% of observations); the authors validated it on 100 randomly sampled separate opinions and the corresponding majority opinions, finding "would/could/should" language 34% higher in separate opinions (6.40 versus 4.76 per 1,000 words, p = .00). Consistency was a dichotomous indicator coded 1 if the judge decided in the same ideological direction in both the initial and the revisited case. Accountability at T1 and T2 was the number of dissenting votes in each case, not counting the target judge's vote, ranging 0-4. Covariates were judge age and gender plus House Conservatism and Senate Conservatism from Judicial Common Space scores. Estimation was mixed-level (multilevel) logistic regression with random effects for each judge, robust standard errors, and term and issue-area fixed effects. Regret was not measured in this data set, so Hypotheses 2 and 3 could not be tested here; the authors state these archival data necessitate measurement proxies and do not lend themselves to measuring emotional reactions or testing causal relationships.

Study 2 (experimental recall of real-world decisions). 228 adult, full-time-employed Amazon Mechanical Turk participants who identified as a manager, boss, supervisor, or other type of superior (M age = 41.1, SD = 11.4, 43% female), in a 2 x 2 x 2 experimental design: bias suppression versus biased decision at T1, crossed with high versus low accountability at T1 and at T2, with random assignment to all three factors. Sample parameters, hypotheses, conditions, and measures were preregistered on AsPredicted. Bias suppression (= 1) was induced by asking participants to recall a past decision about their subordinates in which "you based your decision on neutral principles," versus the biased-decision condition (= 0), "you based your decision on your self-interests or strong views." Accountability followed Hall and colleagues' (2003) definition, manipulated as whether participants expected their decision to be evaluated by others who might reward or punish them, and restated for the T2 decision. Counterfactual thinking was coded dichotomously from participants' open-ended descriptions of their decision by three research assistants with no knowledge of the study (ICC(1) = .42, p = .00), with disagreements resolved by discussion. Regret used Buchanan, Summerville, and Lehmann's (2016) five-item affective regret scale (alpha = .95). Consistency used five items based on Leventhal's (1980) construct definition (alpha = .95). Manipulation checks confirmed the bias-suppression and both accountability manipulations, with no significant spillover between the bias-suppression and T1 accountability treatments. Pride, used only in the supplemental analysis, was Tracy and Robins' (2007) seven-item authentic pride scale (alpha = .96), measured on the same real-world Study 2 decision.

Study 3 (quasi-experimental self-focused vignette). 257 adult, full-time-employed Amazon Mechanical Turk participants recruited on the same manager/supervisor criteria (M age = 40.1, SD = 10.6, 47% female), in a 2 x 2 x 2 quasi-experimental design: the participant's own non-randomized decision to suppress their bias at T1, crossed with randomized high versus low accountability at T1 and at T2; also preregistered on AsPredicted. Participants imagined supervising at a computer software company where a valuable and relied-upon new employee committed, for the second time, an offense warranting termination, with the vignette written to invoke both self-interest and a strongly held belief that everyone deserves another chance. Bias suppression was coded from the participant's own choice to "report [the] incident based on neutral principles" (= 1) versus "not report [the] incident based on self-interest or strong views" (= 0); 35% chose to suppress their bias. Counterfactual thinking was coded from open-ended responses using the Study 2 procedure (ICC(1) = .40, p < .001); regret and consistency used the Study 2 scales (alpha = .95 and .96 respectively). Because bias suppression was self-selected rather than randomly assigned, the bias-suppression path in this study is quasi-experimental rather than randomized.

Across all studies, analyses were run in Stata 16, and all indirect effects were calculated with the SEM command using bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals based on bootstrapped standard errors with 10,000 replications.

**Key Findings**
Proof of concept. In OLS regression, bias suppression was negatively associated with consistency (b = -.29, p = .01). CEOs who initially moved pay arrangements away from their ideological biases were less likely to make a consistent decision the following year, whereas CEOs who initially moved pay arrangements toward their biases tended to maintain or escalate those decisions.

Study 1 supported H1 and was consistent with H4, but could not test H2 or H3. Bias suppression was positively associated with counterfactual thinking overall (slope = .02, p = .00). As predicted by H1, that association was positive when T1 accountability was high (slope = .10, p = .00) but not when it was low (slope = -.02, p = .17): under high accountability the probability of filing a separate opinion was .16 (SE = .02) for judges who made a biased decision versus .26 (SE = .03) for judges who suppressed their biases, while under low accountability it was .17 (SE = .03) versus .15 (SE = .03). H2 and H3 could not be directly tested because the data lacked any measure of or proxy for regret; consistent with the model, however, counterfactual thinking was negatively associated with consistency when T2 accountability was low (slope = -.13, p = .00) but not when it was high (slope = .04, p = .07). As implied by H4, the indirect effect of bias suppression on consistency was negative and significant only for judges with high T1 and low T2 accountability (indirect effect = -.014, 95% CI [-.025, -.005]); it was nonsignificant when T1 and T2 accountability were both high (.006, 95% CI [-.000, .014]), both low (.003, 95% CI [-.000, .009]), or low at T1 and high at T2 (-.001, 95% CI [-.006, .000]). The direct effect of bias suppression on consistency was larger but statistically nonsignificant under both high (-.09, 95% CI [-.24, .05]) and low (-.09, 95% CI [-.23, .05]) T1 accountability. The authors note the magnitude of these effects is quite small, perhaps because of the imperfect separate-opinion proxy or the unusually long lag (5.9 years on average) between each initial and revisited decision.

Study 2 supported H1, H2, H3, and H4. Bias suppression was positively associated with counterfactual thinking (b = .18, p = .00) and, as predicted by H1, only when T1 accountability was high (slope = .31, p = .00), not when it was low (slope = .01, p = .92). Counterfactual thinking was positively associated with regret (H2; b = .45, p = .01). Supporting H3, the negative association between regret and consistency was stronger when T2 accountability was low (slope = -.51, p = .00) than when it was high (slope = -.27, p = .00) — note that in this study the association was significantly negative at both levels of T2 accountability, not only under low accountability. Supporting H4, bias suppression had its largest and statistically significant negative indirect effect on consistency when accountability was high at T1 and low at T2 (indirect effect = -.07, 95% CI [-.17, -.05]); unlike Studies 1 and 3, a smaller but also significant negative indirect effect appeared when T1 and T2 accountability were both high (-.04, 95% CI [-.12, -.01]). The indirect effect was nonsignificant when both were low (.00, 95% CI [-.04, .03]) and when accountability was low at T1 and high at T2 (.00, 95% CI [-.02, .01]).

Study 3 supported H1, H2, H3, and H4. Bias suppression was positively associated with counterfactual thinking (b = .26, p = .00) and, as predicted by H1, only when T1 accountability was high (slope = .36, p = .00), not when it was low (slope = .02, p = .80). Counterfactual thinking was positively associated with regret (H2; b = .48, p = .00). Supporting H3, regret was negatively associated with consistency when T2 accountability was low (slope = -.49, p = .00) but not when it was high (slope = -.04, p = .61). Supporting H4, the indirect effect of bias suppression on consistency was negative and statistically significant only when accountability was high at T1 and low at T2 (indirect effect = -.09, 95% CI [-.18, -.01]); it was nonsignificant when both were high (-.01, 95% CI [-.06, .03]), both low (-.01, 95% CI [-.06, .03]), or low at T1 and high at T2 (.00, 95% CI [-.02, .00]). The direct effect of bias suppression on consistency was positive when T1 accountability was low (direct effect = .89, 95% CI [.44, 1.34]) and nonsignificant when it was high (.22, 95% CI [-.16, .61]).

Supplemental and exploratory analyses. In a supplemental, exploratory analysis on the Study 2 data — which the authors flag as not originally theorized and as arising through peer review — pride mediated the negative association between bias suppression and consistency: assignment to the bias-suppression condition was negatively associated with pride (b = -.83, p = .00), pride was positively associated with consistency (b = .27, p = .00), and the pride-mediated indirect effect of bias suppression on consistency was negative (indirect effect = -.33, 95% CI [-.49, -.20]). Neither T1 accountability moderated the bias-suppression-to-pride link (b = -.27, p = .34) nor T2 accountability the pride-to-consistency link (b = -.05, p = .69), so biased decision-making raised pride regardless of accountability. A further exploratory test of moral licensing found bias suppression was not positively associated with moral credits (b = -.08, p = .55) or moral credentials (b = -.70, p = .00), although both were positively associated with consistency (b = .25 and b = .32, p = .00 for both), contrary to a moral-licensing account.

Overall pattern. In each of the three hypothesis-testing studies the theorized asymmetry held: prior bias suppression undermined consistency when accountability fell from high to low, whereas when accountability rose from low to high there was no significant indirect effect, so initially biased decisions persisted even under subsequently higher accountability.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shows that, unlike other justice rules, bias suppression is not self-reinforcing, challenging the dominant model of justice enactment and the assumption that bias suppression and biased decision-making simply occur under high versus low accountability respectively. It reframes justice enactment as dynamic and sequence-dependent, demonstrating that accountability operates jointly with internal self-regulatory processes (counterfactual thinking, regret, and—surprisingly—pride) rather than as a standalone external control, and argues for studying the bias suppression rule in isolation rather than aggregating it with other procedural-justice facets.

**Practical Implication**
Sustaining bias suppression is an uphill battle: accountability used intermittently can backfire because withdrawing it removes the only force holding biased decisions in check. Organizations should apply accountability consistently or, better, cultivate internalized commitment to organizational values; for high-discretion roles, they should pursue "bias alignment" (matching decision-makers to decisions where their values serve the organization) rather than relying on compliance, and recognize that adding accountability later may be "too little, too late."

**Limitations**
Study 1 operationalized bias suppression only via decisions inconsistent with a judge's general ideology and relied on an imperfect archival proxy (separate opinions) for counterfactual thinking, with effects of small magnitude and a long average lag (5.9 years) between initial and revisited decisions. The proof of concept and Study 1 focus on political-ideology bias, while Studies 2-3 used a generic conceptualization of bias; the studies did not examine the full range of contextual antecedents that generate bias suppression in the first place.

**Future Research**
Future work should test whether the asymmetric accountability effect generalizes to other bias types made explicit (e.g., gender or racial bias), examine the broader set of contextual factors and antecedents that shape the post-bias-suppression process, and illuminate the internal motives or innate individual differences that initially drive or help sustain bias suppression over time regardless of external accountability.
