# Paradoxical Leadership and Innovation in Work Teams: The Multilevel Mediating Role of Ambidexterity and Leader Vision as a Boundary Condition

**Abstract**
In light of ever-increasing demands for innovation in work teams, we recommend paradoxical leadership to manage team and individual innovation. Integrating the paradox perspective and ambidexterity theory, we propose a multilevel model of how and when team leaders’ paradoxical leadership enhances team and individual innovative outcomes. We conducted two multi-wave, multisource field studies and one online survey study with a cross-lagged panel design. Our findings show that paradoxical leadership has a positive indirect effect on team members’ individual innovation through individual ambidexterity and a positive indirect relationship with team innovation through team ambidexterity. Our results further show that leader vision strengthens the effects of paradoxical leadership on team and individual ambidexterity and innovative outcomes. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications of these findings.

**Research Question**
How and when does team leaders' paradoxical leadership — taking a "both–and" approach to competing organizing and belonging demands — promote innovation at both the individual and team levels in work teams tasked with providing innovative solutions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Team leaders' paradoxical leadership has a positive relationship to team members' individual ambidexterity.
H2. Team leaders' paradoxical leadership has a positive relationship to team ambidexterity.
H3. Team leaders' paradoxical leadership has a positive relationship to team members' individual innovation through individual ambidexterity.
H4. Team leaders' paradoxical leadership has a positive relationship to team innovation through team ambidexterity.
H5a. Leader vision moderates the positive relationship between paradoxical leadership and team members' individual ambidexterity, such that the relationship is stronger under strong (vs. weak) leader vision.
H5b. Leader vision moderates the positive relationship between paradoxical leadership and team members' individual innovation through individual ambidexterity, such that the indirect relationship is stronger under strong (vs. weak) leader vision.
H6a. Leader vision moderates the positive relationship between paradoxical leadership and team ambidexterity, such that the relationship is stronger under strong (vs. weak) leader vision.
H6b. Leader vision moderates the positive relationship between paradoxical leadership and team innovation through team ambidexterity, such that the indirect relationship is stronger under strong (vs. weak) leader vision.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Paradoxical leadership (leaders' seemingly competing yet interrelated behaviors addressing the organizing and belonging paradoxes)
- DV(s): Individual innovation and team innovation
- Mediators: Individual ambidexterity (individual exploration × exploitation) and team ambidexterity (team exploration × exploitation)
- Moderators: Leader vision (clarity of a communicated overarching team direction)

Drawing on the paradox perspective and ambidexterity theory, paradoxical leaders deliver divergent role expectations and model how to manage competing demands at the individual level, while building a simultaneously autonomous and structured work atmosphere at the team level. These dynamics raise individual and team ambidexterity — the integration of exploratory and exploitative activities — which in turn fuels the generation and implementation of novel ideas, increasing innovation. A clear leader vision lets members make sense of paradoxical behaviors, strengthening the leadership-to-ambidexterity-to-innovation pathways (tested in Studies 2 and 3 — leader vision was not measured in Study 1 — and supported in both, though Study 3's cross-lagged model itself reports a significant reverse path from individual innovation to individual ambidexterity and tests causality for only part of the model).

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (field): seven companies in northern China in information technology, telecommunications and medical technology; HR managers identified R&D and technical teams whose main tasks were innovative solutions, giving a pool of 130 leaders and 760 members. Data came from members and leaders at three time points two weeks apart; matched data yielded a final sample of 562 members in 105 teams. T1 — members rated paradoxical leadership over the previous six months on the 22-item dual-sided scale from Zhang et al. (2015) (0 = not at all to 4 = a lot; α = .94). T2 — members rated their own exploration and exploitation on the 11-item Mom, Van Den Bosch and Volberda (2007) measure (6-point; α = .88 for both) and team exploration and exploitation on two 5-item Kostopoulos and Bozionelos (2011) scales (6-point; α = .92 and .86), using a reference-shift consensus model. T3 — leaders rated individual innovation (4-item Welbourne, Johnson & Erez, 1998; α = .84) and team innovation (4-item De Dreu & West, 2001; α = .72). Individual ambidexterity = individual exploration × individual exploitation, and team ambidexterity = team exploration × team exploitation (both ranging 1–36); additive operationalizations gave consistent results. Controls: member age and education at the individual level, team size and mean team tenure at the team level. Team-level variables were aggregated after ICC1/ICC2 and rWG checks; hypotheses were tested by multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8, with Monte Carlo parametric bootstrapping for indirect effects.

Study 2 (field): five companies in northern China — all different from the Study 1 firms — in information technology and services, software technology and services, media technology and medical technology; same three-wave, multisource procedure; 820 members were surveyed at T1 and the final matched data set comprised 607 members working under 93 leaders. Measures of paradoxical leadership, ambidexterity and innovation were identical to Study 1, plus leader vision (3 items adapted from Griffin et al., 2010; 6-point; α = .91). Transformational leadership (7-item, Carless, Wearing & Mann, 2000; α = .80) and supportive leadership behavior (6-item, Carmeli, Gelbard & Gefen, 2010; α = .88) were added as controls to rule out alternative explanations.

Study 3 (online cross-lagged survey): a Western sample recruited via Prolific Academic, restricted to full-time employees working in an organizational team whose work involved developing new ideas and applying them to processes, methods, products or services. 442 participants completed T1, 357 completed T2 (80.77%) and 312 completed T3 (87.39%); only complete cases were retained, giving n = 312 (47.44% female; mean age 31.98, SD = 9.20). The same measures were used, except that the repeated-measure time frame was "the last two weeks" across the three waves and participants rated their *own* individual innovation; leader vision was measured at T1. Configural and metric measurement invariance were established before estimating a cross-lagged panel model in Mplus 8.

Identification is associational throughout: all three studies are surveys, with no experimental or quasi-experimental design. The authors describe the Study 3 cross-lagged panel model as a way to "disentangle the causal directions" of paradoxical leadership, individual ambidexterity and individual innovation, and state in the limitations that it tests causality for only part of the model.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (562 members, 105 teams; leader vision was not measured, so H5 and H6 were not tested): paradoxical leadership was positively related to team ambidexterity (γ = 2.03, SE = 0.57, p < .01) and to individual ambidexterity (γ = 1.44, SE = 0.45, p < .01), and the paper reports both H1 and H2 as supported — though the Study 1 results text attaches the H1 and H2 labels to these two coefficients in the reverse order to its own hypothesis statements. Individual ambidexterity was positively related to individual innovation (γ = 0.08, SE = 0.02, p < .01) and the indirect effect of paradoxical leadership on individual innovation via individual ambidexterity was 0.11, 95% CI [0.04, 0.21], supporting H3. Team ambidexterity was positively related to team innovation (γ = 0.05, SE = 0.01, p < .01), indirect effect 0.11, 95% CI [0.05, 0.16], supporting H4.

Study 2 (607 members, 93 leaders; transformational and supportive leadership controlled): H1 supported (γ = 1.50, SE = 0.59, p < .05) and H2 supported (γ = 1.20, SE = 0.54, p < .05). Individual ambidexterity related positively to individual innovation (γ = 0.18, SE = 0.05, p < .01), indirect effect 0.27, 95% CI [0.07, 0.49] — H3 supported; team ambidexterity related positively to team innovation (γ = 0.06, SE = 0.02, p < .05), indirect effect 0.07, 95% CI [0.005, 0.16] — H4 supported. Leader vision positively moderated the paradoxical leadership–individual ambidexterity path (γ = 0.67, SE = 0.27, p < .05; H5a supported) and the paradoxical leadership–team ambidexterity path (γ = 0.72, SE = 0.20, p < .01; H6a supported). Moderated mediation: the indirect effect on individual innovation was stronger under strong leader vision (0.36, p < .01) than weak leader vision (0.19, p < .05; difference 0.17, 95% CI [0.03, 0.30]), supporting H5b; the indirect effect on team innovation was 0.10 (p < .05) under strong vision versus 0.04 (non-significant) under weak vision (difference 0.06, 95% CI [0.02, 0.18]), supporting H6b.

Study 3 (n = 312; individual-level paths only — the team-level predictions H2, H4, H6a and H6b were not tested here): paradoxical leadership T1 was positively related to individual ambidexterity T2 (γ = 0.15, SE = 0.05, p < .01) and individual ambidexterity T2 was positively related to individual innovation T3 (γ = 0.17, SE = 0.06, p < .01), with an indirect effect of 0.06, 95% CI [0.02, 0.13]. The cross-lagged model also showed a bidirectional effect: individual innovation T2 had a lagged effect on individual ambidexterity T3 (γ = 0.23, SE = 0.06, p < .01). Leader vision positively moderated the paradoxical leadership T1–individual ambidexterity T2 relationship (γ = 0.86, SE = 0.30, p < .01), and the lagged indirect effect on individual innovation was 0.08 (p < .05) under stronger leader vision versus 0.02 (non-significant) under weaker leader vision (difference 0.06, 95% CI [0.02, 0.14]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces the paradox perspective to innovation management and specifies ambidexterity as the multilevel mediating mechanism linking paradoxical leadership to individual and team innovation. It extends ambidexterity theory by identifying *which* specific leader behaviors promote ambidexterity and *when* (conditioned on leader vision), and it advances team-leadership research by showing paradoxical leadership fits complex, volatile team contexts.

**Practical Implication**
Managers leading innovation-focused teams should adopt paradoxical leadership principles — e.g., ensuring flexibility while enforcing work requirements and holding output control while granting process autonomy — and organizations should provide paradoxical-leadership and "paradoxical mindset" training. To maximize effectiveness, leaders must also communicate a clear, forward-looking vision so members can "see the big picture" behind seemingly contradictory behaviors.

**Limitations**
Team innovation was rated subjectively by leaders rather than measured objectively (e.g., patents), and the cross-lagged study relied on participants' self-rated individual innovation, raising possible social-desirability bias. The limited number of teams (105 and 93) forced separate testing of structural and measurement models, and the cross-lagged design validated causality only for the individual-level portion of the model. The dual-sided measure and the multiplicative ambidexterity operationalization capture only some facets of these constructs.

**Future Research**
Future work could incorporate objective or multi-source measures of team innovation, use quasi-experimental designs to strengthen causal inference for team-level effects, and qualitatively unpack the differing forms of individual and team ambidexterity. Researchers should also test alternative underlying mechanisms (e.g., role modeling), examine when ambidexterity may be undesirable, explore other facets of vision (shared vs. organization-consistent), and clarify the team–individual innovation relationship.
