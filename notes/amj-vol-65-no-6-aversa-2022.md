# The Grand Tour: The Role of Catalyzing Places for Industry Emergence

**Abstract**
We advance the concept of “catalyzing places” that support industry emergence and growth through an ongoing, cyclical process of three forces: “centripetal” (i.e., attracting), “catalyzing” (i.e., reacting), and “centrifugal” (i.e., ejecting). The forces attract communities of practice to the place, expose them to intense, transformational experiences toward entrepreneurship, and induce them to establish their businesses elsewhere. By redeploying the resources and reputation acquired in the place, these scattered communities enact a collective phenomenon of user entrepreneurship and ultimately industry emergence. We claim that the ongoing activities of the place, and the periodic ones of the event, are mutually reinforcing. We advance two novel elements—“portable economies” and “springboard firms”—that, in catalyzing places, exert the antithetical effect of “agglomeration economies” and “anchor firms” in clusters. We discuss our contribution to research on industry emergence, new practices, and user entrepreneurship.

**Research Question**
"What are the mechanisms by which certain places catalyze the emergence of industries in areas away from their birthplace?" The paper asks why some industries emerge away from the locations to which their origin is ascribed—that is, why certain places catalyze industry emergence without producing a local cluster of firms.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs: "catalyzing places" (renowned locations that support emergence and growth of non-clustered industries); "communities of practice" bound by shared passion; "portable economies" (valuable, redeployable resources/reputation not bound to the place); "springboard firms" (local firms with a dual role).
- Three sequential forces (aggregate dimensions): centripetal (attracting communities to the place via distinctive resources and personal incentives), catalyzing (reacting—exposing visitors to intense, transformational experiences that convert enthusiasts into entrepreneurs), centrifugal (ejecting—inducing them to establish ventures elsewhere).
- Treatment vs. selection effects: "treatment" (serendipitous/unplanned exposure, more common in incubation) and "selection" (planned exposure once the place is renowned, in emergence/growth).
- Mutual reinforcement: ongoing place activities and the periodic RockMaster event reinforce each other via place-specific emotional energy and spatial/temporal compression.

The process model explains industry emergence as cyclical rather than linear and cumulative: a catalyzing place repeatedly attracts a scattered community of practice, transforms its members' passion into entrepreneurial capability and reputation (portable economies), and then—because springboard firms saturate local demand and the area lacks agglomeration economies—ejects them to found ventures in other geographies. Because the acquired resources are portable and often gain value elsewhere, the result is a collective phenomenon of user entrepreneurship that builds an industry not clustered where it originated, while the place continually re-energizes the community on return visits.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive, longitudinal single-case study of the town of Arco (Sarca Valley, northeast Italy) and its RockMaster competition, conducted in the Gioia et al. (2013) tradition to develop a process model; the design is interpretive and historical rather than variable-based, so constructs were coded from the data rather than measured, and there is no estimation and no causal identification strategy. Table 1 lists three data sources. Published secondary sources: industry reports (4), history books (12), online (archival) newspaper and journal articles and web pages (62), documentaries (6), and books and publications about Arco (9), totaling 2,247 pages. Archival secondary sources: the Societa degli Alpinisti Tridentini (SAT) archive at the Biblioteca della Montagna in Trento (Rivista CAI 1980–2004; Rivista della Montagna 1980–2000; Alp 1985–2000; Alp–Speciale Ritratti 2008–2010; High 1985–1990; Rotpunkt 1985–2001; 458 pages) and the S.S.D. Arrampicata Sportiva Arco archive (national press releases 1987–2018; event posters and playbills; lists of participants, winners, and sponsors and company statutes; technical documentation for climbing walls and competitions; photographic material; 260 pages) — approximately 3,000 pages of documents in total. Interviews: 41 semi-structured interviews with the informants listed in Table 2 — preliminary interviews with experts who experienced the birth of sport climbing, then focused interviews with professional climbers and entrepreneurs who used to visit Arco and with competitors, entrepreneurs, organizers, and experts involved in the early RockMaster editions — totaling 1,534 minutes (25.5 hours), all conducted in English, recorded, and transcribed. Publicly available data were also collected on the 39 main manufacturers of climbing walls, holds, and shoes, whose founders (or current entrepreneurs) and some key employees were interviewed, yielding 39 mini case studies of each manufacturer's history and its connection to Arco and RockMaster. Observation: attendance at the 2019 IFSC youth world championship and the 33rd edition of RockMaster in 2019, both in Arco, with informal conversations ranging from brief exchanges to longer talks with organizers, athletes, journalists, fans, entrepreneurs, and volunteers. To minimize retrospective sensemaking and biased interpretations, the older pre-commercialization phase was investigated through archival sources, while interviews and direct observation carried the more recent post-commercialization phases. Analysis proceeded in three steps: (1) historical reconstruction of a detailed timeline of main events from early activities in the 1970s until the 2010s, periodized following Agarwal et al. (2017: 290) into an industry "incubation" phase and an "emergence and growth" phase divided by the industry "inception" — identified as the very first sale of artificial walls used at RockMaster in 1988 — with the founding of the 39 manufacturers, new technologies, changes in climbing practices, and the formation of supporting institutions placed in chronological order and firm locations plotted on an interactive Google map; (2) open coding of interviews and secondary data in parallel (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013), iterating between analysis and additional data collection, producing 28 first-order codes labeled wherever possible with informants' own "concepts in use"; and (3) axial coding collapsing the 28 first-order codes into nine more abstract second-order themes and then three aggregate dimensions — centripetal, catalyzing, and centrifugal forces (Figure 1) — after which the authors returned to the historical timeline and the individual mini-cases to establish the temporal sequence among the three forces. To confirm reliability, the authors introduced their interpretations to some key informants and used the feedback to refine the process model.

**Key Findings**
The central emergent finding is that Arco functions as a "catalyzing place" — a renowned location that supports the emergence and growth of industries that are not clustered near their origin — through an ongoing, cyclical process rather than the linear, cumulative one assumed in cluster research. The catalyzing place exerts three forces over dispersed communities of practice, sustaining four sequential phases: (1) centripetal forces "attract" communities to the place to experience its tangible and intangible features; (2) catalyzing forces make individuals "react" to those features and experiences and equip them with resources, know-how, and reputation to start a business; (3) centrifugal forces "eject" individuals from the place because of local entry barriers and inferior conditions for entrepreneurship; and (4) individuals establish business activities in areas away from the catalyzing place. New visitors are continuously attracted while former visitors periodically return, which is what makes the process cyclical rather than one-directional.

Arco's imprint on the industry is empirically pervasive. Of a reconstructed timeline of 57 main industry events — grouped into firms (18), technologies (16), practices (13), and institutions (9) — 47 either happened in Arco, happened at RockMaster, or were linked to or catalyzed by Arco, and every single element after the 1988 inception presented some connection to Arco and/or RockMaster. Of the 39 firms that make up the manufacturing core of the industry (artificial walls, holds, and climbing shoes), 32 (about 82%) were found to be directly catalyzed by Arco, while seven (circa 18%) had no connection. In the emergence and growth phase, these 32 companies came to Arco for business ideas (14), opportunities to design products (27), opportunities to test products (29), and/or opportunities to launch new products (13). The paper reports that a total of 30 (76.9%) companies intentionally visited Arco to access these opportunities — a "selection" effect, or planned exposure — while for the other 12 (30.7%) connected companies the actors initially visited for reasons unrelated to an existing business and only serendipitously encountered experiences that nurtured business ideas — a "treatment" effect, or unplanned exposure; all 12 of those firms subsequently returned to Arco purposefully for a planned exposure. The treatment effect better characterizes the pre-commercialization (incubation) phase and the selection effect the later emergence and growth phase, and the two are not mutually exclusive. The influence was not constant over time: initially all firms' origins were linked to Arco, but from the 1990s some are not, which the authors read as suggesting that other generative mechanisms emerge as the industry grows.

Centripetal forces — defined as the characteristics of a place and its sociocultural activities that induce community members to visit — operated through three second-order themes. Distinctive resources and features: Arco's renowned reputation for sport climbing, its ideal setting, pioneering climbing practices and competition formats, and groundbreaking technologies in walls, holds, and shoes that visitors came to scout. Personal incentives to visit: personal reputation built by climbing in Arco and competing at the invitation-only RockMaster, material rewards (the 1986 RockMaster winner received 40 million lira and a car, and local hosts covered athletes' expenses in full), learning opportunities created by the growing presence of expert climbers, and positive emotions attached to the place. Community incentives to visit: exchanges of knowledge, resources, goods, and contacts; collective sensemaking and identity building around the new practice; and a distinctive mix of competitive and cooperative interactions.

Catalyzing forces — local conditions that intensify community interactions, knowledge sharing, and emotional associations in the place — worked through place-specific individual and community sharing (of technologies, practices, and rituals and memories, plus exposure to diverse actors whose business roles became role models that visitors could project themselves into), place-specific emotional energy (excitement, a sense of belonging and familiarity, and personal ambition), and spatial and temporal compression: Arco is a small (24 square miles) town of 16,000 inhabitants isolated in a valley, visits are short and seasonal, and RockMaster crowds an intense program into three days — around 35,000 of Arco's 185,000 yearly visitors (18.9%) concentrate in that window. Two locally embedded "springboard firms," Sint Roc (walls and holds) and La Sportiva (shoes), were central: their collaborations with visiting athletes on product design, development, and testing transferred climbing know-how and made business opportunities visible. These experiences were transformational, with actors morphing their identity, values, and activities to embrace a new role in the sport climbing world — for example, moving from professional climber to business professional.

The most counterintuitive result is centrifugal. The authors report being surprised to find that visits to Arco almost never turned into permanent relocations: despite the place's attractive features, those who identified a business opportunity ultimately left Arco to pursue their venture elsewhere. Three second-order themes account for the ejection. Personal incentives to leave: attachment to other meaningful locations such as hometowns, and a strive for recognition in other areas where the reputation acquired in Arco could be converted into funding, partners, and customers. Portable economies: the reputational, knowledge, and financial resources acquired in Arco were not bound to the place and could be redeployed elsewhere, sometimes gaining value in the process — RockMaster prize money that was a small amount in Italy was a large amount in the poorer economies of Eastern Europe, favoring ventures founded in weaker-currency, lower-cost countries. Local entry barriers: resource-based barriers (scarce local demand, few and often isolated spaces for industrialization, and limited venture capital, related industries, and logistics — bringing raw materials to Arco meant roughly a 3% increase in overall total costs) and competition-based barriers created by the same springboard firms, since La Sportiva had dominated local climbing footwear since the 1920s and Sint Roc was the exclusive artificial wall supplier for RockMaster and local gyms, so local demand was saturated. Springboard firms thus had a dual role: providing know-how to aspiring entrepreneurs while preempting local demand and disincentivizing new firms from colocating. Across the manufacturers, the motivations mentioned for locating away from Arco were the origin of the founder (37 mentions), favorable business conditions (27), local demand (20), favorable climbing conditions (13), prior foundation of the firm (9), and other reasons (2). The net outcome is the paper's headline result: the sport climbing industry ultimately did not cluster in Arco, the place that catalyzed it, but spread across the world.

Finally, the ongoing activities of the place and the periodic activities of the event are mutually reinforcing rather than substitutes. The event bolsters the visibility and reputation of the place and is itself an additional attraction, enhancing the centripetal forces; during the event, interactions, emotions, and engagements are more concentrated and intense, enhancing the catalyzing forces; and the event both leaves visitors with additional resources (prizes, money, knowledge, contacts, reputation) to redeploy elsewhere and, because it is time-bounded, pushes them to leave, enhancing the centrifugal forces. When the features of the place fit the aims and needs of the event, the place provides an ideal stage for the event while the event strengthens the reputation and visibility of the place.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study advances the concept of catalyzing places and a cyclical, three-force process model of industry emergence, putting "geography back on the map" for industries that are not locally clustered—a phenomenon cluster theory, with its linear-cumulative logic and emphasis on agglomeration economies and anchor firms, cannot fully explain. It introduces "portable economies" and "springboard firms" as the antithetical counterparts of "agglomeration economies" and "anchor firms," and contributes to the literatures on user entrepreneurship, new practices, institutional work (places that shape rather than are shaped by institutions), and events.

**Practical Implication**
By understanding the geographical dimensions of industry emergence ex ante, executives and policymakers can take a more proactive, informed approach to phenomena such as clustering, spillovers, and delocalization rather than addressing them ex post. The authors invite executives and policymakers to consider how targeted actions can facilitate the catalyzing role of places and events, creating generative mechanisms that support the transformation of amateur practices into businesses.

**Limitations**
The qualitative design relies on interviews that may carry informant biases or sensemaking, which the authors tried to limit through careful use of archival data, and it cannot provide a test of causality regarding the relative influence of the place versus the event. The model is primarily applicable to the industry "emergence and growth" phase and cannot explain what causes early users to first select a place that later becomes catalyzing; the authors also acknowledge alternative explanations (e.g., the rise of the Internet and modern communication closing geographic and social distances).

**Future Research**
Future studies should use different (e.g., quantitative) techniques to identify and measure new factors, mechanisms, and outcomes and to test the causal nexus between places and events with ad hoc instruments. Researchers should examine the selection and genesis of catalyzing places more systematically, and explore the catalyzing role of community-based "virtual places" (e.g., spanning academia, arts, science, and activism), whose role—amplified by COVID-19—remains unclear.
