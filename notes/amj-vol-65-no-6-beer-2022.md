# Meaning, Mission, and Measurement: How Organizational Performance Measurement Shapes Perceptions of Work as Worthy

**Abstract**
A compelling organizational mission can contribute to employees' sense of work as worthy and thereby meaningful. Yet realizing this potential depends on whether and how the mission is conveyed to employees and connected to their day-to-day work, with organizational performance measurement practices playing a critical but poorly understood role. To develop empirically grounded insights into how measurement practices shape individuals' perceptions of work as worthy, we leverage a qualitative, inductive study of two U.K. social enterprises. We find that employees' encounters with measurement practices both affirm and challenge perceptions of work as worthy by influencing whether employees can accomplish their work tasks, see the impact of their work, and have a credible and valued voice in their interpersonal interactions. Building on these findings, we develop a model that theorizes practical, existential, and relational pathways through which measurement encounters create ongoing expansions and contractions of work worthiness. Taken together, our findings and model broaden understandings of the sources and processes of meaningful work, develop a dynamic conception of meaningfulness, and point toward a more agentic view of organizational performance measurement processes.

**Research Question**
How do performance measurement practices shape employees' perceptions of work as worthy? The authors ask how employees' day-to-day encounters with formal, standardized measurement tools affirm or challenge the sense that their work is worthy and thereby meaningful.

**Mechanism Process**
- Core unit / construct: "measurement encounters" — instances in which employees engage with specific measurement tools (KPIs, SROI, outcome star, case studies, blogs) in the context of work activities and interpersonal interactions.
- Practical pathway: encounters either interfere with work tasks (burdensome data collection, irrelevant information) creating practical challenges, or "provide traction" (planning, focusing, deciding) creating practical affirmation of work worthiness.
- Existential pathway: encounters "miss the mark" when tools fail to capture mission or impact (means-ends decoupling), creating existential challenges, or "convey impact" of human/social/environmental change, creating existential affirmation.
- Relational pathway: encounters that "silence" employees (no voice in designing/using tools) create relational challenges, while those that "give voice" foster respect and high-quality connections, creating relational affirmation.
- Emergent outcome: work worthiness as continually expanding and contracting (an "elastic" quality) along the three co-occurring pathways.

The process theorizes that measurement encounters shape perceptions of work as worthy through interdependent practical, existential, and relational mechanisms grounded in whether employees can "do" their work, "see" its impact, and "connect" with others. By grounding the poetics of meaningfulness in the precision of measurement, the model explains how expressive and instrumental aspects of organizational life — usually treated as separate — are interwoven, and how worthiness dynamically contracts and expands as encounters affirm or challenge it.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: qualitative, inductive, two-case comparative study of U.K. social enterprises selected as "unusually revelatory" cases; the analysis is interpretive and process-oriented, and the paper claims no causal identification strategy.
- Case 1 — Youth Futures (YF): founded 1972 and operating in the Midlands region of the United Kingdom, with a mission to reduce homelessness and poverty by helping at-risk youth become economically independent. At the time of data collection YF employed 250 staff, served approximately 4,500 young people per year, had annual revenues of over £8.5 million, and delivered services across 27 accommodation "projects."
- Case 2 — Organic Earth (OE): founded 1958, with a mission to address health and environmental sustainability by promoting organic growing. At the time of data collection OE employed around 50 full-time staff, had 900 volunteers and approximately 40,000 active supporters, had annual revenues of £2.2 million, and was based on a 22-acre property.
- Interviews: 53 semi-structured interviews with board members, managers, frontline staff, and external stakeholders, each lasting 30–120 minutes (average 50 minutes). Of these, 33 were at YF with 31 unique individuals (8 senior managers, 6 managers, 18 frontline workers, 1 external partner) and 20 were at OE with 18 unique individuals (10 senior managers, 4 managers, 5 frontline workers, 1 external partner). All but four interviews were recorded and transcribed. Volunteers were excluded because they were only marginally involved in performance measurement.
- Observation: 27 days of day-to-day observation — 14 days at YF (10 at head office, 4 at accommodation projects) and 13 days at OE (12 at head office/gardens, 1 at programs) — plus 15 observed meetings at which performance reports were, or had the potential to be, reviewed and discussed.
- Archival data: 86 internal documents — 39 at YF (4 business plans, 14 KPI reports, 4 feedback documents, 7 external and 10 internal communications) and 47 at OE (9 business and strategy plans, 9 KPI reports, 11 project bids and evaluations, 1 member survey, 6 external and 11 internal communications).
- Focal measurement instruments (Table 2): at OE, strategic priority KPIs, the SROI ratio, the Eating Right report, the Compost Leader report, and the Garden and Grow blog; at YF, funder KPIs, support KPIs, housing KPIs, the outcome star, and case studies.
- Operationalization and coding: the "measurement encounter" was adopted as the core unit of analysis. Brief summaries and corresponding data excerpts were compiled for each encounter described in the interview and observation data, resulting in 184 encounter "cases" grouped by employee. Analysis proceeded through open (first-order) and then axial (second-order) coding into aggregate dimensions (Gioia methodology; data structure in Figure 1), with all three authors independently coding subsets of encounters and meeting to reconcile interpretations, and NVivo used for systematic analysis. Trustworthiness was addressed through triangulation across data sources, peer debriefing, member checks with informants in both organizations, thick description, stepwise replication, and a documented audit trail.

**Key Findings**
- Overall pattern: employees' encounters with measurement practices both affirm and challenge perceptions of work as worthy — the study finds no single valence for measurement. Across the two cases, the similarities in employees' experiences of measurement were more profound than the differences, so the authors unpacked those similarities to build one common explanation applying across both YF and OE rather than contrasting them.
- Practical pathway: encounters interfered with work when the data collection process was burdensome, when information from measurement tools was not relevant to employees' work activities, or when that information could not be used to make decisions and take action; this created a practical challenge to work worthiness, with employees feeling discouraged and ineffective in making progress toward the mission. Conversely, encounters provided traction when tools helped employees develop a plan, understand a work problem, or decide how to allocate resources or time, creating a practical affirmation expressed as a sense of accomplishment and efficacy.
- Existential pathway: encounters "missed the mark" when information from measurement tools was not relevant for assessing human, social, or environmental change, did not show interim progress toward the mission, or did not show the employee's contribution to accomplishing it — a means–ends decoupling — creating an existential challenge in which employees felt uncertain about their contributions and impact. Encounters conveyed impact when tools provided evidence of mission accomplishment, interim progress, or the employee's contribution, creating an existential affirmation expressed as pride in oneself and the organization.
- Relational pathway: encounters silenced employees when tools were designed and implemented without employee involvement, or when performance information was used to monitor and critically evaluate them without opportunities for input; this created a relational challenge in which employees did not feel respected or valued by others. Encounters gave voice when employees were included in the design, implementation, and active use of tools and when performance information was discussed collaboratively, creating a relational affirmation in which employees felt respected and valued by their interaction partners.
- Cross-case difference: silencing was more widespread in the data from YF, which the authors attribute in part to the extensive safeguards and regulations YF had to follow given the beneficiaries it served, leading to tools that allowed only very limited employee participation and input. Silencing was quite rare in the OE data, where only one senior manager described an encounter in which she felt silenced.
- Dynamics: the three pathways are analytically distinct but not mutually exclusive and in practice co-occur, sometimes within the same instrument and at the same moment. For some employees experiences across multiple encounters were all of the same valence, but in many cases individuals experienced a mixture of positive and negative experiences. From these patterns the authors theorize work worthiness as continually expanding and contracting along the three pathways, implying that work meaningfulness has an "elastic quality" whose contours stretch and shrink even as it retains its overall shape.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study brings meaningfulness and measurement together by theorizing mechanisms through which formal performance measurement practices not only challenge but also affirm meaningfulness, broadening the recognized sources of meaningful work to include highly standardized tools (not just job crafting or leader sensegiving). It advances a dynamic, "elastic" conception of work worthiness as continually expanding and contracting, and it points toward a more agentic view of measurement — a shift from measurement as a noun (control) to measuring as a verb (employees actively shaping and benefiting from measurement processes).

**Practical Implication**
Performance measurement practices can foster meaningful work, and collaborative, inclusive approaches to designing measurement tools (engaging employees across levels and functions, plus resource providers and beneficiaries) can produce instruments that better reflect users' needs and help them progress toward the mission. Involving beneficiaries in data collection can convey impact and thereby affirm work worthiness. Practitioners should adopt a more expansive view of measurement that takes account of its expressive, not just instrumental, purposes.

**Limitations**
The two cases are "extreme," revelatory organizations with strong social/environmental missions and well-developed measurement practices, which aids theory building but raises questions about generalizability. The core mechanisms, though likely to apply more broadly, may vary in strength and prevalence across settings — for example, they may be less influential in organizations with more limited and homogenous measurement practices, and the balance of challenge versus affirmation may differ with mission content and employees' attachment to the mission.

**Future Research**
The authors encourage studies that refine and extend the insights across a wider variety of settings and that leverage variation across organizations to identify managerial practices and individual differences (e.g., sensegiving by managers/funders, comfort with quantitative data, expertise and status) that foster worth-affirming versus worth-challenging encounters. They call for deeper investigation of how affirming and challenging experiences interact and cumulate over time, recommending longitudinal studies to examine how past experiences shape present responses and buffer challenges to work worthiness.
