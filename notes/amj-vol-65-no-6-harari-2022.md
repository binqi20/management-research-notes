# When Helping Hurts Helpers: Anticipatory versus Reactive Helping, Helper’s Relative Status, and Recipient Self-Threat

**Abstract**
Research on workplace helping has suggested that helpers receive positive outcomes from recipients in return for their help. We argue that this predominantly positive view of recipient reactions to helpers is because the literature has not adequately distinguished the outcomes of reactive helping (i.e., assistance provided in response to a request) from those of anticipatory helping (i.e., assistance offered or provided in advance of being asked). We propose that anticipatory helping, especially from helpers with higher status than the recipient, is more self-threatening to recipients than is reactive helping; hence, recipients are less likely to accept this help and more likely to lower their evaluations of both the helper’s performance and their relationship with the helper. We find support for these hypotheses with four studies and one supplemental study that use experimental and field methodologies across a range of work contexts and social exchange relationships. Because our findings imply that both peer- and higher-status employees should withhold anticipatory help, which is impractical and potentially detrimental, we identify how these helpers can instead mitigate the negative effects of anticipatory helping by signaling a more balanced social exchange relationship with the recipient. We discuss how our findings expand research on helping, social exchange, and status.

**Research Question**
Why and when does workplace helping lead to *negative* rather than positive reactions toward the helper? Specifically, how does anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping affect recipients' self-threat, help acceptance, and performance and relational evaluations of the helper, and how do the helper's relative status and the balance of the social exchange relationship moderate these effects?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. Anticipatory helping will be more self-threatening to recipients than reactive helping.
H2. Anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping will have a negative indirect effect through recipient self-threat on (a) the recipient's acceptance of help from the helper, (b) the recipient's performance evaluation of the helper, and (c) the recipient's relational evaluation of the helper.
H3. The negative indirect effects of anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping through recipients' self-threat on (a) acceptance of help, (b) performance evaluation of the helper, and (c) relational evaluation of the helper will be stronger (weaker) when the helper has higher (lower) status than the recipient.
H4. The positive effect of the higher-status helper's anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping on the recipient's self-threat will be weaker (stronger) the more balanced (unbalanced) the social exchange relationship is.
H5. The negative indirect effects of the higher-status helper's anticipatory (versus reactive) helping through the recipient's self-threat on (a) acceptance of help, (b) performance evaluation of the helper, and (c) relational evaluation of the helper will be weaker (stronger) the more balanced (unbalanced) the social exchange relationship is.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Helping type (anticipatory vs. reactive helping)
- DV(s): Recipient's willingness to accept help; performance evaluation of the helper (including bonus allocation); relational evaluation (likeability) of the helper
- Mediator: Recipient self-threat (perceived challenge to competence and status)
- Moderators: Helper's relative status (lower vs. peer vs. higher than recipient); balance of the social exchange relationship (and a signaled past/future reciprocity balance in Study 4)

Integrating social exchange theory with self-affirmation theory, the authors argue that anticipatory help (offered without being requested) signals to recipients that their competence is doubted or their status is challenged, generating self-threat. Threatened recipients defensively protect their self-worth by refusing or dismissing the help and by derogating the helper's performance and likeability. Because people weight evaluations from higher-status others more heavily, these self-threatening effects are stronger when the helper has higher (or peer) rather than lower status. Recipients who perceive (Study 3) or are shown a signal of (Study 4) a balanced, mutually reciprocal exchange relationship experience less self-threat, which mitigates the negative downstream consequences of anticipatory helping.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1a (randomized scenario experiment, n = 1,052 of 1,334 working adults recruited from the United States via Prolific Academic, after excluding 252 low-effort responders and 30 who were not currently working): 2 (helping: anticipatory vs. reactive) x 3 (helper's relative status: lower vs. peer vs. higher) between-subjects vignette set in a fictional marketing services firm, "Finch & Baker." Self-threat = 6 items adapted from Burris (2012) (alpha = .93); willingness to accept help = 4 items (alpha = .97); performance evaluation of the helper = 2 items adapted from Griffin et al. (2007) (alpha = .89) plus a behavioral measure, the size of an optional $0-100 bonus allocated to the helper; relational evaluation = likable / friendly / warm (alpha = .95). Manipulation checks used a 5-item relative-status scale adapted from Djurdjevic et al. (2017) (alpha = .99) and 3-item reactive and anticipatory helping checks. Unconditional and conditional path models estimated in Mplus 8.3 with maximum likelihood; indirect effects via bias-corrected bootstrapping with 10,000 draws. Random assignment gives this study a causal test of helping type and helper status.
- Supplemental Study 1b (separate sample of 1,094 participants): same design but status manipulated through status characteristics (e.g., tenure, job title), with embarrassment and perceived social worth added as alternative parallel mediators.
- Study 2 (field study in a consulting division of a multinational professional services firm located in the United Kingdom; final matched sample of 64 unique helper-recipient dyads, 63 for self-threat because of missing data): independent variables came from helpers and the mediator and outcomes from recipients, making this a cross-sectional, associational matched-dyad design rather than a causal one. New five-item anticipatory and reactive helping scales were developed first (content validation n = 394; exploratory factor analysis n = 192; confirmatory factor analysis n = 198) and then used in the main collection, where helpers rated the frequency of reactive (alpha = .99) and anticipatory (alpha = .97) helping toward the recipient. Helper's relative status = difference score of helper status minus recipient status from a 2-item adaptation of Djurdjevic et al.'s (2017) workplace status scale (difference-score reliability .92). Recipient measures: self-threat = 3 items (alpha = .93), help accepted = 2 items (alpha = .90), performance evaluation = 2 items (alpha = .89), relational evaluation = 3 items (alpha = .87). Controls: formal-rank dummies (six organizational levels from associate to partner) and interaction frequency.
- Study 3 (critical-incident recall study, n = 629 of 674 working adults recruited via Prolific Academic, after excluding 5 who had not experienced the interaction and 40 low-effort responders): participants were randomly assigned to recall and describe either a reactive or an anticipatory helping incident with a named coworker. Helper's relative status was self-classified as higher (26.1%), equal (53.2%), or lower (20.7%). Self-threat = 6 items (alpha = .90); help accepted = 4 items (alpha = .93); performance evaluation = 2 items (alpha = .95); relational evaluation = 3 items (alpha = .94); balanced social exchange = 5 items adapted from Bernerth et al. (2007) with the referent changed to coworker (alpha = .85); interaction frequency as control.
- Study 4 (randomized online experiment, 1,500 participants): 3 (helping: reactive vs. anticipatory vs. balanced anticipatory) x 2 (helper status: peer vs. higher) between-subjects design using Study 1a's helping manipulations and outcome measures and Supplemental Study 1b's status-characteristics manipulation. The new balanced anticipatory helping condition added a signal of past or future reciprocity to the helper's offer. No lower-status helper condition was included.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1a: H1 supported - anticipatory helping increased recipient self-threat relative to reactive helping (B = 0.79, SE = 0.08, p < .001). H2a-c supported - anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping had negative indirect effects via self-threat on willingness to accept help (-.43, 95% CI [-0.53, -0.34]), performance evaluation (-.30, [-0.38, -0.23]), bonus allocated (-4.01, [-5.55, -2.75]), and relational evaluation (-.24, [-0.30, -0.18]). H3 supported - the anticipatory helping x higher-status interaction on self-threat was significant (B = 0.75, SE = 0.19, p < .001), as was the anticipatory helping x peer-status interaction (B = 0.41, SE = 0.19, p < .05), and the negative indirect effects were significantly stronger for higher- than lower-status helpers on all four outcomes. The higher- versus peer-status contrast on self-threat did not reach conventional significance (B = 0.34, SE = 0.19, p = .07).
- Supplemental Study 1b: supported H1-H3 even with embarrassment and perceived social worth included as parallel mediators.
- Study 2: H1 supported - anticipatory helping had a positive main effect on recipient self-threat (B = 0.33, SE = 0.15, p < .05) while reactive helping did not (B = 0.17, SE = 0.13, p = .20), and anticipatory helping explained more incremental variance in self-threat (.070) than reactive helping (.017). Support for H2 was partial: only H2b and H2c were supported (unconditional negative indirect effects on performance evaluation -.10, [-0.26, -0.004]; relational evaluation -.08, [-0.24, -0.003]); H2a was not supported, because self-threat did not significantly predict help accepted (B = -0.05, SE = 0.14, p = .87) and the indirect effect on help accepted was non-significant (-.02, [-0.17, 0.05]). Support for H3 was likewise partial: the anticipatory helping x relative status interaction on self-threat was significant (B = 0.12, SE = 0.05, p = .01) and the conditional indirect effects were negative and significant at higher helper status for performance evaluation (-.19, [-0.46, -0.03]) and relational evaluation (-.15, [-0.42, -0.02]) but not at lower status, so the paper claims support for H3b and H3c only. H4 and H5 were not tested in Study 2.
- Study 3: H1 and H2 supported - anticipatory (vs. reactive) helping significantly increased self-threat (B = 0.30, SE = 0.07, p < .001), and the indirect effects via self-threat were negative on help accepted (-.11, [-0.19, -0.05]), performance evaluation (-.15, [-0.24, -0.08]), and relational evaluation (-.12, [-0.20, -0.06]). H3 supported - significant interactions of helping condition with higher-status (B = 0.63, SE = 0.21, p < .01) and peer-status (B = 0.47, SE = 0.18, p < .05) helpers, with no anticipatory-versus-reactive difference in self-threat for lower-status helpers (B = -0.11, SE = 0.16, p = .47). H4 supported - the three-way interactions with balanced social exchange were significant for higher-status (B = -0.70, SE = 0.19, p < .001) and peer-status (B = -0.62, SE = 0.17, p < .001) helpers, with anticipatory helping raising self-threat only at low balance. H5 supported - the negative indirect effects were significant at low balance and absent at high balance, and the differences between high and low balance were significant for all three outcomes.
- Study 4: signaling a balanced exchange reduced but did not remove the threat. Because self-threat did not differ between the higher- and peer-status helper conditions (p > .40), status conditions were collapsed and relative status controlled. Anticipatory helping that signaled balance was significantly less self-threatening (M = 2.90, SD = 1.32) than anticipatory helping without such a signal (M = 3.17, SD = 1.40), t(1496) = -3.20, p < .01, but was still significantly more self-threatening than reactive helping (M = 2.31, SD = 1.19), t(1496) = 7.03, p < .001. Signaling balance had positive indirect effects on willingness to accept help (.17, [0.06, 0.29]), performance evaluation (.11, [0.04, 0.19]), and relational evaluation (.10, [0.04, 0.16]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper distinguishes anticipatory from reactive helping and shows that the helping literature's "helping pays off" conclusion applies primarily to reactive helping, offering a self-threat explanation for when positive helping turns negative. It extends social exchange theory by specifying why and when a positively valenced act (anticipatory helping) elicits a negative response rather than reciprocity. It advances status research by identifying an exception to the premise that higher-status actors enjoy greater latitude—here, higher-status helpers provoke stronger defensive reactions when helping in anticipatory fashion.

**Practical Implication**
Lower-status employees can offer anticipatory help—a more assertive, socially risky behavior—without triggering defensive recipient reactions, giving them an avenue to contribute without backlash. Peer- and higher-status helpers should pause before offering unrequested help and can reduce its threat by signaling a balanced exchange (e.g., framing help as reciprocating past or future assistance). Leaders can build helping norms by encouraging and rewarding help-seeking, help-giving, and help-accepting.

**Limitations**
Absolute self-threat levels were low (though consistent with prior research and differential across conditions), partly because people are reluctant to admit feeling threatened. The field study (Study 2) had a small sample (n=64 dyads) and operationalized relative status as a difference score, raising reliability and generalizability concerns. Outcomes were rated by direct recipients rather than third-party observers, which could alter the effects.

**Future Research**
Future work could use manipulations of the psychological process to circumvent recipients' reluctance to self-report threat, and compare recipient versus third-party observer ratings of helper outcomes. Researchers could apply the newly developed anticipatory and reactive helping scales in field settings, examine additional moderators (e.g., recipient workload, helping norms, interactional justice, whether help is seen as controlling), and study how recipients value reactive versus anticipatory help over time.
