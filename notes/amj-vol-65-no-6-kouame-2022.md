# Creating and Sustaining Stakeholder Emotional Resonance with Organizational Identity in Social Mission-Driven Organizations

**Abstract**
How do senior managers of social mission-driven organizations build and sustain stakeholders’ emotional resonance with organizational identity beliefs over time in the face of repeated existential threats? This is an important question, given the dependence of many such organizations on external stakeholders who provide the resources necessary for survival. In this paper, we investigate the case of Solidum, a philanthropic organization devoted to poverty causes. Drawing on ethnographic, interview, and archival data over 20 years, we develop a process model showing how senior managers may create and sustain stakeholder emotional resonance through three practices of emotional resonance work: (a) building emotional bridges, (b) enrolling stakeholders in collective soul-searching, and (c) materializing an appealing identity symbol. We show that stakeholder emotional resonance needs to be continually renewed and reshaped in the face of ongoing challenges associated with macro-organizational trends and the routinization of existing practices that can result in the dissipation of emotional resonance over time. The paper contributes to the literature on organizational identity maintenance by drawing attention to the active managerial work required to sustain stakeholder emotional resonance over time to allow mission-driven organizations to survive and prosper.

**Research Question**
How do senior managers of social mission-driven organizations build and sustain stakeholders’ emotional resonance with organizational identity beliefs over time in the face of repeated existential threats?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: stakeholder emotional resonance with organizational identity beliefs; tension between preserving a "caring" core identity/mission and ceding to corporate donors' pressure for "designated donations" (mission-drift risk).
- Practices (emotional resonance work): (1) building emotional bridges — seeding resonance via direct immersion of stakeholders in mission activities, stimulating identification; (2) enrolling in collective soul-searching — engaging central stakeholders in sharing existential worries and joint deliberation; (3) materializing an appealing identity symbol — rendering identity beliefs tangible to propagate resonance to a wider community.
- Triggering conditions: recurring existential/identity threats (strategic CSR, financial crisis, generational change), each provoking interest in "designation."
- Effects (proximal to distal): displayed emotions (compassion, shock, enthusiasm); expressed alignment/commitment to Solidum's cause and identity; shifts in wider-community donations and participation.
- Process dynamic: the three practices have distinct rhythms, can fall out of sync, and resonance dissipates through routinization and macro trends, requiring continual renewal.

Through interaction with central corporate stakeholders, senior managers iteratively seed, amplify, and propagate emotional resonance with the organization's identity beliefs. Identification is the underpinning mechanism: bridging stimulates it, enrolling consolidates it, and materializing gives stakeholders tangible tools to act as "evangelists" toward the wider community. Because resonance is fleeting and dissipates over time, this "emotional resonance work" is a never-ending cycle punctuated by recurring existential threats.

**Data & Measures**
Longitudinal single-case study of Solidum (pseudonym), a public foundation in "CapitalCity" (a large North American city) that mediates between mostly corporate donors and a 350-strong network of small community organizations. Table 1 reports the 20-year corpus as 117 meeting observations, 182 semi-structured interviews, 5,525 pages of archival data, and 16 videos, assembled in five tranches: retrospective archival data for 1994-1996 (643 pages); a first intensive wave in 1997-2003 (82 interviews — 35 internal, 47 with external stakeholders; 61 meeting observations; 2,577 archival pages); follow-up data for 2004-2010 (8 workshops and meetings; 997 pages); a second intensive wave in 2011-2016 (81 interviews — 70 internal, 11 with key external stakeholders; 48 observations; 1,183 pages); and follow-up data for 2018-2021 (19 interviews — 5 with managers and professionals, 14 with key external stakeholders; 144 pages plus 16 testimonial videos). Formal interviews lasted 30-120 minutes, with 80% recorded and transcribed and extensive notes taken on the remaining 20%. Minutes of 548 TMT and board meetings from 1994 to 2015 were collected retrospectively, from which 206 meetings where actors explicitly discussed tensions surrounding Solidum's core identity beliefs were identified. Researcher roles followed an insider-outsider design: the second author was an action researcher (1997-2000), then ethnographer and participant observer (2000-2011), then board member (2012-2018); the first author joined in 2011 as a nonparticipant observer; the third and fourth authors joined in 2016 as "outsider" researchers. Analysis was abductive and unfolded in five steps: building an event chronology and grouping key episodes into three periods used as units of analysis via comparative "temporal brackets" (Langley, 1999); exploratory coding of senior managers' work on identity challenges; coding evidence of emotional resonance using Giorgi's (2017) cognitive-versus-emotional resonance distinction; coding the three practices of emotional resonance work plus three types of effect indicators running from proximal (displayed emotions such as compassion, shock, enthusiasm) through expressed alignment and commitment to distal metrics of wider-community donations and participation; and developing the conceptual (Figure 2) and empirical (Figure 3) models. Two authors first coded a sample of data separately to check convergence before collective discussion, and interpretations were validated with informants (former and current CEOs, other senior managers, key stakeholders) at three stages of analysis (2016, 2018, 2020). The design is interpretive and theory-building; no causal identification is claimed.

**Key Findings**
The study induces a process model of "emotional resonance work": facing a perceived existential threat arising from weakened emotional resonance of identity beliefs, senior managers repeatedly enact three practices with central stakeholders (corporate leaders) — building emotional bridges (seeding resonance and stimulating identification), enrolling in collective soul-searching (consolidating identification), and materializing an appealing identity symbol (making identity beliefs tangible so leaders can propagate resonance to the wider community). The authors report the most intense emotional resonance effects for bridging, in which stakeholders were brought into direct contact with the organization's activities; enrolling consolidated that identification; and materializing supplied the tools to act on it and evangelize others. The pattern differs across the three periods. In Period 1 (1996-2007) all three practices were enacted: neighborhood visits and a philanthropy forum moved corporate leaders who had initially favored designated donations toward Solidum's caring identity, the "door should be closed definitively on the option of designation," and the materialized "solidarity donation" — validated by the board in June 2003 — was seen as successful, particularly among corporate leaders, and increased emotional resonance around the caring mission. In Period 2 (2008-2012) the process was truncated: bridging and enrolling continued but materializing was not renewed, the solidarity donation being treated as a "sacred cow" that could not be questioned, and with the 2008 financial crisis and generational change resonance dissipated with the wider community — some large companies' support was lost, employee donations declined, and 2012 brought Solidum's greatest decrease in donations in over 20 years, a decline that continued the following year. In Period 3 (2013-2018) all three practices were re-enacted in a different order — rematerializing the symbol as the "directed donation" (four broad cause categories plus a fifth "common fund"), re-enrolling corporate leaders including those who had pushed for designation, and rebuilding emotional bridges to reach employees directly via the Solidum Plus pilot — and resonance was renewed: the pilot was initially deployed in 11 organizations and soon expanded to 30 large corporations, reaching more than 100,000 employees over its first three years; all the firms running the new-concept campaign exceeded their donation goals, some by as much as 20%; and 90% of employees chose the common fund during the electronic campaigns, so the fear that donors would abandon it for specific causes was not borne out. The overarching finding is that stakeholder emotional resonance is not a one-off achievement: because the three practices carry distinct rhythms and can fall out of sync, and because materializations lose freshness while macro-organizational trends shift, resonance dissipates over time unless it is continually renewed and reshaped.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the literature on organizational identity maintenance and stakeholder relations by unpacking the active, ongoing managerial work required to sustain stakeholder emotional resonance with identity beliefs over long periods, rather than at single moments of crisis or pivot. Its core contribution is identifying three interrelated practices of "stakeholder emotional resonance work" — bridging, enrolling, and materializing — and theorizing how they operate together (with distinct rhythms) to seed, amplify, and propagate emotional resonance, framing this as a form of social-symbolic work that must be continually renewed.

**Practical Implication**
Managers of social mission-driven organizations can engage central stakeholders' emotions to build support for core identity beliefs by bringing them into direct contact with the mission, enrolling them in collective soul-searching, and materializing identity in concrete symbols. They should reflect on the trade-off between acceding to immediate stakeholder demands and preserving the organization's valued mission, and recognize that emotional resonance is powerful but fleeting and must be continually renewed to stay fresh.

**Limitations**
The study was conducted in the specific setting of a single philanthropic organization, and access to data was uneven across time (Periods 1 and 3 were more intense and better documented than Period 2). The stratified, closely intertwined nature of Solidum's stakeholder network may be somewhat idiosyncratic, and the second researcher's involvement as an action researcher in Period 1 required reflexivity, though the authors argue it did not substantively bias the findings.

**Future Research**
The authors hope further research will build on these ideas to develop studies of emotional resonance work in other social-mission-based organizations, including settings where key stakeholders are more widely dispersed and less intertwined with the organization. They also suggest examining how emotional resonance work might be enacted to promote entirely new ventures and how recurring tension points might be anticipated or avoided.
