# It’s Not Always Sunny in Relationally Rich Jobs: The Influence of Negative Beneficiary Contact

**Abstract**
Contact with beneficiaries has been described as an important job characteristic for shaping perceptions about the social value of work; however, little is known about how to navigate experiences in which contact with beneficiaries is negative, which can explicitly undermine the conclusion that work is socially valuable. We draw from two tenets of social information processing theory to propose that negative contact with beneficiaries has a dual effect on employees. Whereas negative contact may make employees perceive low social worth, it may simultaneously lead employees to believe they are engaging in self-sacrifice for a worthy cause—a relatively positive justification of such experiences. We investigated these ideas in three studies. In Study 1, a three-wave survey of registered nurses and their supervisors supported the hypothesized dual effect. Further consistent with our theorizing, the effect of perceived self-sacrifice on job satisfaction and performance was contingent on coworker emotional support: with higher support, perceived self-sacrifice exhibited a null relationship with satisfaction and a positive relationship with performance; with lower support, these effects were negative. In Study 2, we again studied nurses using an experimental vignette method, showing that negative contact exhibits a causal effect on employee perceptions, and that negative contact is more likely to lead to perceived self-sacrifice when the contact is attributed to the nature of the work versus one’s own performance. In Study 3, a two-wave survey of people from various occupations replicated the effects of negative contact on perceived social worth and perceived self-sacrifice. Moreover, the effect of negative contact on sacrifice was contingent on affective commitment to beneficiaries.

**Research Question**
How does negative contact with beneficiaries affect employees' perceptions of the social value of their work, and under what conditions can employees derive positive meaning from such negative contact? Specifically, when is negative beneficiary contact rationalized as self-sacrifice, and when does that rationalization produce greater satisfaction and performance rather than less?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Negative beneficiary contact is negatively related to perceived social worth.
- Hypothesis 2. Negative beneficiary contact is positively related to perceived self-sacrifice.
- Hypothesis 3. Perceived social worth is positively related to job satisfaction.
- Hypothesis 4. Perceived social worth is positively related to (a) job performance and (b) helping behavior.
- Hypothesis 5. Coworker emotional support moderates the relationship between perceived self-sacrifice and job satisfaction, such that the relationship is more positive at higher levels of support.
- Hypothesis 6. Coworker emotional support moderates the relationship between perceived self-sacrifice and (a) job performance and (b) helping behavior, such that the relationship is more positive at higher levels of support.
- Hypothesis 7. An attribution that a high level of negative contact is caused by the nature of the work will be associated with higher levels of perceived self-sacrifice than will a performance attribution.
- Hypothesis 8. Affective commitment to beneficiaries will moderate the relationship between negative beneficiary contact and perceived self-sacrifice, such that the relationship is more positive at high levels of affective commitment.

Hypotheses 1–6 were developed before Study 1 and tested there. Hypotheses 7 and 8 were developed after Study 1 to address boundary conditions on the first stage of the model, and were tested in Study 2 and Study 3 respectively.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Negative beneficiary contact (also positive beneficiary contact as a comparison predictor); manipulated negative-contact and attribution conditions (Study 2)
- DV(s): Job satisfaction, task performance (job performance), helping behavior; with perceived social worth and perceived self-sacrifice as proximal outcomes/mechanisms
- Mediators: Perceived social worth (information-saliency path) and perceived self-sacrifice (rationalization path) linking negative contact to employee outcomes
- Moderators: Coworker emotional support (self-sacrifice → satisfaction/performance, Study 1); attribution for contact — nature-of-work vs. own performance (negative contact → self-sacrifice, Study 2); affective commitment to beneficiaries (negative contact → self-sacrifice, Study 3)

Drawing on two distinct tenets of social information processing theory—information saliency and the relatively neglected rationalization tenet—the authors argue that negative beneficiary contact conveys salient information lowering perceived social worth, while simultaneously prompting employees to rationalize the work as self-sacrifice for a worthy cause. Because self-sacrifice carries both a sense of victimhood and a sense of contribution, its effect on satisfaction and performance is ambiguous and depends on coworker emotional support, which lets employees focus on the contribution side rather than the cost. Rationalizing contact as self-sacrifice is more likely when employees attribute it to the nature of the work (an external, credible attribution) and when they feel strong affective commitment to beneficiaries.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1: a three-wave, time-lagged field survey of registered nurses and their supervisors at a large academic medical center in the Midwestern United States, with each employee survey separated by two weeks and supervisor evaluations collected two weeks after the third employee survey; 328 employees agreed to participate and 262 (80%) provided data usable under a full-information maximum likelihood approach, nested within 28 supervisors or units (89% female, average age 32.5, SD = 10.9). Unless indicated otherwise, measures were rated from 1 ("strongly disagree") to 7 ("strongly agree"). Negative beneficiary contact (the focal IV) was measured with 18 items (α = .96) from Wang, Liao, Zhan, and Shi (2011) capturing customer-related social stressors and aggressive or unfair customer behaviors, with patients and/or patients' families as the referent. Perceived social worth was measured with three items (α = .84) from Grant and Gino (2010); perceived self-sacrifice with eight items (α = .93) taken from the leader self-sacrifice literature and re-referenced to the employee–beneficiary relationship; coworker emotional support (the moderator) with three items from Colbert et al. (2016) (α = .93). Job satisfaction used a 3-item measure from Cammann, Fichman, Jenkins, and Klesh (1979) (α = .95), and nurse managers rated task performance with seven items from Williams and Anderson (1991) (α = .93) and helping with six items from Lee and Allen (2002) (α = .93). Trait positive affect (α = .79) and trait negative affect (α = .78) were measured with five items each from the short-form PANAS and modeled as covariates, and positive beneficiary contact was measured with two Grant and Gino (2010) items plus four adapted items (α = .95). Hypotheses were tested by structural equation modeling with a mean-centered product indicator for the perceived self-sacrifice × coworker support latent interaction and a dummy variable for each manager (a "fixed effects" approach) to absorb rater nonindependence, because a multilevel model was not identified with only 28 second-level units. The design is time-lagged and associational rather than causal: the authors state that their ability to make causal inferences is limited by the study design.

Study 2: a randomized experimental vignette study of 336 employed registered nurses — 285 from a nursing college alumni pool and 51 from a healthcare system near a large metropolitan area — with mean age 44 (SD = 15), mean professional tenure 20 years (SD = 14), 95% women and 91% White. Participants imagined having completed two years in a job virtually identical to their current nursing job and were randomly assigned to one of three vignettes: high negative contact attributed to the nature of the work (n = 111), high negative contact attributed to their own job performance (n = 111), or a low negative contact condition built from neutral O*NET descriptions of generic nursing tasks (n = 114); the two attribution groups aggregate to the high negative contact group (n = 222). Manipulation checks used five negative-contact items drawn from the Study 1 scale plus two items each for the nature-of-work and performance attributions. Perceived social worth and perceived self-sacrifice used the same Study 1 scales adapted to the scenario on a 7-point scale, and a 3-item realism check from Klotz and Bolino (2016) used a 5-point scale. Because a factorial design was inappropriate given the vignette content, hypotheses were tested with two separate multivariate ANOVAs (high vs. low negative contact; nature-of-work vs. performance attribution) with social worth and self-sacrifice as the dependent variables. Random assignment gives this study causal identification of the contact and attribution manipulations.

Study 3: a two-wave survey of participants recruited on the Prolific platform from the United States who worked full-time in a "customer-facing" position, spanning a wide variety of occupations (customer service representatives, salespeople, clerks, analysts, healthcare professionals, inspectors, educators, accountants); 350 participants were recruited for the first survey, 324 (93%) completed the second, and all 350 were used in analysis via full-information maximum likelihood procedures. Mean age was 34.5 (SD = 9.7), mean job tenure 7.3 years (SD = 7.0), 47% were women, and 78% were White; response quality was screened with a reCAPTCHA question, an attention check, and a free-response question asking respondents to describe their job. Survey 1 measured beneficiary contact and trait affect with the Study 1 instruments plus affective commitment to beneficiaries (the moderator) using a 3-item scale from Grant et al. (2007) rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Survey 2, administered one week later, measured perceived social worth and perceived self-sacrifice with the identical Study 1 scales, with respondents explicitly told that "customer" referred to people who benefit from or are directly affected by their work. Analysis was confirmatory factor analysis followed by structural equation modeling using the same parceling approach as Study 1, with trait positive and negative affect as covariates; like Study 1, this design is associational rather than causal.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 262; standardized structural coefficients) supported the hypothesized dual effect. Hypothesis 1 was supported: negative beneficiary contact exhibited a negative relationship with perceived social worth (β = −.26, SE = .07, p < .01). Hypothesis 2 was supported: negative contact exhibited a positive relationship with perceived self-sacrifice (β = .52, SE = .07, p < .01); positive contact related positively to social worth (β = .49, SE = .06, p < .01). Hypothesis 3 was supported: perceived social worth had a significant positive relationship with job satisfaction (β = .20, SE = .08, p = .02). Hypotheses 4a and 4b were **not** supported: perceived social worth exhibited null effects on task performance (β = −.04, SE = .09, p = .60) and helping behavior (β = .00, SE = .08, p = .99). Hypotheses 5, 6a, and 6b were supported: the perceived self-sacrifice × coworker emotional support interaction significantly predicted job satisfaction (β = .14, SE = .06, p = .02), task performance (β = .20, SE = .06, p < .01), and helping behavior (β = .22, SE = .06, p < .01). Simple slopes showed that for job satisfaction, perceived self-sacrifice was a significant negative predictor at low (−1 SD) support (β = −.13, SE = .05, p = .01) and attenuated to a null relationship at high (+1 SD) support (β = .01, SE = .05, p = .80); for task performance it was marginally negative at −1 SD (β = −.09, SE = .05, p = .07) and significantly positive at +1 SD (β = .11, SE = .05, p = .04); for helping behavior it was marginally negative at −1 SD (β = −.12, SE = .07, p = .09) and significantly positive at +1 SD (β = .16, SE = .07, p = .03). The indirect effect of negative contact on satisfaction via perceived social worth was significantly negative (−.04, 95% CI [−.10, −.01]), whereas the indirect effect of positive contact was significantly positive (.10, 95% CI [.02, .20]); the index of moderated mediation was significant for task performance (.06, 95% CI [.01, .11]) and helping behavior (.09, 95% CI [.02, .16]) but not for job satisfaction (.05, 95% CI [−.01, .10]).

Study 2 (N = 336 nurses; multivariate ANOVA) again supported Hypotheses 1 and 2 under random assignment: those in the high negative contact condition reported lower perceived social worth (M = 2.63, SD = 1.45) than those in the low negative contact condition (M = 5.77, SD = 0.93; F[1, 334] = 430.01, p < .01) and higher perceived self-sacrifice (M = 4.31, SD = 1.33 vs. M = 2.95, SD = 1.17; F[1, 334] = 84.77, p < .01). Hypothesis 7 was supported: those in the nature-of-work attribution condition reported higher perceived self-sacrifice (M = 4.91, SD = 1.01) than those in the performance attribution condition (M = 3.71, SD = 1.34; F[1, 223] = 58.13, p < .01); they also reported higher perceived social worth (M = 3.18, SD = 1.49 vs. M = 2.06, SD = 1.17; F[1, 223] = 38.85, p < .01), a result the authors flag as notable rather than hypothesized. Hypotheses 3 through 6 were not tested in Study 2.

Study 3 (N = 350 occupationally diverse customer-facing workers; standardized structural coefficients) replicated the first stage of the model. Hypothesis 1 was supported: negative beneficiary contact had a significant negative effect on perceived social worth (β = −.22, SE = .05, p < .01). Hypothesis 2 was supported: it had a significant positive effect on perceived self-sacrifice (β = .17, SE = .06, p < .01). Hypothesis 8 was supported: the interaction of negative contact with affective commitment to beneficiaries had a significant positive effect on self-sacrifice (β = .16, SE = .07, p = .03); at low levels (−1 SD) of commitment negative contact had a null relationship with self-sacrifice (β = .02, SE = .09, p = .82), while at high commitment (maximum value) the relationship was significantly positive (β = .32, SE = .07, p < .01). A post hoc regression found no significant interaction between sex and negative beneficiary contact in predicting self-sacrifice, but women in this sample reported significantly lower self-sacrifice (F[1, 322] = 6.68, p = .01) and lower social worth (F[1, 322] = 6.31, p = .01) than men.

Support pattern across studies: Hypotheses 1 and 2 were supported in all three studies (nurse field survey, nurse vignette experiment, occupationally diverse two-wave survey). Hypotheses 3, 5, 6a, and 6b were tested and supported only in Study 1, which is also the only study to test Hypotheses 4a and 4b — both rejected, so perceived social worth predicted job satisfaction but not task performance or helping behavior. Hypothesis 7 was tested and supported only in Study 2, and Hypothesis 8 only in Study 3; Studies 2 and 3 addressed only the first stage of the model and did not retest the perceived self-sacrifice → outcome effects.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends relational job design and the meaningful-work literature by theorizing and testing the effects of negative (rather than positive) beneficiary contact on the perceived social value of work, surfacing perceived self-sacrifice as a key mechanism. It highlights a duality in relational job design—contact both makes work's value salient and exposes employees to value-threatening information—and shifts the meaningful-work literature from a "realization" perspective toward an understudied "justification" perspective (Lepisto & Pratt, 2017). It also reconceptualizes self-sacrifice as an interpersonal cognitive frame born of justification rather than a collective leadership behavior, and shows affective mechanisms (commitment, coworker support) are central to how employees make meaning of negative contact.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should help frontline employees develop justifiable accounts of negative work experiences (e.g., a self-sacrifice account) and cultivate supportive coworker relationships so that self-sacrifice translates into performance rather than disillusionment. Because self-sacrifice interpretations are credible only when employees are affectively committed to beneficiaries and when contact is genuinely inherent to the work, managers should foster such commitment and avoid manipulatively encouraging external attributions for poor performance; organizations weighing job redesign that increases beneficiary contact should weigh the costs of exposure to negative contact against its motivational benefits.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 were confined to nursing—a gendered, calling-prototypic occupation with likely range restriction on affective commitment—limiting generalizability and conclusions about how men are affected. Study 1's design limits causal inference about the effects of self-sacrifice (Studies 2 and 3 replicated only the first stage of the model, not the self-sacrifice → outcomes effects), and the close spacing of the first and third surveys may have limited observable change in job satisfaction.

**Future Research**
Future work could replicate the self-sacrifice → outcome effects in other contexts using longitudinal or laboratory designs, test whether negative contact and perceived self-sacrifice are reciprocally related, and examine demographic moderators such as occupational status or wage level and the reasons men report higher social worth and self-sacrifice. Researchers might also examine cross-level interactions between job-level and beneficiary-specific attributions, distinguish primary from secondary beneficiaries, study the content of coworker messages, and clarify the job types and performance metrics for which perceived social worth predicts performance.
