# Advancing Reform: Embedded Activism to Develop Climate Solutions

**Abstract**
Activists increasingly seek to influence organizations that also espouse support for social movement goals, encouraging the use of collaborative tactics. While there has been growing research on insider activists who import social movement resources, little is known about how internal activism might operate through a coordinated and collaborative approach with external social movement organizations, which we refer to as embedded activism. Likewise, collaborative activists encounter organizations with a wide range of prior reform experience, and the resulting “opportunity structure” for collaboration is not well understood. We investigate how a network of embedded activists can operate to advance reform efforts across diverse organizations. Our analysis combines surveys, interviews, and archival records from the Environmental Defense Fund’s Climate Corps program, which embeds graduate students in partner organizations to advance climate change reforms. Embedded activists accomplish this work by matching external resources with the organizational context in order to generate a fertile mixture of support and ambiguity and create new solutions. External resources are especially important for organizations that are at the extremes of prior issue development.

**Research Question**
How can a network of embedded activists—who coordinate and collaborate with external social movement organizations—operate to advance internal reform across organizations that differ in their prior issue development? Specifically, how do embedded activists use movement resources to navigate the tension between organizational support and issue ambiguity to create new solutions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: organizational support (resources to act) vs. issue ambiguity (opportunities to act); the "opportunity structure" for collaborative (vs. contentious) activism.
- Contributing conditions (organizational context): new initiative, program newcomer, open-ended project, small organizational scale.
- Movement resources: external search (intensity of EDF resource use) and external focus (external vs. internal advice seeking).
- Outcome: high solution creation (additions to project scope from initial proposal to final report).
- Process / configurations (fsQCA): three equifinal groupings—Low Ambiguity (catalyzing established issue understandings via external resources), Moderate Ambiguity (navigating organizational fit via internal focus), High Ambiguity (building legitimacy via external search plus internal focus).

Embedded activists span movement–partner boundaries, matching external movement resources to the partner organization's balance of support and ambiguity. Where prior issue development is high (low ambiguity), external resources catalyze established efforts by introducing new perspectives; under moderate ambiguity, fellows turn internally to sell the issue and cultivate allies; under high ambiguity, fellows combine external search for knowledge and exemplars with an internal focus that legitimizes proposed solutions. The configurational analysis shows external resources are especially consequential at the extremes of prior issue development, and the inverse-outcome analysis confirms the absence of external resources characterizes low solution creation.

**Data & Measures**
Single field study of the 2016 Environmental Defense Fund Climate Corps cohort, which encompassed 126 fellows at 99 host organizations (66 companies, 20 government agencies, the rest nonprofits and higher education organizations). Data sources (Table 1): 118 initial project proposals and 118 final project reports (one per fellow), 620 historical Climate Corps engagement records, a three-wave fellow survey administered at the beginning, middle, and end of the fellowship (20 close-ended items repeated across panels plus open-ended questions), and 55 semi-structured interviews of roughly 45 minutes drawn from four distinct populations: 31 fellows of the 2016 cohort (19 in August-October 2016 and 12 in February 2020), 12 program alumni (May-June 2016), 9 EDF engagement managers (May-June 2016), and 3 host supervisors (2017). For the fsQCA, 94 fellows (75%) completed at least one survey, missing data reduced this to 86, and six cases from three pairs of fellows working on related projects at the same host were dropped, leaving a final sample of 80 fellows with the fellow as the unit of analysis.

The outcome and all six conditions were calibrated as fuzzy sets (Table 2). Outcome, high solution creation: the number of discrete reforms added to project scope between the host-designed initial proposal and the fellow-authored final summary, coded from both documents by two research assistants using a modified version of the U.S. Department of Energy's Assessment Recommendation Code, then direct-calibrated at 0 fully out, 1.5 crossover, and 4 fully in (M = 2.6, SD = 1.6; median 2; 8% added none and 5% added five or more). Organizational-context conditions: new initiative (reverse-coded additive index of binary mentions across proposal and report of climate commitments, prior work, and other energy-specific resources); program newcomer (reverse-coded count of the host's previous Climate Corps engagements, with 0 previous engagements fully in); open-ended project (reverse-coded average specificity of the initial proposal on a four-level hierarchical coding scheme); small organizational scale (employee count and geographic scope collected from organizational websites, calibrated with Ragin's (2008) indirect method across five thresholds). Movement-resource conditions, both from the fellow survey: external search (use of each of seven EDF resource categories in the last month, namely other fellows, engagement manager, resource library, consultants, webinars, LinkedIn group, and mentors; 5 fully out, 10.1 crossover, 20 fully in; M = 13.1, SD = 10.3) and external focus (ratio of advice seeking from the Climate Corps network to advice seeking inside the host organization; 0.5 fully out, 0.99 crossover, 1.25 fully in; M = 0.8, SD = 0.3).

Analysis used the QCA module in R: a truth table over all condition combinations, a 0.8 consistency threshold for positive rows, enhanced standard analysis for logical remainders, and retention of paths with consistency above 0.8 and proportional reduction in inconsistency above 0.7; robustness was rechecked at a 0.85 threshold, and a check for necessary conditions found none. The design is set-theoretic and configurational, identifying combinations of conditions that are sufficient for the outcome rather than estimating effects under a causal-identification strategy. Interviews and open-ended survey responses were coded in NVivo by analytic abduction with analyst triangulation, including two research assistants blinded to the QCA, and the paper states these data were not used to determine any of the conditions analyzed in QCA.

**Key Findings**
The primary fsQCA (Table 3) identifies six configurations sufficient for high solution creation, which the authors read as evidence of equifinality, with overall solution consistency of 0.828 and coverage of 0.65; of the 80 cases, 49 fall in positive truth-table rows and 34 are covered by the solution. The six paths are grouped by contextual ambiguity. Low ambiguity ("catalyzing established issue understandings") covers Paths 1-3: Path 1 pairs external search with small organizational scale and strongly defined (not open-ended) project plans (12 cases; consistency 0.861, raw coverage 0.242); Path 2 pairs external search with external focus in a large, well-prepared organization (5 cases; 0.860, 0.210); Path 3 pairs external focus with an absence of external search in small organizations that had prior fellows (5 cases; 0.878, 0.129). Moderate ambiguity ("navigating organizational fit") covers Paths 4-5: Path 4 is a large, well-prepared organization that is a program newcomer, with strongly defined plans as a peripheral condition (7 cases; 0.848, 0.316); Path 5 is a small, well-prepared organization with prior Climate Corps engagements but open-ended project plans (3 cases; 0.914, 0.151). High ambiguity ("building legitimacy") consists of Path 6 alone, a large-scale program newcomer with open-ended projects and extensive external search (8 cases; 0.841, 0.359), the highest-coverage path in the solution.

The central directional result is that every low-ambiguity path and the high-ambiguity path contain at least one movement resource, while in both moderate-ambiguity paths movement resources are absent or irrelevant and fellows emphasize internal advice seeking instead. The paper states this as external resources being especially important for organizations at the extremes of prior issue development: external search catalyzes an already-elaborated issue under low ambiguity, and supplies missing guidance, expertise, and legitimacy under high ambiguity.

The inverse-outcome analysis is weaker and the authors present it as suggestive rather than confirmatory. Standard QCA procedures produced no consistent pathway to low solution creation; relaxing the threshold to the minimum recommended 0.75 consistency produced three paths (Table 5) covering nine of the 17 cases in positive rows, with overall solution consistency 0.753 and coverage 0.383 (Path A: 7 cases, consistency 0.784, raw coverage 0.316; Path B: 2 cases, 0.781, 0.231; Path C: 1 case, 0.789, 0.152). All three paths include the absence of external resources, which the authors read as reinforcing the importance of movement resources, though they note the significance of these results is borderline and that Path B suggests large organizations with prior fellows but new initiatives limit solution creation regardless of movement resources.

Qualitative triangulation converges with the configurational pattern. Fellows in both low- and high-ambiguity contexts drew on the EDF network for social support to overcome obstacles and for expertise and tools to highlight new opportunities, but used exemplars differently: to reinvigorate existing programs under low ambiguity, and to show the feasibility of new ideas and legitimate them internally under high ambiguity (Table 4). Fellows in moderate-ambiguity contexts were more likely to discuss the EDF network in ways disconnected from their fellowship, most often as a job-search resource rather than a resource for project work.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces "embedded activism" to characterize activists who are neither insiders nor outsiders but cross movement–partner boundaries, combining knowledge of organizational opportunity structures with access to a wider range of movement resources. It develops a revised theory of opportunity structures fitted to collaborative (rather than contentious) tactics, centered on the organization's prior issue development, and theorizes a non-linear tension between support and ambiguity that integrates issue-selling and proactivity research.

**Practical Implication**
Leaders should cultivate external connections—with SMOs, professional associations, and alumni networks—to empower internal climate-change advocates, especially when their organizations are either new to an issue or entrenched in an established approach. These ties supply not only motivation and social support but also knowledge, tools, and expertise that can be matched to the specific organizational context to catalyze reform.

**Limitations**
The study is an initial, exploratory analysis of a single prominent program (EDF Climate Corps), so generalization is bounded to collaborative initiatives that cross movement–partner boundaries and seek internal (rather than external-facing) change. fsQCA imposes tight limits on the number of conditions, so influential features such as goal alignment, openness to change, and progressive values were not modeled; the inverse-outcome findings rely on a relaxed consistency threshold and are described as suggestive.

**Future Research**
Field experiments could randomize the use of external resources to test whether they are more effective at high or low ambiguity than at moderate ambiguity. Future work could examine when temporary organizational members (embedded activists, consultants, professionals) act as issue sellers, study the boundary work that lets organizations admit activists, and investigate the link between reform ideas and implementation as well as alternative ways of conceptualizing and measuring reform depth and impact.
