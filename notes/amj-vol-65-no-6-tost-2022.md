# Different Roots, Different Fruits: Gender-Based Differences in Cultural Narratives about Perceived Discrimination Produce Divergent Psychological Consequences

**Abstract**
We further argue that these effects contribute to a reduction of well-being among members of both genders, with the negative effect on well-being being more pronounced among women than among men. We examine these predictions in five studies of working adults. We discuss the implications for research on perceptions of discrimination and for organizations seeking to reduce the negative consequences of perceptions of discrimination.

**Research Question**
Do women and men differ in their understandings of the nature of discrimination against people of their gender, and do these different understandings lead to differences in workplace-related consequences for women and men?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Women are more likely than men to explain discrimination against people of their gender by referencing (a) the derogation of people of their gender's workplace-relevant abilities, (b) a belief that people of their gender should be attending to domestic duties or are likely to prioritize domestic duties over work duties, and (c) the sexual objectification of people of their gender.
- Hypothesis 1b. Although women might also use other categories of explanation, these three categories of description are the most frequently cited among women (i.e., they constitute the dominant narrative).
- Hypothesis 2a. Men are more likely than women to explain discrimination against people of their gender by referencing reverse gender discrimination associated with gender diversity initiatives.
- Hypothesis 2b. Although men might also use other categories of explanation, this category of description is the most frequently cited among men (i.e., this constitutes the dominant narrative).
- Hypothesis 3a. Subjection to a patriarchal narrative, but not a reverse gender discrimination narrative, has a negative effect on one's self-efficacy.
- Hypothesis 3b. There is a negative relationship between perceived gender discrimination and self-efficacy among women but not among men.
- Hypothesis 4a. Subjection to either a patriarchal narrative or a reverse gender discrimination narrative has a negative effect on one's sense of belonging.
- Hypothesis 4b. There is a negative relationship between perceived gender discrimination and belonging among both women and men.
- Hypothesis 5. There is a negative relationship between perceived gender discrimination and gestalt well-being, but it is smaller among men.
- Hypothesis 6. The negative relationship between perceived gender discrimination and gestalt well-being among women is mediated by self-efficacy and belonging.
- Hypothesis 7. The negative relationship between perceived gender discrimination and gestalt well-being among men is mediated by belonging.

Studies 1 and 2 test Hypotheses 1a, 1b, 2a, and 2b; Study 3 tests Hypotheses 3a and 4a; Studies 4a and 4b test Hypotheses 3b, 4b, and 5 through 7. The authors state that they make no theoretical predictions about the role of race and ethnicity and examine it only through exploratory analyses in each study.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived workplace gender discrimination (and, in Study 3, an experimentally manipulated cultural narrative: patriarchal vs. reverse gender discrimination vs. control setback)
- DV(s): Self-efficacy, sense of belonging, and gestalt well-being
- Mediators: Self-efficacy and belonging (mediating the discrimination → gestalt well-being path)
- Moderators: Gender (woman vs. man); race/ethnicity tested exploratorily as a non-significant moderator

The authors argue that women and men appeal to different dominant cultural narratives to make sense of perceived gender discrimination: women interpret it through a patriarchal narrative (derogation of abilities, ascribed domestic roles, sexual objectification), whereas men interpret it through a reverse-gender-discrimination narrative (diversity/affirmative-action efforts that favor women). Because the patriarchal narrative impugns competence while the reverse-discrimination narrative does not, perceived discrimination lowers self-efficacy only among women, while lowering belonging among both genders. These cognitions in turn reduce gestalt well-being, with a stronger (more consistent) effect for women because two mechanisms operate (self-efficacy and belonging) rather than one (belonging).

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (open-ended survey): Prolific Academic was contracted to provide 400 U.S. residents over the age of 24 (200 women, 200 men); 414 completed the study, of whom 50% were female, 58% employed full time, 80.2% Caucasian, with an average age of 36.14 years (SD = 10.36). Participants first reported demographics so questions could be gender-matched, then described gender discrimination they had personally experienced or, failing that, had observed in someone of their gender, or (if neither) a hypothetical case. Among women, 38% reported direct experience, 22% an observation, and 40% a hypothetical case; among men the split was 14%, 7%, and 79%. Two independent coders micro-coded responses, one developing codes and definitions and both then coding each response, yielding 19 primary categories of gender discrimination (mean 1.28 categories per response; 1.40 for women, 1.15 for men). Blind to gender distributions, two authors then independently sorted the primary categories into higher-order narratives: patriarchal (lesser workplace-relevant abilities, domestic/parental roles, sexual harassment or objectification), reverse gender discrimination (unfair outcomes tied to a gender-based diversity or affirmative action initiative), non-theorized categories, and responses giving no narrative explanation. Tests were chi-square comparisons of category frequency by gender and t-tests comparing composites; race was added as a moderator (non-White vs. White) in exploratory reruns.

Study 2 (preregistered ranking and rating survey): Prolific Academic was contracted to provide 800 U.S. residents over the age of 18 (400 women, 400 men); 733 completed the study and passed an attention check, of whom 53.8% were female, average age 30.65 years (SD = 10.87) with 10.64 years (SD = 10.45) of work experience. Participants read a scenario about a firm, Telepro, in which a colleague tells them they are likely to encounter gender discrimination there, then both ranked and rated (1 = not at all likely, 7 = extremely likely) four potential experiences worded for their own gender: three patriarchal items (assumed lower ability, assumed childcare or parental-care duties, sexual harassment) and one reverse gender discrimination item (unfair treatment because of a gender-based diversity or affirmative action initiative). The three patriarchal items were combined into a patriarchal composite. Rankings were analyzed with Mann-Whitney U tests (between gender) and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (within gender); ratings with independent-samples and repeated-samples t-tests. Because interaction effects cannot be examined straightforwardly with these tests, the race analysis reran each hypothesis test on the 224 participants of color rather than adding a moderator.

Study 3 (preregistered randomized vignette experiment): Prolific Academic was contracted to provide 400 U.S. residents over the age of 18 (200 women, 200 men); 377 completed the study and passed an attention check, of whom 51.2% were female, average age 30.74 years (SD = 10.55), and 50.1% currently working full time. Participants were assigned to one of three descriptions of a workplace situation facing a gender-neutral colleague, CL: a patriarchal narrative condition, a reverse gender discrimination narrative condition, or a control condition in which CL faced a general setback caused primarily by industry-wide economic challenges. Self-efficacy doubts were measured with four items aligned with Riggs et al. (1994) (e.g., "CL may start to doubt his/her own competence"; 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree, alpha = .923); belonging doubts with three items adapted from Brands and Fernandez-Mateo (2017) (e.g., "CL would doubt his/her fit in the organization"; alpha = .904). Participants also guessed CL's gender (1 = man, 2 = woman). Analysis followed the preregistered plan: one-way ANOVA with contrast coding (patriarchy = 2, reverse discrimination = −1, control = −1 for self-efficacy doubts; control = 2, reverse discrimination = −1, patriarchy = −1 for belonging doubts), supplemented by pairwise t-tests and two-way ANOVAs adding participant gender.

Studies 4a and 4b (two independent survey replications): Sample A (Study 4a) was a Qualtrics panel of 800 U.S. residents working full time who passed an attention check, 50.0% female, average age 43.34 years (SD = 13.51); Sample B (Study 4b) was a separate Prolific Academic sample of a planned 800 U.S. residents working full time (400 women, 400 men), of whom 738 completed the study and passed two attention checks, 51.4% female, average age 35.66 years (SD = 10.26). Both samples answered about their current work organization while imagining ascending its hierarchy. Perceived gender discrimination was a 3-item scale adapted from James, Lovato, and Cropanzano (1994) ("I would have to deal with gender discrimination"; "I would be confronted with gender bias"; "I would see people exhibit bias against my gender"; 1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree; alpha = .943 in Sample A, .980 in Sample B). Self-efficacy used six items from Riggs et al. (1994) (alpha = .867, .893); belonging used 22 items from Brands and Fernandez-Mateo (2017) across four sub-dimensions of membership, acceptance, fade, and trust (alpha = .947, .925); gestalt well-being used a 5-item scale asking how happy, healthy, peaceful, socially connected, and possessed of meaningful relationships participants would feel (1 = not at all, 7 = very much so; alpha = .898, .877). Gender was coded 0 = man, 1 = woman, and only participants identifying as men or women were retained. Controls were age, socioeconomic status (the 10-rung ladder scale of Adler et al., 2000), current position in the organizational hierarchy, education, and race/ethnicity (0 = White, 1 = non-White). Hypotheses 3b, 4b, and 5 were tested by regressing each outcome on the controls, gender, standardized perceived gender discrimination, and their interaction; Hypotheses 6 and 7 were tested simultaneously with Hayes PROCESS model 8 in SPSS, with gender as moderator and self-efficacy and belonging as mediators. Study 3 is the only randomized design; Studies 1, 2, 4a, and 4b are cross-sectional surveys, so their relationships are associational.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 414) supported all four narrative hypotheses it tested. Women were significantly more likely than men to describe gender discrimination as others doubting their abilities (chi-square(1, 414) = 20.03, p < .001) and as others believing they had domestic duties that interfere with work (chi-square(1, 414) = 13.55, p < .001), and marginally more likely to describe it as sexual harassment (chi-square(1, 414) = 3.03, p = .082); combining the three categories, women were significantly more likely than men to provide a patriarchal narrative (t(412) = −5.744, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 1a. Women were also more likely to provide a patriarchal narrative than the next most frequent category, generalized rudeness (t(205) = 7.225, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 1b. Men were significantly more likely than women to describe discrimination as rooted in reverse gender discrimination, often mentioning quotas (chi-square(1, 414) = 39.05, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 2a, and mentioned reverse gender discrimination more frequently than the next most cited category, sexualization (t(207) = 2.58, p = .011), supporting Hypothesis 2b. The alternative narrative that men are seen as insufficiently communal found little support: only four hypothetical responses touched on it. Adding race as a moderator produced no significant interactions, though among men of color only, being assigned harder work was listed as frequently as reverse gender discrimination.

Study 2 (n = 733, preregistered) replicated all four with rankings and ratings. Women ranked the patriarchal narratives as more likely than men did (M rank = 2.23 vs. 2.74, U = 20951, p < .001) and rated them as more likely (M = 5.09 vs. 3.53, t(731) = −17.996, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 1a. Women ranked (Z = −12.672, p < .001) and rated (t(393) = 15.256, p < .001) the reverse gender discrimination item as significantly less likely than the patriarchal composite, supporting Hypothesis 1b. Men ranked the reverse gender discrimination item as more likely than women did (M rank = 1.78 vs. 3.30, U = 20951, p < .001) and rated it higher (M = 4.90 vs. 3.51, t(731) = 10.333, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 2a, and ranked (Z = −10.966, p < .001) and rated (t(338) = 13.231, p < .001) the patriarchal composite as significantly less likely than the reverse gender discrimination item, supporting Hypothesis 2b. Rerunning the tests on the 224 participants of color again supported Hypotheses 1a, 1b, 2a, and 2b.

Study 3 (n = 377, preregistered experiment) supported Hypotheses 3a and 4a. The patriarchy condition produced significantly higher self-efficacy doubts about CL than the other two conditions combined (F(2, 376) = 6.07, p = .003); pairwise, patriarchy (M = 5.55, SD = 1.12) exceeded control (M = 5.08, SD = 1.22; t(247) = 3.13, p = .002) and reverse gender discrimination (M = 5.09, SD = 1.29; t(253) = 3.02, p = .003), while reverse gender discrimination and control did not differ (t(248) = .04, p = .971), supporting Hypothesis 3a. The control condition produced significantly lower belonging doubts than the other two conditions combined (F(2, 376) = 25.54, p < .001); pairwise, control (M = 4.98, SD = 1.24) was lower than patriarchy (M = 5.93, SD = .94; t(247) = 6.83, p < .001) and than reverse gender discrimination (M = 5.55, SD = .97; t(248) = 4.06, p < .001), supporting Hypothesis 4a. An unpredicted difference also emerged, with patriarchy higher in belonging doubts than reverse gender discrimination (t(253) = 3.18, p = .002). Two-way ANOVAs found no effect of participant gender or interaction on either outcome. As expected, participants assumed CL was a woman in the patriarchy condition and a man in the reverse gender discrimination condition. Race/ethnicity did not moderate the results, but restricting the sample to people of color removed the patriarchy-versus-reverse-discrimination difference in self-efficacy doubts, implying both narratives lowered self-efficacy in that subsample, a test the authors note was underpowered.

Studies 4a and 4b produced consistent support for Hypotheses 3b, 4b, and 6, partial support for Hypothesis 5, and mixed support for Hypothesis 7. Hypothesis 3b was supported in both samples: the gender by perceived gender discrimination interaction on self-efficacy was significant (Study 4a: b = −.124, p = .015; Study 4b: b = −.235, p < .001), with a significant negative effect for women (Study 4a CI95% = −.221, −.088; Study 4b CI95% = −.245, −.085) and no significant effect for men (Study 4a CI95% = −.105, .045; Study 4b CI95% = −.023, .163). Hypothesis 4b was supported in both samples: perceived gender discrimination was negatively associated with belonging (Study 4a: b = −.154, beta = −.158, p < .001; Study 4b: b = −.291, beta = −.297, p < .001) and the gender interaction was not significant (Study 4a: b = −.111, beta = −.080, p = .142; Study 4b: b = −.054, beta = −.037, p = .555), so the relationship held for both genders. Hypothesis 5 received only partial support in both samples: the interaction on gestalt well-being was significant (Study 4a: b = −.187, p = .001; Study 4b: b = −.259, p < .001), but the negative effect appeared only among women (Study 4a CI95% = −.219, −.070; Study 4b CI95% = −.285, −.132) and was not significant among men (Study 4a CI95% = −.042, .127; Study 4b CI95% = −.038, .139), whereas the hypothesis had predicted a smaller but still negative effect for men.

Hypothesis 6 was supported in both samples: for women, the indirect effects of perceived gender discrimination on gestalt well-being through self-efficacy (Study 4a CI95% = −.051, −.014; Study 4b CI95% = −.055, −.015) and through belonging (Study 4a CI95% = −.125, −.038; Study 4b CI95% = −.141, −.061) were both significant, with the direct effect becoming non-significant in Study 4a (CI95% = −.094, .025) but remaining significant in Study 4b (CI95% = −.138, −.013). Hypothesis 7 was supported in Study 4b but not in Study 4a: for men, neither the indirect effect through self-efficacy (CI95% = −.021, .008) nor through belonging (CI95% = −.084, .009) was significant in Study 4a, whereas in Study 4b the indirect effect through belonging was significant (CI95% = −.134, −.033) while the one through self-efficacy was not (CI95% = −.003, .034). In both samples, for men, the direct effect of perceived gender discrimination on gestalt well-being was positive and significant (Study 4a CI95% = .018, .151; Study 4b CI95% = .048, .192). None of the race/ethnicity interactions were significant in the tests of Hypotheses 3b, 4b, or 5 through 7.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces a theoretical framework explaining when and why the psychological effects of perceived gender discrimination diverge between advantaged and disadvantaged groups, addressing the long-noted absence of a well-supported explanation (and the mixed support for the rejection-identification/pervasiveness account). Its second contribution is importing the construct of cultural narratives into the conversation on perceived discrimination, showing that understanding outcomes requires attending not just to the extent of perceived discrimination but to the substantive meaning individuals assign to it.

**Practical Implication**
Leaders who understand the dominant cultural narratives operating in their organization can better identify and eliminate managerial behaviors that elicit perceived gender discrimination; because narratives vary, leaders should gather qualitative data rather than assume which narrative dominates. Interventions should bolster both men's and women's sense of belonging while additionally bolstering self-efficacy among women.

**Limitations**
Study 1's sample was too small to examine how non-dominant narratives varied across response types (personal, observed, hypothetical). Study 3, though experimental, relied on lower-stakes vignettes, and Studies 4a/4b are subject to common method bias (mitigated by the dichotomous, nonsubjective gender-interaction design). The work focuses on people identifying as women or men and excludes trans/nonbinary individuals, and statistical power was insufficient to confidently detect race/ethnicity moderation.

**Future Research**
Future work should probe why the gestalt well-being effect for men was inconsistent across Studies 4a/4b, examine the cultural narratives that nonbinary and transgender individuals adopt, and extend the framework to other forms of discrimination (race, religion, age, disability, LGBTQ) and to intersectional combinations. Larger, more highly powered samples are needed to examine race/ethnicity differences, and intervention studies could test the effectiveness of narrative-aware managerial training.
