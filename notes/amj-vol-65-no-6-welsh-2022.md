# The Social Aftershocks of Voice: An Investigation of Employees’ Affective and Interpersonal Reactions after Speaking Up

**Abstract**
We draw on cognitive-motivational-relational theory to build a theoretical model that outlines how speaking up affects voicers’ emotions and subsequent social behavior. Across three studies—an experimental pilot study, a daily within-person study of employee–coworker dyads, and a preregistered experiment—we test our proposal that promotive voice elicits pride due to a sense of social accomplishment, whereas prohibitive voice elicits anxiety due to a sense of social uncertainty. We demonstrate that these feelings of pride and anxiety have diverging effects on voicers’ tendency to withdraw from social interaction during the rest of the day. In turn, these diverging effects on voicers’ interpersonal avoidance influence voicers’ daily interpersonal citizenship behaviors. We further propose that recipients of voice have the potential to “hijack” voicers’ affective appraisals in a manner that can amplify or attenuate their emotional reactions and subsequent social behavior. Our results disentangle the complex experience of speaking up and provide novel insights into how voicers and organizations can maximize the benefits of voice while minimizing its harmful social side effects.

**Research Question**
How does speaking up shape the voicer's own emotions and subsequent social behavior — specifically, when does voice generate pride versus anxiety, how do these emotions affect interpersonal avoidance and downstream interpersonal citizenship behavior, and how does a recipient's reception of the voice alter these appraisal processes?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Promotive voice has a positive relationship with pride.
- H2: Prohibitive voice has a positive relationship with anxiety.
- H3: Promotive voice has a negative indirect effect on interpersonal avoidance, through pride.
- H4: Prohibitive voice has a positive indirect effect on interpersonal avoidance, through anxiety.
- H5: Interpersonal avoidance has a negative relationship with interpersonal citizenship behavior.
- H6: Promotive voice that receives positive voice reception will lead to higher levels of pride than promotive voice that receives (a) no voice reception or (b) negative voice reception.
- H7: Prohibitive voice that receives positive voice reception will lead to lower levels of anxiety than prohibitive voice that receives (a) no voice reception or (b) negative voice reception.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Promotive voice; prohibitive voice (manipulated in the experiments, daily-measured in the ESM)
- DV(s): Interpersonal avoidance (proximal); interpersonal citizenship behavior (distal terminal outcome)
- Mediators: Pride (from promotive voice) and anxiety (from prohibitive voice) as primary appraisal-driven emotions; interpersonal avoidance as the serial behavioral mediator linking emotions to citizenship
- Moderators: Voice reception (positive vs. negative vs. no reception) "hijacks" the appraisal, amplifying/attenuating pride and anxiety

Grounded in cognitive-motivational-relational (appraisal) theory, the affective process is modeled as a decision tree: promotive voice is appraised as a beneficial, credit-worthy event that elicits pride, whose expansive action tendency reduces social withdrawal; prohibitive voice is appraised as an uncertain social threat that elicits anxiety, whose restrictive action tendency increases withdrawal. Because pride broadens and anxiety narrows social engagement, the two voice types exert opposite serial indirect effects on interpersonal citizenship behavior. A recipient's response operates as a relational lever that can redirect the appraisal down a different branch, intensifying or dampening the dominant emotion.

**Data & Measures**
Pilot study: 219 undergraduates at a Midwestern U.S. university randomly assigned to a promotive or a prohibitive voice condition and asked to give their actual current-semester professor non-anonymous written feedback (promotive = "practices and procedures that could be used to improve [their class]"; prohibitive = "practices and procedures that are harmful to [their class]"), with the manipulation based on Liang et al.'s (2012) conceptualization; pride = Fredrickson, Tugade, Waugh, and Larkin's (2003) 3-item measure ("proud," "confident," "self-assured"; alpha = .90) and anxiety = Warr's (1990) 3-item measure ("tense," "uneasy," "worried"; alpha = .92), both rated immediately after voicing on 5-point scales; manipulation checks were adapted Liang et al. (2012) items plus ratings by two research assistants blind to the hypotheses (kappa = 0.80); analysis by MANOVA. Study 1: a separate, referral-recruited experience-sampling sample of full-time employees and their coworkers from various organizations, surveyed Monday–Friday for three consecutive work weeks; employees rated daily promotive voice and prohibitive voice on Liang et al.'s (2012) 5-item scales in a 12:00 pm survey (average alpha = .97 and .96), and pride, anxiety (same pilot measures; average alpha = .95 and .90) and interpersonal avoidance (3 items from McCullough et al., 1998; average alpha = .95) in a 4:30 pm survey, while coworkers rated the employee's daily interpersonal citizenship behavior on Dalal et al.'s (2009) 6-item scale (average alpha = .91); previous-day ratings of pride, anxiety, avoidance, and citizenship were entered as controls. Inclusion required at least three complete dyad-days and at least one hour of coworker contact that day, yielding 179 employee–coworker dyads and 2,103 completed survey responses over 15 days (Level 1 n = 2,103; Level 2 n = 179; average 11.75 days per dyad). Estimation used multilevel path analysis in Mplus 7.4 with group-mean-centered, random-slope Level-1 variables, with indirect effects tested by parametric bootstrapping and Monte Carlo confidence intervals (RMediation); the design is a within-person field design, and the authors describe the midday–afternoon time lag and previous-day controls as evidence of temporal precedence while stating that "field methodology is unable to establish causality." Study 2: a preregistered scenario experiment run on Prolific with 420 full-time U.S. employees recruited and a final sample of 358 after screening on completion and an instructed-response item; a 2 (promotive vs. prohibitive voice) x 3 (positive, negative, no voice reception) design plus a baseline control condition involving no voice, built around a commuter-airline scenario in which the participant is the voicer; pride and anxiety used the same 3-item measures on 1–7 anchors (alpha = .93 and .96), interpersonal avoidance the same 3-item measure (alpha = .97), and interpersonal citizenship behavior was operationalized behaviorally as the number of minutes the participant would spend helping a colleague who requested help with a presentation (1 = 0 minutes to 7 = 60 minutes); analyses used MANOVA, SPSS PROCESS Model 4 and Model 6 with 5,000 bootstrap resamples and 95% confidence intervals, and Sidak-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons.

**Key Findings**
Pilot study: H1 supported — participants assigned to promotive voice reported higher pride (M = 3.71, SD = .77) than those assigned to prohibitive voice (M = 3.44, SD = .89; F[1, 117] = 5.71, p = .02); H2 supported — prohibitive voice produced higher anxiety (M = 2.48, SD = 1.14) than promotive voice (M = 2.09, SD = .96; F[1, 102] = 7.52, p = .01). H3–H7 were not tested in the pilot. Study 1: H1 supported (promotive voice positively associated with pride, gamma = .19, p < .001) and H2 supported (prohibitive voice positively associated with anxiety, gamma = .05, p = .01), while the unhypothesized cross-paths were nonsignificant (promotive voice to anxiety, gamma = –.02, p = .21; prohibitive voice to pride, gamma = .05, p = .18). H3 supported: negative indirect effect of promotive voice on interpersonal avoidance through pride (–.02; 95% CI –.031, –.007). H4 supported: positive indirect effect of prohibitive voice on interpersonal avoidance through anxiety (.01; 95% CI .003, .020). H5 supported: interpersonal avoidance negatively related to interpersonal citizenship behavior (gamma = –.04, p = .01). Both serial indirect effects on citizenship were significant (promotive voice → pride → avoidance → citizenship = .001, 95% CI .0001, .002; prohibitive voice → anxiety → avoidance → citizenship = –.0004, 95% CI –.001, –.00003). H6 and H7 were not tested in Study 1. Study 2: H1 supported — pride was higher in the promotive voice, no reception condition (M = 6.01, SD = .68) than in the control condition (M = 4.28, SD = 1.49; F[1, 99] = 59.76, p < .001) or the prohibitive voice, no reception condition (M = 4.74, SD = 1.22; F[1, 111] = 45.74, p < .001). H2 supported — anxiety was higher in the prohibitive voice, no reception condition (M = 4.83, SD = 1.53) than in the control condition (M = 2.80, SD = 1.64; F[1, 99] = 40.59, p < .001) or the promotive voice, no reception condition (M = 2.89, SD = 1.48; F[1, 111] = 46.14, p < .001). H3 supported (b = –.20, SE = .10, 95% CI [–.39, –.02]); H4 supported (b = .29, SE = .08, 95% CI [.16, .46]); H5 supported via significant serial indirect effects on interpersonal citizenship behavior (promotive voice b = .05, SE = .03, 95% CI [.01, .13]; prohibitive voice b = –.06, SE = .02, 95% CI [–.11, –.02]). H6 received only partial support: under promotive voice, positive reception produced higher pride than negative reception (M = 6.21 vs. 3.42; F[2, 159] = 86.89, Mdiff = 2.79, p < .001) but not higher than no reception (M = 6.01; Mdiff = .20, p = .77), while negative reception produced lower pride than no reception (Mdiff = –2.59, p < .001). H7 supported: under prohibitive voice, positive reception produced lower anxiety (M = 3.22) than both negative reception (M = 5.12; F[2, 153] = 17.69, Mdiff = –1.90, p < .001) and no reception (M = 4.83; Mdiff = –1.61, p < .001), although negative reception did not produce significantly higher anxiety than no reception (Mdiff = .29, p = .76).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper resolves a standing tension in the voice literature — whether speaking up makes voicers feel accomplished or anxious — by showing that promotive and prohibitive voice branch into distinct emotions (pride vs. anxiety) rather than a single mixed affective state, using episodic within-person theory rather than retrospective between-person assessment. It reveals that one discretionary behavior (prohibitive voice) can inhibit another (interpersonal citizenship behavior) through anxiety-driven avoidance, extending the nomological network of voice. It also extends cognitive-motivational-relational theory by identifying recipients as relational agents who can "hijack" the appraisal process and redirect which emotion is elicited.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations benefit from promotive voice not only through the ideas communicated but indirectly via increased interpersonal citizenship after speaking up, so managers need not respond enthusiastically to every idea — avoiding a negative reception is what matters, since both positive and no reception preserved voicer pride. Rather than suppressing prohibitive voice (which risks missing critical problems), managers should respond constructively and in a positively framed way to defuse the anxiety it provokes.

**Limitations**
The authors focused on constructive voice (promotive and prohibitive per Liang et al., 2012), so other forms such as supportive, defensive, and destructive voice were not examined and may elicit different appraisal processes. The framework targets the impact of voice events rather than the anticipation of voicing, leaving pre-voice affect (e.g., anticipatory pride or hope) unexamined. The studies capture short-term, same-day affective and behavioral consequences rather than cumulative or long-term dynamics.

**Future Research**
Researchers could apply the affective framework to other voice types (supportive, defensive, destructive) to see how they shape voicers' emotions and behavior, and examine the pre-voice phase, including the role of hope and anticipatory appraisals. Longitudinal growth models could trace how discrete voice events accumulate to shape employees' overall propensity to voice and the potential overlap between avoidance and silence over time.
