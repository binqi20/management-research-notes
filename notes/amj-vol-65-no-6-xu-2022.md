# Leader Perfectionism—Friend or Foe of Employee Creativity? Locus of Control as a Key Contingency

**Abstract**
This research aims to explain whether leader perfectionism toward employees fosters or hinders employee creativity. From a self-regulation perspective, we theorize that depending on employees' locus of control, leader perfectionism can influence two regulatory states of employees (i.e., engagement and emotional exhaustion) linearly or curvilinearly, which in turn affect their creativity in opposite directions. In a lab experiment and a multisource, multiwave field study, we found that for internals, leader perfectionism had a curvilinear effect on their engagement (but no effect on emotional exhaustion) and subsequent creativity such that the effect was positive but became weaker when leader perfectionism was extreme. By contrast, we found partial support across the two studies that for externals, leader perfectionism had a positive effect on their emotional exhaustion (but no effect on engagement), which undermined their creativity. We discuss the theoretical contributions of this research and its practical implications for organizations.

**Research Question**
Does a leader's perfectionism toward employees foster or hinder employee creativity, and through what self-regulatory mechanisms (engagement and emotional exhaustion), for whom (employee locus of control), and to what degree (linear vs. curvilinear effects of perfectionism level)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The effect of leader perfectionism on employee engagement is moderated by employees' locus of control, such that (a) for internals, it shows an inverted U-shape (curvilinear), and (b) for externals, it is negative (linear).
- H2: The effect of leader perfectionism on employee emotional exhaustion is moderated by employees' locus of control, such that (a) for internals, it is nonsignificant but becomes positive when leader perfectionism is too high (curvilinear), and (b) for externals, it is positive (linear).
- H3: The indirect effect of leader perfectionism on employee creativity via engagement is moderated by employees' locus of control, such that (a) for internals, it shows an inverted U-shape (curvilinear), and (b) for externals, it is negative (linear).
- H4: The indirect effect of leader perfectionism on employee creativity via emotional exhaustion is moderated by employees' locus of control, such that (a) for internals, it is nonsignificant and then becomes negative when leader perfectionism is too high (curvilinear), and (b) for externals, it is negative (linear).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Leader perfectionism toward employees (manipulated in Study 1 as no / perfectionism / extreme perfectionism; employee-perceived continuous variable in Study 2), modeled with linear and quadratic (curvilinear) terms.
- DV(s): Employee creativity (Study 1: consensual assessment of an idea-generation proposal by expert judges; Study 2: leader-rated creativity using Zhou & George's 13-item scale).
- Mediators: Employee engagement and employee emotional exhaustion (two opposing self-regulatory states).
- Moderators: Employee locus of control (internal vs. external).

Drawing on self-regulation theory, the authors argue that leader perfectionism creates a discrepancy between current and desired work outcomes that drives employees to regulate their efforts (raising engagement) while also depleting personal resources (raising emotional exhaustion). Whether these states translate into higher or lower creativity depends on employees' locus of control: internals, who believe they control outcomes, convert perfectionistic challenge into engagement while being buffered from exhaustion; externals, who attribute outcomes to external forces, experience leader perfectionism mainly as a stressor that escalates emotional exhaustion and undermines creativity. For internals the model predicted an inverted U-shape, with engagement turning down once leader perfectionism became overwhelming. Neither study found that downturn: both instead showed a positive slope with diminishing returns, which the authors attribute to the leader's perfectionism not being experienced as overwhelmingly as theorized.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (lab experiment): 191 undergraduates (Mage = 21.72, 65.40% female) recruited from a major university in Northern China and randomly assigned to no perfectionism (n = 63), perfectionism (n = 64), or extreme perfectionism (n = 64). Leader perfectionism was manipulated through a prewritten message from a fictitious leader ("Yang") that varied the height of the standards, the tolerance of mistakes, and the demand for revisions; a separate pilot of 113 college students and a manipulation check in the main study, both using Hewitt and Flett's (1991) 15-item other-oriented perfectionism scale (alpha = .96), confirmed that the three conditions differed, F(2, 188) = 236.87, p < .001. Locus of control was measured before the manipulation with Valecha and Ostrom's (1974) 11-item forced-choice short form of Rotter's (1966) I-E scale, with higher scores indicating a more internal locus (alpha = .65); engagement with Rich, LePine, and Crawford's (2010) 18-item scale (alpha = .96); emotional exhaustion with the 9-item Maslach Burnout Inventory (Maslach & Jackson, 1981) (alpha = .91); and creativity by the consensual assessment technique (Amabile, 1982), in which three judges from an architectural design firm, unaware of the research purpose, rated each office-space proposal from 1 to 7 (rwg = .78, ICC2 = .50). This is a randomized between-subjects experiment; estimation used GLM with orthogonal polynomial contrasts (linear code X1 = (-1, 0, 1); quadratic code X2 = (-1, 2, -1)) plus Preacher and Hayes's (2008) PROCESS macro for conditional indirect effects. Study 2 (field study): research and development teams at four high-technology firms in Northern China, surveyed on paper at two time points six weeks apart; 120 randomly sampled team leaders named 718 subordinates, yielding a final matched sample of 102 leaders (Mage = 36.63, 33.30% female) and 503 subordinates (Mage = 30.95, 45.10% female). At Time 1 subordinates rated their perception of leader perfectionism on Hewitt and Flett's (1991) 15-item other-oriented perfectionism scale (alpha = .73) and their own locus of control (alpha = .68); at Time 2 subordinates reported engagement (alpha = .96) and emotional exhaustion (alpha = .88, rated on a 5-point scale) using the Study 1 scales, and leaders rated their subordinates' creativity on Zhou and George's (2001) 13-item scale (alpha = .96). Controls were employee gender, education, organizational tenure, leader-employee dyadic tenure, leader-member exchange (Graen & Uhl-Bien, 1995; 5 items, alpha = .89), job complexity (Morgeson & Humphrey, 2006; 4 items, alpha = .96), and three organization dummies. Study 2 is a two-wave, multisource correlational survey with no causal-identification strategy; estimation used a two-level random-intercept path model in Mplus 7.4 with all predictors specified at Level 1 and supervisor-level variance partitioned out. A separate supplementary study (Appendix B) had 236 Chinese managers recruited through an online panel rate one low-creativity and one high-creativity Study 1 proposal on a 5-point scale in a one-way within-subjects design, solely to test whether a leader's own perfectionism biases creativity ratings.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (lab experiment, n = 191). H1a partially supported: the perfectionism condition x locus of control interaction on engagement was significant, F(2, 185) = 3.49, p < .05, and for internals the conditional quadratic effect was significant (B = .13, p < .05), with engagement rising from the no perfectionism (M = 5.16) to the perfectionism condition (M = 5.68; slope = .52, t = 2.27, p < .05), but the predicted decline at extreme perfectionism (M = 5.42) did not appear (slope = -.26, t = -1.13, n.s.). H1b not supported: for externals neither the conditional quadratic (B = -.11) nor the conditional linear effect (B = -.05) was significant. H2a not supported: internals' emotional exhaustion did not differ between no perfectionism and perfectionism (slope = .05, n.s.), as predicted, but also failed to rise at extreme perfectionism (slope = .34, n.s.). H2b partially supported: for externals the conditional linear effect was positive (B = .59, p < .05), with exhaustion rising from no perfectionism (M = 2.65) to perfectionism (M = 3.48; slope = .82, t = 3.06, p < .01) but not further at extreme perfectionism (slope = .35, n.s.). H3a partially supported: for internals the indirect effect on creativity via engagement was positive from no perfectionism to perfectionism (effect = .060, 95% CI [.004, .133]) and nonsignificant from perfectionism to extreme perfectionism (effect = -.030), and the two differed (difference = .089, 95% CI [.005, .174]), so the pattern was curvilinear but never turned negative. H3b not supported. H4a not supported: for internals both indirect effects via emotional exhaustion were nonsignificant. H4b partially supported: for externals the indirect effect via emotional exhaustion was negative from no perfectionism to perfectionism (effect = -.115, 95% CI [-.213, -.038]) but nonsignificant from perfectionism to extreme perfectionism (effect = -.049). Study 2 (field study, 102 leaders and 503 subordinates). H1a partially supported: the squared leader perfectionism x locus of control interaction on engagement was significant (γ = -.06, p < .001), and for internals both the conditional curvilinear (γ = -.09, p < .05) and the conditional linear effect (γ = .19, p < .001) were significant, but engagement increased at both lower (slope = .22, p < .001) and higher (slope = .16, p < .01) levels of perfectionism instead of declining. H1b not supported: for externals the conditional linear effect was nonsignificant (γ = -.01) and neither simple slope reached significance. H2a not supported: for internals neither the conditional curvilinear (γ = -.02) nor the conditional linear effect (γ = -.08) on emotional exhaustion was significant. H2b supported: for externals the conditional linear effect was positive (γ = .18, p < .01), with positive slopes at lower (.18, p < .01) and higher (.17, p < .01) levels of perfectionism. H3a not supported: for internals the indirect effect via engagement was positive at the lower level (effect = .023, 95% CI [.000, .045]) but not at the higher level (effect = .016), and the two did not differ. H3b not supported. H4a not supported. H4b not supported at the 95% level: for externals the indirect effect via emotional exhaustion was -.022 at the lower and -.021 at the higher level with 95% CIs including zero, although the corresponding 90% CIs excluded zero. Overall, the two studies converge in showing that internals benefited from leader perfectionism through enhanced engagement with diminishing returns and without added emotional exhaustion, whereas externals experienced increased emotional exhaustion without the engagement gains; the theorized negative downturn for internals at extreme perfectionism was found in neither study, and the exhaustion-mediated harm to externals' creativity was significant only in Study 1 and marginal in Study 2.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reconciles conflicting predictions about leader perfectionism and employee creativity by showing it is a double-edged sword whose effect runs through two opposing self-regulatory states (engagement and emotional exhaustion) contingent on employees' locus of control and the level of perfectionism. It extends the perfectionism literature beyond self-oriented perfectionism by theorizing leader (other-oriented) perfectionism as an interpersonal leader characteristic, and extends the locus of control literature by demonstrating the motivational limit of internal locus of control when perfectionism becomes extreme.

**Practical Implication**
Leaders should manage their level of perfectionism, recognizing it as a double-edged sword: even for internals the favorable effect weakens or vanishes when perfectionism is extreme, so leaders should avoid standards far beyond employees' capabilities and tolerate inevitable errors. Leaders should monitor employees—especially externals—for exhaustion and provide resources, timely positive feedback, and instrumental and socioemotional support, while encouraging trial-and-error and constructive negative feedback to sustain creativity.

**Limitations**
Because locus of control was measured rather than manipulated, causal inference about the moderator is limited. In the field study, leaders rated subordinates' creativity, raising potential rating bias (the authors ran a supplementary experiment suggesting perfectionism did not systematically bias ratings). The two studies did not fully replicate each other—particularly the indirect effect on externals' creativity via emotional exhaustion—and both were conducted in China, a high-power-distance culture, which may limit cross-cultural generalizability.

**Future Research**
Future work could manipulate locus of control to strengthen causal inference and identify interventions that shape employees' sense of control; obtain objective or coworker/customer ratings of creativity to mitigate leader rating bias; examine temporary components, other related contextual factors, and the research setting to explain cross-study inconsistencies; test whether employees in low-power-distance cultures react more negatively to extreme leader perfectionism; and study the effects of leaders' self-oriented perfectionism on employees.
