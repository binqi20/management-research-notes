# Fast or Slow: How Temporal Work Design Shapes Experienced Passage of Time and Job Performance

**Abstract**
Experienced passage of time, the extent to which employees perceive the passage of work time as being fast or slow, is a fundamental aspect of work experience. We identify two novel temporal work design characteristics that can speed up employees' experienced passage of time: temporal predictability and task segmentation. Jobs with high temporal predictability do not make employees go through uncertain wait times before embarking on their next task. High task segmentation occurs when a large chunk of work time is segmented by categorically different temporal markers. We tested a model in which temporal predictability and task segmentation affect experienced passage of time, which in turn influences job performance, with five studies: two experiments that established the internal validity of temporal predictability and task segmentation (Studies 1a and 1b), a naturalistic field study in a factory that investigated the natural consequences of distinct temporal work design (Study 2), an organizational field study that constructively replicated the model using a sample of knowledge workers and their supervisors (Study 3), and an online survey in which we connected our model with the broader work design literature (Study 4). Altogether, the studies support a new temporal approach to work design.

**Research Question**
Is it possible to alter employees' experienced passage of time via work design—and, if it is possible, are there any performance benefits of doing so?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Temporal predictability is positively associated with experienced passage of time.
- Hypothesis 2. Task segmentation is positively associated with experienced passage of time.
- Hypothesis 3. Experienced passage of time mediates the effect of temporal predictability on job performance.
- Hypothesis 4. Experienced passage of time mediates the effect of task segmentation on job performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Temporal predictability (extent to which a job minimizes unpredictable waiting periods before a task or event); task segmentation (extent to which a large chunk of work time is divided into smaller blocks by categorically distinct temporal markers).
- DV(s): Job performance (objective task performance in Studies 1a/1b; archival ratings in Study 2; supervisor ratings in Study 3; self-rated in Study 4).
- Mediators: Experienced passage of time (perceiving work time as passing fast vs. slow).
- Moderators: None hypothesized; the five established job-characteristics dimensions (autonomy, task variety, task significance, task identity, feedback) and their critical psychological states are modeled as controls/competing mechanisms in Study 4.

Drawing on the attentional-gate perspective, the paper argues that temporal and non-temporal information compete for the same neural resources, so when work design directs attention away from temporal cues, employees process the passage of time less and therefore experience time as passing faster. Temporal predictability removes uncertain "fore-periods" that draw attention to time, and task segmentation inserts categorically different markers that restore diminishing task attention before it drifts to temporal cues. The resulting faster, more pleasant time experience is treated as an intermediate work experience that, in turn, improves job performance.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1a: a one-factor between-subject randomized experiment with 116 American working adults recruited from MTurk. Temporal predictability was manipulated by giving the experimental condition a clear schedule of task and break times, while the control condition did not know how long each task or break would take and kept waiting until instructed to move on; both conditions completed three 5.5-minute calculation-checking tasks separated by 20-, 30-, and 40-second breaks, task segmentation and other work characteristics (job autonomy, job variety) were held constant, and both groups were told the overall study time was 16.5 minutes so that perceived time pressure would not differ. Experienced passage of time was self-reported about two thirds of the way through the study on four 5-point items adapted from Agarwal and Karahanna (2000) (α = .83), timed that way to establish its temporal precedence over performance. Task performance was the number of correct answers in the calculation-checking tasks. A four-item reverse-coded manipulation check (α = .89) confirmed the manipulation (M = 2.78 vs. 2.36; F(1, 114) = 5.57, p = .02, η² = .05), and a single 5-point item captured perceived time pressure. Random assignment identifies the effect of the manipulated temporal predictability; the mediation was estimated by bootstrapping in Mplus 8.0.

Study 1b: the same procedure run on a separate sample of 118 American MTurk participants, none of whom had taken part in Study 1a. Task segmentation was manipulated by alternating three 5.5-minute calculation-checking tasks with three 3-minute typo-checking tasks that served as categorically different temporal markers (experimental condition) versus completing all three calculation-checking tasks first and all three typo-checking tasks afterwards (control condition), with no break in either group; the task and marker durations came from a separate pilot of 50 different MTurk participants that timed when focused attention lapsed (231 seconds for calculation checking, 117 seconds for typo checking) before adding three standard deviations. Experienced passage of time used the same scale as Study 1a (α = .84) and performance was again the number of correct calculation-checking answers. A four-item task-segmentation manipulation check (reliability .72) confirmed the manipulation (M = 3.78 vs. 3.31; F(1, 116) = 6.73, p = .01, η² = .06), and perceived time pressure and perceived task variety were measured to rule out alternative explanations. Mediation was again bootstrapped in Mplus 8.0.

Study 2: a naturalistic field study in a large Chinese garment factory of more than 2,000 workers, exploiting natural variation between sewing machinists (automatic assembly lines, very short or almost no wait times, and several externally enforced garment-quality and machine-station checks) and support workers (on standby at their workstations waiting for unpredictable calls for support). All 46 support employees were invited and a matched sample of 60 sewing machinists was drawn by stratified random sampling; the final sample was 96 employees — 43 support workers and 53 sewing machinists, response rates 93% and 88%, 55% men, average age 29 years, average job tenure 4.2 years. Temporal predictability and task segmentation were measured with the same items used as manipulation checks in Studies 1a and 1b, reworded for the job context (α = .85 and .81), and experienced passage of time with the Study 1a/1b scale with minor wording changes (α = .92). Job performance came from the factory's archival record released one month after data collection, rated from one to five stars across multiple aspects of work quality. A CFA using maximum likelihood estimation (MLM) in Mplus 8.0 fit the three latent variables well, χ²(51) = 87.95, CFI = .93, RMSEA = .09, SRMR = .07, and indirect effects were bootstrapped. The design is associational: it exploits naturally occurring differences in temporal work design rather than manipulating them.

Study 3: an organizational field study in the Shanghai branch of a large international logistics company. The HR department identified 276 knowledge workers in jobs with enriching characteristics across dozens of positions (logistics control, analysts, research and development, search engine optimization, visual design, consultants, purchasing) and paired them with 38 direct supervisors; Qualtrics surveys with a US$12 incentive returned employee and supervisor response rates of 76.4% and 89.5%, yielding a final sample of 211 employees matched to 34 supervisors (60.2% male, average age 29.2 years, average job tenure 4.4 years). Temporal predictability, task segmentation, and experienced passage of time used the same measures as Study 2 on 5-point Likert scales (α = .86, .82, .88) and were collected in the employee survey; job performance was rated in the supervisor survey on Williams and Anderson's (1991) five-item scale (α = .87). CFA fit was good, χ²(120) = 222.14, CFI = .94, RMSEA = .06, SRMR = .05; the model was estimated by multilevel modeling with a Bayesian estimator, with indirect effects and their confidence intervals produced by Monte Carlo simulation. The design is associational, with the supervisor-rated outcome separating the DV source from the IV source.

Study 4: a three-wave time-lagged online survey of full-time employed MTurk participants, with the lags introduced to reduce common method variance. First-wave links went to 400 participants and matching the three waves produced 270 complete and valid sets (68% response; 53% male, 93% held one job, average age 39.9 years, average tenure 8.1 years) drawn from diverse industries and jobs including project coordinator, IT analyst, teacher, driver, construction worker, and plumber. Time 1 measured temporal predictability, task segmentation, the five job-characteristics dimensions, and demographics; Time 2, a week later, measured experienced passage of time plus experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results; Time 3, another week later, measured job performance. Unless otherwise stated, variables used 7-point Likert scales. Temporal predictability, task segmentation, and experienced passage of time used the scales from the earlier survey studies (α = .95, .86, .94). Job performance was self-rated on a three-item scale (Ashford & Black, 1996; Wu, Parker, & De Jong, 2014) with participants instructed to answer as their supervisor would evaluate them (α = .89). Controls were the five job-characteristics dimensions from Morgeson and Humphrey's (2006) work design questionnaire — autonomy (α = .92), task variety (α = .96), task significance (α = .96), task identity (α = .91), feedback (α = .95) — plus three competing mediators representing the classic critical psychological states: experienced meaningfulness (Bunderson & Thompson, 2009; α = .97), experienced responsibility (Hackman & Oldham, 1975; α = .80), and knowledge of results (Hackman & Oldham, 1975; α = .86). CFA fit was good, χ²(879) = 1591.29, CFI = .93, RMSEA = .06, SRMR = .05; the integrative model was estimated in Mplus 8.0 with bootstrapped indirect effects. The time lags reduce common method bias but the design remains associational rather than causal.

**Key Findings**
Study 1a (n = 116) tested Hypotheses 1 and 3 and supported both. A one-way ANOVA found a significant main effect of temporal predictability on experienced passage of time, F(1, 114) = 5.14, p = .03, η² = .04, with the high-predictability condition perceiving time as passing significantly faster (M = 3.94) than the low-predictability control (M = 3.63), so Hypothesis 1 was supported. Bootstrapping showed the effect of temporal predictability on performance was mediated by experienced passage of time (indirect effect = 14.83, 95% CI [2.83, 29.43]), so Hypothesis 3 was supported. A supplementary ANOVA also found a significant main effect on task performance, F(1, 114) = 24.41, p = .00, η² = .18, with more correct answers under high predictability (M = 219.09) than control (M = 144.90), while perceived time pressure did not differ across conditions (M = 3.86 vs. 3.81, F < 1).

Study 1b (n = 118) tested Hypotheses 2 and 4 and supported both. Task segmentation had a significant main effect on experienced passage of time, F(1, 116) = 7.17, p = .01, η² = .06, with the high-segmentation condition perceiving time as going by faster (M = 3.93) than the low-segmentation control (M = 3.52), so Hypothesis 2 was supported. Bootstrapping showed the effect of task segmentation on performance was mediated by experienced passage of time (indirect effect = 10.60, 95% CI [2.02, 23.78]), so Hypothesis 4 was supported. Task segmentation also exerted a significant main effect on overall performance, F(1, 116) = 24.60, p = .00, η² = .18 (M = 220.83 vs. 151.25), while neither perceived time pressure (M = 3.75 vs. 3.78) nor perceived task variety (M = 3.90 vs. 3.93) differed across conditions (both F < 1).

Study 2 (n = 96) produced partial support. Temporal predictability was associated with experienced passage of time at B = .30, p = .03, but task segmentation only at B = .22, p = .08. Bootstrapped indirect effects were statistically significant for both temporal predictability (indirect effect = .10, 95% CI [0.00, 0.29]) and task segmentation (indirect effect = .07, 95% CI [0.00, 0.26]). The authors state that Hypotheses 1, 3, and 4 were supported and Hypothesis 2 was marginally supported. Exploratory analysis confirmed that sewing machinists and support workers differed on temporal predictability, F(1, 94) = 7.80, p = .01, and on task segmentation, F(1, 94) = 3.50, p = .06.

Study 3 (211 employees, 34 matched supervisors) supported all four hypotheses. In a multilevel analysis with a Bayesian estimator, temporal predictability was positively related to experienced passage of time (B = .13, p = .00), task segmentation was positively related to experienced passage of time (B = .21, p = .00), and experienced passage of time was positively related to supervisor-rated job performance (B = .28, p = .00). Both hypothesized indirect effects were significant — temporal predictability (indirect effect = .03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.08]) and task segmentation (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.13]) — and the paper reports that Hypotheses 1, 2, 3, and 4 were all supported.

Study 4 (n = 270) supported all four hypotheses net of the classic work-design variables. After controlling for autonomy, task variety, task significance, task identity, and feedback, temporal predictability was positively associated with experienced passage of time (B = .11, p = .03) and task segmentation was also positively associated with it (B = .15, p = .04), so Hypotheses 1 and 2 were supported. Experienced passage of time was positively related to job performance (B = .11, p = .01) even after controlling for experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results, and the bootstrapped indirect paths to job performance through experienced passage of time were significant for both temporal predictability (indirect effect = .01, 95% CI [0.00, 0.03]) and task segmentation (indirect effect = .02, 95% CI [0.00, 0.04]), supporting Hypotheses 3 and 4.

Support pattern by study: Hypothesis 1 was supported in every study that tested it (1a, 2, 3, 4); Hypothesis 2 was supported in Studies 1b, 3, and 4 but only marginally supported in Study 2; Hypothesis 3 was supported in Studies 1a, 2, 3, and 4; Hypothesis 4 was supported in Studies 1b, 2, 3, and 4. Studies 1a and 1b each tested only one independent variable (temporal predictability and its mediation in 1a, task segmentation and its mediation in 1b), so the single point of heterogeneity in the overall pattern is Study 2's marginal result for task segmentation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends work design theory beyond enrichment-based mechanisms (the job characteristics model and its relational and motivational extensions) by introducing two attention-based temporal work characteristics—temporal predictability and task segmentation—that shape outcomes not by enriching jobs but by redirecting attention away from temporal cues. It establishes experienced passage of time as a previously neglected intermediate work experience linking work design to performance, and connects work design research to the emerging management literature on time.

**Practical Implication**
Managers can design work time, not just work content, to help employees stay focused: eliminate or reduce uncertain wait times (e.g., giving idle "standby" workers other tasks), design better-scheduled meetings, and punctuate the workday with categorically different temporal markers/breaks timed to when attention wanes. Because these levers are flexible and cheap relative to job enrichment, they are especially valuable for simple, standardized, routinized jobs that are hard to enrich, but segmentation should be customized and used cautiously for complex jobs.

**Limitations**
The authors identify only two temporal work characteristics rather than an exhaustive set, and they do not examine boundary conditions (e.g., individual differences such as mindfulness, or whether segmentation is self-initiated versus externally imposed). Although the design attempts to establish the temporal precedence of experienced passage of time over performance, reverse causality—performance shaping experienced time—cannot be fully ruled out. The two experiments (Studies 1a, 1b) are relatively weak in external validity.

**Future Research**
Future work should explore additional temporal work characteristics that alter experienced passage of time (e.g., unusual schedules such as night shifts for ER doctors or air traffic controllers, including chronotype differences), investigate boundary conditions and moderators such as mindfulness and whether segmentation is self-initiated or imposed, and further disentangle the causal ordering between experienced passage of time and job performance.
