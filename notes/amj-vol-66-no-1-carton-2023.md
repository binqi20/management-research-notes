# Painting a Clear Picture while Seeing the Big Picture: When and Why Leaders Overcome the Trade-Off Between Concreteness and Scale

**Abstract**
One of the most effective ways leaders can promote change is by generating visions with image-based rhetoric ("make children smile"). By conjuring visual snapshots of the future, leaders paint a portrait of what their organizations can one day achieve. It would thus stand to reason that leaders who naturally think and speak in terms of picture-like detail (a "concrete orientation") would promote more organizational change than those who are inclined to think vaguely (an "abstract orientation"). Yet, research has established that individuals with a concrete orientation tend to focus on short-term, narrow details (e.g., small features of a single product) rather than long-term visions requiring the coordinated effort of all employees. To determine how and when concrete-thinking leaders induce large-scale change, we integrate theory on attention, organizational hierarchy, and construal. We predict that leaders who have a concrete orientation will promote change by redirecting their attention toward long-term visions of the future, if their organizations have strong, rather than weak, hierarchies. By contrast, hierarchical strength will have no such effect on leaders with an abstract orientation. We test these predictions in an archival study of CEOs and then examine the attention-based process that helps explain this effect in a preregistered experiment.

**Research Question**
When and why do leaders with a concrete cognitive orientation — who naturally focus on short-term, narrow details — overcome that predisposition and instead spearhead large-scale organizational change? Specifically, how does organizational hierarchy interact with leader concreteness to shape attention toward large-scale goals and, in turn, organizational change?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1. A CEO's concrete (vs. abstract) orientation will interact with hierarchical strength in predicting change, such that concrete-oriented, but not abstract-oriented, leaders will initiate more change when they helm organizations with strong versus weak hierarchies.
H2. The interactive effect between a CEO's concrete (vs. abstract) orientation and hierarchical strength on organizational change will be mediated by attention toward large-scale goals.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): CEO concrete (vs. abstract) orientation (dispositional, measured via linguistic analysis of earnings-call transcripts in Study 1 and the Reyt & Wiesenfeld 2015 scale in Study 2).
- DV(s): Organizational change, indexed as the z-scored average of R&D spending, acquisitions, and property sales (Study 1) or CEO decisions on the same three dimensions (Study 2).
- Mediator: CEO attention to large-scale (long-term and broad-scope) goals.
- Moderator: Organizational hierarchical strength (number of management layers and concentration of authority; five operationalizations in Study 1, manipulated in Study 2).

The decoupling mechanism runs as follows: leaders with a concrete orientation normally co-attend to short-term, narrow problems because concreteness and small-scale focus tend to covary in cognition. When such leaders helm organizations with strong hierarchies, however, structural distance from day-to-day operations and a wide span of control redirect their finite attention toward long-term, organization-wide problems. Because concrete thinkers can calibrate attentional shifts in discernible increments, they translate this redirected attention into pursuit of large-scale change (R&D, acquisitions, property sales). Abstract-oriented leaders, lacking the same precise sense of timeline and scope, are not similarly affected by hierarchy.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (archival) data sources: Compustat and ExecuComp for hierarchy, change, and controls; The Official Board for the group- and division-CEO management layers; and Factiva for corrected transcripts of quarterly earnings calls. The final sample of 303 companies came from a stratified random draw (100 S&P 500, 76 S&P 400, 120 S&P 600, and 99 nonindexed firms) out of the 1,836 firms with adequate data; the core data set runs 2014 to 2018, with a one-year lag between the independent and dependent variables.
- Study 1 DV — organizational change: the z-scored average of three Herrmann and Nadkarni (2014) factors (R&D spending, acquisitions, property sales) drawn from Compustat, taken in absolute-value terms and computed as absolute change two years after the independent variable minus absolute change one year after it.
- Study 1 IV — CEO concreteness/abstraction: a composite built in R that averages three linguistic dictionaries (the MRC concreteness and imagability dictionaries plus Brysbaert, Warriner, and Kuperman, 2014) applied to the entirety of the CEO's speech in the question-and-answer portion of each earnings call (α = .92); an LIWC-based index served as an alternative measure.
- Study 1 moderator — organizational hierarchical strength: the core measure is the z-scored total number of downward hierarchical connections in the organization (adapted from Cheng et al., 2015); four alternatives are centralization/inequality across levels combined with distance between levels, number of layers of management (Rajan & Wulf, 2006), structural centralization (U-form vs. M-form), and vertical centralization (Baker, 2000). The structural-centralization model was run on 267 firms and dropped the industry dummies because the matrix would not otherwise converge.
- Study 1 controls and estimation: logged number of employees, segment-based Herfindahl index, seven SIC dummies, a CEO-change dummy, CEO age, compensation, and tenure, prior change (t1 − t0), analyst concreteness, CEO analytic ability (the LIWC "analytical thinking" scale), and return on assets (t0), estimated with hierarchical OLS regression. The design is lagged and observational; the paper assigns causal (internal) validity to the experiment rather than to the archival study.
- Study 2 (preregistered experiment) sample: 403 Prolific participants retained from the 483 recruited after the preregistered exclusions (54 failed an attention check, 10 were part-time, 9 exceeded three standard deviations above the mean task time, and 7 fell below one third of the mean). All were United States nationals living in the United States, employed full-time with direct reports; average age 34.36 (SD = 10.49), 63.3% female, and 7.38 direct reports on average.
- Study 2 design and manipulation: a 1 × 2 between-subjects random assignment to a strong or weak hierarchy at Sprig Comforts, a fictional home-products company navigating the COVID-19 delta wave. The strong condition had a CEO, a COO, and three top executives (three layers, seven downward connections, network centrality score 4); the weak condition had a CEO and four top executives (two layers, four downward connections, centrality score 0). The two-item manipulation check had α = .98.
- Study 2 measures: dispositional concrete versus abstract orientation via the 18-item Reyt and Wiesenfeld (2015) scale (α = .85), counterbalanced with the scenario; attention toward large-scale goals via 12 items (α = .90), six on time horizon and six on breadth of scope; and change as the z-scored average of three decisions mirroring the Study 1 index (buy or sell between zero and five stores, spend nothing or 10% to 50% of the budget on R&D, and take no action or acquire a small, medium, or large firm). Hypothesis 1 was tested with analysis of covariance including a counterbalancing covariate; Hypothesis 2 with moderated mediation (Hayes, 2017) using 10,000 bootstrap samples.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 303) supports Hypothesis 1. Positive main effects emerged for CEO concreteness (B = .139, SE = 0.068, t = 2.03, p = .043) and for the core measure of hierarchical strength (B = .151, SE = 0.068, t = 2.22, p = .027), and the concreteness × hierarchy interaction on organizational change was positive and significant (B = .1874, SE = 0.0410, t = 4.5697, p = .000007), uniquely explaining about 5.67% of organizational change.
- Study 1 simple slopes run in the predicted direction: concrete-oriented CEOs spurred more change when they helmed strong hierarchies (B = .3133, SE = 0.0762, t = 4.1131, p = .0001) but not weak ones (p = .5192), and hierarchical strength predicted change for concrete-oriented CEOs (B = .3590, SE = 0.0800, t = 4.4852, p = .00001) but not for abstract-oriented CEOs (p = .7790).
- Study 1 robustness: the interaction was significant for three of the four alternative hierarchy measures — centralization across levels of management (B = .2273, SE = 0.0573, p = .0001), structural centralization (B = .2118, SE = 0.1036, p = .0420), and vertical centralization (B = .1831, SE = 0.0430, p < .0001) — and only marginally significant for the number of levels of hierarchy (B = .1752, SE = 0.0953, p = .0669). For every alternative measure, including the marginal one, the concreteness simple slope was positive and significant when organizations had strong but not weak hierarchies. The LIWC-based concreteness measure reproduced the interaction (B = .2685, SE = 0.0460, t = 5.8335, p = .00000001).
- Study 2 (n = 403) does not support Hypothesis 1. The manipulation worked (F(1, 402) = 511.08, p < .00001; strong hierarchy M = 5.99, SD = 1.18 vs. weak hierarchy M = 2.94, SD = 1.53), but the interaction between concreteness and hierarchical strength on change was not significant, F(1, 398) = 0.815, n.s.
- Study 2 supports Hypothesis 2 only partially. The index of moderated mediation did not exclude zero at the 95% level (B = .01; 95% CI [−.0005, .0357]) though it did exclude zero at the 90% level (B = .01; 90% CI [.001, .0138]). The interactive effect of hierarchical strength and concreteness on attention toward large-scale goals was significant (B = .1935, SE = 0.0819, p = .0186), such that hierarchy increased the attention concrete-thinking CEOs paid to large-scale goals but had no such effect for abstract-thinking CEOs. Among leaders of organizations with weak hierarchies, concrete-thinking leaders pursued less change than abstract-thinking leaders because they focused on smaller-scale goals (B = −.0136; 95% CI [−.0286, −.0019]), whereas among leaders of strong-hierarchy organizations there was no such difference (B = .0004; 95% CI [−.0077, .0088]). The path from attention toward large-scale goals to change was only marginally significant (B = .0724, SE = 0.0392, t = 1.844, p = .0660).
- The two methods therefore diverge on the focal interaction: the archival study finds it on change across five hierarchy measures and two concreteness measures, while the experiment does not reproduce it on change and instead supports the proposed attention mechanism. The authors describe the resulting evidence as tentative and preliminary.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances construal level theory by introducing the notion of cognitive decoupling — showing that concreteness of thought and scale of attention, long presumed to covary, can be deliberately reconfigured by organizational structure. It builds a bridge between macro-organizational theory (organizational hierarchy) and organizational psychology (attention and construal) using attention as a linking pin, and it challenges person–job fit assumptions by showing that the leaders with the greatest change potential are initially misaligned with the top-leader role until structure redirects their attention.

**Practical Implication**
Boards selecting CEOs should evaluate person–job fit not just on individual cognitive style but on the interaction between style and organizational hierarchy: concrete-thinking leaders are better positioned to drive substantial change in strongly hierarchical organizations, while flat organizations may need a different selection logic. Executive search firms that already assess leader concreteness should also encourage clients to account for hierarchical structure when making hiring and promotion decisions.

**Limitations**
Study 1 lacks complete panel data, preventing fine-grained tracking of changes over time, and data for The Official Board variables were collected in a different year than Compustat/ExecuComp data, raising temporal-equivocality concerns. The concreteness measure captures speech rather than thought directly. Study 2's predicted interaction on change itself was not significant (only on the attention mediator), and most participants lacked CEO experience, limiting generalization to the Study 1 population.

**Future Research**
Future scholars can examine how powerfully and consistently cognitive decoupling can occur across contexts, and integrate the present findings on attention redirection with prior research on how concreteness aids collective-action mobilization during change implementation. Researchers can also more systematically account for the evolving interplay between hierarchy and concreteness as the change process moves from enactment through implementation phases.
