# The Role of Discernment and Modulation in Enacting Occupational Values: How Career Advising Professionals Navigate Tensions with Clients

**Abstract**
Enacting occupational values is vital to expert professionals' solidarity and sense of purpose. Yet, many professionals face audiences in their relational contexts—especially powerful clients—who can hold incongruent values and may threaten professionals' jurisdictional control. How can experts enact their values without jeopardizing their jurisdictional control amid clients holding incongruent values? We examine career advisers in undergraduate business schools, whose occupational values often contrasted with values common among their student clients. Through an ethnography of one school's career advisers, combined with interviews of such advisers throughout the United States, we find that advisers navigated interactions by discerning student values and accordingly modulating their value-enactment practices through masking, moderating, or magnifying their values. This allowed advisers to uphold their jurisdictional control when facing students exhibiting incongruent values, while enacting their values with students exhibiting unclear or congruent values. We contribute to the relational perspective on occupations and professions by positing how discernment and modulation help experts navigate relational tensions by recognizing and drawing on intraclientele heterogeneity, unpacking how professionals might not entirely resist or change amid incongruence but instead pursue a more mixed approach, and highlighting when and how experts mask or moderate rather than overtly enact their values.

**Research Question**
How can expert professionals enact their occupational values without jeopardizing their jurisdictional control when working with powerful clients who hold incongruent values?

Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: occupational values (purpose, individualized fit) vs. client values (wealth, prestige, social approval); jurisdictional control as the legitimacy and autonomy with which experts hold their task domain.
- Triggering conditions: skewed but heterogeneous clientele and downstream threats to jurisdictional control (negative student feedback to deans, school-ranking surveys, parental complaints).
- Discernment: advisers notice cues in the interaction (a student's declared career commitment, type of questions, openness to exploration) to infer whether the client's values are incongruent, unclear, or congruent.
- Modulation — three value-enactment approaches: (a) value-masking when values are discerned as incongruent — impartial career-attainment help, encouraging future visits, exclusively pragmatic resources; (b) value-moderating when values are unclear — neutrally questioning extrinsic pressures, finding middle ground, covertly using value-driven resources; (c) value-magnifying when values are congruent — undermining extrinsic pressure, encouraging alternative career paths, drawing on values-rich resources.
- Emergent outcome: experts uphold jurisdictional control while still enacting occupational values whenever the client situation permits; negative cases occur when discernment or modulation fails.

Across one ethnographic site and 48 interviews at other U.S. business schools, advisers navigated relational tensions not by abandoning their values nor by overtly resisting incongruent clients, but by reading each student's enacted values and adjusting how visibly and strongly to enact their own. The process unifies covert concealment, low-risk moderation, and rare overt enactment within a single mixed strategy for protecting jurisdictional control amid client power asymmetry.

**Data & Measures**
Two-phase qualitative corpus, reported separately by phase (Table 1). Phase 1 was an ethnography of the career-advice office at "Ipsum University" (IpsU), an undergraduate business school in the northeastern region of the United States, conducted over one and a half years: 51 observational sessions totaling 47.5 hours (37 one-on-one advisory sessions, five career classes taught by advisers, five career events such as career fairs, and four stretches of general career-office activity), 37 participant-observation sessions totaling 59.5 hours in which the second author volunteered for an academic year as a part-time junior career adviser (26 office hours and 11 mock interviews), 33 ethnographic interviews totaling 29.25 hours (11 interviews across six advisers and 22 interviews with undergraduate business students, averaging about one hour each, semi-structured with open-ended questions about details, perceptions, and outcomes of interactions), and 78 archival career-advice documents (newsletters, resource documents, event advertisements). Fieldnotes were recorded soon after pauses in interactions. Phase 2 was a separate interview study of career advisers outside IpsU: the authors used Poets and Quants' 2018 list of the 88 ranked U.S. undergraduate business schools, found that 76 of the 88 had dedicated career advisers for undergraduate business students, contacted at least one adviser from each of those 76 schools using public contact information, and obtained 48 interviews, each with an adviser from a different school (46.25 hours; average about one hour). Of these 48 advisers, 43 held master's degrees, mostly in higher education administration or counseling, and one held a doctoral degree; average tenure in the occupation was about eight years. Across both phases the totals are 88 observation or participant-observation sessions (about 107 hours) and 81 interviews (75.5 hours).

Constructs were operationalized by inductive coding rather than by measurement. Data from both phases were analyzed inductively using grounded theory techniques (Charmaz, 2014): the authors iteratively coded the data, linked codes to theoretical constructs in consultation with scholarship, and connected constructs into an explanatory framework until theoretical saturation, in four stages. Stage 1 coded advisers' aspirations for students as "fulfilling purpose" and "find the right fit" and linked them to the construct of occupational values (Van Maanen & Barley, 1984). Stage 2 coded three aspirations common among students as "wealth," "prestige," and "social approval," also linked to the construct of values, and coded advisers' fear of students' negative session reviews as "negative feedback," linked to the construct of a potential threat to jurisdictional control, defined as the degree of legitimacy and autonomy with which advisers had hold over their tasks (Abbott, 1988; Bechky, 2003). Stage 3 marked and coded material and discursive value-enactment practices, identifying nine such practices and grouping them into three categories of three practices each, labeled "value-masking," "value-moderating," and "value-magnifying," with "discernment" naming advisers' noticing and inference about students' value congruency and "modulation" naming the corresponding adjustment. Stage 4 connected the constructs into an overarching explanation and combed the data for negative cases (instances of potential threats to jurisdictional control) to strengthen the credibility of the findings. Table 2 summarizes the three approaches; Online Appendix Tables A and B provide additional supporting data. The design is inductive and interpretive; the paper claims no causal identification.

**Key Findings**
Advisers held occupational values around helping students find purposeful work and individualized fit, while they perceived that many undergraduate business students enacted values of wealth, prestige, and social approval. Incongruence manifested in two linked tensions: advisers' value of purpose versus students' desire for jobs prioritizing wealth or prestige, and advisers' value of individualized fit versus students' prioritization of jobs eliciting social approval. Because students were clients whose dissatisfaction could reach business school leaders through satisfaction surveys, complaints to deans, parents, and student surveys feeding school rankings, overt value enactment carried a standing threat to advisers' jurisdictional control, the legitimacy and autonomy with which advisers held their tasks.

The central emergent finding is that advisers navigated this tension by discerning students' enacted values and modulating their own value enactment accordingly, across three approaches that each comprised three practices (relating to client values, inscribing occupational values, and using relevant resources) and differed in how visibly values were enacted. Value-masking was the most common: advisers most often discerned that students' enacted values were incongruent with their own, cued by students firmly declaring commitment to an often popular career or asking only specific, pragmatic questions, and responded by impartially assisting career attainment, encouraging future advisory sessions and staying in touch, and exclusively using pragmatic resources, thereby avoiding value enactment altogether while preserving the relationship for a possible later opportunity. Value-moderating occurred occasionally, when advisers discerned unclear congruence, typically a student uneasily considering an often popular career: they neutrally questioned extrinsic pressures, sought middle ground, and covertly used value-driven resources, a lower-risk way to enact values at least partially. Value-magnifying occurred on rare occasions, when advisers discerned congruence, cued by students openly conveying dissatisfaction with popular careers or being uncertain and open to career exploration: they undermined extrinsic pressures, encouraged alternative career paths, and openly used value-driven resources; students in these interactions commonly reported positive experiences and returning frequently for more career advice.

Negative cases establish the same relationship in the opposite direction. When advisers failed to discern accurately and applied value-magnifying practices to students committed to popular careers, students reacted negatively: they refused to return for advice, circulated negative sentiment about advisers among peers, and left negative appointment feedback and low satisfaction ratings that reached bosses and deans, any of which could undercut advisers' legitimate and autonomous hold over their tasks. The authors conclude from these cases that overtly and undiscerningly enacting occupational values has the potential to undermine advisers' jurisdictional control. Overall, discernment and modulation allowed advisers to uphold their jurisdictional control when facing students exhibiting incongruent values, while enacting their values with students exhibiting unclear or congruent values.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the relational perspective on occupations and professions by theorizing discernment and modulation as previously overlooked mechanisms through which experts navigate value tensions with clients. It reframes the field's "compliance-or-resistance" dichotomy as a continuum that includes situated masking, moderating, and magnifying, and shows that recognizing intraclientele heterogeneity is itself a strategic resource — overturning the common assumption that an occupation's clientele is monolithic. It also identifies a middle ground (value-moderating) between overt value enactment and outright value abstention that prior work on concealment has not articulated.

**Practical Implication**
For professionals whose clients have grown more powerful (career advisers, lawyers, physicians, therapists, consultants), the study suggests cultivating diagnostic skill at reading client values and a repertoire of variably visible value-enactment practices, rather than committing wholesale to either accommodation or resistance. Occupational training that emphasizes relational skills and a service-oriented disposition toward heterogeneous clients can help experts protect their jurisdiction without surrendering their professional purpose.

**Limitations**
Findings derive from one ethnographic site and an interview sample of business-school career advisers at U.S. ranked undergraduate institutions, so the specific value tensions (purpose/fit vs. wealth/prestige/social approval) may not generalize beyond similar advice-giving contexts. The authors propose two boundary conditions: discernment and modulation likely require relatively lengthy client interactions and occupational socialization that emphasizes relational, service-oriented skills; occupations with brief, scripted, or adversarial client encounters may not show the same mechanisms.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine variation across occupations in how experts read and exploit intraclientele heterogeneity, including whether experts actively cultivate (rather than merely discern) client diversity, and whether individual-level differences in discernment ability shape outcomes. Studies of occupations where negative cases dominate could reveal when sustained value enactment with unreceptive clients threatens an occupation's overall survival, and additional contexts could test the proposed boundary conditions on interaction length and occupational socialization.
