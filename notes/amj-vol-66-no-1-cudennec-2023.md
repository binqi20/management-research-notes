# Valuing Spanners: Why Category Nesting and Expertise Matter

**Abstract**
Organizations need to both differentiate themselves while conforming to their audiences' expectations. To meet this demand, organizations may span different categories. However, valuing spanners is challenging for audiences. We contend that spanners' valuation depends on category nesting, as the congruence of informational cues varies between basic categories and subcategories. Furthermore, we expect that more expert audiences find spanners to be more congruent (and hence, more valuable) at a subordinate level than at a basic level of categorization. We test our hypotheses using a mixedmethods design in the context of venture capital investments. We analyze observational data on more than 29,000 venture capital deals and develop two experimental studies. Our findings support our hypotheses that subcategory spanning lowers valuation, and that this effect is attenuated as investors' expertise increases. Our experimental studies further show that congruence is a causal mechanism explaining these effects. Our findings have important implications for research on organizational conformity and optimal distinctiveness, categorization in markets from an information processing perspective, and the impact of expertise on valuation.

**Research Question**
How does category nesting (subordinate vs. basic category spanning) and audience expertise jointly affect investors' valuation of category-spanning firms, and to what extent does perceived inter-category congruence mediate this relationship?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: Subordinate category spanning leads investors to provide a lower valuation of a firm relative to basic category spanning.
- Hypothesis 2: Investors' perceptions of inter-category congruence mediate the negative effect of subordinate category spanning (versus basic category spanning) on firm valuation.
- Hypothesis 3: Investors' expertise positively moderates the negative effect of a firm's subordinate category spanning (versus basic category spanning) on perceptions of congruence, which in turn positively impacts a firm's valuation.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Category membership condition (subordinate category spanning [SubCS], basic category spanning [BCS], basic category membership [BCM]); congruence treatment (within-domain vs. across-domain thinking) in Study 3
- DV(s): Firm valuation (pre-money value in Study 1; willingness to recommend investing in Studies 2 and 3)
- Mediators: Perceived inter-category congruence (assessed via Fleck & Quester's six-item scale)
- Moderators: Investor expertise (operationalized via prior investment experience in the focal category in Study 1; a 12-item objective finance knowledge test in Studies 2 and 3)

The central mechanism is information processing: subordinate categories are more specific but less distinctive than basic categories, so finding congruence among spanned subcategories demands greater cognitive effort. Average investors fail to perceive harmony among subcategory cues and therefore devalue subcategory spanners relative to basic-category spanners. Expertise increases the ability to discriminate subcategories within one's knowledge domain and to perceive their complementarity as congruent, which attenuates and can reverse the subcategory-spanning penalty for expert evaluators.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (observational): Pitchbook data on VC deals inked worldwide between 1994 and 2017; because several independent variables use six-year rolling windows (t-6 to t-1), the analysis covers 2000-2017. The data set contains 29,033 funding-deal observations involving 4,932 investors and 15,811 companies, with companies headquartered mostly in North America (88.2%), Europe (9.5%), and East Asia (1.4%). DV valuation = the company's pre-money value (the second-stage value prior to the capital infusion), log-transformed because it is right-skewed. IV category membership comes from the Pitchbook industrial classification (41 basic and 218 subordinate categories) and is decomposed into three mutually exclusive conditions: subordinate category spanning (SubCS, 30.7% of observations), basic category spanning (BCS, 46.3%), and basic category membership (BCM, 23.0%). Moderator investor expertise = the number of investment rounds the lead investor participated in by investing in companies in the target company's primary basic category during the six years preceding the deal year (mean unlogged score 30.00, SD 55.86; log-transformed). Controls: investor general experience, investor reputation, syndicate size, competitive intensity, category heat, investment stage, round number, "slices" of novel activities, and residual category. Because pre-money value is reported for only 33.2% of observed deals (29,033 of 87,405), the authors estimate a Heckman two-stage model with "valuation displayed" as the selection-stage outcome and company description length as the exclusion restriction, then include the inverse Mills ratio in the second stage, with year fixed effects and double clustering by investors and companies. The authors describe Study 1 as "a correlational analysis" providing "primary ecological evidence," so this study is associational rather than causally identified.

Study 2 (preregistered three-cell quasi-experiment): a random online sample of undergraduate students from the paid participant pool of a large public research university in Hong Kong; 360 students were recruited, 53 failed the instructional manipulation checks, and the final sample comprised 307 participants (slightly more than 51 per condition), on average 21.01 years old (SD 1.93), 72.31% women, from Hong Kong (86.0%), Mainland China (12.4%), Taiwan (1.3%), and Macao (0.3%). Category membership was manipulated by replacing the industry classification in an otherwise identical 55-word start-up profile with "Microfinance, Credit Risk" (SubCS), "Finance, Information Technology" (BCS), or "Finance" (BCM). DV valuation = "Would you recommend investing in the start-up?" on a 0-9 scale (mean 4.75, SD 1.62). Mediator perceived congruence = six agreement items adapted from Fleck and Quester (2007) and Rifon et al. (2004), averaged (Cronbach's alpha .95; mean 5.52, SD 1.36). Moderator expertise = a 12-item objective finance-knowledge questionnaire scored out of 12 and split at the median (5), yielding 155 nonexperts (50.49%) and 152 experts (49.51%); validity was checked against a three-item subjective expertise scale (r = .24). Analyses used ANOVA/ANCOVA and Hayes's (2017) PROCESS macro in SPSS with 10,000 bootstrapped samples.

Study 3 (preregistered manipulation-of-mediator experiment): a 2 (category spanning: BCS, SubCS) x 2 (congruence: within-domain, across-domain) between-subjects concurrent double-randomization design with an online sample of U.S. participants recruited on Prolific; 480 observations were collected and, after excluding participants who failed the instructional manipulation checks, the final sample comprised 396 individuals, on average 42.48 years old (SD 15.18), 52.53% women. The mediator was manipulated with a task asking participants to solve eight verbal analogies drawn either within one domain or across two different domains, coded 1 for the across-domain condition and 0 for the within-domain condition. Valuation, perceived congruence (Cronbach's alpha 0.97; mean 6.38, SD 1.64), category membership, and expertise use the same measures as Study 2; valuation has a mean of 5.70 (SD 1.80), and the median split at 5 of 12 points yields 206 experts (52.02%) and 190 nonexperts (47.98%). Analyses used a 2x2 factorial ANOVA, linear regression with the interaction term (controlling for age and perceived category status), and PROCESS mediation.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (29,033 deals), Hypothesis 1 supported: across the full models (3 to 6), SubCS firms received lower valuations than BCS firms (Model 3, all industries: b = -.06, p = .000; Model 4, finance and IT: b = -.07, p = .013; round-1 Models 5 and 6: b = -.06, p = .013 and b = -.08, p = .021). SubCS firms also received lower valuations than BCM firms (Model 3: b = -.04, p = .001).
- Study 1, Hypothesis 3 supported: the negative effects are attenuated as investor expertise increases — Model 3 shows expertise positively moderating the effect of SubCS relative to BCS (b = .04, p < .001) and relative to BCM (b = .04, p < .001), with the same positive interaction in the finance and IT subsample (b = .05, p = .001 vs. BCS; b = .08, p = .000 vs. BCM) and at round 1.
- Study 1 provides no test of Hypothesis 2: the models "do not reveal information about mediation as per Hypothesis 2, since no measure of congruence is available."
- Study 2 (N = 307), Hypothesis 1 supported: SubCS leads to a lower valuation than BCS (b = -.44, p = .047), and the direct effect becomes stronger once the expertise interaction is included (b = -.88, p = .005).
- Study 2, Hypothesis 3 supported: expertise positively and significantly moderates the effect of SubCS (versus BCS) on valuation (b = .89, p = .042); the SubCS-BCS valuation gap holds for nonexperts but is not significant among experts.
- Study 2, Hypothesis 2 only partially supported: across the full sample congruence does not significantly mediate the SubCS effect (path a = -.24, p = .187; path b = .56, p < .001; indirect effect = -.134, CI [-.332, .068]). Splitting by expertise, mediation is significant for nonexperts (path a = -.51, p = .039; path b = .70, p = .004; indirect effect = -.180, CI [-.437, -.004]) but absent for experts (indirect effect = -.022, CI [-.319, .382]).
- Study 3 (N = 396), Hypothesis 2 supported causally: the interaction between SubCS (versus BCS) and across-domain thinking (versus within-domain thinking) on valuation is negative and significant (b = -.98, p = .004 with controls; b = -.75, p = .039 without). Participants exposed to across-domain thinking gave significantly lower valuations of SubCS firms than those exposed to within-domain thinking (b = -.66, p = .008), whereas the valuation of BCS firms rose slightly under across-domain thinking without reaching significance (b = .30, p = .178).
- Study 3 manipulation check and supplementary mediation: across-domain thinking lowered perceived congruence for SubCS companies (b = -.44, p = .056) but did not significantly affect it for BCS companies (b = .04, p = .866). For SubCS firms, perceived congruence mediates the congruence treatment's effect on valuation (path b = .44, p < .001; path c = -.66, p = .008; path c' = -.46, p = .043), though the indirect effect is only marginally significant because its confidence interval barely includes zero (indirect effect = -.193, CI [-.424, .002]); there is no significant mediation for BCS companies (indirect effect = .017, CI [-.188, .212]). Unreported analyses show this mediation for SubCS holds only for nonexperts (indirect effect = -.325, CI [-.726, -.029]) and not for experts (indirect effect = -.079, CI [-.397, .188]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to three streams. First, it advances the optimal distinctiveness literature by showing that category nesting and audience information-processing capacity are essential dimensions of an organization's positioning. Second, it reframes congruence in market categorization from an evaluation of similarity to preexisting types into a meaningful correspondence among categorical cues across nested levels, recalibrating the supposed benefits of categorical purism (subcategory spanners may have been miscounted as purists in prior studies). Third, it documents how expertise differentially modulates valuation penalties at subordinate versus basic levels of nesting, resolving prior "mixed results" on expertise effects on valuations of deviations from categorical standards.

**Practical Implication**
Identifying with subcategories can be a rewarding differentiation strategy, but only if entrepreneurs target investors whose expertise lies within the focal domain. Founders should pay attention not only to market structure and category nesting but also to their target audience's degree of expertise in order to maximize valuation. Category spanning is best understood as a tool for achieving simultaneous conformity and distinctiveness through congruent informational cues.

**Limitations**
The industrial category structure used is relatively simple and does not account for spanning across subordinate categories belonging to different basic categories or for highly normative categorical boundaries that could trigger moral sanctions. The study focuses on VC investors as a single audience type; other audiences (consumers, crowdfunding users, media, regulators) may evaluate categorical combinations differently. The expertise measure relies on objective knowledge tests in experimental settings rather than real-world expert samples or direct manipulation of expertise.

**Future Research**
Scholars could replicate the framework with more complex nested category associations, test the theory under highly normative categorical boundaries, examine other audiences (consumers, media, legal bodies, crowdfunding users), and ensure exogeneity of expertise through real-life expert samples or direct manipulation. Alternative or complementary mechanisms such as flexibility, framing (concrete "how" vs. abstract "why"), interest, conscientiousness, or intelligence deserve further scrutiny, as does whether more expert investors actually make better valuation decisions.
