# Gains and Losses: Week-To-Week Changes in Leader–Follower Relationships

**Abstract**
Although the importance of leader–follower relationships in the workplace has been widely acknowledged, the impact of changes in such relationships has not been adequately examined in the literature. Leader–member exchange (LMX) theory and research have suggested that leader–follower relationships develop, mature, and then stabilize. Integrating recent work that has found important and impactful within-individual variations in what was thought to be relatively unvarying relationships, we theorize that leader–follower relationships (like other linkage types), are not only differentiated (varying between people) but also fluctuate across time periods (varying within people). We also discuss how people can understand their relationships better by comparing them to a relevant and proximal referent; this underlines how prior levels of LMX serve as a lens by which followers evaluate their current LMX. Across two studies, we discuss how comparing this week's LMX to last week's LMX can signal improvement (i.e., gains) or deterioration (i.e., losses) in the relationship, with differential implications for positive and negative affect. We also provide additional insights into LMX fluctuations by contrasting within- and between-individual variance in established and newly founded relationships, and by examining those incidents that are more likely to influence LMX in a given week.

**Research Question**
How do week-to-week changes (gains and losses) in leader–member exchange (LMX), evaluated against the follower's own prior-week LMX as a referent standard, relate to followers' positive and negative affect, and what within-week incidents and prior states explain such LMX change?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Week-to-week gains in LMX will be positively associated with PA.
- Hypothesis 2. Week-to-week losses in LMX will be positively associated with NA.
- Hypothesis 3. Stable and high LMX from week to week is associated with (a) higher PA and (b) lower NA compared to stable and low LMX from week to week.
- Hypothesis 4. The effect of week-to-week LMX losses on NA will be stronger than the effect of week-to-week LMX gains on PA.
- Hypothesis 5. Mature LMX relationships will have greater variance compared to newly established LMX relationships at the between-relationship level, but not at the within-relationship level.
- Hypothesis 6. The intensity of positive (negative) incidents experienced during the week will be positively (negatively) associated with LMX perceptions for that week.
- Hypothesis 7. The relevance of incidents experienced during the week to elements of exchange will moderate the intensity effect, such that the intensity of more relevant positive (negative) incidents will have a greater positive (negative) effect on LMX compared to that of less relevant incidents.
- Hypothesis 8. Prior LMX perceptions will moderate the relationship of experienced incidents on LMX, such that higher LMX perceptions will result in greater (a) negative impact of negative incidents on LMX and (b) positive impact of positive incidents on LMX, compared to lower LMX perceptions, respectively.

Hypotheses 1–4 are stated and tested in Study 1 and re-tested in Study 2; Hypotheses 5–8 are introduced and tested only in Study 2.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Current-week LMX, prior-week LMX, and their interaction/quadratic terms (operationalizing gains, losses, and stability); positive- and negative-incident intensity and incident relevance to LMX (Study 2).
- DV(s): Weekly positive affect (PA), weekly negative affect (NA), and weekly LMX (in incident models).
- Mediators: None formally tested.
- Moderators: Incident relevance to LMX (Study 2, H7); prior-week LMX as a moderator of incident effects (Study 2, H8). H5 is not a moderation test but a variance-components contrast: mature versus newly established relationships are compared on between- and within-relationship LMX variance.

Drawing on social exchange and LMX theory, the authors argue that followers evaluate their LMX by comparing the current week to the most proximal and available referent — their own previous week — and that the resulting gains or losses generate affective responses through approach (PA) and avoidance (NA) systems. Losses are theorized to weigh more heavily than gains; stable-high LMX yields higher PA and lower NA than stable-low LMX; and weekly incidents drive LMX change, with both positive and negative incidents predicted to be amplified by their relevance to exchange (H7) and by prior LMX (H8) — a conditionality the study's results then confirmed for positive incidents but not for negative ones.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1: participants were recruited through the survey research center of a large public university, supplemented by graduate business students enrolled at another large public university; all were employed adults, compensated with up to $50 in gift cards (prorated). After an initial sign-up survey they completed a survey every Friday for a total of 10 weeks. Of 102 sign-ups, 73 completed sufficient surveys (between-individual response rate 71.5%); a usable response required two consecutive weeks of measurement, yielding 464 weekly observations (within-individual response rate 50.5%). Participants averaged 35.9 years of age (SD = 10.7) and 3.1 years with their supervisor (SD = 4.2); 56% identified as female and 85% as Caucasian. Current-week LMX (average α = .93) was assessed with the 7-item Graen and Uhl-Bien (1995) measure on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = very strongly disagree to 7 = very strongly agree), referring to the week just ended; lagged values of current-week LMX served as the measure of past-week LMX. PA (average α = .95) and NA (average α = .84) were measured with the 20-item PANAS (Watson & Clark, 1994) on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = extremely). Lagged values of the focal response variables were used as controls (group-mean centered). Hypotheses were tested with polynomial regression and response surface methodology (Edwards, 2002) adapted to the within-individual context, estimated with hierarchical linear modeling to account for weeks nested within people; current- and prior-week LMX were centered at scale midpoints, change was assessed along the gain–loss line (x = −y) and stability along the line x = y, with instantaneous slopes recalculated at ±1 SD, and a piecewise regression surface served as a robustness check.

Study 2: participants were full-time employees of a sales company located across the United States and were not compensated; the company's HR department supplied contact information. They received an online survey each Friday for eight weeks reporting current LMX, PA and NA, the frequency of interactions with their leader, and free-form descriptions of positive and negative incidents involving their supervisor (a critical incidents technique approach). Of 1,080 employees invited, 962 (729 longer-tenured and 233 newly hired) completed enough surveys (89% response rate); after requiring two consecutive weekly responses, applying data-quality exclusions, and dropping observations with fewer than two supervisor interactions in the week (832 observations excluded), the main longer-tenured sample was 2,136 observations from 613 participants (42% within-individual and 84% between-individual valid response rate). A separate newly hired subsample, used to contrast newly formed with established relationships, yielded 324 observations from 145 participants (20% within-individual and 62% between-individual response rate). Longer-tenured participants were 74% male and 69.0% Caucasian, mean age 27.7 (SD = 7.3), with 8.7 years of work experience (SD = 7.8) and about one year with their supervisor (SD = 1.42); newly hired participants were 52% male and 74.5% Caucasian, mean age 24 (SD = 3.8), with 5.4 years of work experience (SD = 4.7) and two months of organizational tenure (SD = 1.8). LMX was measured with the same 7-item scale as in Study 1 but on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with average α = .92 for longer-tenured and .87 for newly hired participants; PA and NA were measured with a shortened 10-item scale (Mackinnon et al., 1999) on a 5-point Likert scale (average α = .90 and .82). Incident intensity was built from the weekly open-ended reports: three coders (coauthors) developed first-order codes in NVivo, coded each event's type and rated its severity on a 5-point scale (1 = very minor experience, 5 = extreme experience), with agreement of α = .76, ICC(1) = .52, ICC(2) = .77 for positive incidents and α = .85, ICC(1) = .64, ICC(2) = .84 for negative incidents; incidents not involving a supervisor interaction were coded missing. Incident relevance was rated by three different coauthor-raters, who scored each code on how far it captured components of social exchange (mutual trust, obligation, commitment, significance) on a 5-point scale (1 = no relationship at all to 5 = concepts are fully aligned), with α = .83, ICC(1) = .50, ICC(2) = .75. Because 1,732 (81%) of the 2,136 observations reported positive interactions but only 618 (29%) reported negative ones, positive and negative incidents were modeled separately; the positive-incident models used 1,100 weekly observations from 402 individuals and the negative-incident models 356 weekly observations from 190 individuals (Table 6). Lagged values of the predicted variable were again used as controls, with relative LMX, average sales, study week, and a three-level specification as supplementary robustness checks. Both studies are associational within-person designs: the authors discuss alternative causal orders, note that positively establishing causality is a difficult proposition, and state that the results cannot be interpreted as providing clear causal guidance.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (464 weekly observations from 73 individuals) supported all four hypotheses it tested. Both gain–loss lines had positive curvatures (γ = .23, p < .01 for PA; γ = .25, p < .05 for NA), with a positive gain–loss slope at the origin for PA (γ = .23, p < .01) and a negative one for NA (γ = −.12, p < .01); because the slopes run from greater loss to greater gain, the negative NA coefficient means NA falls as losses shrink. Instantaneous effects showed the same asymmetric pattern: in the region of gain the slope was positive for PA (γ = .52, p < .01) and nonsignificant for NA (γ = .20, n.s.), while in the region of loss it was nonsignificant for PA (γ = −.06, n.s.) and negative for NA (γ = −.44, p < .01). Hypotheses 1 and 2 were therefore supported. For Hypothesis 3, the stability slope was positive for PA (γ = .24, p < .01) with null curvature and negative for NA (γ = −.10, p < .05) with a small positive curvature (γ = .05, p < .01), so stable-high LMX weeks carried higher PA and lower NA than stable-low weeks and NA never rose along that line; Hypothesis 3 was supported. Hypothesis 4 was supported: after scaling for the different variances of PA and NA, the loss effect on NA exceeded the gain effect on PA (Δ = .09, CI95% = .017, .166).

Study 2's longer-tenured sample (2,136 weekly observations from 613 individuals) replicated the Study 1 tests of Hypotheses 1–4. The response surface was convex along the gain–loss line for both outcomes (γ = .18, SE = .06, p < .01 for PA; γ = .20, SE = .05, p < .01 for NA), with a positive slope at the origin for PA (γ = .13, SE = .06, p < .05) and a negative slope for NA (γ = −.13, SE = .04, p < .01). Instantaneous slopes one within-individual SD into each region were positive for gains on PA (γ = .33, SE = .08, p < .01) and negative for losses on NA (γ = −.34, SE = .07, p < .01), which the authors read as gains being associated with higher PA and losses with increasing NA. Along the stability line the slope was positive for PA (γ = .33, SE = .04, p < .01) and negative for NA (γ = −.26, SE = .04, p < .01), with an unexpected slight positive curvature for both PA (γ = .06, SE = .03, p < .01) and NA (γ = .05, SE = .02, p < .01); probing showed the PA stability slope was nonsignificant at the lowest LMX levels (γ = .11, CI95% [−.154, .375]) and significant only from LMX 1.76 upward, while the NA stability slope was negative at the lowest LMX levels (γ = −.45, CI95% [−.666, −.237]) and nonsignificant from LMX 4.70 upward.

Hypotheses 5–8 were tested only in Study 2. Hypothesis 5 was supported: between-individual LMX variance was higher for longer-tenured than for newly hired employees (Δ = .21, SE = .05, p < .01), while within-individual variance did not differ across the two groups (Δ = −.01, SE = .02, n.s.), indicating that LMX differentiates more as relationships mature but does not become more stable. Hypothesis 6 was supported: positive incident intensity was positively associated with that week's LMX (γ = .10, SE = .03, p < .01) and negative incident intensity was negatively associated with it (γ = −.19, SE = .08, p < .01), controlling for prior LMX. Hypothesis 7 was only partially supported: the positive incident intensity × relevance interaction was significant (γ = 1.08, SE = .38, p < .01; simple slope .19, SE = .05, p < .01 at high relevance versus .00, SE = .05, n.s. at low relevance), but the negative incident intensity × relevance interaction was nonsignificant (γ = −.16, SE = .24, n.s.), so negative incident intensity was universally aversive rather than conditional on relevance; an unexpected significant negative main effect of positive-incident relevance on LMX also appeared (γ = −.25, SE = .12). Hypothesis 8 was likewise partially supported: positive incident intensity interacted with prior LMX (γ = .31, SE = .11, p < .01), with a conditional effect of .19 (SE = .05, p < .01) at 1 SD above the mean of prior LMX versus .01 (SE = .05, n.s.) at 1 SD below, while the negative incident intensity × prior LMX interaction was nonsignificant (γ = −.18, SE = .18, n.s.).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper relaxes LMX theory's long-standing stability assumption and shows that even mature LMX dyads exhibit meaningful week-to-week fluctuation, with gains tied to PA and losses tied to NA in an asymmetric pattern. It distinguishes differentiation (between-dyad variance, which grows with relationship maturity) from stability (within-dyad variance, which does not shrink with maturity), and integrates referent-comparison logic into social-exchange theorizing on hierarchical relationships.

**Practical Implication**
Leaders should not treat established LMX relationships as static: maintaining relationships matters as much as cultivating them, because week-to-week deterioration generates negative affect even in otherwise high-quality dyads. Low-quality relationships can be improved but more slowly, since prior LMX moderates the impact of positive incidents; negative incidents, by contrast, hurt regardless of context, so leaders should guard against them and "cushion" unavoidable declines.

**Limitations**
The studies examined only one leadership construct (LMX), one temporal frame (week), one referent (prior self), and one main outcome family (affect), all from a single (follower) source. Common-method concerns remain despite the time-separated, nonlinear design. The newcomer subsample had a low within-individual response rate, limiting tests of change dynamics in newly formed relationships.

**Future Research**
Future work should integrate other referents (self vs. others, episode vs. average), other time frames, and other leadership constructs; examine antecedents of sustained upward or downward LMX trends and "crossing-over" between in- and out-groups; investigate person- and situation-level moderators (e.g., neuroticism, power distance) of sensitivity to gains and losses; and study leader-side reactions to perceived LMX changes for a more complete dyadic view.
