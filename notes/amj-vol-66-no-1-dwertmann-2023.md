# Receiving Service from a Person with a Disability: Stereotypes, Perceptions of Corporate Social Responsibility, and the Opportunity for Increased Corporate Reputation

**Abstract**
Whereas advocates point to benefits of employing people with disabilities for organizations, employers’ concern over negative customer reactions is still a barrier to the employment of people with disabilities in service occupations. We contribute to this discussion and the management literature on disability by examining the effects of receiving service from employees with a hearing disability and employees who use a wheelchair on corporate reputation. Based on signaling theory, stereotypes, and valuation-by-association logic, we argue and find in a multistudy, multimethod approach that employing people with disabilities can be perceived as corporate social responsibility and leads to better corporate reputation. A field study with 317 customers of a large international supermarket chain in Lithuania demonstrates higher ratings of corporate reputation for customers receiving service from an employee with a hearing disability than for customers receiving service from an employee without disabilities. In an online experiment using a Solomon four-group design, we utilize video vignettes to test our model with corporate social responsibility perceptions as a mediator. Together, our findings show that managers’ concerns about how biased customers might respond to service employees with disabilities are likely unfounded and highlight—in addition to an ethical case for inclusion—the potential for organizations to benefit from employing of people with disabilities, as it leads to favorable reputation effects for organizations.

**Research Question**
Does customer experience of receiving service from an employee with a disability improve customer-based corporate reputation, and if so, through what mechanism (CSR perceptions) and under what boundary conditions (contact quality, CSR motive attributions)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Nine numbered hypotheses, several with sub-parts. Hypotheses 1–3 are tested in the Study 1 field survey and Hypotheses 4–9 in the Study 2 experiment; Table 4 maps the two sets onto one another (the differences between Hypotheses 3 and 9, and between Hypotheses 2a/2b and 7, arise because contact quality is held constant in Study 2).
- Hypothesis 1. Receiving service from a person with a disability is positively related to corporate reputation.
- Hypothesis 2a. Receiving service from a person with a disability is related to more positive competence stereotypes toward people with disabilities when contact quality is higher but not when it is lower.
- Hypothesis 2b. Receiving service from a person with a disability is related to more positive warmth stereotypes toward people with disabilities when contact quality is higher but not when it is lower.
- Hypothesis 2c. The interaction effect between receiving service from a person with a disability and contact quality is stronger for competence than for warmth.
- Hypothesis 3. The interaction effect between receiving service from a person with a disability and contact quality on corporate reputation is mediated by (a) competence stereotypes and (b) warmth stereotypes.
- Hypothesis 4. Customers receiving service from a person with a disability will perceive the organization as higher on CSR.
- Hypothesis 5a. Sincere CSR motives will moderate the positive link between receiving service from a person with a disability and CSR such that it will be stronger when sincere motives are higher.
- Hypothesis 5b. Egoistic CSR motives will moderate the positive link between receiving service from a person with a disability and CSR such that it will be weaker when egoistic motives are higher.
- Hypothesis 6. CSR will mediate the link between receiving service from a person with a disability and corporate reputation.
- Hypothesis 7. Receiving service from a person with a disability will lead to more positive disability–job fit stereotypes for people with the same disability.
- Hypothesis 8. The positive effect of receiving service from a person with a disability on disability–job fit stereotypes for people with the same disability will be stronger for the disability for which the service provides stronger stereotype-disconfirming information (i.e., stronger for deaf than paraplegic).
- Hypothesis 9. The positive effect between receiving service from a person with a disability and corporate reputation is mediated by disability–job fit stereotypes.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Receiving service from a person with a disability (hearing disability; wheelchair user vs. no disability)
- DV(s): Customer-based corporate reputation; CSR perceptions; disability stereotypes (competence, warmth, and disability–job fit)
- Mediators: CSR perceptions; competence stereotypes; disability–job fit stereotypes
- Moderators: Contact quality (service satisfaction); attributed CSR motives (sincere vs. egoistic)

Drawing on signaling theory, the authors argue that hiring people with disabilities in customer-facing roles signals organizational concern for social well-being and is interpreted as a costly, sincere CSR act, which in turn boosts corporate reputation. Stereotype-disconfirming information during a satisfactory service encounter improves customers' disability stereotypes (competence and warmth) and prompts deeper cognitive processing. Causal reasoning then accounts for the link between the employee, as the stigma source, and the organization, as the target, so that through valuation by association the positive evaluation of the stigmatized employee transfers to the employing organization, raising its reputation. Attributed CSR motives moderate the earlier link from receiving service to CSR perceptions, which is predicted to be stronger when sincere motives are higher (H5a) and weaker when egoistic motives are higher (H5b).

**Data & Measures**
Both studies are quantitative; the two methods are a cross-sectional field survey (Study 1) and a randomized video-vignette experiment (Study 2), with distinct populations that should not be pooled.
- Study 1 (field survey; Hypotheses 1–3). Data: a self-administered customer satisfaction survey distributed by an interviewer during two random hours a day for four weeks in six stores in two major Lithuanian cities, at a subsidiary (about 60 supermarkets, over 3,000 employees) of an international retail chain; 322 questionnaires were completed and five were dropped for missing data on a focal variable, leaving n = 317. IV, receiving service from a person with a disability: a dummy (cashier with a hearing disability = 1, cashier without a disability = 0); 169 respondents (53%) checked out with a cashier with a hearing disability, identifiable through the interaction and a uniform badge reading "I cannot hear". Competence and warmth stereotypes toward people with disabilities: nine items from Fiske et al. (2002) on a 7-point scale from 1 (definitely not) to 7 (definitely yes), alpha = .88 and .96 respectively. Corporate reputation: Walsh and Beatty's (2007) 15-item customer-based corporate reputation scale covering five dimensions, retained on CFA grounds as a single second-order factor, alpha = .94. Contact quality: one item, "How satisfied are you with the cashier's service today?", from 1 (very dissatisfied) to 7 (very satisfied). Controls: contact frequency with people with disabilities, customer loyalty (purchases per month), customer health, and two dummies for how the respondent chose the checkout lane; Table 1 additionally reports employee and customer gender, customer age, and education. Estimation: independent-samples t-tests plus regression with mean-centered contact quality and bootstrapping in the PROCESS macro (Hayes, 2018; 5,000 bootstrap samples), with one-tailed tests and 90% CIs given the directional hypotheses. The design is associational rather than causally identified: the field setting did not permit random assignment of customers to cashiers, so the authors measured lane choice (41.3% by chance, 47.5% shortest queue, 10.9% a specific cashier) and re-ran the model without those who chose a specific cashier.
- Study 2 (online experiment; Hypotheses 4–9). Data: an online panel fielded by the provider Norstat, balanced on gender and age and covering all Lithuanian regions; 720 questionnaires were completed, 33 respondents were excluded for failing either of two attention checks and 125 for failing the manipulation check, leaving n = 562. Design: an experimental Solomon four-group design with random assignment, implemented as six groups (hearing disability, wheelchair, and control conditions, crossed with pretest versus no pretest). Participants read an introductory vignette about "Supermarket", a fictitious grocery chain, then watched one of three randomly assigned 30-second checkout video vignettes recorded at the Study 1 partner chain with the same professional female cashier and identical products, varying only disability (an "I cannot hear" badge, a wheelchair, or no disability). CSR: three items from Wagner, Lutz, and Weitz (2009), pretest alpha = .93, posttest alpha = .96. Corporate reputation: the same Walsh and Beatty (2007) measure as Study 1, pretest and posttest alpha = .98. Disability–job fit stereotypes: following Colella and Varma (1999), how well an average person who is deaf, or an average person with paraplegia, can perform a cashier job relative to an average person without a disability, on a 0 to 100 scale, administered in the posttest only. CSR motives: two egoistic items adapted from Vlachos et al. (2009) (r = .62, p ≤ .001) and two sincere items from Yoon et al. (2006) (r = .91, p ≤ .001). Controls: social desirability (Strahan & Gerbasi, 1972; 10 items, alpha = .71), rated realism of the vignette, a pretest dummy, age, gender, contact frequency, health, having a family member or friend with a disability, and prior experience of receiving service from someone with a disability; an unobtrusive behavioral measure counted the words written when respondents were asked to suggest checkout improvements. Estimation: regression with two condition dummies controlling for pretest (Table 7 reports n = 508 for the CSR model and n = 272 for the disability–job fit models), and PROCESS (Hayes, 2018) parallel-mediation and moderation models estimated on the posttest-only Groups 3, 4, and 6 (n = 272), again one-tailed with 90% CIs. Random assignment supports causal interpretation within the experiment; pretest sensitization was tested and was non-significant for all focal variables, but a significant pretest main effect led the authors to control for pretest.

**Key Findings**
Support is heterogeneous, so it is reported per study. The two studies converge on the headline claim that receiving service from a person with a disability goes with better corporate reputation, and Study 2 shows CSR perceptions carry that effect; they diverge on the stereotype pathway, which mediated in Study 1 (competence stereotypes) but not in Study 2 (disability–job fit stereotypes).
- Study 1 (field survey, n = 317; hearing disability only). Hypothesis 1 supported: corporate reputation was higher for customers served by a cashier with a hearing disability (M = 6.07, SD = 0.69) than by a cashier without a disability (M = 5.59, SD = 0.88; t(283) = 5.35, p ≤ .001; regression B = 0.40, t = 4.57). Hypothesis 2a only partly supported: the disability by contact quality interaction on competence stereotypes was significant (B = 0.27, t = 1.64, p = .05; change in R2 = .01, p = .05) and the simple slope was steeper at high contact quality (B = 0.71, t = 4.67, p ≤ .001), but contrary to the prediction it remained significant at low contact quality (−1 SD; B = 0.38, t = 1.93, p = .03). Hypothesis 2b supported: the interaction on warmth stereotypes was significant (B = 0.26, t = 2.35, p = .01; change in R2 = .02, p = .01), with the simple slope positive and significant at high contact quality (B = 0.39, t = 3.81, p ≤ .001) but not at low contact quality (B = 0.07, t = 0.51, p = .31). Hypothesis 2c supported: the effect was significantly stronger on competence (B = 0.60, t = 4.39) than on warmth (B = 0.28, t = 3.08; contrast B = 0.32, F(1, 309) = 7.75, p = .01). Hypothesis 3a supported: the indirect effect via competence stereotypes was significant (.06, 90% CI [0.009, 0.112]) and stronger at high (.08, 90% CI [0.021, 0.151]) than at low contact quality (.04, 90% CI [0.004, 0.097]), with a significant index of moderated mediation (.03, 90% CI [0.002, 0.074]). Hypothesis 3b not supported: the indirect effect via warmth stereotypes was not significant (.03, 90% CI [−0.002, 0.069]).
- Study 2 (online experiment, n = 562; hearing disability and wheelchair conditions). Hypothesis 4 supported in both conditions: CSR was higher than control for the hearing disability condition (B = 0.50, t = 3.28, p ≤ .001) and for the wheelchair condition (B = 0.30, t = 1.98, p = .02). Hypothesis 5a supported: the interaction with sincere motives was positive and significant for hearing disability (B = 0.19, t = 1.66, p = .05) and for wheelchair (B = 0.22, t = 2.06, p = .02), and the condition effect on CSR was significant only when sincere motives were attributed as high (hearing B = 0.77, t = 3.00, p ≤ .001; wheelchair B = 0.86, t = 3.53, p ≤ .001), not when low (B = 0.17, t = 0.67, p = .25; B = 0.18, t = 0.76, p = .22). Hypothesis 5b supported with the predicted negative interaction: hearing disability by egoistic motives B = −0.33, t = −2.43, p = .01 and wheelchair by egoistic motives B = −0.24, t = −1.88, p = .03, with the condition effect on CSR significant when egoistic motives were low (hearing B = 1.04, t = 3.37, p ≤ .001; wheelchair B = 0.82, t = 2.89, p = .002) but not when high (B = 0.02, t = 0.07, p = .47; B = 0.08, t = 0.27, p = .39). Hypothesis 6 supported: the indirect effect on corporate reputation through CSR was significant for both the hearing disability (.31, 90% CI [0.097, 0.543]) and wheelchair (.27, 90% CI [0.048, 0.494]) conditions. Hypothesis 7 only partially supported: disability–job fit stereotypes for deaf people were higher in the hearing disability condition (B = 25.94, t = 5.02, p ≤ .001), but disability–job fit for paraplegia did not shift in the wheelchair condition (B = −0.57, t = −0.13, p = .45). Hypothesis 8 supported: the two coefficients differed significantly (chi-square = 20.56, df = 1, p ≤ .001), the effect being stronger for the hearing disability. Hypothesis 9 not supported: neither indirect effect through disability–job fit stereotypes was significant. In a behavioral robustness check, participants in the hearing disability (B = 5.11, t = 3.10, p ≤ .001) and wheelchair (B = 3.21, t = 2.01, p = .02) conditions wrote significantly more words when asked to suggest checkout improvements than participants in the control condition.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper provides the first multimethod empirical evidence linking employment of people with disabilities to better corporate reputation through CSR perceptions, refining stigma-by-association theory by demonstrating positive "valuation by association" at the organizational level. It connects disability research with signaling theory and the stereotype content model, and identifies CSR motive attributions as a critical boundary condition that determines whether disability-inclusive hiring is interpreted as reputational asset or liability.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should view employment of people with disabilities in customer-facing roles as an opportunity rather than a reputational threat: such employment signals CSR and improves corporate reputation. Organizations should communicate sincere, value-driven motives for inclusive hiring rather than emphasizing tax incentives or self-interest, and should ensure good disability–job fit through accommodations, onboarding, and training so that service contact is satisfactory.

**Limitations**
Both studies were conducted in Lithuania with one supermarket chain. The authors treat the industry limit as the open one — whether the results would apply to other industries "remains an area for future research" — while anticipating comparable or potentially more positive results in other national contexts. Social desirability is a concern in disability research, although the authors mitigate it via unobtrusive measures, control variables, and the Solomon four-group design. CSR and corporate reputation correlate highly (.71–.79) in Study 2, raising discriminant-validity questions despite supportive CFA results.

**Future Research**
Future studies should test whether positive contact effects on stereotypes generalize across disabilities or remain disability-specific, investigate effects in different industries, and disentangle the distinct roles of disability visibility vs. customer awareness of the disability. Researchers should also probe the CSR–corporate reputation distinction further given the high observed correlation.
