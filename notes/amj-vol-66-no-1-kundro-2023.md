# Does Power Protect Female Moral Objectors? How and When Moral Objectors’ Gender, Power, and Use of Organizational Frames Influence Perceived Self-Control and Experienced Retaliation

**Abstract**
Organizational scholars have called upon higher-power individuals to serve as moral objectors to combat unethical behavior at work because it is assumed they will face less retaliation. However, research has painted an unclear picture of whether power protects women in the same way as it does men. In this paper, we draw on two distinct role theories (i.e., power role theories and gender role theories) as well as expectancy violation theory, theorizing and finding that female moral objectors benefit less from power than male moral objectors because they are viewed as lower in self-control. We further investigate an alternative remedy, or organizational frame, that may mitigate retaliation against higher-powered female moral objectors, finding that using this frame increases perceptions of self-control and reduces retaliation. We test and find support for our theory across four studies, including an archival study (n 5 33,715), a critical incident technique experiment, and two preregistered experiments testing our intervention.

**Research Question**
Does structural power protect female moral objectors from retaliation in the same way it protects men, and under what conditions (i.e., how a moral objection is framed) can higher-powered female moral objectors avoid retaliation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1: A moral objector's structural power is negatively related to retaliation.
- Hypothesis 2: A moral objector's gender moderates the relationship between power and retaliation, such that the relationship is more negative for men compared to women.
- Hypothesis 3: Structural power, gender, and moral objection framing interact, such that when a moral objection has a standard frame, the relationship between structural power and retaliation is more negative for men compared to women; however, when a moral objection has an organizational frame, retaliation is mitigated and consistent across gender and structural power levels.
- Hypothesis 4: When a moral objection has a standard frame, the negative indirect effect of structural power on retaliation, through self-control, is stronger when the moral objector is a man versus a woman; when a moral objection has an organizational frame, there is not any difference in the indirect effects of structural power on retaliation, through self-control, for male or female moral objectors.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Moral objector's structural power; moral objector's gender; moral objection framing (standard vs. organizational)
- DV(s): Experienced/enacted retaliation against the moral objector
- Mediator: Observers' perceptions of the moral objector's self-control
- Moderators: Gender (moderates power -> retaliation); moral objection framing (moderates the power x gender interaction)

Drawing on power role theories, gender role theories, and expectancy violation theory, the authors argue that moral objection is an agentic act that observers expect from higher-power actors but not from women. When higher-powered women morally object using a standard frame, they violate salient gender role expectations, are perceived as lower in self-control (impulsive and selfishly imposing their morality on the group), and face retaliation. Reframing the same objection in organizational/communal language signals other-oriented benefit to the group, neutralizes the gender role violation, restores self-control perceptions, and reduces retaliation across gender and power levels.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 (archival survey). The data are archival responses to a survey the U.S. Merit Systems Protection Board administered to 40,526 U.S. federal employees in 2007. The authors retained only respondents indicating that one or more moral objection questions applied to them (34,085), of whom 33,715 provided complete data on retaliation, gender, structural power, and controls and constitute the final sample; it was 59% men, 78% White, with a mean age between 40 and 50 years. Agencies represented include the Environmental Protection Agency, the Department of Justice, and NASA. Structural power was operationalized as the respondent's supervisory level: each participant selected one of five levels reflecting whom, if anyone, they supervised (1 = entry-level employee supervising no one; 2 = provides project guidance without formal supervisory responsibility; 3 = supervisor responsible for performance appraisals; 4 = manager supervising one or more supervisors; 5 = executive), ranked 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest). Gender was self-reported male or female (coded 1 = male, 0 = female). Retaliation was binary, coded 1 if the respondent reported having experienced retaliation within the past two years for one or more of five moral objection behaviors (disclosing health and safety dangers, unlawful behavior, fraud, waste, or abuse; testifying for or otherwise assisting an individual exercising whistleblowing, equal opportunity, or appeal rights; refusing to obey an unlawful order; reporting unwanted sexual attention or sexual harassment; disagreeing with management decisions), and 0 otherwise. Controls were minority status, organizational tenure, education, and age. Estimation used logistic regression with structural power entered as a continuous variable plus a power x gender interaction term. The design is associational: the authors state these data are correlational and did not allow them to address the directionality of the effects.

Study 2 (critical incident experiment). 250 full-time employees were recruited from TurkPrime (now CloudResearch), described at recruitment as 55% male, mean age 36.03 (SD = 9.80), 77% White, and working across a range of industries and organizations; 19 who could not recall a matching event and 12 who wrote no description were removed, leaving a final sample of 219. The design combined the critical incident technique with a 2 (power: average versus high) x 2 (gender: female versus male) between-subjects randomized experiment: participants were randomly assigned to recall and describe a real workplace occasion on which a high- or average-power man or woman morally objected. Retaliation was self-reported enacted retaliation on five items adapted from Cortina and Magley's (2003) social retaliation measure, rated 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); a sample item was "I retaliated against this individual" (alpha = .85). Manipulation checks covered the objector's perceived decision-making authority and rank and the objector's gender. Estimation used between-subjects ANOVA with simple-effects tests.

Study 3 (preregistered vignette experiment). 454 working adults were recruited from TurkPrime, reported at recruitment as mean age 39.63 (SD = 11.59), 17.98 years of work experience (SD = 10.92), 55% male and 81% White; per the preregistration, 36 who failed one of two attention checks and 19 who wrote nonsense open-ended responses were excluded, leaving a final sample of 399. The design was a preregistered 2 (structural power: average versus high) x 2 (gender: female versus male) x 2 (moral objection framing: standard versus organizational) between-subjects randomized vignette experiment in which participants imagined working at a mid-sized insurance firm and read an email from a colleague; gender was manipulated by name (Michael versus Michelle), and the organizational frame added bracketed language stating that the objected-to practice could hurt the company and that stopping it would protect the organization and its members. Retaliation intentions used the same five items as Study 2 (alpha = .89). Perceived self-control used three reverse-coded items following Righetti and Finkenauer (2011) (alpha = .92). Alternative mechanisms were measured as warmth (alpha = .94), competence (alpha = .96), dominance (alpha = .88), and self-threat (alpha = .95); a six-factor confirmatory factor analysis showed adequate fit and fit better than five-factor alternatives. Estimation used between-subjects ANOVA plus moderated path analysis (PROCESS Model 12) with 10,000 bootstrap samples.

Study 4 (preregistered behavioral experiment). 451 working adults were recruited from TurkPrime, reported at recruitment as mean age 35.88 (SD = 11.29), 51% male, 14.91 years of work experience (SD = 10.74) and 70% White; per the preregistration, 45 who failed one of two attention checks and 47 who wrote nonsense chat messages were excluded, leaving a final sample of 359. The design was a preregistered 2 x 2 x 2 (power x gender x framing) between-subjects immersive online experiment adapted from Wellman and colleagues (2016): participants believed they were working in real time with five other participants to brainstorm ways to improve MTurk using chat software called ChatPlat, and a programmed "teammate" (Kevin versus Kate) morally objected to sourcing ideas from the internet on plagiarism grounds. Retaliation was measured behaviorally: participants wrote a chat message to the moral objector, and two independent coders blind to hypotheses and conditions rated its retaliatory content on a 5-point scale (1 = very positive, 5 = very negative), with the two raters' scores averaged for analysis (ICC2 = .92). Perceived self-control used the same three items as Study 3 (alpha = .90), alongside warmth (alpha = .95), competence (alpha = .95), dominance (alpha = .89), and self-threat (alpha = .93); a five-factor confirmatory factor analysis showed good fit. Estimation mirrored Study 3: between-subjects ANOVA plus moderated path analysis with 10,000 bootstrap samples.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (archival survey, n = 33,715) supports both hypotheses it tested. Hypothesis 1 supported: structural power is negatively related to experienced retaliation (b = -.12, SE = .02, OR = .89, p < .001). Hypothesis 2 supported: the power x gender interaction is significant (b = -.08, SE = .03, OR = .92, p = .007), and simple slopes show that the negative power-retaliation slope is steeper for men (b = -0.15, SE = 0.02, p < .0001) than for women (b = -.07, SE = .02, p = .004) — the effect is negative for both genders but "significantly greater for men." A supplementary archival analysis of an earlier Merit Board survey found similar support for Hypotheses 1 and 2.
- Study 2 (critical incident experiment, n = 219): Hypothesis 1 not supported — a between-subjects ANOVA revealed no main effect of power on retaliation (F[1, 216] = 2.34, p = .13). Hypothesis 2 supported — the power x gender interaction was significant (F[1, 215] = 5.18, p = .02, partial eta-squared = .02): high-power men faced less retaliation (M = 2.52, SD = 1.46) than average-power men (M = 3.27, SD = 1.40; F[1, 215] = 7.23, p = .008), whereas high-power women (M = 3.15, SD = 1.76) faced levels similar to average-power women (M = 2.98, SD = 1.39; F[1, 215] = .34, p = .56).
- Study 3 (preregistered vignette experiment, n = 399): Hypothesis 1 supported — participants were less likely to retaliate against the high-power objector (M = 2.42, SD = 1.44) than the average-power objector (M = 2.77, SD = 1.32; F[1, 395] = 7.37, p = .007, partial eta-squared = .02). Hypothesis 2 not supported — there was no significant two-way power x gender interaction on retaliation (F[1, 392] = 1.91, p = .17). Hypothesis 3 supported — the three-way power x gender x framing interaction was significant (F[1, 391] = 6.75, p = .0097, partial eta-squared = .02); the power-by-gender interaction was significant under the standard frame (F[1, 391] = 7.60, p = .006) but not the organizational frame (F[1, 391] = 1.09, p = .30), and under the standard frame men (F[1, 391] = 12.20, p = .001) but not women (F[1, 391] = 0.18, p = 1.00) faced less retaliation when they had high power.
- Study 3, Hypothesis 4 supported: the three-way interaction on perceived self-control was significant (b = -1.32, SE = .63, p = .037), and self-control was negatively associated with retaliation (b = -.50, SE = .03, p < .0001). Under the standard frame, the indirect effect of power on retaliation through self-control was significant and negative for male objectors (indirect effect = -.40, SE = .16, 95% CI [-.72, -.09]) but not for female objectors (indirect effect = .09, SE = .14, 95% CI [-.18, .36]), with a significant difference between them (standard-frame index of moderated mediation = -.49, SE = .21, 95% CI [-.91, -.08]). Under the organizational frame there was no male-female difference (IMM = .16, SE = .23, 95% CI [-.30, .59]). The overall index of moderated mediation was significant (IMM = .65, SE = .31, 95% CI [.05, 1.27]).
- Study 4 (preregistered behavioral experiment, n = 359): Hypothesis 1 not supported — participants were not significantly less likely to retaliate against the high-power objector than the average-power objector (F[1, 355] = 2.56, p = .11). Hypothesis 2 supported — the power x gender interaction was significant (F[1, 352] = 12.46, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .03): high-power men were retaliated against less (M = 1.79, SD = 1.01) than average-power men (M = 2.30, SD = 1.21; F[1, 352] = 12.40, p < .001), whereas high-power women (M = 2.34, SD = 1.31) faced levels similar to average-power women (M = 2.08, SD = 1.21; F[1, 352] = 3.04, p = .08). Hypothesis 3 supported — the three-way interaction was significant (F[1, 351] = 5.47, p = .02, partial eta-squared = .02); the power-by-gender interaction was significant under the standard frame (F[1, 351] = 17.40, p < .001) but not the organizational frame (F[1, 351] = .71, p = .40), and under the standard frame men (F[1, 351] = 14.70, p < .001) but not women (F[1, 351] = 4.36, p = .08) faced less retaliation when they had high power.
- Study 4, Hypothesis 4 supported: the three-way interaction on self-control was significant (b = -1.31, SE = .61, p = .03), and self-control was negatively associated with retaliation (b = -.24, SE = .03, p < .0001). Under the standard frame, the indirect effect of power on retaliation through self-control was significant for male objectors (indirect effect = -.24, SE = .09, 95% CI [-.42, -.08]) but not female objectors (indirect effect = .06, SE = .06, 95% CI [-.07, .20]), with a significant difference (standard-frame IMM = -.30, SE = .11, 95% CI [-.54, -.10]); under the organizational frame there was no male-female difference (IMM = .02, SE = .11, 95% CI [-.20, .24]). The overall index of moderated mediation was significant (IMM = .32, SE = .16, 95% CI [.03, .65]).
- Support pattern across the four studies is heterogeneous and should not be read as uniform. Hypothesis 1 was supported in Studies 1 and 3 but not in Studies 2 and 4. Hypothesis 2 was supported in Studies 1, 2, and 4 but not in Study 3, where the power-by-gender effect emerged only within the three-way interaction with framing. Hypotheses 3 and 4 were tested only in Studies 3 and 4 and were supported in both. In Studies 3 and 4 the experimental conditions interacted to predict perceived self-control but did not significantly interact to predict warmth, competence, or dominance, and the moderated-mediation indices for those alternative mechanisms (and for self-threat) included zero, so self-control was the only mediator supported. The authors report that effect sizes across the studies ranged from small to medium.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to gender and power research by systematically showing that power does not automatically protect female moral objectors—instead, moral objection framing functions as a more effective antidote because organizational frames do not violate gender role expectations. It extends threat-based accounts of moral objection by establishing perceived self-control as a key mediating mechanism explaining retaliation, and it contributes to organizational ethics theory by demonstrating that, counter to prior assumptions, power does not shield all moral objectors—particularly women—from retaliation.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should encourage all moral objectors, not just women, to use organizational rather than standard frames, since organizationally framed objections face less retaliation across the board. Managers can train employees in framing, debias observers' tendency to view female moral objectors as lower in self-control through perspective-taking workshops, and publicly highlight the communal benefits of moral objections to reduce their perceived agentic threat.

**Limitations**
Hypothesis 1 (main effect of power on retaliation) was not supported in Studies 2 and 4, though the interaction effects were consistent. The studies tested framing only at average and high power levels, leaving extremely low-power positions unexamined, and three of four studies relied on MTurk samples. Retaliation was treated as a unitary construct rather than its multiple forms, and the objected-to behavior was not itself gendered.

**Future Research**
Future work should investigate framing effects at lower organizational levels, examine multiple forms of retaliation, test whether organizational framing helps women objecting to gendered topics like sexual harassment or gender equity, and explore alternative non-other-oriented frames. Researchers should also study other downstream consequences of competence perceptions, such as promotion and investment opportunities for female moral objectors.
