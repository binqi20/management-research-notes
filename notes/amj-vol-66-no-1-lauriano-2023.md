# Losing Control: The Uncertain Management of Concealable Stigmas When Work and Social Media Collide

**Abstract**
Scholars have typically portrayed employees' management of concealable stigmas in face-to-face encounters in which social groups are easily separable (e.g., friends, family, and coworkers). This analytical predisposition overlooks the possible roles of social network sites (SNSs) such as Facebook and Instagram. These online platforms enable a cohabitation of different audiences—that is, a context collapse that allows a growing, invisible audience to easily access information about one's stigma. In our qualitative analysis in a Latin American organization, we develop a model that documents the everyday dynamics of context collapse among gay male employees. In a disclosure dilemma, employees are uncertain about how to be a professional online and simultaneously keep SNSs as platforms where they can show more relaxed versions of themselves. As a response, most employees adopt mirroring, and attempt to reflect their face-to-face disclosure levels on SNSs. Some employees engage in destigmatization efforts online, and as an outlier case we mapped an employee in collapse denial. Our study questions the established idea of disclosure as a relatively controlled process in microinteractions. We also nuance the assumption of SNSs as safe spaces and show the unintended impacts of context collapse on the stigmatized.

**Research Question**
How does the context collapse caused by social network sites (SNSs) impact employees' management of concealable stigmas?

**Mechanism Process**
- Types of context collapse: bottom-up context collisions at work (spontaneous, taken-for-granted online–offline interactions between employees) and top-down context collusions at work (managers' intentional stimuli to use SNSs for work-related purposes)
- Disclosure dilemma: "How can I be a professional online and keep SNSs as safe spaces?"
- Tensions: professionalism understood as heteronormativity vs. SNSs as safe spaces; default publicness of SNSs vs. need to compartmentalize audiences
- Response patterns (employees with concealable stigmas):
  - Mirroring (dominant): replicating face-to-face disclosure levels on SNSs, either toward concealing or toward revealing — characterized by reproducing face-to-face professionalism as heteronormativity on SNSs and constant effort to maintain online safe spaces
  - Online destigmatization efforts (minor): strategic use of SNSs to normalize and differentiate being gay at work
  - Collapse denial (outlier): treating online and face-to-face as independent contexts, producing mismatched disclosure behaviors

The process documents how SNSs collapse previously separable audiences, generating uncertainty about who sees what and when. Gay male employees lose the relative control over disclosure that prior literature assumed they possessed in face-to-face microinteractions. To cope, most fall back on what they already know — face-to-face heteronormative scripts — and attempt to mirror these online, even though SNSs' default publicness, peers' unsolicited tagging, and managers' work-related demands repeatedly break that mirroring and force renewed boundary work. A minority leverages SNSs as activist platforms, while a denial case illustrates the costs of treating the two contexts as separate.

**Data & Measures**
Single-site inductive, grounded qualitative case study at Globalcar (pseudonym), the Latin American subsidiary of a multinational car manufacturer. Nothing is measured in the variable sense: the authors state they did not test theory but instead compared everyday data sources to formulate theoretical explanations, and no causal identification strategy is claimed. Observational corpus: the main researcher sent a letter to the HR vice president in 2018 and was allocated to the sustainability department from October 2018 to January 2019, undertaking 480 hours of participant observation adhering to Globalcar's working hours (Monday–Friday, 8 a.m.–5 p.m.), participating in meetings, spreadsheet analyses, presentations, and informal gatherings, and writing daily chronological accounts into a 200-page Word document; he additionally shadowed one informant (Luciano) for around 16 hours over a month and made informal follow-up visits to five informants. Interview corpus: the sequence began with two pilot interviews, with Luciano and Gabriel, which triggered snowball sampling; the Methods section then reports 20 formal interviews with 18 gay male employees, Luciano and Gabriel each being interviewed a second time at the end of the in-loco period, and Appendix Table A1 lists 18 informants with those same two marked as interviewed twice — so the corpus is 20 interviews across 18 people, not two separate rounds. The semi-structured protocol covered behaviors across social groups; what, how, and when employees concealed or revealed their stigmas online and at work; and possible dilemmas therein. Each interview lasted 60–120 minutes and was conducted in the participants' native language, which the researcher also speaks. Appendix Table A1 profiles the 18 informants: six analysts, ten coordinators, and two executives, with tenure ranging from 0.5 to 22 years. Nine potential interviewees refused to participate for fear of exposure. A third data stream came from informants who spontaneously sent friendship requests after their interviews, giving the researcher access to many of their SNS profiles; this was used to double-check self-reported behavior (for example, Mario's stated use of SNSs to promote LGBTQIA+ causes was later confirmed in his Instagram stories).

Constructs were operationalized by coding rather than measurement, with data collection and analysis intertwined. Preliminary analysis consisted of weekly debrief meetings to discuss emerging findings, make corrections, and reflect on positionality; the team iterated observing, interviewing, analyzing, and categorizing until new interview examples were reflected in the primary constructs and no longer changed the reading of the case. After leaving the field the authors started organizing their coding system using NVivo, engaged in constant comparison of cases and observations, and after several rounds of coding and discussion determined three broader analytical dimensions: (a) types of context collapse, (b) possible tensions, and (c) strategies to conceal or reveal stigmas in face-to-face and online interactions. Each dimension was then made sense of by returning to the literature: Davis and Jurgenson's (2014) distinction between incentivized (collusions) and spontaneous (collisions) collapse structured the two types of context collapse; social media studies on LGBTQIA+ individuals and studies of professionalism as heteronormativity (Rumens & Kerfoot, 2009) informed the disclosure dilemma; and informants' narratives were compared and triangulated against the researcher's observations and conversations with other peers to map the three response patterns. The data structure, analytical rationale, and exemplar quotes are presented in Table 1 and the resulting model in Figure 2. Trustworthiness measures included an insider–outsider design in which the second author acted as a devil's advocate on the immersed first author's premises and conclusions, member checks with Globalcar's diversity coordinator during fieldwork, and a further round of member checking in which the assembled analysis was presented to selected informants; accounts were written up as descriptive vignettes complemented by interview and observation passages.

**Key Findings**
Two types of context collapse operate concurrently at Globalcar. Bottom-up context collisions at work are the spontaneous, taken-for-granted interactions among employees that collapse online and face-to-face information — colleagues add each other on SNSs, bring up each other's posts in face-to-face conversation, and platform architecture propagates friendship suggestions across SNSs owned by the same conglomerate. Top-down context collusions at work are managers' intentional stimuli to use SNSs for work-related purposes, issued with unclear guidelines on online behavior. Their confluence generates a disclosure dilemma for employees with concealable stigmas — "How can I be a professional online and keep SNSs as safe spaces?" — because being validated as a professional at Globalcar is bound up with heteronormative behavior, while SNSs are the platforms on which these employees express more untailored versions of themselves.

The dominant response is mirroring: most employees adopt mirroring and attempt to reflect their face-to-face disclosure levels on SNSs, either toward concealing (Gabriel in Vignette 3, and Robert, Arnold, Antonio, and Travis) or toward revealing (Mario in Vignette 4, and Oscar and Omar). Mirroring has two characteristics — the reproduction of face-to-face professionalism as heteronormativity on SNSs, and constant efforts to keep online safe spaces. The key finding here is negative: mirroring does not resolve the uncertainty that prompts it. The authors liken it to a fun-house mirror in which what employees intend to disclose is not what mirroring produces, so further adjustment is required. Employees repeatedly lost control of who saw what and when despite privacy work — friends tagged them in photos (Antonio, Keaton), a colleague printed a Facebook picture of Keaton and his boyfriend and circulated it on the production line, and colleagues traced the partner Gabriel had disguised as a girlfriend, so his concealment effort paradoxically produced the gossip he had tried to avoid.

Online destigmatization efforts are a minor pattern: employees who still feel constrained but decide to accept the risks put disproportional effort into the strategic use of SNSs to normalize and differentiate being gay at work — Joseph advocates against his stigma in explanatory Facebook posts and Instagram stories, Harry changed his profile picture to show support for LGBTQIA+ causes, Gabriel came to treat his posts as more consequential than his face-to-face behavior and used them to come out to multiple invisible audiences at once, and Ethan adopted "a more activist posture" (Vignette 5). These employees remain hesitant about the best online approach, but see themselves as occupying privileged positions (executives, or with job stability) that let them pioneer such discussions without fearing negative professional repercussions. Collapse denial is an outlier represented by a single case: Francis is out on SNSs but not at Globalcar, takes no measures to limit access to his online content, and continues to use SNSs daily for work purposes, so peers and superiors read the two contexts as contradictory (his manager says he "seems to be one person here and another outside"). Denying the collapse paradoxically highlights it, and the tensions this mismatch provokes are what the authors argue support why mirroring is dominant.

Overall, the study finds that SNSs significantly change gay employees' control over who sees what and when online, so that disclosure becomes an uncertain rather than a relatively controlled process and employees struggle to perform the contextual cost–benefit analysis prior work assumes. Contrary to typical expectations in research treating SNSs as safe spaces, what prevailed in this analysis were not positive experiences of community-building and authenticity but the difficulties and efforts SNSs provoke; maintaining a safe space requires continuous monitoring and behavioral adjustment across both online and face-to-face interactions. The authors also find that context collapse at work does not produce similar outcomes across employee groups: most SNS-related interactions and managerial initiatives at Globalcar were not intended to be onerous for gay employees, yet they were.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper sets boundary conditions on the literature that has portrayed concealable-stigma disclosure as a relatively controlled individual decision, showing that under context collapse this process becomes uncertain and employees lose control over who sees their stigma-related information and when. It nuances the assumption of SNSs as safe spaces by documenting the continuous monitoring and behavioral adjustment required to keep them so, especially when work invades them. It also challenges the assumption in context-collapse-at-work research that the phenomenon affects all employees similarly, surfacing unintended negative impacts of context collapse on employees with concealable stigmas.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should recognize the repercussions of spontaneous and incentivized online interactions for employees with concealable stigmas — antidiscriminatory policies and HR training should explicitly cover online behavior. Managers should be aware that incentivizing SNS use for work-related purposes can be disproportionately burdensome for LGBTQIA+ employees. Identifying employees already engaging in online destigmatization efforts and involving them in diversity committees can help organizations develop more responsive practices, as Globalcar did after the study.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on gay male employees in one Latin American automotive subsidiary, so patterns may not generalize to other stigmatized groups or industries. The automotive setting adds further complexity of its own, which the authors attribute to a masculine environment that directly equates professionalism with heteronormativity. Access constraints and the topic's sensitivity meant that nine potential informants declined to participate for fear of exposure, which the authors read as reaffirming the topic's sensitivity.

**Future Research**
Future work should extend the model to lesbian, bisexual, and transgender employees and examine intersections with race, religion, class, and culture. Researchers could study specific organizational practices such as the role of social media in hiring, the impact of COVID-19 on SNS-mediated work interactions, and longitudinal feedback loops between disclosure and outcomes. Comparative work across different SNS affordances (e.g., Facebook vs. Reddit vs. anonymous platforms) could deepen understanding of how platform features shape stigma management.
