# When Do Novel Business Models Lead to High Performance? A Configurational Approach to Value Drivers, Competitive Strategy, and Firm Environment

**Abstract**
The strategy literature has viewed novel business model design as a universally positive antecedent of high performance in entrepreneurial ventures. Not only have scholars emphasized novelty as a necessity, but they have almost considered it a sufficient condition for performance. Challenging this assumption, we hypothesize that novelty can produce high performance only in combination with specific configurations that feature other value drivers, such as efficiency, lock-in, and complementarity, which help firms not only create value but also capture more of it. Fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis of two samples of Internet-enabled firms largely supports our hypotheses. We find that novelty alone is insufficient for high performance, even when it appears as a necessary condition for very high performance in new technological environments. Our results highlight how novelty combines effectively with other value drivers (in particular with efficiency) and strategies (in particular with differentiation) contingent on the intensity of competition, firm size, and firms’ technological environment. Our study contributes to literature streams on value creation, business model design, and innovation.

**Research Question**
When and how does novel business model design lead to high firm performance, given the interdependence between business model value drivers (novelty, efficiency, lock-in, complementarity), competitive strategy (differentiation, low-cost), and firm environment (competition, firm size, technological maturity)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Novelty is insufficient for high firm performance when other value drivers and strategies are absent from a configuration.
- Hypothesis 2. In high-performing configurations, novelty and efficiency will be complements rather than substitutes.
- Hypothesis 3. In competitive industries, it is necessary to combine novelty with either differentiation or low-cost strategy to achieve high performance.
- Hypothesis 4a. In mature technological environments, the high-performing configurations with novelty will feature greater configurational complexity of complementary design elements in small firms than in large firms.
- Hypothesis 4b. In new technological environments, the high-performing configurations with novelty will feature greater configurational complexity of complementary design elements in large firms than in small firms.
- Hypothesis 5. Novelty is necessary for very high performance when a business model is enabled by new technology, but not when the technology is mature.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Business model value drivers — novelty, efficiency, lock-in, complementarity; competitive strategies — differentiation, low-cost
- DV(s): High firm performance and very high firm performance (calibrated from Tobin's Q)
- Moderators / contingencies: Intensity of competition, firm size (small vs. large), technological-environment maturity (new vs. established Internet)
- Mediators: Not modeled; the configurational approach analyzes conjunctural causation and equifinality rather than mediation

The paper develops a configurational mechanism in which novelty primarily drives value creation ("growing the pie") but is insufficient on its own to deliver high firm performance because it does little for value capture. Performance arises only when novelty co-occurs with at least one value-capture-oriented driver (efficiency, lock-in, or complementarity) and a coherent competitive strategy, and the right configuration depends on firm size and whether the enabling technology is new or mature. fsQCA reveals equifinal high-performing configurations and shows novelty as necessary for very high performance only in new-technology contexts.

**Data & Measures**
Two separate cross-sectional samples of publicly traded Internet-enabled firms, drawn with a sampling strategy the authors describe as identical to Zott and Amit (2007, 2008): a firm counted as Internet-enabled if it generated sales via online channels and actively promoted itself as an e-business or was promoted as such by industry experts. Sample 1 represents the new-technology context. It was restricted to Internet-enabled firms that had gone public between (and including) 1996 and 1999 on the five largest stock exchanges in North America and Europe (Euronext, Frankfurt, London, NASDAQ, NYSE); from 384 initially eligible firms the authors drew a random sample of 201, and the final number fell to 125 firms because of unclear ownership structures, limited data availability, or lack of access. All business model, strategy, competition and financial data for Sample 1 are from the year 1999. Sample 2 represents the mature-technology context and is a distinct population 15 years later, with data collected from the year 2014. Because IPO activity had fallen, the IPO-window criterion was dropped and public e-businesses were considered regardless of the year they went public, with the other sampling criteria unchanged; firms were retained only if a clear majority of revenue came from one main business model. After assessing more than 300 potentially eligible firms the sample stood at 173 e-businesses, and the same data-availability exclusions reduced it to 169 firms. The two samples are analyzed separately, not pooled.

The outcome, firm performance, is measured as Tobin's Q — the ratio of a firm's market value to its total assets — collected from Compustat and the sample firms' annual reports. It is calibrated into two distinct outcome sets. High performance uses fully-out 0.70, crossover 1.00, and fully-in 2.00, the crossover set at 1 on theoretical grounds (perceived value equals asset value) and the other two anchors taken from a Compustat population of all public firms listed on any North American exchange between 1998 and 2019, whose yearly median Tobin's Q ranged from 0.45 to 0.99. Very high performance uses fully-out 2.00, crossover 3.50, and fully-in 5.00, corresponding to the 75th and 90th population percentiles. The four business model design themes and two strategies were rated on the Zott and Amit (2007, 2008) scales, with the lock-in and complementarity items taken from a Zott and Amit (2002) working paper; because the competition items were not publicly available, the authors developed their own four-item scale based on existing literature such as Porter (1980). In total 13 items measured novelty, 11 efficiency, 11 lock-in, nine complementarity, three differentiation strategy, four low-cost strategy, and four competition, with Cronbach's alphas ranging from 0.54 to 0.77 across the 1999 and 2014 data. Items were hand-coded from annual reports and IPO prospectuses, supplemented by press releases, news articles, industry analyses and reports, company websites (including Google Cache and the Wayback Machine) and other SEC filings, by eight trained students who each collected data on about 60 firms and then cross-checked against a second student; average interrater consistency was 0.71 for the 1999 data and 0.79 for the 2014 data (Pearson correlation; Cohen's kappa 72% and 78%), rising to 100% agreement after one to three rounds of discussion refereed by the authors. All multi-item conditions were calibrated at 0.75 (fully in), 0.50 (crossover) and 0.25 (fully out) for 0–1 scales and at 4, 3 and 2 for 1–5 scales; firm size was calibrated on employee counts at 1,000 (fully in), 250 (crossover) and 50 (fully out), following the common SME definition.

Analysis is fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis within the neo-configurational approach: a truth table of all theoretically possible combinations, a raw consistency threshold raised from the recommended 0.75 to 0.80, PRI kept above 0.75 in all analyses, and a frequency threshold of two cases per configuration (which retained at least the recommended 75% of sample firms). The counterfactual analysis assumed only that the presence of each business model value driver contributes positively to high performance, with no directional assumptions for the other four conditions. A separate necessity analysis scored each individual condition against the 0.90 benchmark. The design is set-theoretic and associational rather than causally identified — it establishes sufficiency and necessity relations among conditions in cross-sectional data, with no instrument, experiment, or panel-based identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
The necessity analysis rejects novelty as a necessary condition. Novelty's necessity score for high performance is 0.47 in 1999 and 0.54 in 2014, and for very high performance 0.57 in 1999 and 0.58 in 2014 — all far below the 0.90 benchmark. Efficiency, by contrast, is at or near necessity: 0.895 for high and 0.916 for very high performance in 1999, and 0.899 and 0.937 respectively in 2014. Competition also reaches 0.927 for very high performance in 2014. Because no condition was fully necessary across both samples and both outcome definitions, all were retained in the configurational analysis.

Hypothesis 1 is supported. Across all configurations there is no evidence of a (very-)high-performing configuration featuring novelty without other value drivers and strategies; every configuration in which novelty is or can be present carries at least one additional value driver. Novelty is present in HP1, HP11 and VHP1–VHP3, absent in HP5–HP8 and HP10, and does not matter in HP2–HP4, HP9a and HP9b. When the enabling technology is new, novelty must be joined by efficiency and a differentiation strategy; when it is mature, novelty must be combined with at least efficiency and either lock-in or complementarity. Hypothesis 2 is only partly supported. Novelty and efficiency co-occur in HP1 and HP11 and in all three very-high-performing configurations, and whenever novelty is present efficiency is present too — but efficiency appears without novelty (HP5–HP8, HP10) and in configurations where novelty does not matter (HP2–HP4, HP9a, HP9b). The authors therefore conclude that novelty and efficiency are both complements and substitutes for high performance, and complements for very high performance only when the enabling technology is new. Hypothesis 3 is supported: when novelty is present the data support it in 5 of 5 configurations — novelty always co-occurs with intense competition and a differentiation strategy, regardless of firm size and technological environment — and when novelty does not matter it holds in 3 of 5 configurations (HP2–HP4 versus HP9a and HP9b, which can feature novelty yet have both strategies absent).

The data provide full support for Hypothesis 4a and only partial support for Hypothesis 4b. Under mature technology (Sample 2), the sole small-firm high-performing configuration HP11 combines all four value drivers plus differentiation, while the large-firm configurations HP9a and HP9b contain only two other value drivers in addition to novelty when it is present — the greater complexity in small firms that H4a predicted, making this context "easier" for novel large firms. Under new technology (Sample 1), the large-firm configuration HP1 combines novelty with efficiency and both strategies, while the only small-firm configuration, HP2, needs only efficiency and differentiation when novelty is present; but HP3 is relatively complex and spans both small and large firms, which is why support for H4b is partial rather than full. Hypothesis 5 is partly supported. Novelty is present, and is a core condition, in all three very-high-performing configurations (VHP1–VHP3) under new technology, which supports the first half of the prediction; the second half — that novelty is not necessary for very high performance once the technology matures — could not be confirmed, because no very-high-performing configuration was identified at all in the mature-technology sample. The authors note the tension with novelty's low individual necessity score (0.57) and attribute it to the frequency threshold of two, which excludes configurations observed in only one firm from the standard analysis.

Solution statistics: under new technology, overall solution consistency and coverage are 0.91 and 0.58 for high performance and 0.80 and 0.37 for very high performance; under mature technology they are 0.83 and 0.43 for high performance. The causal-asymmetry analysis found no configurations consistently producing low or not-high performance in the new-technology sample (consistency levels well below 0.75, implying many ways to underperform), but under mature technology it did identify a set of seven configurations that did not achieve very high performance (overall consistency 0.83, coverage 0.59). There, configuration LP3 shows that novelty as the only value driver present is sufficient to prevent very high performance regardless of firm size — further support for Hypothesis 1 — and all solutions indicate that the absence of efficiency is likely to prevent very high performance. Robustness checks that recalibrated the set-membership thresholds (0.70/0.30, 0.80/0.20, 0.85/0.15, 1.00/0.00) and lowered the consistency threshold from 0.80 to 0.75 left the core elements and the interpretation qualitatively unchanged.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends transaction-design and business-model-innovation research by showing that novelty is neither sufficient nor universally necessary for high performance; rather, it works through interdependent combinations with other value drivers, strategy, and environmental contingencies. By taking a neo-configurational lens, the authors uncover equifinality across novelty-centric, novelty-enhanced, novelty-neutral, novelty-averse, and novelty-phobic configurations, and demonstrate that novelty and efficiency can act as either complements or substitutes depending on strategy and competition.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should avoid a "novelty trap": novel business model design pays off only when it is connected to clear value-capture mechanisms (efficiency, lock-in, or complementarity) and to a coherent differentiation or low-cost strategy in competitive industries. As environments mature, firms should re-evaluate their business model design choices and ensure that any novel configuration remains worth more than the sum of its parts.

**Limitations**
Researcher choices about which conditions to include constrain fsQCA model complexity, potentially omitting relevant firm- and industry-level controls. The method's inferential power and out-of-sample generalizability are still debated, and the sampling strategy may introduce survival bias. The exclusive focus on Internet-enabled IT-sector firms may limit how the findings travel to less innovation-friendly or more capital-intensive industries.

**Future Research**
Future work could deploy content analysis or NLP on IPO prospectuses and annual reports to scale up business model coding and enable longitudinal designs across industries. Researchers could also examine how business model configurations evolve via vicarious learning or institutional pressure, and whether Red Queen dynamics or causal ambiguity explain why firms converge externally yet diverge in performance.
