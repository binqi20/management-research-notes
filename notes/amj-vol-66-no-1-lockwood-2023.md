# Polishing the Gilt Edge: Elite Category Endurance and Symbolic Boundaries in U.S. Luxury Hotels, 1790–2015

**Abstract**
We conducted an inductive study of discourse associated with the U.S. luxury hotel category, from its beginnings in 1790 through 2015, to explain how cultural processes contribute to elite category endurance. Analyzing data from historical archives, interviews, and observations, we use both qualitative and quantitative methods to reveal how the endurance of an elite category is an ongoing cultural accomplishment that occurs via periodic redefinition of its "symbolic boundary"—that is, the set of meanings and practices that distinguish the elite from nonelite. Modeling the boundary in terms of semiotic codes of opposition, we find that the encoding of the symbolic boundary serves as a temporary settlement that conveys both the category's elite exclusivity and its cultural consonance with broader societal beliefs, tastes, and practices. Over time, however, with industry and societal changes, tensions erupt in the balance between the needs for exclusivity and consonance, propelling a redefinition of what it means to be elite. Our research extends current theorizations of elite category endurance by revealing how the elite symbolic boundary is neither permanent nor unassailable; rather, we show how it changes to enable long-term elite category endurance.

**Research Question**
How does an elite market category endure over the very long term in the face of recurrent industry- and society-driven threats to its status? Specifically, how is the symbolic boundary that distinguishes the elite from the nonelite discursively constructed, settled, disrupted, and redefined across historical periods?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: elite exclusivity vs. cultural consonance; symbolic boundary as a temporary "settlement" encoded in a semiotic code of oppositions (connotations and denotations) between luxury and nonluxury.
- Process / phases: three historical periods — Grand Hotel Period (1790s–1920s, luxury = opulence), Chain Hotel Period (1930s–mid-1990s, luxury = cosmopolitanism), Lifestyle Hotel Period (late 1990s–2015, luxury = originality) — each demarcated by symbolic boundary settlement followed by disruption.
- Practices (three discursive mechanisms): social partitioning (drawing the boundary around an idealized elite clientele), oppositional appropriation (selectively absorbing elements from the nonelite category), and temporal translation (reinterpreting the category's own past for the present).
- Triggering conditions: industry shifts (e.g., chain hotels, lifestyle hotels) and societal sociocultural movements (modern progressive, artisanal) that heighten the tension between exclusivity and consonance.
- Emergent outcomes: a new symbolic boundary settlement and a new semiotic code that re-partitions luxury from nonluxury, sustaining elite category endurance.

Across all three periods the three mechanisms interlock — one is "primary" at category emergence (social partitioning), growth (oppositional appropriation), or maturity (temporal translation), while the other two play supporting roles. The process resembles a punctuated equilibrium of long stable settlements separated by brief disruption-and-redefinition phases, with a notable correspondence between the primary settlement mechanism and the source of the next disruption.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive, longitudinal historical case study of one category (luxury) within one industry (the U.S. hotel industry), using a "concurrent triangulation strategy" that combines archival, interview, and observational data. The archival corpus totals approximately 16,000 pages: popular press (New York Times, 1853–2015; 600 pages drawn from 197, 157, and 132 articles returned by three title/text search strategies, after manually skimming the 3,200 articles initially returned); the trade periodical The Hotel Monthly (1898–1962), renamed Hotel and Motel Management (1963–2012) and later Hotel Management (2012–2015), sampled by hand-collecting and hand-scanning April, August, and December issues from selected years between 1898 and 2014 (9,500 pages in total); 16 books by hoteliers or industry experts (published 1848–2015); 11 books by historians, sociologists, and other scholars or industry observers; all available AAA Diamond rating guidelines (1985–present); and category performance data from STR, Inc. (1991–present) and PKF International (1936–present). Firsthand data comprise 25 semi-structured interviews with industry and luxury hotel category experts and executives (averaging 45 minutes, recorded and transcribed), roughly 50 informal informational interviews, site visits to 12 luxury hotels, and about 120 hours of participant observation at the 2013, 2014, and 2015 International Hotel, Motel and Restaurant Show.

The focal construct, the category's symbolic boundary, is operationalized as a semiotic code of oppositions ("Modeling the boundary in terms of semiotic codes of opposition"), with connotative meanings (the cultural sentiments and tastes associated with luxury) in the upper portion of each period's code and denotative practices (their material instantiations in service, décor, location, clientele, and use) in the lower portion. Analysis ran in three stages. (1) Inductive qualitative analysis following grounded theory and case study research, using temporal bracketing and constant comparison across 27 books plus industry-association and research-firm material to build a timeline of the industry's 225-year history and demarcate discrete periods. (2) Semiotic chain analysis following the procedures of Weber et al. (2008: 537–538): all authors independently identified the connotative and denotative aspects of the semiotic code for each period, reconciled differences over several rounds of discussion, then assessed change in the code across periods. (3) Latent Dirichlet allocation (LDA) topic modeling of the full New York Times corpus after machine-readable conversion, punctuation removal, and stemming; multiple runs identified a 24-topic model as the best fit, from which a subset of 13 topics prominent during category formation and inter-period transitions was retained, independently labeled, triangulated against interview, observational, and other archival data, and aggregated into three conceptual dimensions presented as the mechanisms of symbolic boundary settlement. The design is interpretive and historical; no causal identification is claimed.

**Key Findings**
The U.S. luxury hotel category endured across three historical periods, each anchored in a distinct semiotic code encoding the symbolic boundary between luxury and nonluxury, and in each period one of the three discursive mechanisms was primary while the other two interlocked in supporting roles. In the Grand Hotel period (1790s–1920s), luxury was encoded as opulence, balancing the grandeur and entitlement of elite exclusivity against progressivism and public access, with social partitioning primary at category emergence. In the chain hotel period (1930s–mid-1990s), luxury was re-encoded as cosmopolitanism, meaning global excellence realized through disciplined professionalization and corporate growth, balancing standardized efficiency (consonance) against opulent excellence (exclusivity), with oppositional appropriation primary at category growth. In the lifestyle hotel period (late 1990s–2015, ongoing), luxury was encoded as originality, balancing privilege and iconicity against unassuming authenticity and personalization, with temporal translation primary at category maturity.

Each settlement proved temporary, and the balance that made it work also seeded its disruption. The Grand Hotel settlement was undone by the modern progressive movement, which recast displays of opulence as "pretentious" and "showy," together with the rise of standardized, cost-cutting nonluxury chain hotels, leaving luxury hostelries "practically extinct." The chain hotel settlement was undone by nonluxury hotels' rapid replication of luxury amenities and by the artisanal movement's turn toward craftsmanship, authenticity, and localization, so that by the early 2000s "luxury ha[d] become a negative" and the category recorded "the greatest decline [ever] experienced" in daily rates and occupancy. The authors report a notable correspondence between the primary mechanism used to settle a boundary and the principal source of the threat that ensues, which makes disruption more predictable than prior research allows. The category nonetheless retained its elite economics throughout: about 6% of industry room supply but about 15% of industry revenue in the late 1800s/early 1900s, about 4% of supply and about 8.3% of revenue by the 1990s, and about 4% of supply with over 12% of revenue and price premiums about 125% above the industry average by 2015. Extrapolating from these findings, the authors advance a general model in which elite category endurance is "neither a fait accompli nor an inertial tendency" but an ongoing accomplishment achieved through repeated discursive encoding of the symbolic boundary, following a patterned periodicity of long settlements punctuated by phases of rapid disruption and redefinition.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper recasts elite category endurance as an active, ongoing cultural accomplishment rather than an inertial property of status hierarchies, showing how symbolic boundaries adapt by holding elite exclusivity and cultural consonance in productive tension. It identifies three novel, interlocking discursive mechanisms — social partitioning, oppositional appropriation, and temporal translation — and exposes a periodicity in which each mechanism takes the lead at a different phase of the category's evolution. It also bridges debates between impermeable and porous category boundaries by showing that elite symbolic boundaries oscillate between sharp definition in stable times and greater fluidity under threat.

**Practical Implication**
Managers of elite categories and luxury brands should monitor shifts in broader societal tastes and values, not just competitive moves, because cultural consonance is as crucial to enduring eliteness as exclusivity. Sustaining elite status calls for organizational ambidexterity: preserving continuity with the category's storied past while selectively absorbing nonelite practices and reinterpreting tradition to align with contemporary values.

**Limitations**
The study is based on a single category (luxury) within a single industry (U.S. hotels), which constrains generalizability across sectors and national settings. The analysis takes a broad-brush, category-level approach to public discourse and does not deeply track individual organizations, specific actors, or membership-level boundary dynamics.

**Future Research**
The authors call for replication of the symbolic boundary settlement–disruption framework in other cultural-industry contexts (restaurants, retail, watches, wine, haute couture, "masstige" brands) and in non-cultural or credence-good settings. They also encourage cross-national comparisons of how class structures shape boundary dynamics and finer-grained tracking of which actors and organizations come to influence elite category discourse over time.
