# Balancing “Protective Disguise” with “Harmonious Advocacy”: Social Venture Legitimation in Authoritarian Contexts

**Abstract**
This paper seeks to advance understanding of how new social ventures can gain legitimacy in authoritarian contexts. Through a study of a new disability rights organization in post-revolutionary Egypt, we theorize how authoritarianism poses distinct challenges for social ventures that require different legitimation strategies than those commonly reported in the literature. Specifically, we use our case study to build a theoretical model that suggests social ventures need to achieve “optimal assimilation” by balancing “protective disguise” with “harmonious advocacy.” By explicitly theorizing social venture legitimation in authoritarian contexts, we advance the budding literature on social venture legitimation that has so far predominantly considered legitimation in more democratic contexts. Moreover, our study shows that organizational legitimacy may need to be conceptualized differently when examining social ventures—and indeed other forms of organization—in authoritarian regimes.

**Research Question**
"How do new social ventures gain legitimacy in authoritarian contexts?"

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: protective disguise vs. harmonious advocacy; political vs. cultural manifestations of authoritarianism; "fitting in" vs. advocating for beneficiaries
- Legitimation challenges: (a) political manifestation — social activism perceived as threat to national interests; (b) cultural manifestation — entrenched beliefs and practices that devalue the human rights of marginalized groups
- Protective disguise practices: (1) cloaking social mission under rhetorical alignment with regime goals (economic prosperity, national stability, tourism); (2) camouflaging social action through an innocuous organizational design (positioning as a commercial accessibility consultancy rather than a human-rights NGO)
- Harmonious advocacy practices: (3) experiential construction of social problems (community days, blindfolded simulations, ambassador stories that build empathy); (4) reactive campaigning for social mission (leveraging conducive events such as the 2017 disability law and the "Year of People with Disabilities," while explicitly shielding government from criticism)
- Emergent outcomes: venture perceived as nonthreatening, then as worthy of support; growing audience backing
- Boundary conditions: continuous balancing required; ventures may need to pivot back toward disguise during crackdowns and forward toward advocacy during conducive periods

The process Amal followed shows that legitimation in authoritarian contexts is underpinned by a continuous balancing of disguise and advocacy that the authors label "optimal assimilation." Disguise is necessary because confrontation triggers regime aggression; advocacy is necessary because pure disguise prevents the venture from acting on its mission. Disguise lays the platform that makes later, carefully calibrated advocacy possible, and the two strategies are continuously rebalanced in response to political-cultural dynamics.

**Data & Measures**
- Design: a single inductive, interpretive case study of one social venture ("Amal," a pseudonym), treated as an "extreme case" (Siggelkow, 2007); the analysis considers Amal's strategies from 2014 onwards, when it registered as an NGO. No causal identification strategy is claimed — the authors write that strategies "seemingly" built legitimacy and that audience support "appeared" to grow, so the design is interpretive and theory-building rather than causal.
- Interview corpus: 45 semi-structured interviews with actors in Egypt, conducted in four rounds and lasting between 20 minutes and 2 hours. Table 2 breaks these into 8 interviews with Amal representatives, 13 with experts (4 disability experts, 9 social enterprise experts), 14 with key audiences (4 corporate employers, 4 former government officials, 6 beneficiaries), and 10 with peers (4 other social enterprises, 6 other NGOs working on disability inclusion in Egypt). The Data Collection narrative describes the expert group as six interviews; Table 2's breakdown is the one that reconciles to the stated total of 45.
- Archival corpus: 93 articles on Amal in national and international online media published between 2012 and 2020; 12 recorded interviews and speeches (4 radio interviews and 8 public talks given by officials between 2014 and 2020); 3 academic studies and books on social entrepreneurship in Egypt and the MENA region; plus marketing material produced by Amal.
- Observational data: two 14-day field visits to Egypt by the first author in February and April 2018, yielding approximately 12 hours of observations across visits to Amal's premises and to offices and factories of corporate employers in Cairo and Giza; recorded in a field diary shared with the other authors containing descriptive information and impressions of interactions between nondisabled people and people with disabilities.
- Coding and analysis: five sequential (though iterative) steps following Langley's (1999) recommendations for process research — (1) build understanding of the focal context and compile a timeline of key events (Table 1), enabling inductive "process-tracing" (George & Bennett, 2004) and identification of the three key audience groups (government actors, private sector companies, nondisabled employees); (2) develop grounded theoretical constructs, separating political from cultural manifestations of authoritarianism; (3) open coding following Gioia, Corley, and Hamilton (2013), clustering first-order categories into second-order themes and then into the aggregate dimensions "protective disguise" and "harmonious advocacy" (supporting excerpts in Table 3); (4) relate the two strategies to key environmental events over the study period; (5) assemble the theoretical model of "optimal assimilation" (Figure 1).
- Trustworthiness and ethics: one of Amal's founders was asked for feedback on the key ideas and concepts from the analysis and his thoughts were incorporated; pseudonyms were used for the organization and its members, and for politically sensitive material the authors relied predominantly on quotes from informants close to but not part of the venture, plus secondary data on civil society in post-revolutionary Egypt.

**Key Findings**
- Political legitimation challenge: social activism was viewed as a potential threat to national interests and could be met with political aggression; rights-based activism was increasingly seen as threatening national stability, so the state engaged in close monitoring and control of social ventures.
- Protective disguise produced the outcome "venture perceived as nonthreatening." Cloaking the social mission under rhetorical alignment and camouflaging social action through an innocuous organizational design allowed Amal to gain recognition as a service provider rather than a human rights NGO; government actors came to describe it as fulfilling an important function in the Egyptian economy, it avoided the scrutiny human rights organizations experienced, and it created a revenue stream that provided a resource platform for growth.
- Cultural legitimation challenge: entrenched beliefs and practices devalued the human rights of marginalized groups — the authors' data indicate that people in Egypt often "pitied" disabled people because they were perceived as "weak" and "needy."
- Harmonious advocacy produced the outcome "venture's mission perceived as worthy of support." Experiential construction of social problems (community days, simulations, ambassador stories) shifted the emotional register from pity to empathy, with participants reporting feeling "inspired" and "touched"; corporate partners publicly endorsed Amal, and government representatives expressed explicit support, including a minister crediting the venture with "changing mind-sets." By the end of the observation period Amal had established over 1,000 corporate partnerships and been contracted to conduct over 5,000 corporate training sessions, and by the end of 2020 it had conducted over 1,000 accessibility audits across the country, many commissioned with and executed alongside local government partners.
- Temporal pattern: protective disguise was more prominent early on, and as the venture came to be perceived as nonthreatening it became progressively safer to advocate openly and to scale back some of the disguise — disguise forms the platform from which advocacy proceeds. Advocacy became more overt around two conducive events: the 2017 parliamentary debate and passage of the disability law, and the President's 2018 launch of the "year of people with disabilities."
- Overarching emergent model: "optimal assimilation" — legitimation in authoritarian contexts is underpinned by a continuous balancing of protective disguise and harmonious advocacy rather than a process unfolding through discrete phases, with strategies reversed and scaled back when legitimation appears unsuccessful or the context evolves toward stronger authoritarianism. Instead of striving to be "as different as legitimately possible" (Deephouse, 1999), such organizations need to strive to be as unobtrusive as strategically possible, and rather than moving from "illegitimate" to "accepted" they typically occupy a liminal zone between being evaluated as "debated" and "proper."
- The authors qualify this pattern: the progression from a relative emphasis on disguise to a relative emphasis on advocacy may be less linear than observed in their single case, and they describe that aspect of their theorizing as tentative.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper builds a process model of social-venture legitimation in authoritarian contexts that identifies "protective disguise" and "harmonious advocacy" as the two overarching strategies and "optimal assimilation" as the governing principle — a counterpart to "optimal distinctiveness" in democratic contexts. It addresses the democratic-society bias in organizational-legitimacy research by showing that core assumptions (e.g., the evaluative benefits of "standing out," the four-state progression toward "accepted") do not transfer to authoritarian regimes, where ventures occupy a liminal zone between "debated" and "proper" and political elites uniquely bestow or withdraw legitimacy.

**Practical Implication**
Social ventures operating in authoritarian regimes should resist transplanting democratic-context playbooks: confrontational framing, hero/villain rhetoric, and overt rights-based campaigning can endanger founders and trigger forced closure. Founders should instead cloak their mission in language that aligns with regime priorities, adopt an innocuous organizational form (e.g., a service-providing consultancy), and use empathy-building experiential activities plus reactive campaigning tied to government-initiated events to advance their cause without attracting unwanted political attention.

**Limitations**
The study rests on a single case in one authoritarian context (post-revolutionary Egypt), focused on a relatively less politically contentious issue (disability inclusion); the findings may differ for ventures tackling more sensitive issues (e.g., free speech, LGBTQ rights) or operating in other authoritarian or hybrid regimes (China, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Albania, Nigeria). The authors also flag that whether "optimal assimilation" is the morally best path is a normative question they cannot answer.

**Future Research**
Comparative work across authoritarian and hybrid regimes is needed to test whether the disguise-advocacy balance generalizes. Future studies should examine more politically and culturally contentious causes, explore how a commercial vs. social orientation shapes legitimation under different regimes, and investigate the state's role as a uniquely powerful audience whose interdependency with other audiences shapes legitimacy judgments — even in democracies.
