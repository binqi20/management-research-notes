# Toward a Configural Theory of Job Demands and Resources

**Abstract**
Job demands–resources research has largely adopted a variable-centered approach to test main and interactive effects of demands and resources on employee outcomes. However, this approach cannot detect distinct configurations of job demands and resources that may lead to the same outcomes, and the many multiplicative models attempting to do so have received little support. We take a person-centered theoretical approach to examine configurations of demands and resources that are sufficient to produce exhaustion, engagement, and other strain outcomes. Using fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis across three studies, we find three equifinal configurations sufficient to produce exhaustion: (a) high workload and emotional demands, (b) low social support, and (c) high workload and low autonomy. We also find evidence for causal asymmetry in both exhaustion and engagement. Our person-centered approach yields more theoretically consistent results compared to a variable-centered approach. The findings highlight that certain job demands may only be buffered by specific resources, and that certain configurations of demands cannot be buffered at all.

**Research Question**
Which configurations of job demands and resources are sufficient to produce the presence and absence of exhaustion and engagement (and other strain outcomes), and do these configurations exhibit equifinality and causal asymmetry that variable-centered models cannot detect?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. The configuration of high workload and low autonomy is sufficient for employees to experience exhaustion.
Hypothesis 2. The configuration of high workload and low social support is sufficient for employees to experience exhaustion.
Hypothesis 3. There are more configurations sufficient for the presence of exhaustion than configurations sufficient for its absence.
Research Question 1. Are there configurations sufficient for the presence of exhaustion that include only high levels of job demands?
Research Question 2. Which configurations of demands and resources are sufficient for engagement and for its absence?
Research Question 3. Controlling for individual differences, how strongly is configuration membership related to exhaustion and engagement?
(The paper's five numbered Propositions 1–5 are derived inductively in the General Discussion from the results, not tested as a priori predictions.)

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Workload, emotional demands (job demands); autonomy, social support, feedback (job resources)
- DV(s): Exhaustion, engagement, turnover intentions, physical symptoms
- Mediators: Not modeled; strain and motivational processes are the theoretical mediating mechanisms posited by JDR theory
- Moderators: The configural approach replaces traditional moderation logic — configurations of conditions (not interactions) jointly produce outcomes through equifinality and causal asymmetry

Drawing on the demand–control–support extension of JDR theory and the "bad-is-stronger-than-good" cognition literature, the authors theorize that several qualitatively different configurations of high demands and low resources can be equally sufficient to produce exhaustion (equifinality), and that the configurations producing an outcome are not the inverse of those producing its absence (causal asymmetry). They predict that high-workload/low-autonomy and high-workload/low-social-support combinations are each sufficient for exhaustion (H1, H2) and that there are more pathways to negative than positive outcomes (H3). Whether any configuration comprising only high demands — one that no resource would buffer — is sufficient for exhaustion is deliberately left unhypothesized: citing "the lack of specific theory," the authors posit it as Research Question 1.

**Data & Measures**
Three independent employee samples, each analyzed with fuzzy set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) and, in Studies 2 and 3, supplementary OLS regression. The design is observational throughout: fsQCA establishes set-theoretic *sufficiency* of configurations for outcomes, and the authors make no claim of causal identification (they list the open causal ordering of JDRs and outcomes as a limitation).

Study 1: 222 full-time employees "from various occupations in the United States" recruited from Amazon's Mechanical Turk; after removing 16 who did not respond on any demands or resources and 7 who failed an attention check, the final sample was 199 (Mage = 35.41, 76.9% White, 39.7% female). Cross-sectional, single-source.

Study 2: 124 participants recruited by snowball sampling from an MBA program alumni group and a major technology company in the United States during the COVID-19 pandemic (Mage = 38.42, 70.2% White, 53.7% female); participants chose $5 compensation or a $5 charity donation. Cross-sectional, single-source.

Study 3: preregistered two-wave study on Prolific Academic; 600 recruited for wave 1, of whom 412 completed the second survey two weeks later (Mage = 36.35, 76.2% White, 41.7% female). Outcomes were measured two weeks after demands and resources to address common method variance.

Measures (identical scales across studies unless noted): workload = 8-item Janssen (2001) scale and emotional demands = 6-item Bakker, Demerouti, and Schaufeli (2003) scale, both rated 1 = never to 5 = always; autonomy = 4 items selected from Morgeson and Humphrey's (2006) 9-item scale, social support = their 6-item scale, feedback = their 3-item scale, all rated 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree; exhaustion = 8-item exhaustion subscale of the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory; engagement (Studies 2–3) = 9-item Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (1 = never to 7 = always); turnover intentions (Study 3 only) = 4-item Kelloway, Gottlieb, and Barham (1999) scale; physical symptoms (Study 3 only) = four items from Bono et al. (2013). Alphas ranged from .83 to .90 in Study 1 and are reported in Tables 1 and 4.

Calibration and analysis: conditions were calibrated by the direct method using external benchmarks rather than sample statistics — a search of eight premier management journals over the past 10 years yielded 27 articles comprising 9,562 participants, and thresholds were set at one SD above the weighted mean ("fully in"), one SD below ("fully out"), and at the mean (crossover); Study 3's additional outcomes were calibrated from a further 4,833 published participants. Truth tables were built with the fs/QCA 2.0 truth table algorithm over the 32 combinations of five conditions, using a minimum raw consistency of .80 and PRI consistency of .70, with frequency thresholds of 5 cases in Study 1 (retaining 82% of cases), 3 in Study 2 (83%), and 11 in Study 3 (81%). Studies 2 and 3 then regressed exhaustion and engagement on configuration membership scores dichotomized at the 0.5 crossover, controlling for age, gender, ethnicity (Black and White dummies), extraversion, and neuroticism (4-item Donnellan et al., 2006 scales).

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 199): two configurations were sufficient for the presence of exhaustion — high workload with emotional demands (Configuration 1) and high workload with low autonomy and low social support (Configuration 2); combined coverage .69, solution consistency .86 — and one configuration was sufficient for its absence (Configuration 3; coverage .36, consistency .91). Neither hypothesized configuration appeared in isolation; because Configuration 2 combined both, the authors read this as **partial support for H1 and H2**. **H3 was supported** (two configurations for the presence of exhaustion versus one for its absence). RQ1 was answered affirmatively: high workload plus emotional demands was sufficient for exhaustion regardless of resources. Supplemental moderated OLS found only one significant interaction (emotional demands × social support, B = .12, SE = .05, p = .035), and its form ran *contrary* to the buffering hypothesis — the emotional demands–exhaustion relationship was more positive when social support was high (B = .18, p = .003) than low (B = .02, p = .768).

Study 2 (n = 124): three configurations were sufficient for exhaustion (solution coverage .75, consistency .90) and **none** for its absence. **H1 was supported** (high workload–low autonomy, Configuration 3). **H2 was not supported**: the predicted high workload–low social support configuration was not found; instead a more general configuration comprising only low social support (Configuration 1) was sufficient, implying that the absence of social support can itself function as a demand. **H3 was supported** (three configurations versus none). RQ1 replicated (high workload with emotional demands, Configuration 2). RQ2: one configuration was sufficient for the presence of engagement (high workload with autonomy, social support, and feedback; coverage .43, consistency .92) and one for its absence (low autonomy, social support, and feedback; coverage .40, consistency .90). RQ3: with controls, membership in the low-social-support configuration (B = .39, SE = .09, p < .001) and the high workload–emotional demands configuration (B = .25, SE = .11, p = .024) was positively related to exhaustion, while the high workload–low autonomy configuration was not significant (B = .13, SE = .12, p = .279); the engagement configuration was positively (B = .71, SE = .20, p < .001) and the lack-of-engagement configuration negatively (B = −.55, SE = .25, p = .028) related to engagement. A significant emotional demands × autonomy interaction on exhaustion (B = .18, SE = .09, p = .041) again ran contrary to buffering (emotional demands slope more positive when autonomy was high, B = .24, p = .004, than low, B = −.03, p = .723); no interaction significantly predicted engagement.

Study 3 (n = 412, time-lagged, preregistered): three configurations were sufficient for exhaustion (coverage .82, consistency .82) and one for its absence (coverage .44, consistency .87). **H1 was supported** (high workload–low autonomy, Configuration 3); **H2 was again not supported**, the low-social-support-only configuration (Configuration 1) appearing in its place; **H3 was supported** (three configurations versus one). RQ1 replicated (Configuration 2, high workload with emotional demands). RQ2: one configuration was sufficient for engagement (high workload with autonomy, social support, and feedback; coverage .39, consistency .86) and one for its absence (low social support and feedback; coverage .54, consistency .86), the latter more general than Study 2's. RQ3: membership in low social support (B = .30, SE = .05, p < .001), high workload with emotional demands (B = .25, SE = .05, p < .001), and high workload–low autonomy (B = .15, SE = .06, p = .011) was positively related to exhaustion; the engagement configuration was positively (B = .67, SE = .14, p < .001) and the lack-of-engagement configuration negatively (B = −.67, SE = .12, p < .001) related to engagement. For the added strain outcomes, no configuration was sufficient for the *presence* of turnover intentions while two were sufficient for its absence (coverage .46, consistency .81), and one configuration (high workload and emotional demands with low autonomy, social support, and feedback) was sufficient for physical symptoms (coverage .21, consistency .84), with none for their absence. Supplemental OLS: workload × social support predicted exhaustion (B = −.10, SE = .05, p = .047) in a buffering-consistent form (workload slope more positive when social support was low, B = .30, than high, B = .15), but workload × feedback (B = .14, SE = .05, p = .002) ran contrary to buffering; workload × social support on engagement (B = .32, SE = .12, p = .009) was consistent with the coping hypothesis but took the opposite form of the same interaction in Study 2.

Across the three studies the support pattern is heterogeneous: H1 received partial support in Study 1 and support in Studies 2 and 3; H2 received partial support in Study 1 and was not supported in Studies 2 or 3; H3 was supported in all three. The authors conclude that three configurations — low social support, high workload with emotional demands, and high workload with low autonomy — are sufficient for the presence of exhaustion, while no configuration is *consistently* sufficient for its absence (Study 2 found none; Studies 1 and 3 each found one), and that only one configuration, high workload combined with high autonomy, social support, and feedback, is sufficient for engagement. Evidence for multiplicative JDR models was weak throughout, ranging from zero to two significant interactions out of 10 per study, with interaction forms largely inconsistent with both the buffering and coping hypotheses.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper reinterprets the JDR model in configural terms, replacing the additive/multiplicative variable-centered logic with set-theoretic propositions about equifinal and causally asymmetric configurations of demands and resources. It shows that certain demands (high workload combined with emotional demands) cannot be buffered by any resource, that the absence of social support can itself function as a demand, and that there are more configurations sufficient for exhaustion than for engagement, instantiating a "bad-is-stronger-than-good" pattern within JDR theory.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should diagnose employees' overall configurations of job demands and resources rather than intervening on any single demand or resource in isolation: simply lowering workload will not help an employee whose exhaustion comes from low social support, and adding social support will not help when high workload combines with high emotional demands. Because exhaustion has multiple sufficient pathways but its absence has none, prevention is more tractable than cure.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 measured demands, resources, and outcomes at the same time from the same source, raising common-method concerns (addressed in Study 3 with time-lagged measurement). The observational design leaves the causal ordering of JDRs and outcomes open, and samples were drawn primarily from the United States, which may limit cross-cultural generalizability. The set of demands and resources examined was finite and did not include nonwork antecedents of strain.

**Future Research**
The authors call for longitudinal designs that test lagged effects between JDRs and outcomes, replications in non-U.S. and especially collectivistic cultural contexts to test whether social support and autonomy play different buffering roles, and extensions to additional outcomes such as performance. They also suggest examining antecedents of configuration membership (e.g., personality traits, organizational and national institutions) and identifying configuration-specific mediating mechanisms.
