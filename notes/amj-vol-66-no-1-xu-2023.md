# The Impact of Communist Ideology on the Patenting Activity of Chinese Firms

**Abstract**
The Chinese Communist Party began decoupling its policies and practices from Maoist communist ideology more than four decades ago. Yet, why does Maoism continue to have an impact on the behavior of Party members? In this study, we argue that, although the influence of Maoist ideology has become weaker among younger Party members and Party members with higher educational attainment, such ideological decay is countered by a process of secondhand ideological imprinting. Based on data from 1,298 nonstate-owned Chinese listed firms for 2000–2017, we find that firms with Party-member board chairs file fewer patent applications and are more likely to commit patent infringement. These effects are weaker if a board chair is younger or has higher educational attainment. Importantly, the moderating effect of young age is reduced as the presence of older Party-member corporate directors in a region becomes more prominent. However, the moderating effect of education appears to be unaffected by the presence of older Party-member directors. These findings generate fresh insights on the dynamics of ideological decay and persistence.

**Research Question**
Why does Maoist communist ideology continue to affect the patenting behavior of Chinese Communist Party members—including younger members who never directly experienced the Maoist era—and through what specific imprinting mechanism does this ideological influence persist amid decades of decoupling between Party policy and Maoist doctrine?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. In Chinese non-SOEs, a board chair's Communist Party membership has (a) a negative effect on the number of patent applications submitted and (b) a positive effect on the likelihood of patent infringement.
- Hypothesis 2. The effects of a board chair's Communist Party membership on (a) patent applications and (b) patent infringement are weaker if a board chair is of a younger age.
- Hypothesis 3. The effects of a board chair's Communist Party membership on (a) patent applications and (b) patent infringement are weaker if a board chair has higher educational attainment.
- Hypothesis 4. The moderating effects of a board chair's young age on the relationships between Party membership and (a) patent applications and (b) patent infringement proposed in Hypothesis 2 are reduced when the presence of older Party-member directors in a region is more prominent.
- Hypothesis 5. The moderating effects of a board chair's educational attainment on the relationships between Party membership and (a) patent applications and (b) patent infringement proposed in Hypothesis 3 are reduced when the presence of older Party-member directors in a region is more prominent.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Board chair's Communist Party (CCP) membership (binary)
- DV(s): Number of patent applications (count); likelihood of patent infringement (binary)
- Moderators: Board chair's young age (100 − actual age); educational attainment (5-level categorical); presence of older Party-member directors in the region (% of older Party-member directors among all Party-member directors in the province)
- Controls: Founder chair, government/SOE experience, overseas experience, chair ownership, R&D intensity, state shares, firm size, firm age, ROA, industry competition (1 − HHI), city-level GDP growth, Internet development, provincial legal development, year and industry dummies

Drawing on imprinting theory, the authors argue that older Party members carry firsthand Maoist imprints from the politically sensitive 1960s–1970s, while younger Party members—who joined under Dengism—acquire Maoist orientations only indirectly, through "secondhand imprinting" by older Party-member peers in their elite social and role category. Maoist beliefs (anti-private-property, anti-IPR, devaluation of scientific work and intellectuals) translate into lower patent application volumes and higher patent infringement propensity. Higher education and younger age weaken this effect (ideological decay), but a high regional presence of older Party-member directors reactivates Maoist influence among younger chairs (offsetting age-based decay), though it does not offset education-based decay.

**Data & Measures**
- Single-study panel design. Sample frame: non-state-owned (ultimate controlling owner is not the government or its agencies) manufacturing firms listed on the Shanghai and Shenzhen Stock Exchanges from 2000 through 2017. 2000 is the start year because patent infringement information became available only after the Patent Law was revised in 2000. All explanatory variables are lagged one year, so explanatory-variable information runs 2000–2016 and dependent-variable information runs 2001–2017, giving 9,262 firm-year observations from 1,543 listed firms before matching.
- Data sources: Party membership of board chairs, CEOs, and other board members hand-collected from listed firms' annual reports; personal demographics of chairs and CEOs (age, education, ownership, government background, overseas experience) plus firm- and industry-level variables from CSMAR (China Stock Market and Accounting Research); patent data from the State Intellectual Property Office of China (SIPO); patent infringement litigation from the China Law Info database (www.lawinfochina.com); provincial legal development from the National Economic Research Institute (NERI) marketization index; regional control variables from China Statistical Yearbooks.
- DV 1 — patent applications: the total number of the three SIPO patent types (invention, utility, and design) applied for, treated as an overdispersed count.
- DV 2 — patent infringement: a dummy coded 1 if the firm experienced a patent infringement lawsuit as the defendant in a given year that resulted in conviction. Defendant names were matched to listed-company names, and the lawsuit filing date (not the trial date) was used to assign the infringement year.
- IV — Party membership: a binary variable indicating whether a firm's board chair is a CCP member (1 = yes, 0 = no), following prior Chinese-context studies that treat party affiliation as the key identifier of ideological orientation.
- Moderator 1 — young age: the board chair's age reverse-coded as 100 minus actual age, so a larger value indicates a younger chair.
- Moderator 2 — educational attainment: a categorical variable (1 = high school, 2 = community college, 3 = bachelor's degree, 4 = master's degree, 5 = doctoral degree).
- Moderator 3 — older Party-member directors: the percentage of older Party-member corporate directors out of the total number of Party-member directors of all listed firms in the province where the focal firm is headquartered, with "older" defined as Party members who reached age 18 before 1978 (born before 1960).
- Controls: chair level — founder chair, government/SOE experience, overseas experience, chair ownership (share of outstanding shares held by the chair); firm level — R&D intensity (R&D expenditures / total sales), state shares, firm size (log of total employees), firm age (years since IPO), ROA; industry level — competition measured as 1 − HHI; regional level — city GDP growth, Internet development (share of Internet users in the city population), and provincial legal development. Year and industry dummies are included, with industry grouping based on the three-digit classification by the China Securities Regulatory Commission.
- Estimation: a negative binomial model (chosen because patent applications is a count variable with significant overdispersion) for patent applications, and a logit model with standard errors clustered by firm for patent infringement. Random-effects rather than fixed-effects estimation is used because board chairs' Party membership is time invariant.
- Identification: the paper treats joining the Communist Party as a board chair's personal choice and therefore endogenous, and addresses this with propensity score matching — a first-stage logit on all controls (area under the ROC curve = 0.74), matched with a caliper of 0.25 standard deviation, yielding a final matched sample of 1,298 firms and 5,731 firm-year observations (1,922 treatment, 3,809 control), 61.88% of the original observations and 84.12% of the original firms, with 1.2% post-matching bias and a post-matching pseudo-R² of .004. This is matching on observable covariates rather than an exogenous shock, so the design remains observational; robustness checks add coarsened exact matching, entropy balancing, full-sample estimation without PSM, an alternative ideology measure (share of Party-member directors on the board), invention patents and R&D intensity as alternative dependent variables, a city-level rather than province-level measure of older Party-member directors, and an impact-threshold-of-a-confounding-variable analysis for omitted-variable bias.

**Key Findings**
- H1a and H1b supported. In Model 2 of Tables 2 and 3, the coefficient of Party membership is negative for patent applications (b = −0.22, p < .001) and positive for patent infringement (b = 1.65, p < .001). Effect sizes: having a Party-member chair is associated with a drop in patent applications from 32.84 to 13.55 and a rise in the patent infringement rate from 0.8% to 3.9%. True marginal effects at the variable means are −0.15 (p < .001) for patent applications and 0.01 (p < .001) for patent infringement, which the paper reads as strong support for H1a and H1b.
- H2a and H2b supported ("fully support"). The Party membership × young age interaction is positive for patent applications (b = 0.01, p < .05) and negative for patent infringement (b = −0.07, p < .05) in Model 7 — that is, the Maoist penalty weakens for younger chairs. Simple slopes: for older chairs, Party membership is associated with fewer applications (b = −0.29, p < .001) and more infringement (b = 2.09, p < .001); for younger chairs the same relationships hold but are weaker (b = −0.12, p < .05 for applications; b = 0.91, p < .05 for infringement).
- H3a and H3b supported. The Party membership × educational attainment interaction is positive for patent applications (b = 0.11, p < .01) and negative for patent infringement (b = −1.16, p < .001). Simple slopes: at lower education, Party membership is negatively associated with applications (b = −0.32, p < .001) and positively with infringement (b = 2.67, p < .001); at higher education both relationships become non-significant (b = −0.09, p > .05 for applications; b = 0.33, p > .10 for infringement).
- H4a and H4b supported. The three-way interaction among Party membership, young age, and older Party-member directors is negative for patent applications (b = −0.07, p < .05) and positive for patent infringement (b = 0.63, p < .05) — the age-based decay is offset where older Party-member directors are more prominent. Being a younger chair has no impact on patent applications where the presence of older Party-member directors is low (b = −0.00, p > .10) but becomes negative where it is high (b = −0.23, p < .01); for infringement, no impact where the presence is low (b = 0.22, p > .10) but a positive impact where it is high (b = 1.61, p < .01).
- H5a and H5b not supported. The parallel three-way interaction among Party membership, educational attainment, and older Party-member directors is non-significant for both patent applications (b = −0.09, p > .10) and patent infringement (b = 1.91, p > .10); the paper states that "Hypotheses 5a and 5b received no support," so education-based ideological decay is not offset by the regional presence of older Party-member directors.
- Two additional (non-hypothesized) analyses reported in Appendix A, Table A4: board chairs' Party membership relates positively to firm diversification, measured as the number of three-digit industries in which a firm operates (Model 2: b = 0.07, p < .05), and Party-member chairs are more likely to appoint Party-member CEOs (Model 4: b = 5.58, p < .001), which the authors read as evidence that one channel through which chair ideology takes effect is influencing CEO selection.
- Robustness: in a split-sample test of H2, Party membership has significant effects in both the older-chair sample (b = −0.21, p < .001 for applications; b = 2.62, p < .001 for infringement) and the younger-chair sample (b = −0.18, p < .01 for applications; b = 0.95, p < .05 for infringement), with a partition test confirming the effects are stronger for older chairs (p < .05 for applications, p < .01 for infringement).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends imprinting theory by empirically demonstrating secondhand imprinting in a setting where the original imprinting environment has disappeared—showing that older Party-member directors in the same regional elite category pass Maoist ideological imprints onto younger Party-member board chairs who never experienced the Maoist era directly. It contributes to the political ideology literature by modeling the interplay between ideological decay (via age, education) and ideological persistence (via secondhand imprinting) within a single integrated framework, departing from the Western liberal/conservative dichotomy that dominates extant work.

**Practical Implication**
When assessing Chinese non-SOEs as innovation partners or rivals, observers should weigh board chairs' ideological background (CCP membership) alongside their age, education, and the demographic composition of older Party-member directors in their regional social network—because firms led by Party-member chairs file fewer patents, more often violate other firms' patent rights, and may instead diversify into multiple industries as an alternative growth strategy. Education in particular appears to robustly counteract Maoist ideological influence on innovation.

**Limitations**
The study isolates Maoist ideological elements, but these are intermixed with broader Chinese cultural features (e.g., Confucian skepticism of individualism and IPR; historically modest cultural emphasis on science and technology) that also bear on innovation activity. The authors caution against overgeneralizing the ideology effect to all corporate decisions, noting that when evidence is unambiguous Party members often act pragmatically (e.g., founding private firms after SOE collapse).

**Future Research**
Cross-national comparisons of how different versions of communist ideology shape firm behavior in China, Cuba, Vietnam, and former Soviet Bloc countries would test the generalizability of the secondhand-imprinting mechanism. Future work could also examine how a board chair's home province, prior SOE/planned-economy experience, or other contextual moderators interact with political ideology to shape innovation orientations.
