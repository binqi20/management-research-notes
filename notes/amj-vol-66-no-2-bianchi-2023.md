# Does Job Satisfaction Rise and Fall with the Economy? Cross-Sectional, Longitudinal, and Experimental Evidence That Job Satisfaction Increases during Recessions

**Abstract**
While recessions have many adverse consequences for individuals and organizations, we propose that they have positive implications for job satisfaction. We argue that during bad economic times, people will be less attuned to other possible jobs, and more likely to see their own jobs favorably. We find support for these predictions across three studies. Study 1 (n 5 23,335) utilizes a large cross-sectional survey of American adults collected over four decades and finds that job satisfaction increases during recessions and declines during booms. Study 2 (n 5 12,859) replicates this result using a large longitudinal survey of British adults and finds that job satisfaction rises and falls with the unemployment rate even within the same people. Finally, Study 3 (n 5 512) uses an experimental design and finds that the relationship between economic conditions and job satisfaction is mediated by the reduced salience of alternative jobs. While scholars have long recognized that job satisfaction is affected by situational features inside organizations, our findings suggest that conditions outside the workplace can also influence how people think about and evaluate their jobs.

**Research Question**
Do macroeconomic conditions outside the workplace shape how satisfied people are with their jobs? Specifically, the authors ask whether job satisfaction rises during recessions (when unemployment is high) and falls during booms, and whether this relationship is driven by the reduced salience of alternative jobs.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. There is a positive relationship between the unemployment rate and job satisfaction (predicted positive: higher unemployment, higher job satisfaction).
- Hypothesis 2. The positive relationship between the unemployment rate and job satisfaction is mediated by the reduced salience of alternative jobs (predicted positive indirect effect: higher unemployment lowers the salience of alternative jobs, which in turn raises job satisfaction).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Economic conditions, operationalized as the unemployment rate (national and state-level in Study 1; UK national in Study 2; experimentally manipulated bad-vs-good economy in Study 3).
- DV(s): Job satisfaction (single-item GSS measure in Study 1; single-item BHPS/Understanding Society measure in Study 2; Brayfield & Rothe 5-item scale in Study 3).
- Mediators: Reduced salience of alternative jobs (Miller's adapted 6-item Attention to Alternatives Index, Study 3).
- Moderators: None formally tested (the paper proposes personality/demographic moderators as future work).
- Controls: Age, age squared, gender, income, linear time trend; industry and occupation; tenure (Study 3); state dummies (Study 1 state models).

When job alternatives are plentiful and salient, jobs are judged against higher reference standards and comparisons surface their negative features, depressing satisfaction (the Cornell frame-of-reference and investment models). During recessions, scarce and unreachable alternatives become less salient, so people focus on the positive features of their existing jobs and use system-justifying, compensatory-control strategies to view their situation more favorably. Study 3's mediation confirms that worse economic conditions lower the salience of alternatives, which in turn raises job satisfaction.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 data source: the General Social Survey (GSS), a nationally representative repeated cross-sectional survey of American adults; 23,335 respondents interviewed in one of 29 waves administered between 1974 and 2016, retained if they were working full time at interview and had valid data on all independent and dependent variables (55.1% male, 81.2% White, mean age 40.5 years, SD = 12.1).
- Study 1 IV: economic conditions operationalized as the national unemployment rate, chosen because it tracks job availability and perceptions of the economy more tightly than GDP, stock market performance, or inflation-adjusted indicators, and because it is only modestly correlated with year (r = −.21 over the study window). A second set of models used the state unemployment rate (range 2.3% to 15.3%), obtained as restricted state-level data from the National Opinion Research Center and merged on state of current residence and survey year, giving a slightly smaller sample (n = 20,890).
- Study 1 DV: a single GSS item — "On the whole, how satisfied are you with the work you do—would you say you are very satisfied, moderately satisfied, a little dissatisfied, or very dissatisfied?" — recoded so higher values reflect greater job satisfaction. Because this item measures work rather than job satisfaction and is single-item, the authors validated it in a separate 250-person Amazon Mechanical Turk survey against the Brayfield and Rothe (1951) 5-item scale (r = .76, p < .001).
- Study 1 controls and estimation: age, age squared, gender, log inflation-adjusted income (built from GSS category midpoints), and a linear time trend; fuller models added the 16 industry categories of the North American Industry Classification System 2007 and six 2010 occupational categories, and the state models added state dummy variables. All models are ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions with random errors clustered by year for national analyses and by state-year for state analyses.
- Study 2 data source: the British Household Panel Survey (BHPS) and its Understanding Society continuation, collected 1991–2013 by the Institute for Social and Economic Research at the University of Essex; all 12,859 original BHPS respondents (regional and other oversamples excluded), averaging 7.7 years per participant and 98,153 to 99,314 year-observations depending on the measure.
- Study 2 IV and DV: the UK national unemployment rate from Eurostat (M = 6.49, SD = 1.66 over the period) as the IV; job satisfaction measured by the single item "All things considered, how satisfied or dissatisfied are you with your present job overall?" from 1 ("not satisfied at all") to 7 ("completely satisfied"), asked only of respondents employed at the time of the survey. Controls: age, age squared, and log inflation-adjusted annual labour income (imputed variables wfiyrl in the BHPS and w_fimnlabgrs_dv in Understanding Society) at level 1, and the time-invariant gender variable at level 2.
- Study 2 estimation: hierarchical linear models with random coefficients and a first-order autoregressive residual structure, chosen because the intraclass correlation for job satisfaction was .30, with standard errors clustered by person and non-consecutive waves treated as gaps. A companion specification entered a dummy variable for every participant (individual fixed effects) to absorb all observed and unobserved person-level variation and so test within-person fluctuation. A supplementary model restricted the sample to respondents with no spells of unemployment during the study — reported as 8,587 participants in Table 4, Model 4, while the Sample section describes this restricted sample as 8,617 respondents, or 67% of the sample.
- Study 3 design and manipulation: a randomized between-subjects experiment on 512 usable Amazon Mechanical Turk respondents (700 recruited; 188 excluded for failing an attention check or taking more than two standard deviations longer than average), all currently employed, mean age 35.87 (SD = 8.75), mean tenure 14.33 years (SD = 4.80). Under a memory-recall cover story, participants were randomly assigned to read a modified New York Times article reporting a bad economy ("New Unemployment Claims Rose Again Last Week", 112 words, adapted from a 2021 article) or a good economy ("Job Openings Are Everywhere", 114 words), viewing it for at least 30 seconds; the manipulation was modeled on Hill et al. (2012, Study 2) and checked with two items on job-market strength and the perceived unemployment rate.
- Study 3 measures: DV = Brayfield and Rothe's (1951) 5-item job satisfaction scale, 1 ("strongly disagree") to 7 ("strongly agree"), sample item "I feel fairly well satisfied with my present job". Mediator = Miller's (1997) 6-item Attention to Alternatives Index, adapted from the salience of alternative romantic partners to the salience of other possible jobs, sample item "I often think about other job opportunities", same 1–7 response format. Controls: age, age squared, gender, income, job tenure, the 17-category North American Industry Classification System, and the 6-category Census Occupational Classification system.
- Study 3 estimation: mediation estimated with the lavaan R package, with indirect-effect significance assessed using bias-corrected accelerated bootstrap 95% confidence intervals from 5,000 resamples based on the product of coefficients for each path; because bias-corrected bootstrapping can inflate Type I error, the authors also ran a joint-significance test with Monte Carlo confidence intervals (5,000 iterations).
- Identification: only Study 3 randomly assigns economic conditions, which the paper describes as allowing a more direct test of causality. Studies 1 and 2 are associational — the paper states that the correlational nature of these datasets precluded it from establishing causality. Study 2's individual fixed effects and continuously-employed subsample rule out stable person-level confounds and cyclical change in workforce composition, but the design remains observational rather than a causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1 was supported in each of the three studies, but the paper emphasizes that the magnitude of the effects is relatively small.
- Study 1 (national unemployment rate, OLS): higher unemployment predicted greater job satisfaction with only a linear time trend (b = 0.011, SE = 0.005, p < .05), after adding age, age squared, gender, and income (b = 0.011, SE = 0.004, p < .05), and after adding industry and occupational controls (b = 0.010, SE = 0.004, p < .05). Model R² ranged from 0.015 to 0.043.
- Study 1 (state unemployment rate with state dummies, n = 20,890): higher state unemployment predicted greater job satisfaction with a time trend only (b = 0.009, SE = 0.003, p < .01), with individual-level characteristics added (b = 0.008, SE = 0.003, p < .01), and with industry and occupational controls added (b = 0.007, SE = 0.003, p < .01).
- Study 1 robustness with alternative indicators: substituting other economic measures for the unemployment rate, the paper reports that indicators linked to perceptions of the economy were predictive of job satisfaction — inflation-adjusted median income divided by 10,000 (b = −0.005, SE = 0.002, p < .05), consumer confidence (b = −0.033, SE = 0.014, p < .05), consumer sentiment (b = −0.010, SE = 0.004, p < .01), and the Consumer Price Index (b = −0.0064, SE = 0.0019, p < .01) — whereas indicators not tightly tied to consumer perceptions were not (real per capita GDP in 1,000s: b = −0.005, SE = 0.004, n.s.; S&P 500: b = 0.000, SE = 0.000, n.s.).
- Study 2 (BHPS multilevel models): worse economic conditions were associated with greater job satisfaction (b = .013, SE = .003, p < .001), and the result held with age, age squared, gender, and income controlled (b = .012, SE = .003, p < .001). The individual fixed-effects specification also found higher unemployment associated with higher job satisfaction (b = .064, SE = .003, p < .001), showing the relationship holds within the same people over time. Restricting the sample to continuously employed respondents reproduced the effect (b = .018, SE = .004, p < .001), so the pattern is not explained by cyclical change in the composition of the workforce.
- Study 3 (experiment, preliminary test of Hypothesis 1): participants who read the bad-economy article reported greater job satisfaction (M = 4.83, SD = 0.89) than participants in the comparison condition (M = 4.68, SD = 0.88), t(510) = 1.94, p = .05 — the Results sentence labels this comparison group the "control condition", although the design contrasts a bad-economy with a good-economy article. Controlling for age, age squared, gender, tenure, and income, worse economic conditions continued to predict higher job satisfaction (b = 0.20, SE = 0.08, p = .01). Manipulation checks worked as intended: the bad-economy group rated the job market as weaker (M = 5.52, SD = 1.66 vs. M = 6.04, SD = 1.08; t(510) = 4.23, p < .001) and estimated unemployment as higher (M = 5.99, SD = 1.24 vs. M = 5.65, SD = 1.55; t(510) = 2.74, p < .01).
- Hypothesis 2 was tested only in Study 3 — Studies 1 and 2 contained no measure of the mediator — and was supported there. The indirect effect of economic conditions on job satisfaction through the salience of alternatives was significant (b = 0.12, SE = 0.05, 95% CI [0.04, 0.23], p < .01), with bad economic conditions reducing the salience of alternatives (b = −0.29, SE = 0.11) and the salience of alternatives negatively related to job satisfaction (b = −0.42, SE = 0.04); the total effect was 0.18 (SE = 0.08), CI [0.03, 0.34]. Similar results emerged with and without controls, and the joint-significance test with Monte Carlo confidence intervals also excluded zero.
- The reverse mediational pathway (job satisfaction as the mediator and salience of alternatives as the outcome) produced much weaker evidence: indirect effect b = −0.11 (SE = 0.06), CI [−0.257, 0.003], p = .072.
- Post hoc analyses in Studies 1 and 2 found that life satisfaction fell with the economy even as job satisfaction rose, which the authors read as evidence that the affective boost during downturns is limited to work rather than extending to all domains of life.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends job-satisfaction research—which has focused almost exclusively on situational conditions inside the workplace—by showing that contextual conditions outside organizations also shape workplace attitudes. It contributes to work on the psychological effects of recessions and provides theoretical and empirical support for the idea that adverse societal events can promote positive (not just disengaging) sense-making processes that bolster workplace attitudes.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations may benefit from acknowledging and addressing external economic events rather than ignoring them. Because employees seem driven to view their jobs favorably during downturns, efforts to raise satisfaction may be well-received then, and hiring during recessions may yield workers who rate their jobs more highly. Conversely, firms should attend more to satisfaction during booms, when salient alternatives make dissatisfaction costlier and turnover easier.

**Limitations**
The magnitude of the effects is relatively small, plausibly because countervailing forces—greater workplace stress and job insecurity during recessions—depress satisfaction at the same time the identified sense-making processes elevate it. The correlational nature of the first two studies' datasets precluded the authors from establishing causality; by manipulating rather than measuring economic conditions, Study 3 allowed "a more direct test of causality" and tested the mediator.

**Future Research**
Future work could simultaneously capture the competing forces that elevate and depress satisfaction during downturns, and examine when the elevating factors dominate. Researchers could also test moderation by personality (e.g., need for cognitive closure) or demographics (recessions are unevenly experienced, e.g., low-wage workers), and explore additional psychological mechanisms such as tempered expectations or gratitude, and why alternatives undermine satisfaction.
