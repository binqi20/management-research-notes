# Do I Dare? The Psychodynamics of Anticipated Image Risk, Leader-Identity Endorsement, and Leader Emergence

**Abstract**
Although many organizations value leadership across levels, individuals are reluctant to step up and lead. We explore how anticipated image risk (i.e., individuals' beliefs that the act of leading might harm their image with others) and lay beliefs about leadership ability (i.e., that this ability is fixed versus malleable) may diminish individuals' endorsement of a leader identity and thus their leadership emergence. Across MBA consulting teams, supervisor–employee dyads, and virtual workers, we find that lay theories of leadership ability moderate the negative relationship between anticipated image risk in leadership and leader-identity endorsement, which affects subsequent leadership emergence (Study 1). Using qualitative survey data, Study 2 identifies three specific image concerns that individuals associated with leading: seeming bossy, seeming unqualified, and seeming different from one's peers. Study 3 tests whether a newly developed measure of three specific image risks similarly predicts leader-identity endorsement and emergence. Last, in Study 4 we experimentally manipulate the anticipation of image risk to show its causal impact on leader-identity endorsement and emergence.

**Research Question**
Why don't more people internalize a leader identity and emerge as leaders? Specifically, how does anticipated image risk—individuals' belief that leading might harm how others see them—reduce leader-identity endorsement and thereby leadership emergence, and how do lay (fixed vs. malleable) theories of leadership ability moderate this process?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The quantitative components state six formal hypotheses, tested across Studies 1, B, 3, and 4. Study 2 is exploratory and qualitative and advances no a priori hypotheses.
- Hypothesis 1. When individuals anticipate that leadership will be risky for their image, they will be less likely to emerge as leaders.
- Hypothesis 2. When individuals anticipate that leadership will be risky for their image, they will be less likely to endorse a leader identity.
- Hypothesis 3. Individuals' endorsement of a leader identity is positively associated with their leadership emergence.
- Hypothesis 4. Anticipating image risk has a negative indirect effect on leadership emergence, through leader-identity endorsement.
- Hypothesis 5. The negative effect of anticipated image risk on leader-identity endorsement is less pronounced for those who hold a malleable view than for those who hold a fixed view of leadership ability.
- Hypothesis 6. The negative indirect effect of anticipated image risk on leadership emergence through leader-identity endorsement will be stronger for those who hold a fixed view of leadership than for those who hold a malleable view of leadership ability.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Anticipated image risk in leadership (general measure; and three specific image risks—fear of seeming bossy, unqualified, and different)
- DV(s): Leadership emergence (peer- and supervisor-rated; willingness to lead)
- Mediators: Leader-identity endorsement
- Moderators: Lay (fixed vs. malleable) theories of leadership ability

When individuals anticipate that leading will damage their image, ego/self-protection prompts a psychodynamic form of identity work in which they decline to endorse "leader" as a self-descriptor ("that's just not who I am"), neutralizing the threat while avoiding seeing themselves as lacking courage. Reduced leader-identity endorsement in turn lowers leadership emergence. A fixed lay theory of leadership ability strengthens this negative path because reputational damage is seen as permanent and growth as impossible, whereas a malleable view buffers it. The qualitative study specifies three concrete image concerns (bossy, unqualified, different) underlying the construct, with fear of seeming unqualified the most debilitating.

**Data & Measures**
The dominant method is quantitative (Studies 1, B, 3, and 4), with Study 2 supplying qualitative construct elaboration.
- Study 1 (longitudinal field survey; one of the two studies testing all six hypotheses, alongside Study 4): 411 first-year MBA students working in 83 multidisciplinary consulting teams of four to six at a large U.S. midwestern university in 2017, surveyed by the MBA program office three weeks before team formation (Time 1) and just as the seven-week project completed (Time 2), roughly 10 weeks apart. IV anticipated image risk in leadership: 5-item Likert scale from Zhang et al. (2020), alpha = .75, measured at Time 1 (sample item: "If I step up and attempt to lead the team, others in the group will think less of me"). Moderator lay theories of leadership ability: the four-item "implicit theory of leadership" scale from Hoyt et al. (2012), which substitutes "leadership" for "intelligence" in Dweck's (1999) implicit-theory measure, alpha = .84, coded so that high scores represent a fixed view. Mediator leader-identity endorsement: a newly developed 4-item measure administered at Time 2 (sample items "I see myself as a leader" and "Being a leader is very important to my sense of who I am"), also used by Lanaj et al. (2021, 2022). DV leadership emergence: three peer-rated items from Lord, Foti, and De Vader (1984) as used by DeRue et al. (2015), alpha = .93, with every team member rating each other member's contribution to team leadership and the ratings averaged. Controls: age and gender (in all studies), plus leadership self-efficacy (Paglis & Green, 2002; alpha = .76) to test incremental validity. Estimation: multivariate regression with mean-centered predictors and an interaction term, plus PROCESS model 4 (simple mediation) and model 7 (first-stage conditional process) with 10,000 bootstrap resamples (Hayes, 2017). The authors state the Study 1 data are correlational, so the design is associational rather than causally identified.
- Study B (Online Appendix B; preregistered experiment, n = 324, nonoverlapping sample): experimentally manipulated lay theories of leadership ability in order to test the first stage of the model causally rather than by measurement.
- Study 2 (qualitative, exploratory): 327 full-time workers from a variety of industries recruited on Amazon Mechanical Turk for a 15-minute survey paying $1.00, answering deliberately open-ended, non-priming questions about what, if anything, makes them feel uncomfortable (or comfortable) using the label "leader." Research assistants and one author first performed "complete coding" of everything relevant to what makes "leader" an uncomfortable identity, then a second, focused coding of image-risk mentions, with multiple codes allowed per response and periodic coder meetings after batches of 10 to 20 responses; disagreements were resolved by an author. Codes mentioned by at least three respondents were clustered into themes that all authors refined for internal homogeneity and external heterogeneity. 285 respondents (87%) named at least one source of discomfort, and 88 (31% of those) mentioned image-related concerns. A separate survey of 119 Air Force cadets was used to check whether the themes replicated in a different sample.
- Study 3 (multisource field survey): 377 focal employee–supervisor pairs, combining 233 pairs obtained by snowball sampling through an undergraduate management course at a midwestern U.S. university with 144 pairs recruited via the paid Positly.com pool; the authors confirmed the two data sets showed identical patterns before combining them. Employees rated the three specific image-risk scales developed from the Study 2 themes (fear of seeming bossy alpha = .93, fear of seeming different alpha = .94, fear of seeming unqualified alpha = .90) on 7-point agreement scales following the stem "If I step up and attempt to lead in my team at work ...", and rated their own leader-identity endorsement with the Study 1 measure (alpha = .88); supervisors independently rated the employee's leadership emergence with the Study 1 three-item measure (alpha = .86). Controls: age, gender, narcissism, extraversion, leadership self-efficacy, and prior discomfort in leading. Mediation was tested with PROCESS model 4 and 10,000 bootstrap resamples. Predictor and mediator were separated within the survey, but the authors state that common method variance bias is still possible.
- Study 4 (preregistered randomized experiment): 448 virtual workers from Amazon Mechanical Turk (500 targeted; 4 excluded a priori for not reading instructions and 47 for completion times outside 12 to 33 minutes), paid $2.50, randomly assigned to one of two otherwise-identical eight-minute podcasts presenting leadership as riskier than people think (high-risk) or less so (low-risk), with the script built around the three specific image concerns from Study 2 and an "expert" voice reinforcing the framing. Leader-identity endorsement used the Study 1 measure (alpha = .92) plus Bergami and Bagozzi's (2000) five-diagram circles measure of self-identity congruence as a constructive replication with a different operationalization. Because participants did not actually interact in a team, leadership emergence was operationalized as willingness to lead ("If given the opportunity, I would like to step up and lead this group," alpha = .88). Lay theories of leadership ability were measured (as in Study 1), not manipulated. Controls: age, gender, and six preregistered podcast-perception items (difficulty, credibility, persuasiveness, and so forth); a separate pilot (n = 200) confirmed the manipulation moved both general and specific image risk.

**Key Findings**
Support is heterogeneous across studies, so it is reported per study rather than pooled. The mediation chain (Hypotheses 2, 3, and 4) held in every study that tested it, but Hypothesis 1 failed in Study 3 and the lay-theory moderation (Hypotheses 5 and 6) failed in Study 4; the authors themselves write that "the data offer mixed support" for the moderation argument.
- Study 1 (n = 411; correlational field survey): all six hypotheses supported. H1 — anticipated image risk negatively predicted peer-rated leadership emergence at Time 2 (Table 3, Models 7, 8, and 9; B = -.166, SE = .065 in Model 8). H2 — image risk negatively predicted leader-identity endorsement at Time 2 (B = -.17, SE = .07, p = .018). H3 — endorsement was positively related to peer-rated emergence (B = .28, SE = .04, p < .001). H4 — the indirect effect was significant (effect = -.05, SE = .02, 95% CI [-.103, -.005]) alongside a significant direct effect (effect = -.14, SE = .06, p = .017), which the authors read as partial mediation. H5 — the image-risk-by-lay-theory interaction on endorsement was significant (B = -.30, SE = 0.12, p = .013), with simple slopes negative and significant for a fixed view (+1 SD; B = -.31, SE = .10, p = .002) but null for a malleable view (-1 SD; B = .02, SE = .10, p = .871). H6 — the conditional indirect effect was significant under a fixed view (effect = -.08, SE = .03, 95% CI [-.144, -.025]) but not under a malleable view (effect = .005, SE = .03, 95% CI [-.06, .063]); index of moderated mediation = -.08, SE = .04, 95% CI [-.165, -.005].
- Study B (n = 324; preregistered, lay theories manipulated): supported H2 and H5 per the paper's summary table — inducing a malleable view of leadership ability attenuated the negative influence of anticipated image risk on leader-identity endorsement. (The Limitations section additionally describes Study B as finding further support for Hypotheses 5 and 6, whereas Table 1 marks H6 as not tested there.)
- Study 2 (qualitative, n = 327): three specific image concerns emerged — fear of seeming bossy (the most frequently referenced: domineering n = 28, undemocratic n = 6, prone to corruption n = 4, harsh n = 3, selfish n = 3), fear of seeming unqualified (incongruent personality, lacking expertise, expected to have all the answers; reminiscent of imposter syndrome), and fear of seeming different (leaders seen as always in the spotlight, n = 22; not wanting to be looked up to, n = 5). Notably, the fear was not confined to negative evaluation: being singled out positively, even idolized, was also discomforting. The 119 Air Force cadets converged on the same three themes.
- Study 3 (n = 377 employee–supervisor dyads): H1 was not supported — none of the three specific image risks significantly and negatively predicted supervisors' ratings of leadership emergence. H2 was supported for each risk entered separately (fear of seeming bossy B = -.19, SE = .04; fear of seeming different B = -.18, SE = .05; fear of seeming unqualified B = -.32, SE = .04; all p < .001), but when all three were entered simultaneously only fear of seeming unqualified remained significant (B = -.40, SE = .06, p < .001). H3 was supported (employee-rated endorsement was positively related to supervisor-rated emergence). H4 was supported for each risk entered separately (bossy effect = -.04, 95% CI [-.066, -.017]; different effect = -.07, 95% CI [-.097, -.041]; unqualified effect = -.04, 95% CI [-.065, -.016]), but only the fear-of-seeming-unqualified indirect effect survived controlling for the other two (effect = -.08, SE = .02, 95% CI [-.121, -.048]). H5 and H6 were not tested in this study.
- Study 4 (n = 448; image risk manipulated): H1 supported — participants assigned to the high-risk condition were less willing to lead in the upcoming group task (B = -.43, SE = .15, p = .004; M = 3.99 vs. 4.35, t(446) = 2.40, p = .017). H2 supported with the preregistered controls (B = -.30, SE = .13, p = .024), although the uncontrolled t-test on endorsement was not significant (M = 3.86 vs. 4.08, t(446) = 1.70, p = .089). H3 supported (B = .90, SE = .03, p < .001). H4 supported (indirect effect = -.27, SE = .12, 95% CI [-.503, -.039]). H5 and H6 were not supported: the manipulation-by-lay-theory interaction was non-significant (B = -.13, SE = .15, p = .379) and so was the index of moderated mediation (index = -.11, SE = .14, 95% CI [-.395, .169]), even though the simple slopes trended as in Study 1 (fixed view B = -.41, SE = .19, p = .03; malleable view B = -.17, SE = .19, p = .357). The authors suggest the null interaction may be a methodological artifact of manipulating image risk while only measuring lay theories.

Convergence and divergence across methods: the quantitative studies converge on the core account — H2, H3, and H4 were supported wherever tested, and the randomized Study 4 supplies causal evidence that anticipated image risk lowers leader-identity endorsement and emergence. They diverge on the outcome link and the moderator: H1 held in Studies 1 and 4 but failed in Study 3, where emergence was supervisor-rated rather than peer-rated or self-reported, and the lay-theory moderation held in Study 1 (and, for H5, in Study B) but not in Study 4. The qualitative Study 2 converged with and extended the quantitative work by specifying the content of image risk and supplying the three-factor scale used in Study 3, where fear of seeming unqualified proved the most debilitating of the three concerns.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts leader-identity scholarship from a lasting "once and for all" construction toward situational, fluid endorsement, and introduces a rarely invoked ego-defense (psychodynamic) explanation for why people deny a leader identity. It advances the concept of metaperception—how people think others think of them—as an underexplored driver of identity, and elaborates image risk from a molar construct into three specific reputational concerns (seeming bossy, unqualified, different).

**Practical Implication**
Organizations seeking more leadership at all ranks should reckon with negative, outdated stereotypes of leaders as aggressive or domineering and foster a consensus that leadership is independent of formal title. They should also promote a malleable view of leadership ability—reinforcing that leadership is a learnable skill and praising attempts to lead—to help employees circumvent the ego-defense response that suppresses leader-identity endorsement and emergence.

**Limitations**
The studies emphasize within-person perceptions over social context, leaving out other context-specific variables (e.g., perceived organizational support, hierarchy) that may shape image risk and endorsement. Study 1 used single-source survey measures vulnerable to common method variance, and support for the lay-theory moderation hypotheses (H5, H6) was mixed across studies—significant in Studies 1 and B but not when image risk was manipulated and lay theories measured in Study 4.

**Future Research**
Scholars could further test the moderating role of lay theories across varied, multisource settings to clarify when this mindset matters, and probe additional context variables (e.g., organizational support, hierarchy) shaping image risk. Researchers might examine the full range of anticipated reactions (negative, neutral, positive), whether people over- or accurately estimate social backlash, and why fear of seeming unqualified is the most debilitating concern.
