# Leveraging Knowledge Diversity in Hierarchically Differentiated Teams: The Critical Role of Hierarchy Stability

**Abstract**
Past research has been equivocal about the information sharing and performance effects of knowledge diversity in teams. In an attempt to resolve this equivocality, scholars have suggested that status hierarchy may play a role, proposing that status differences can constrict team-level information sharing in diverse teams by privileging the knowledge of higher-status members and discounting the knowledge of lower-status members. Although there is some evidence to support this notion, there is also evidence to suggest that status differences may amplify information sharing in diverse teams by offering status enhancement incentives to members who share unique knowledge. In this paper, we reconcile these different predictions by suggesting that the effects of status hierarchy on the relationship between knowledge diversity and team information sharing will depend on the stability of the hierarchy. Using a diverse sample of 156 teams across 110 organizations, we found that status differences constricted information sharing in knowledge-diverse teams when hierarchy stability was high and amplified information sharing in knowledge-diverse teams when hierarchy stability was low. These information sharing effects, in turn, affected team performance. Our study highlights how hierarchy stability is critical for understanding whether status differences constrict or amplify information sharing in knowledge-diverse teams.

**Research Question**
When and how does a team's status hierarchy constrict versus amplify the relationship between knowledge diversity and team information sharing (and, in turn, team performance), and does hierarchy stability determine which effect prevails?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Hierarchy stability moderates the moderating effect of status hierarchy on the relationship between knowledge diversity and team information sharing, such that status hierarchy will weaken (make more negative) the diversity–information-sharing relationship as hierarchy stability increases and will strengthen (make more positive) that relationship as hierarchy stability decreases.
- Hypothesis 2. Hierarchy stability moderates the moderating effect of status hierarchy on the indirect relationship between knowledge diversity and team performance through its effect on team information sharing, such that status hierarchy will weaken that indirect effect as hierarchy stability increases and will strengthen it as hierarchy stability decreases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Knowledge diversity (composite of educational-background and job-role diversity, Blau's index)
- DV(s): Team information sharing (6-item scale); team performance (3-item supervisor-rated scale)
- Mediators: Team information sharing (mediates knowledge diversity → team performance)
- Moderators: Status hierarchy (peer-rated influence, prominence, respect dispersion) moderated in turn by hierarchy stability (a moderated-moderation / three-way interaction)

The paper argues that status hierarchy has two countervailing effects on diverse teams: a constricting effect (high-status members' perspectives dominate, low-status members feel inhibited) and an amplifying effect (hierarchy offers status-enhancement incentives for sharing unique knowledge). Drawing on the concept of hierarchy stability, the authors propose that stable hierarchies activate four constricting dynamics—greater dominance of one perspective, reduced accountability of high-status members, increased personal risk to low-status members, and weakened status-enhancement incentives—so that diversity's benefit to information sharing (and downstream performance) is weakest when status differences are large and stable. The empirical three-way interaction supports this: knowledge diversity was negatively related to information sharing when status hierarchy and stability were both high, and only marginally positive when hierarchy was high but unstable.

**Data & Measures**
- Data source: a multi-source field survey of intact work teams, recruited through the personal contacts of students in an MBA thesis course coordinated by one of the authors and collected with a standardized, coordinator-monitored procedure (a convenience sample; students could not sample their own organizations). Three temporally separated surveys were used: team-member survey 1 (hierarchy stability, job roles, educational background, demographics, and round-robin dyadic ratings of influence, prominence, and respect); team-member survey 2 two weeks later (team process variables, including team information sharing); and a supervisor survey roughly two weeks after that (team performance). All data were collected within a three-month period.
- Sample attrition: 198 teams approached, 170 teams (1,243 members, 122 organizations) provided data, 1,095 members responded (88%; average within-team response rate 92%), and after excluding teams with missing diversity, round-robin, supervisor, or member data the final sample was 156 teams / 995 individuals / 110 organizations (79% team response rate).
- IV — knowledge diversity: mean of the standardized Blau (1977) index scores for educational-background diversity (subject area of the highest degree, nine listed options plus a tenth category created from open-ended answers) and job-role diversity (open-ended current job position). The two dimensions correlated r = .48.
- Moderator — status hierarchy: peer round-robin 7-point ratings of each member's influence, prominence, and respect (following Anderson et al., 2001). Each member's mean peer rating per item was computed, then the within-team standard deviation of each of the three dimensions, then the mean of those three dispersion scores; Cronbach's alpha = .83.
- Second-stage moderator — hierarchy stability: a 6-item 7-point scale the authors developed because only experimental manipulations existed (e.g., "The pecking order in this team is stable"; reverse-scored items such as "In this team, it is relatively easy to move up in the hierarchy"). Cronbach's alpha = .67, cross-checked with Guttman's lambda-6 = .73 and omega = .76; in a separate online validation study the scale correlated r = .68 (p < .001) with the Hays and Bendersky (2015) measure.
- Mediator — team information sharing: 6-item scale from De Dreu (2007), 1 (never) to 7 (very often); Cronbach's alpha = .82; aggregation to the team level supported by ICC(1) = .27, ICC(2) = .69, median r-wg(j) = 0.84.
- DV — team performance: supervisor ratings on a 3-item scale that compares the focal team with other teams in the same organization with similar composition, tasks, and customers, 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree); Cronbach's alpha = .78; correlated r = .78 (p < .001) with Edmondson's (1999) measure in an online validation study.
- Controls: task routineness/stability (Jehn, Northcraft, & Neale, 1999), membership stability (Wageman, Hackman, & Lehman, 2005), team status mean, and team size.
- Supplementary dyadic data: a roster measure of dyadic advice giving ("I give advice to" each teammate, 3-point scale) as a proxy for dyadic information sharing; knowledge uniqueness scored 0-2 on shared educational background and job role, then reverse-scored; member status group-mean centered. 5,811 dyadic observations, with random intercepts for information provider and information receiver.
- Estimation: multilevel (mixed-effects) models with a random intercept for organization to absorb organization-level nonindependence; the three-way interaction probed with Dawson and Richter's (2006) four-step slope-difference procedure; moderated mediation tested with nonparametric bootstrapping (10,000 draws) and robust standard errors clustered on organization using the R "mediation" package. An a priori Monte Carlo power analysis indicated at least 140 cases were needed to detect a moderate slope difference; post hoc power was 84%.

The design is associational, not causally identified: it is a cross-sectional field survey whose only design safeguards are multiple sources (members and supervisors) and temporal separation of measures. The authors state plainly that they cannot assert causality with their field study and cannot rule out the reverse possibility that information sharing shapes hierarchy or hierarchy stability.

**Key Findings**
- Main effects (Table 2, Model 1): knowledge diversity was unrelated to team information sharing (B = -0.00, SE = 0.04, p = .941), as was status hierarchy (B = -0.01, SE = 0.04, p = .785). Hierarchy stability had a positive main effect on information sharing (B = 0.10, SE = 0.04, p = .008), a result the authors flag and revisit in the Discussion. About 22% of the variance in information sharing was between organizations.
- No two-way interaction was statistically significant: knowledge diversity x status hierarchy was null (B = 0.00, SE = 0.05, p = .923; Model 2), and none of the three two-way interactions in Model 3 reached significance.
- Hypothesis 1 supported: the knowledge diversity x status hierarchy x hierarchy stability interaction predicting team information sharing was negative and significant (B = -0.13, SE = 0.06, p = .017; Model 4). Simple slopes: knowledge diversity was negatively related to information sharing when status hierarchy and hierarchy stability were both high (Est. = -0.21, SE = 0.10, t = -2.02, p = .046) and only marginally positively related when status hierarchy was high and stability low (Est. = 0.17, SE = 0.09, t = 1.84, p = .069). Those two slopes differed significantly (slope difference a: Est. = -0.32, SE = 0.13, t = -2.50, p = .015). Neither low-hierarchy slope was significant (high stability Est. = 0.10, p = .294; low stability Est. = -0.06, p = .461).
- Hypothesis 2 supported: the indirect effect of knowledge diversity on team performance via team information sharing was negative when status hierarchy and stability were both high (Est. = -0.10, 95% CI [-0.23, 0.00], p = .046) and only marginally positive when status hierarchy was high and stability low (Est. = 0.08, 95% CI [-0.00, 0.20], p = .068); the two indirect effects differed significantly (Est. = -0.18, p = .008). Both low-hierarchy indirect effects were non-significant (high stability Est. = 0.05, p = .261; low stability Est. = -0.03, p = .511).
- The support is asymmetric and the paper says so: the constricting effect of a stable steep hierarchy in knowledge-diverse teams is clearly evidenced, whereas the authors judge it less clear that low hierarchy stability exerts an amplifying effect, since both instability-side estimates were only marginal (p = .069 and p = .068).
- Robustness: five additional checks — excluding all control variables (Model 5), a bias-corrected Blau index, industry fixed effects, Sundstrom et al. (1990) team-type fixed effects, and adding task interdependence — each supported both Hypothesis 1 and Hypothesis 2.
- Supplementary dyadic analysis (5,811 dyads, Table 6): knowledge uniqueness was negatively related to advice giving (B = -0.04, SE = 0.01, t = -4.69, p < .001), so commonly held knowledge was discussed more than unique knowledge, and higher-status members gave more advice overall (B = 0.26, SE = 0.02, t = 11.17, p < .001). The knowledge uniqueness x member status interaction was null (B = 0.02, SE = 0.01, t = 1.53, p = .126), while member status x hierarchy stability was negative and significant (B = -0.04, SE = 0.02, t = -2.00, p = .046) and the three-way interaction was significant (B = -0.03, SE = 0.01, t = -2.03, p = .042; and B = -0.03, SE = 0.01, t = -2.07, p = .038 without controls).
- The dyadic result is only partly consistent with the theory, and the paper states this: high-status members were more likely to share information with teammates from different knowledge backgrounds when hierarchy stability was low than when it was high (t = -2.32, p = .020), but low-status members showed no such difference (t = 0.51, p = .607) — a finding the authors describe as "not consistent with our theoretical arguments."
- The authors further note that the pattern in Figure 1 suggests the information-sharing effects of hierarchy stability were more pronounced in steep homogeneous teams than in steep knowledge-diverse teams, contrary to the agnostic stance they had taken in theorizing.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study integrates the historically separate diversity (horizontal differentiation) and hierarchy (vertical differentiation) literatures, showing that the information-sharing and performance benefits of knowledge diversity cannot be understood without accounting for status hierarchy and its stability. It extends hierarchy-stability research—previously focused on homogeneous teams, where stability is beneficial—by demonstrating that stability can instead be detrimental in knowledge-diverse teams with steep hierarchies.

**Practical Implication**
Managers of knowledge-diverse teams should ask, in order, how important diverse-knowledge exchange is, whether status differences exist, and whether the status hierarchy is stable. Where status differences are unavoidable, managers should deliberately destabilize the hierarchy—rotating leadership, changing roles, refreshing membership, and structuring interactions to require all members' input—to keep diverse knowledge flowing.

**Limitations**
Hierarchy stability was measured via team-member perceptions rather than directly observed changes in the hierarchy. The temporally separated but cross-sectional field design cannot establish causality or rule out reverse effects of information sharing on hierarchy. The aggregated team-level information-sharing measure cannot decompose high- versus low-status sharing or unique versus common information content (partially addressed by a supplementary dyadic analysis that still did not capture actual content exchanged).

**Future Research**
Future work should test the correspondence between perceived and actual hierarchy stability and whether they predict different outcomes, use (field) experiments or longitudinal designs to probe the reciprocal information-sharing/hierarchy relationship, examine why stability effects appear stronger in homogeneous than diverse teams, and capture the actual content of information exchanged across diversity, hierarchy, and stability conditions.
