# Mixed Messages: Crisis Communication–Dismissal (In)Coherence and Shareholder Trust Following Misconduct

**Abstract**
We explore how firms’ post-transgression crisis communication and executive dismissals jointly influence shareholder trust following financial misconduct. We argue the coherence of a firm’s crisis management strategy—the degree to which its elements fit together consistently and logically—plays an important but previously unconsidered role in shareholder trust repair. We utilize multiple methods to abductively develop and test our theory. First, we conduct a qualitative comparative analysis of 51 cases of financial misconduct among S&P 1500 firms that disclosed a misstatement via press release and dismissed either the CEO or CFO within 90 days of the disclosure. Analyzing aspects of these firms’ crisis communication and the type of dismissal executed, this study reveals four configurations that highlight the influence of crisis management (in)coherence on shareholder trust. Second, a policy-capturing study examines the underlying mechanisms that drive shareholders’ perceptions and intended behaviors relative to manipulated crisis management strategies in a controlled setting. Together, findings from these studies indicate that shareholder trust following misconduct depends in part on the (in)coherence between what firms say about their misconduct, how they communicate that information, and what they do to resolve the problem.

**Research Question**
How does a firm's communication about its transgression influence shareholder reactions to its dismissal of an executive following financial misconduct? Specifically, how does the (in)coherence between post-transgression crisis communication and executive dismissal shape shareholder trust repair?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs: crisis management strategy coherence (degree to which communication and corrective action fit together logically and consistently); (in)coherence as the focal driver of shareholder trust repair.
- Communicative elements (causal conditions): blame acceptance, severity minimization, first-person disclosure.
- Corrective action (causal condition): type of executive dismissal (CEO vs. CFO).
- Emergent configurations: four "recipes" — e.g., major violation + CEO dismissal and isolated reporting error + CFO dismissal yield favorable reactions; major violation + CFO dismissal and ambiguous problem + unexpected dismissal yield unfavorable reactions.
- Outcomes: cumulative abnormal returns (Study 1); perceived (in)coherence, expected recidivism, and likelihood of increasing/reducing holdings (Study 2).

Shareholders jointly evaluate what a firm says about its misconduct, how it says it, and what it does (executive dismissal), and respond to the coherence among these tactics rather than to any tactic in isolation. When the dismissal logically resolves the problem described in the firm's communication, the strategy reads as coherent and trustworthy; mismatched "mixed messages" signal incoherence, raising expectations of recidivism and prompting divestment. Study 2 shows perceived (in)coherence statistically mediates the interaction of communicative sentiment and dismissal type on shareholders' intended holdings.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 is the dominant component. Its sampling frame came from the U.S. Government Accountability Office's (2002, 2006) Financial Statement Restatement Database, a listing of firms filing material restatements due to accounting irregularities, for 1997 through 2006, extended through 2012 via Lexis Nexis using the GAO's own search criteria; the criteria exclude restatements arising from normal business activity such as minor accounting changes. From that frame, 796 S&P 1500 firms announced a restatement between 1997 and 2012, and 98 of them dismissed their CEO or CFO within 90 days of the disclosure (a 90-day rather than two-year window was chosen to keep dismissals proximal to the disclosure). Excluding 24 cases with missing data, 16 in which the firm never disclosed the misstatement to shareholders, and seven in which the dismissal fell within three days of initial disclosure (confounding the two events) yielded 51 cases. The outcome, shareholder reactions, was operationalized as the cumulative abnormal return (CAR) of each firm's stock in response to the dismissal announcement: the market model was estimated over a 255-day window ending 46 days before the event, with abnormal returns cumulated over a two-day (0, 1) window. Favorable and unfavorable reactions were calibrated as two separate fuzzy sets, using the sample mean CAR (0.32%) as the crossover for partial membership, one standard deviation above the mean for full membership and one standard deviation below for nonmembership, with the calibration inverted for unfavorable reactions.

The four causal conditions were operationalized as follows. Blame acceptance is a tone score produced by applying a purpose-built word-list dictionary to the corpus of each firm's press releases issued between disclosure and dismissal, using LIWC computer-aided text analysis; the word list was developed by the four-step Short et al. (2010) procedure and validated by two independent judges (one management scholar, one finance expert) with inter-rater reliability of .96, and it was calibrated by the standard rank set method (nonmembership at or below 0.01, crossover 0.42, full membership at or above 2.05), after which two research-team members qualitatively checked each case's score against expressed regret or stated apology. Severity minimization is a crisp set coded 1 when firm communications explicitly minimized the severity of the misstatement or its impact and 0 otherwise, hand-coded from press releases by two team members (interrater reliability .87, disagreements resolved by discussion). First-person disclosure is a LIWC word count of 36 singular and plural first-person pronouns, rank-set calibrated (nonmembership at or below 0.01, crossover 0.65, full membership at or above 4.26). CEO dismissal is a crisp set coded 1 if the CEO was dismissed and 0 if the CFO was, with the two cases of simultaneous CEO and CFO dismissal coded 1. The fsQCA used a raw consistency threshold of 0.80, a PRI consistency threshold of 0.70, and a minimum solution frequency of one case per configuration; seven initial configurations were reduced by the Quine-McCluskey algorithm and the intermediate solution is reported.

Study 2 is the secondary component. It recruited 400 active, experienced investors via Lucid Marketplace, screening out individuals who own securities but do not actively manage their holdings, and excluded 51 responses for speeding or nonsense answers to embedded qualitative questions, leaving 349 (described as sufficient based on a power analysis); the instrument was pilot-tested on 82 MBA students at a large, public university in the United States. Each respondent read a vignette about a fictional firm (Reger Corp, given generic details modeled on a typical S&P 500 company) caught misstating earnings, then evaluated all four scenarios in randomized order. Two manipulated indicator variables were crossed within subject: communicative sentiment, coded 1 for blame acceptance and 0 for severity minimization (the two were combined into a single variable because they push in contrasting directions conceptually and in Study 1), and type of dismissal, coded 1 for CEO dismissal and 0 for CFO dismissal. Both communication manipulations incorporated first-person disclosure. Three 7-point Likert-type dependent variables were collected after each scenario: perceived (in)coherence, from 1 ("extremely inconsistent/illogical") to 7 ("extremely consistent/logical"); expected likelihood of recidivism, from 1 ("extremely unlikely") to 7 ("extremely likely"); and likelihood of increasing or reducing holdings, from 1 ("extremely likely to decrease equity holdings") to 7 ("extremely likely to increase equity holdings"), plus a free-text rationale. Controls were sex, age, risk propensity (7-item scale from Meertens & Lion, 2008), investing experience (years managing own investments), and investing style (7-point, extremely passive to extremely active). Models were estimated by hierarchical linear modeling to accommodate the four scenarios nested within each respondent.

On identification, Study 1 is a set-theoretic analysis of archival data that identifies combinations of conditions sufficient for favorable or unfavorable market reactions rather than net effects, and it cannot measure shareholders' perceptions of (in)coherence directly. Study 2 is described by the authors as a quasi-experimental policy-capturing design whose manipulations are controlled but which measures intended rather than actual behavior; the mediation test is labelled an ad hoc mediation analysis. Study 1's robustness checks re-ran the analysis with alternative CAR windows around the misstatement disclosure date (-1, 0 and -1, 1).

**Key Findings**
Study 1's fsQCA produced four configurations, two sufficient for favorable and two for unfavorable shareholder reactions, described by the authors as equifinal and asymmetric rather than a single net effect. Favorable: configuration 1 ("major violation, CEO dismissal") combined blame acceptance with the absence of severity minimization and the absence of first-person disclosure, followed by CEO dismissal (consistency 0.80; raw and unique coverage 0.13; four cases; exemplar Medarex, CAR +5.39%). Configuration 2 ("isolated reporting error, CFO dismissal") combined first-person disclosure, minimization of the misstatement's severity, and CFO rather than CEO dismissal (consistency 0.89; raw and unique coverage 0.07; two cases; exemplar Hooper Holmes, CAR +13.85%). Overall consistency for the favorable solution was 0.83 and overall coverage 0.20. Unfavorable: configuration 3 ("major violation, CFO dismissal") shared configuration 1's blame acceptance and absence of severity minimization but substituted CFO for CEO dismissal (consistency 0.89; raw coverage 0.23, unique coverage 0.08; seven cases; exemplar Thomas & Betts, CAR -6.14%). Configuration 4 ("ambiguous problem, unexpected dismissal") combined first-person disclosure with the absence of both blame acceptance and severity minimization, and preceded unfavorable reactions whether the CEO (exemplar UCBH Holdings, CAR -22.58%) or the CFO (exemplar Symbol Technologies, CAR -6.25%) was dismissed (consistency 0.98; raw coverage 0.22, unique coverage 0.07; five cases). Overall consistency for the unfavorable solution was 0.98 and overall coverage 0.30. The authors read the pattern as coherence between the dismissal and the preceding transgression-related communications tending to generate favorable shareholder reactions and incoherence tending to generate unfavorable ones: when firms accept blame for a misstatement without minimizing its severity, shareholders appear to expect a CEO dismissal and react unfavorably if the CFO is dismissed instead. First-person disclosure contributed in three of the four recipes (1, 2, and 4) and did different work in each: its absence fit configuration 1, where the leaders were presumably implicated; its presence read as candor in configuration 2; and its presence in configuration 4 read as a substitute for actual transparency. Results were consistent across the alternative CAR windows.

Study 2's HLM results converged with Study 1 and located perceived (in)coherence as the mechanism. The blame acceptance by CEO dismissal interaction was positive and significant on perceived (in)coherence (b = 1.06, p < .01; Model 3), negative and significant on expected likelihood of recidivism (b = -0.40, p < .01; Model 6), and positive and significant on the likelihood of increasing or reducing holdings (b = 0.32, p < .01; Model 9). All three interaction plots took the "X" shape the theory predicts: respondents judged the strategy more coherent, expected less recidivism, and were more likely to increase holdings when the communication matched the type of dismissal (blame acceptance with CEO dismissal, or severity minimization with CFO dismissal), and judged it more incoherent, expected more recidivism, and were more likely to reduce holdings when the two mismatched (blame acceptance with CFO dismissal, or severity minimization with CEO dismissal). In the ad hoc mediation test (Model 10), perceived (in)coherence predicted the likelihood of increasing or reducing holdings (b = 0.23, p < .01) while the interaction term fell to nonsignificance (b = 0.08, p > .10), which the authors interpret as perceived (in)coherence mediating the interaction's effect on intended holdings and as recognition of (in)coherence subsuming the direct and interactive effects of specific communications and dismissals; respondents' free-text rationales pointed the same way.

Convergence between the two studies is partial by design rather than uniform, and the paper does not claim otherwise. Study 2 tested only simplified versions of configurations 1 to 3 plus one combination absent from Study 1's solution (severity minimization with CEO dismissal), and it did not test configuration 4, so the ambiguous-problem recipe rests on Study 1 alone; conversely, perceived (in)coherence was measured only in Study 2. Study 1's coverage statistics are modest (0.20 for the favorable and 0.30 for the unfavorable solution) and rest on small numbers of cases per configuration. The authors' integrating claim across both studies is that no single crisis management tactic is always or never effective in restoring investor confidence; the outcome depends on how tactics combine into a coherent or incoherent overall strategy.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends crisis-management and corporate-governance research by theorizing the firm's overarching crisis management *strategy*—and its coherence—as a higher-order construct and important unit of analysis, rather than evaluating communication and dismissal tactics in isolation. Adopting a strategic coherence lens and a neo-configurational (fsQCA) approach, it explains previously inconsistent findings about market reactions to isolated tactics by showing that tactics combine in causally complex, equifinal ways to help or hinder shareholder trust repair.

**Practical Implication**
When restoring shareholder trust after a transgression, firms should avoid sending mixed messages and instead craft a coherent crisis management strategy that deliberately coordinates communications with corrective actions. For example, dismissing the CFO after the CEO has personally accepted blame can lead shareholders to question whether the action resolved the problem; the exemplar cases offer a roadmap for aligning words and deeds.

**Limitations**
Study 1 could not directly measure shareholders' perceptions of (in)coherence and used a relatively small sample with a 90-day dismissal window that created right-censoring. Study 2 tested only simplified versions of select configurations and, as a survey-based quasi-experiment, is susceptible to priming effects and respondent biases; the two studies are designed to counterbalance each other's limitations.

**Future Research**
Researchers could generalize the (in)coherence framework to other stakeholder groups (e.g., employees) and other misconduct types (e.g., consumer-safety or environmental issues), and examine executive *retention* and other corrective actions (e.g., changing reporting or governance practices) as elements of crisis management strategy. Further work could explore sequencing, path dependencies, and contextual factors (severity, magnitude, firm/industry preconceptions) that shape overall coherence.
