# Appraising and Reacting to Perceived Pay For Performance: Leader Competence and Warmth as Critical Contingencies

**Abstract**
While pay for performance (PFP) is widely adopted in organizations to boost employee performance, the empirical evidence on its incentive effect has been mixed. Based on Park and Sturman's PFP perception framework, we integrated two motivation theories—transactional theory of stress and role engagement theory—to delineate two countervailing mechanisms that explain how individuals appraise and react to perceived PFP. Our research considers employees' perception of PFP-I (perceived PFP based on individual performance) and highlights leader competence and warmth as critical contingencies. We propose that perceived PFP-I is appraised by employees as a challenge when leader competence is higher, which leads to employees' work engagement and enhanced task performance, but is appraised as a threat when leader warmth is lower, which leads to employees' work withdrawal and decreased task performance. In two three-wave survey studies, we found support for our model. The present research advances our knowledge regarding the incentive effect of PFP-I by emphasizing the importance of perceived PFP-I and proposing a new way of seeing employee motivation and performance ensuing from perceived PFP-I through the lenses of stress and role (dis)engagement. Our findings provide insights into how organizations and leaders can effectively utilize PFP to motivate employees.

**Research Question**
How can organizations and leaders make employees engage in, rather than withdraw from, work—and thus perform well—under pay for individual performance (PFP-I)? Specifically, how do employees appraise and react to perceived PFP-I, and how do leader competence and warmth shape these appraisals and reactions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Leader competence strengthens the relationship between perceived PFP-I and employee challenge appraisal of perceived PFP-I (positive moderation).
- H2: Leader warmth attenuates the relationship between perceived PFP-I and employee threat appraisal of perceived PFP-I (negative moderation).
- H3: The indirect interactive effect of perceived PFP-I and leader competence on employee work engagement is mediated by employee challenge appraisal of perceived PFP-I.
- H4: The indirect interactive effect of perceived PFP-I and leader competence on employee task performance is mediated serially by employee challenge appraisal of perceived PFP-I and employee work engagement.
- H5: The indirect interactive effect of perceived PFP-I and leader warmth on employee work withdrawal is mediated by employee threat appraisal of perceived PFP-I.
- H6: The indirect interactive effect of perceived PFP-I and leader warmth on employee task performance is mediated serially by employee threat appraisal of perceived PFP-I and employee work withdrawal.
- Stated explicitly without hypotheses: no main effects of perceived PFP-I on challenge or threat appraisal are hypothesized (appraisals are a joint function of stressor and resources), and leader competence is expected to have a null or modest moderating effect on the threat-appraisal link, as is leader warmth on the challenge-appraisal link.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived PFP-I (perceived strength of the linkage between individual performance and pay)
- DV(s): Employee task (in-role) performance
- Mediators: Challenge appraisal → work engagement (serial); threat appraisal → work withdrawal (serial); Study 2 adds control coping and escapist coping as micro-mediators
- Moderators: Leader competence (strengthens the PFP-I → challenge-appraisal link); leader warmth (attenuates the PFP-I → threat-appraisal link)

Integrating transactional theory of stress and role engagement theory within Park and Sturman's PFP perception framework, the paper treats PFP-I as a job stressor whose meaning is not fixed but appraised. When the leader is competent—signaling reliable evaluation and task-related resources—employees appraise perceived PFP-I as a challenge, creating a positive psychological condition (meaningfulness, availability) that fuels work engagement and higher performance. When the leader is warm—signaling fairness and socioemotional support—employees are less likely to appraise perceived PFP-I as a threat, reducing work withdrawal and protecting performance. The two countervailing pathways operate concurrently, so employees can have clear or ambivalent reactions depending on the leader's competence–warmth profile.

**Data & Measures**
Two three-wave, multi-source surveys of matched leader–employee dyads in China. Study 1 recruited employees by snowball sampling: 18 undergraduate students at a public university in south China, doing part-time jobs in organizations that gave them access to full-time workers, invited employees who had interactions with a supervisor who evaluated their task performance, knew whether their organization had implemented PFP, and were eligible for the PFP forms it provided; 380 full-time employees and their 380 supervisors initially participated, consecutive waves were one month apart, and the final sample comprised 247 matched leader–employee dyads from 234 organizations that provided pay raises (i.e., merit pay, a type of PFP-I). Study 2 drew a new sample from four organizations in education, finance, information technology, and manufacturing in China, with two-week lags between waves, each leader paired with only one employee, and a final sample of 192 matched dyads. All measures were translated and rated on 5-point scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) unless indicated otherwise. Perceived PFP-I (T1) used Heneman, Greenberger, and Strasser's (1988) four items, adapted from Perry and Pearce (1983). Leader competence and leader warmth were employees' ratings of how far adjective items from Goodwin, Piazza, and Rozin (2014) characterized their supervisor on a 10-point scale (competence: intelligent, organized, logical, innovative, creative, clever; warmth: kind, helpful, cooperative, grateful, empathetic, giving); Study 2 collected both at T1 together with perceived PFP-I. Challenge appraisal (3 items) and threat appraisal (3 items) of perceived PFP-I (T2) were based on Ohly and Fritz's (2010) items. At T3, work engagement used Schaufeli, Bakker, and Salanova's (2006) nine items and work withdrawal used Spector et al.'s (2006) four items, while task performance was rated by the matched supervisor with Van Dyne and LePine's (1998) four items; Study 2 added employee-reported control coping and escapist coping at T3. Both studies controlled for employee gender, age, organizational tenure, industry (16 categories from the national standard industrial classification) and risk aversion (eight items; Cable & Judge, 1994); Study 2 additionally controlled perceived group bonus, perceived profit sharing and perceived gainsharing, plus PFP-system characteristics (percent of base pay, appraisal accuracy of individual and of group performance, procedural pay transparency, pay secrecy, pay equity, overall fairness, perceived uncertainty, peer comparison of pay). Analysis ran in Mplus: nine-factor confirmatory factor analyses, then all hypotheses tested simultaneously in moderated-mediation path models using a bias-corrected bootstrapping approach with 1,000 replications and 95% confidence intervals, with z-scores of leader competence and warmth, plus latent moderation (Mplus XWITH) re-estimated as a robustness check. The design is associational: the three waves and the two rating sources separate measurement in time and by source, and the paper reports moderated relationships rather than claiming a causal-identification strategy, using added controls to address omitted-variable concerns and arguing that the interaction effects were unlikely to be artifacts of common method variance. The only randomization in the paper is a separate scenario experiment (n = 237) that assigned participants to a perceived PFP-I (pay raise) versus a perceived group bonus condition and found no significant difference in challenge appraisal, threat appraisal, work engagement, work withdrawal, or task performance.

**Key Findings**
All six hypotheses were supported in Study 1 and again in Study 2. Study 1 (n = 247, controlling for gender, age, organizational tenure, industry, and risk aversion): H1 supported — leader competence moderated the perceived PFP-I → challenge appraisal relationship (b = 0.17, SE = 0.05, p < .01, f² = .03), which was positive when leader competence was higher (b = 0.40, SE = 0.07, p < .01, f² = .19) but not when it was lower (b = 0.05, SE = 0.07, p = .45, f² = .00). H2 supported — leader warmth moderated the perceived PFP-I → threat appraisal relationship (b = −0.16, SE = 0.05, p < .01, f² = .03), which was positive when leader warmth was lower (b = 0.34, SE = 0.06, p < .01, f² = .13) but not when it was higher (b = 0.01, SE = 0.07, p = .92, f² = .00). H3 supported — challenge appraisal was positively related to work engagement (b = 0.21, SE = 0.05, p < .01, f² = .05) and mediated the perceived PFP-I × leader competence effect on engagement (indirect effect = 0.04, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.02, 0.08]). H4 supported — the serial indirect effect on task performance via challenge appraisal and work engagement was 0.006 (SE = 0.003, 95% CI [0.002, 0.02]), with a significant contrast between higher and lower leader competence (contrast = 0.01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.003, 0.03]). H5 supported — threat appraisal was positively related to work withdrawal (b = 0.19, SE = 0.07, p < .01, f² = .04) and mediated the perceived PFP-I × leader warmth effect on withdrawal (indirect effect = −0.03, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.07, −0.01]). H6 supported — the serial indirect effect on task performance via threat appraisal and work withdrawal was 0.004 (SE = 0.003, 95% CI [0.001, 0.01]), with a significant contrast by leader warmth (contrast = 0.007, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.001, 0.03]). As the paper anticipated without hypothesizing it, leader competence did not moderate the perceived PFP-I → threat appraisal relationship, and leader warmth did not moderate the perceived PFP-I → challenge appraisal relationship. Study 2 (n = 192, with collective-PFP and PFP-system controls added): consistent with H1, the competence interaction was b = 0.13 (SE = 0.04, p < .01, f² = .02), the perceived PFP-I → challenge appraisal slope being positive only when competence was higher (b = 0.29, SE = 0.09, p < .01, f² = .08; lower competence b = 0.03, SE = 0.08, p = .70); consistent with H2, the warmth interaction was b = −0.30 (SE = 0.14, p < .05, f² = .10), the perceived PFP-I → threat appraisal slope being positive only when warmth was lower (b = 0.33, SE = 0.16, p < .05, f² = .11; higher warmth b = −0.27, SE = 0.17, p = .12); H3 supported (indirect effect = 0.04, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [.01, .08]); in line with H4 (serial indirect effect = 0.01, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [.002, .03]; contrast = 0.02, SE = 0.01, 95% CI [0.003, 0.06]); supporting H5 (indirect effect = −0.08, SE = 0.06, 95% CI [−0.25, −0.02]); and in line with H6 (serial indirect effect = 0.02, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [0.004, 0.06]; contrast = 0.03, SE = 0.03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.12]). Supplementary Study 2 analyses traced the two pathways through coping: challenge appraisal → control coping (b = 0.24, SE = 0.10, p < .05) → work engagement (b = 0.33, SE = 0.11, p < .01) → task performance (b = 0.25, SE = 0.11, p < .05), and threat appraisal → escapist coping (b = 0.19, SE = 0.10, p < .05) → work withdrawal (b = 0.48, SE = 0.09, p < .01) → task performance (b = −0.24, SE = 0.07, p < .01). Exploring predictors of perceived PFP-I, appraisal accuracy of individual performance (b = 0.31, SE = 0.12, p < .05) and overall fairness (b = 0.24, SE = 0.08, p < .01) were positively related to perceived PFP-I, whereas percent of base pay, procedural pay transparency, pay secrecy, pay equity, perceived uncertainty, and peer comparison of pay were not significantly related; the model remained supported with these PFP-system characteristics controlled. Alternative leadership factors (transformational and servant leadership, leader initiating structure, leader interpersonal consideration, leader–member exchange) and employee individual differences (including risk aversion, general self-efficacy, BAS/BIS, and Big Five traits) showed no significant moderation of challenge or threat appraisal.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The research makes a threefold contribution to compensation research: (1) it stresses employees' active role in perceiving, appraising, and reacting to PFP-I, showing perceived PFP-I varies across employees and drives motivational mechanisms for in-role performance; (2) it extends Park and Sturman's (2022) PFP perception framework and integrates leadership and motivation research by positioning leader competence and warmth as fundamental moderators of appraisals and role (dis)engagement; and (3) it foregrounds employees' role (dis)engagement—especially work withdrawal as distinct from (not the opposite of) engagement—as a neglected motivational mechanism that helps reconcile the mixed evidence on the incentive effect of PFP.

**Practical Implication**
Designing a sound PFP system is necessary but not sufficient; organizations and leaders must understand how employees perceive and appraise PFP. Because employees care about leaders' competence and warmth when appraising perceived PFP-I, leaders should develop and visibly communicate both characteristics—e.g., accurate performance evaluation and clear paths to goals (competence) and supportive, considerate behaviors and positive emotions such as gratitude (warmth)—to channel PFP toward engagement rather than withdrawal and to prevent harmful outcomes (performance decrements, ill-being).

**Limitations**
Both studies were conducted in a single, collectivistic culture (China), raising generalizability questions across individualistic cultures. The samples skewed relatively young, presumably because younger employees were more willing to complete the online surveys, so it remains untested whether the findings replicate in samples comprising mostly older employees. Although Study 2 added many controls and explored predictors of perceived PFP-I, the research did not have a theoretical focus on the antecedents of perceived PFP-I.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine nonleader contingencies (e.g., job design features such as autonomy and feedback) that may similarly shape employees' challenge and threat appraisals of perceived PFP-I. Researchers could further probe how employee individual differences moderate reactions to perceived PFP-I (rather than to pay design characteristics), and could more directly theorize and test the antecedents of perceived PFP-I within the broader PFP environment.
