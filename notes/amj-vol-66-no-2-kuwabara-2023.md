# Lay Theories of Instrumental Relations: Explaining Individual Differences in Dispositional Similarity-Attraction

**Abstract**
The growing diversity in today's workplace requires engaging with people who not only look different but also think different. Yet, research on workplace relations has treated similarity-attraction as a human universal and paid limited attention to individual differences in who respects or tolerates different views and values, and why. We address this gap by examining how lay theories people hold about instrumental relations affect dispositional similarity-attraction. Because people who hold a fixed (versus growth) theory of instrumental relations believe that relationships form primarily on the basis of natural compatibility (versus effort), they should be particularly prone to similarity-attraction on the basis of dispositional similarity in values, attitudes, and personality traits. To test our arguments, we first develop a lay theories of instrumental relations scale, which we use to demonstrate that holding a growth theory decreases (moderates) people's tendency to avoid dispositionally dissimilar partners in a naturally occurring network (Field Study) and a dyadic task (Experiment 1). Finally (Experiment 2), we manipulate lay theories to show that inducing growth theories increases people's satisfaction with a task partner who is dispositionally dissimilar. We conclude by discussing theoretical and practical implications for building diverse yet cohesive workplaces.

**Research Question**
Are some individuals more likely than others to choose or get along with people who have different values, attitudes, and personality traits, and why? Specifically, how do lay theories of instrumental relations (LIR) — beliefs about whether interpersonal compatibility is fixed (natural chemistry) versus malleable (effort) — moderate dispositional similarity-attraction in partner choice and partner satisfaction?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Lay theories of instrumental relations moderate dispositional similarity-attraction in partner choice, such that the tendency to avoid dispositionally dissimilar people as interaction partners is stronger for people with fixed rather than growth theories.
- Hypothesis 1b. Expected compatibility mediates the effect of dispositional similarity on partner choice more strongly for people with fixed theories than for people with growth theories.
- Hypothesis 2. Lay theories of instrumental relations moderate dispositional similarity-attraction in task partner satisfaction, such that the association between dispositional similarity and satisfaction is stronger for people with fixed rather than growth theories.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Dispositional similarity (in values, personality traits, or attitudes, depending on study); lay theories of instrumental relations (LIR; fixed vs. growth, measured as a continuum or experimentally manipulated in Experiment 2).
- DV(s): Partner choice / tie formation (perceived instrumental ties in Field Study; sign-up decision in Experiment 1) and satisfaction with a task partner (Experiment 2).
- Mediators: Expected compatibility (mediates the LIR × dispositional-similarity effect on partner choice; Hypothesis 1b).
- Moderators: LIR is the focal moderator of similarity-attraction (Hypotheses 1a and 2); the design statistically or experimentally controls demographic similarity.

People with a fixed theory believe relationships form on natural compatibility ("like pieces of a puzzle"), so they treat dispositional similarity as a quick diagnostic of compatibility and avoid dissimilar partners; people with a growth theory believe compatibility is cultivated through effort ("like muscles"), so they are more receptive to dissimilar others. Because lay theories are self-fulfilling, fixed theorists derive satisfaction from effortless clicking while growth theorists tolerate the effort of working with dissimilar partners. The result is a mediated-moderation model: dispositional similarity-attraction is stronger for fixed theorists, and (for partner choice) this is channeled through expected compatibility.

**Data & Measures**
Field Study: 111 working professionals in two sections of a part-time executive MBA program at an urban university in the United States completed instruments as course assignments at three time points over one semester. LIR (the moderator) was the paper's new 5-item scale (α = .85), administered in a preterm survey, with fixed and growth theories measured as polar ends of a single continuum and higher scores indicating stronger fixed theories. Dispositional similarity (the independent variable) was computed for every possible pair of students from a "values structure" exercise in which each student produced a list of personal values, narrowed them to the eight most important, and identified interdependencies among them; a vector-semantic approach from computational linguistics scored the structural equivalence of values, and each dyad's similarity was the average of the best similarity matchings of their eight values, ranging from 0 (dissimilar) to 1 (similar). The dependent variable was a perceived instrumental tie, coded 1 if the focal student named an alter in an end-of-semester network survey as a significant source of (a) information or advice on career issues, (b) information or advice for getting tasks done, or (c) concrete resources. Controls were the focal student's age, gender, and race, dyadic similarity on each of those attributes, and self-monitoring measured at time 1. Estimation was a logit model of LIR, value similarity, and their interaction predicting a perceived tie in each dyad, with three-way (ego-alter-section) clustered standard errors to address dyadic nonindependence; the final sample was 2,756 ties reported by 111 individuals comprising 6,062 dyadic pairs. The design is associational rather than causal: because neither LIR nor similarity was manipulated, the authors state that they cannot make causal inferences, and they note that the tie measure conflates partner choice with partner satisfaction.

Experiment 1: a preregistered between-subjects randomized experiment (dispositionally similar vs. dissimilar partner) with LIR measured as a continuous moderator, run on a deception-prescreened Prolific pool of full- or part-time workers residing in the United Kingdom or the United States; 600 were recruited and 492 remained after 108 failed to complete the study or failed two attention checks. Survey 1 measured personal background, LIR (α = .82), implicit theories of personality (α = .86), diversity beliefs (α = .90), self-monitoring (α = .83), lay theories of networking ability (α = .83), and eight self-descriptive personality traits on 6-point Likert scales. In Survey 2, three to five days later, dispositional similarity was manipulated by setting a prospective "fictitious stranger" partner's answers on those same personality items to be either similar to or different from the participant's own, while demographic similarity was held high in both conditions (same race and gender, within three years in age). Perceived similarity (2 items, α = .94) served as the manipulation check, expected compatibility (2 items based on Finkel, Burnette, & Scissors, 2007; α = .86) as the mediator, and the dependent variable was the binary decision to sign up for the bonus study with the assigned partner. Estimation was logistic regression on LIR, experimental condition, and their interaction, followed by structural equation modeling of the moderated mediation bootstrapped with 5,000 resamples following Preacher and Hayes (2008); random assignment identifies the effect of the manipulated similarity condition, while LIR itself remains a measured individual difference.

Experiment 2: an in-class randomized experiment with 174 MBA students at an urban business school, with sample size determined by class size; it was conducted before Experiment 1 and was not preregistered. Lay theories were manipulated at the level of individuals rather than dyads by randomly assigning fixed-theory or growth-theory statements (relationships as pieces of a puzzle versus relationships as muscles), asking students to rate their agreement on a biased scale (1 = slightly agree to 5 = completely agree), and having them write about how the statements reflected their own professional experiences; a separate pilot test rather than an in-study manipulation check verified that the induction worked. Dispositional similarity was computed as within-dyad agreement, the inverse of the absolute difference, in responses to five attitude items on office politics based on Kacmar and Ferris (1991), from which an aggregate difference score was computed and rescaled from 0 (complete agreement) to 1 (complete disagreement). The dependent variable was task partner satisfaction, four coworker-satisfaction items based on Bishop and Scott (2000) (α = .87) rated from 1 (extremely dissatisfied) to 7 (extremely satisfied) immediately after a 30-minute paired case discussion. Estimation was OLS regression of satisfaction on dispositional similarity, experimental condition, and their interaction with standard errors clustered within dyads; robustness checks added self and partner demographics, the Big Five traits, self-monitoring, and partner familiarity and interaction frequency, and a Monte Carlo sensitivity analysis with 5,000 replications indicated 92% statistical power.

**Key Findings**
Field Study (exploratory and not preregistered, and by the authors' account unable to test the hypotheses directly): the LIR × dispositional-similarity interaction predicting a perceived instrumental tie was positive and significant, b = .06, robust SE = .03, p = .041, and persisted with demographic controls, b = .06, robust SE = .02, p = .010, and across models adding LIR interactions with demographic similarity. LIR had a negative main effect, b = -.52, robust SE = .17, p < .001, meaning that holding a fixed theory inhibited tie formation given an alter at 50% dispositional similarity, while dispositional similarity had no significant main effect for people at mid-levels (3-4) of the LIR scale, b = -.002, robust SE = .04, p = .948. Marginal-effects analysis showed the effect of dispositional similarity on perceiving a tie became significant only above 4.5 on the LIR, and simple slopes showed that dispositional similarity increased the probability of reporting a tie for a moderate fixed theorist (LIR = 5, slope = .17, p < .001) but not for a moderate growth theorist (LIR = 2, slope = -.42, p = .090). The authors characterize this as preliminary but compelling support for the idea behind Hypothesis 1a; Hypothesis 1b could not be tested because expected compatibility was not measured before any ties formed.

Experiment 1 supported Hypothesis 1a: the LIR × similarity-condition interaction predicting sign-up for the bonus study was positive and significant, b = .47, SE = .19, p = .014, exp(b) = 1.60, over and above a negative LIR main effect, b = -.42, SE = .14, p = .003, exp(b) = .65, and a positive dispositional-similarity main effect, b = .38, SE = .18, p = .039, exp(b) = 1.46. Simple slopes found a significant effect of lay theories in the dissimilar condition (slope = -.10, p = .002) but not in the similar condition (slope = .01, p = .718), and the sign-up rate was lower in the dissimilar than the similar condition only for individuals holding fixed theories (3.6 or above on the LIR scale), not for individuals with growth theories. Hypothesis 1b was also supported: the treatment × LIR interaction predicted expected compatibility, b = .19, SE = .09, p = .039, expected compatibility predicted partner selection, b = .39, SE = .11, p < .001, and the bootstrapped moderated mediation was significant, indirect effect = .08, bias-corrected 95% CI [.01, .20], with the mediation significant for fixed theorists (indirect effect = .31, CI [.08, .68]) but not for growth theorists (indirect effect = .08, CI [-.06, .39]). These results were robust to controlling for implicit theories of personality, diversity beliefs, self-monitoring, lay theories of networking ability, and participant demographics, none of which interacted significantly with the experimental condition or produced moderated mediation via expected compatibility.

Experiment 2 supported Hypothesis 2: dispositional similarity had a positive main effect on task partner satisfaction in the aggregate, b = 1.58, robust SE = .63, p = .013, and examined by condition the effect was significant for students induced into a fixed theory, b = 1.71, robust SE = .61, p = .006, but not for those induced into a growth theory, b = .67, robust SE = .70, p = .342. The fixed-theory simple slope rose more steeply and converged with the growth-theory condition at high levels of similarity, so the two groups differed largely in how they responded to low dispositional similarity. Exploratory analyses revealed no effect of the partner's experimental condition by itself and no actor × partner interaction, suggesting that how people experienced working with a task partner was influenced largely by their own lay theories.

Support pattern across the three studies: Hypothesis 1a received direct experimental support only in Experiment 1, with indirect, exploratory support in the Field Study; Hypothesis 1b was untestable in the Field Study and was supported in Experiment 1; Hypothesis 2 was tested only in Experiment 2, where it was supported. Common to all three studies is that lay theories mattered most where dispositional similarity was low, with differences between fixed and growth theorists diminishing as dispositional similarity increased.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances a novel account of individual differences in dispositional (deep-level) similarity-attraction, departing from prior explanations centered on stereotyping and social categorization of surface-level differences by invoking *compatibility* as the key mechanism. It extends lay-theory research from individuals, intimate relations, and episodic exchanges into ongoing workplace instrumental relations, introducing LIR as both a theoretical construct and a validated measure. It also speaks to the problem of agency in networks, framing LIR as a basis of human agency in how people form and shape instrumental ties rather than treating homophily as a universal.

**Practical Implication**
Measuring or changing employees' lay theories may offer a less heavy-handed route to building diverse yet cohesive workplaces — cultivating growth theories to promote cultural and interpersonal adaptability rather than screening employees for cultural fit. Because lay theories are simple and can be taught "on the spot," brief interventions may help employees value dissimilar partners. The authors caution that growth theories are a barrier-lowering tool, not a silver bullet, and warn against a "false growth mindset" that blames individuals and masks systemic barriers.

**Limitations**
Samples were drawn from students or online populations, limiting generalizability, and the student samples are not large by current standards for testing moderation, warranting caution. Experiment 2 was limited by class size to two treatment groups (no neutral control), so firm conclusions about the relative effects of fixed versus growth manipulations cannot be drawn. The Field Study did not involve experimental treatment and conflates partner choice with partner satisfaction, and the studies capture relatively short-term interactions, raising questions about long-term effects.

**Future Research**
Future work should test whether LIR effects endure over time and shape relationships over longer horizons, and whether LIR affect other relational outcomes such as network multiplexity. More research is needed on the antecedents of lay theories — why some people become fixed rather than growth theorists — including how organizational culture and diversity climate shape employees' LIR. Studies could also examine LIR across non-instrumental relations and the limits of how much deep-level dissimilarity growth theories can overcome.
