# The Relational Dynamics of Issue-Selling: Enacting Different Genres for Dealing with Discontent

**Abstract**
Actors without formal power can initiate and shape organizational change through issue-selling as a means to address their concerns about organizational practices. The literature has emphasized the need for perspective-taking and found discontent to be associated with inhibiting fear. Based on a longitudinal field study of middle managers trying to instigate strategic renewal in six different manufacturing companies, we find that discontent can also energize issue-selling, particularly if sellers and -recipients also engage in perspective-taking. We identify three different genres of issue-selling and enacting discontent that are jointly shaped by sellers and -recipients: (a) productive confrontation, (b) avoiding escalation, and (c) collective moaning. In a process model of issue-selling as framing, mobilizing, and pursuing, we illustrate how the genres form part of an organization's collective repertoire of initiating and responding to change. Our findings have implications for pursuing change, as well as for managers seeking to create a constructive environment for expressing discontent.

**Research Question**
How do issue-sellers and -recipients jointly shape how discontent is enacted or circumnavigated over time? The study investigates how less powerful actors pursue strategic-change initiatives and whether discontent serves as a motivational driver or a hampering force for issue-selling.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: discontent (cognitive disagreement paired with emotional commitment to an issue tied to one's values or professional identity); perspective-taking; relational history (power distance, prior conflicts, quality of previous encounters)
- Process / phases: framing → mobilizing → pursuing, with recipients' responses shaping each trajectory
- Genres (emergent patterns): (a) productive confrontation — framing as far-future opportunity, mobilizing by explicating/openly challenging, pursuing by escalating concerns, recipients acknowledging; (b) avoiding escalation — framing as near-future technical renewal, mobilizing by foreshadowing, pursuing by implicit fighting, recipients deferring; (c) collective moaning — framing as avoiding past errors, mobilizing by indirect alluding/generic complaints, pursuing by appeasing, recipients leaving open
- Boundary conditions: low power distance and constructive relational history enable productive confrontation; high power distance and fear yield avoiding escalation; accumulated frustration and pseudo-egalitarian distance yield collective moaning

The authors theorize issue-selling as a relational, collectively enacted process rather than an individual or dyadic act. Whether discontent energizes or paralyzes change depends not only on the seller's moves but on the jointly enacted genre and the recipient's response: supportive acknowledgment fosters agency and productive confrontation, whereas double-bind or indifferent responses foster fear, frustration, and collective helplessness. Reframing DeCelles et al.'s (2020) fight/flight/freeze responses as collective patterns, the genres function as an organization's shared repertoire for raising and responding to change.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive, longitudinal multiple-case field study embedded in a publicly funded, two-year interdisciplinary research project on the early phases of product innovation. The first author contacted eight companies as potential industrial partners through the research team's network of long-standing contacts with technical directors and senior innovators; partners granted access to observations and interviews in return for joining a partner network, using a novel digital visualization tool, and receiving feedback on their innovation processes. Two cases were excluded because access was gained late in the innovation process or only for a limited period, leaving the six cases analyzed (ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA, DELTA, EPSILON, ZETA; all firm and individual names are fictitious). In total the authors observed 108 participants, consisting of 75 product developers from mechanical engineering (n = 33), design (n = 19), and software and electrical engineering (n = 15), as well as 16 marketing and sales personnel, 14 managers, and 11 people from other functions such as after-sales and manufacturing. Each initiative was followed for 7–12 months. Meetings were the primary data source, treated as naturally occurring "episodes of contact" (Raes et al., 2011) between innovators and senior managers; the first author was present at all meetings, in most cases as a nonparticipant observer and in two (BETA and ZETA) also as coorganizer and external facilitator, taking notes on the issues discussed and on the emotional quality of the discussions. Meetings were video-recorded with a single mobile camera positioned unobtrusively at the back of the room — a cinematic choice that yielded an outsider perspective on the team as a whole at the cost of close-range facial detail — giving between 10 and 48 hours of video per case (Table 1). Shorter meetings of two to three hours were transcribed verbatim in full; for workshops of one or several days, a field-note overview of the meeting was used to identify critical situations in the video for transcription. Because meetings were largely convened in a set of regional dialects, transcription involved translating the spoken word into the corresponding standard language, which the authors note creates a more formal account than the actual verbal interaction. Supplementary data: numerous informal conversations with participants prior to, during, and after meetings; partners' presentations of their innovation-process experiences at project network meetings; face-to-face semi-structured follow-up interviews with the project leader and one or two key team members per initiative, conducted 12 to 15 months after the observations and recorded and transcribed verbatim; and company documents (meeting minutes, records of internal presentations, product catalogs) as background. Constructs were built inductively rather than measured, over several rounds of analysis: (1) the first author created case descriptions from transcripts, field notes, and documents and validated these with the participants; (2) the second author joined and analysis was conducted collaboratively, with only the first author — the sole person familiar with the participants' language and dialects — handling the raw data while the second author acted as a sparring partner for testing interpretations, after which the analysis zoomed in on issue-selling strategies and the response from recipients and the transcripts were coded accordingly; (3) the authors returned to the original video recordings for fine-grained analysis of verbal and nonverbal interaction in the specific episodes that had emerged as prominent instances of issue-selling. The coded unit is the episode, defined as a sequence of interactive turns between issue-seller(s) and -recipients about a specific issue, from the moment the issue was first mentioned until it was either resolved or the discussion moved on, ranging from a few minutes to intense discussions of an hour. Because facial expressions could not be analyzed for all members at all times, emotion was read from tone of voice, bodily movements, and verbal cues, and the absence of any public display of emotion was itself noted where participants had shared their frustration privately. Cross-case comparison and replication (Langley, 2009; Langley et al., 2013), together with analysis of potential turning points in the dynamics, produced the three patterns labeled "productive confrontation," "avoiding escalation," and "collective moaning" (Table 2 sets out the moves and responses with exemplar quotes; Table 3 the per-case trajectories, outcomes, and relational and organizational impacts), which were mapped over time against what the authors had learned about the relational history between sellers and recipients. Interpretations were consolidated in several rounds of analysis and communicative validation with different academic and practitioner audiences. The design is interpretive and process-based: there are no variables, no statistical estimation, and no causal identification strategy.

**Key Findings**
Discontent both energized and hampered issue-selling, and which it did depended not on the seller's moves alone but on the genre jointly enacted with recipients against a particular relational history — power distance, underlying conflicts, and the quality of previous encounters. Three genres emerged, each fairly consistent over time within the same organization, and the authors reinterpret them as collective versions of DeCelles et al.'s (2020) fight, flight, and freeze responses. (1) Productive confrontation ("fight"; ALPHA and BETA, both low power distance — ALPHA with previous successful collaboration and a strong sense of agency, BETA an experimental space open to collaboration with a new team and limited previous encounters): issues were framed as an opportunity for radical change in the far future with an emphasis on shared goals, recipients initially responded with support for the innovative ambition, sellers mobilized by explicating strategic ambitions and openly challenging each other, and pursued by escalating concerns about resources; recipients acknowledged the issue and shared the concern, and both sides engaged in perspective-taking. The confrontations were at times hurtful, but productive: in ALPHA the resource issue was acknowledged and, after further selling and substantiating up the hierarchy, the CEO recognized the initiative's relevance and granted additional resources, with the novel product-service system realized in collaboration with an established third party and the sellers' sense of agency and professional values strengthened; in BETA recipients acknowledged the idea's potential but deselected it as too radical for a medium-sized firm, so the initiative was terminated by mutual agreement with no hard feelings, the sellers dropped the idea without building up frustration, and the firm adopted the methodology of involving externals. Productive confrontation therefore does not mean the initiative is approved — one of the two cases ended in termination. (2) Avoiding escalation ("flight"; GAMMA and DELTA, high power distance with previous autocratic responses and fear of openly challenging or humiliating those in power): issues were framed as renewal for the near future or as factual, technical problems, recipients cast a dilemma of contradictory expectations — encouraging ambition while imposing corporate or local constraints — sellers mobilized by foreshadowing future problems and translating their own goals into the recipients' concerns rather than explicating them, and pursued through implicit fighting and bargaining for resources; recipients responded by deferring the resource decision, in DELTA with an autocratic outburst of anger to enforce obedience. Neither side engaged in sufficient perspective-taking and the rational arguments were not paired with genuine understanding of the other side, so the issues went unresolved: at GAMMA strategic indecisiveness continued, the governance structure the middle managers had built was abandoned, sellers were disenfranchised, two middle managers quit, and the company returned to a less innovative brand image and incremental innovation of existing products; at DELTA the joint collaborative effort failed, technical projects were shelved, and the merger collapsed soon afterward with massive time and cost overruns. The authors present this as a surprising result: the deferential, rationalistic approach prior issue-selling work recommends was the less successful genre in this sample. (3) Collective moaning ("freeze"; EPSILON and ZETA, medium and pseudo-egalitarian power distance — low enough to voice concerns, too high to address the underlying problem — with accumulated frustration from divided interests or previous failure): issues were framed as avoiding errors from past innovation projects and were publicly known but met with indifference, sellers mobilized through unspecified generic complaints, indirect alluding, and cynical collective self-depreciation (Weeks's 2004 "depreciations"), and pursued through appeasing and minimizing their own concerns; recipients left the issue open, acknowledging concerns but reframing them as a "typical problem" of a matrix structure with no action agreed. This created a shared sense of the problem but no concrete action plan, so issues went unresolved and participants experienced futility and learned helplessness ("We from R&D recognize what needs to be done, but we can't do anything about it"): at EPSILON questions about the current initiative and the overall portfolio remained open, one issue-seller quit, and engineering capacity was added by head-hunting an external team while tensions between the sales organizations persisted; at ZETA the sellers became disenfranchised but stayed on, additional R&D team members were hired and also became frustrated, and the new product line was developed and launched although the marketing department did not support the digitized process, hampering a successful start. Collective moaning is reported as a previously unreported phenomenon, and it actively perpetuated the situation the sellers were discontented about — actors could get their quarrels off their chest, creating shared misery without confronting the underlying issues. Moaning could be interrupted, by a facilitator redirecting attention to the organizational problems or by sellers explicating their concerns, but such breakaways from the genre were temporary and would need repeating to change the conversation. Across the three genres the trajectory ran framing → mobilizing → pursuing, with recipients' responses at each step producing either a resolution, a downfall, or a futile continuation, which in turn created the relational fabric for the next encounter. No switching between genres was observed, which the authors read as evidence that selling strategies and response patterns are empirically confounded because they mutually shape each other: supportive responses foster agency and encourage productive confrontation, whereas double-bind and indifferent responses foster fear, frustration, and collective helplessness. The prior literature's finding that discontent is associated with fear that hampers effective issue-selling was confirmed, but only in the cases whose relational history combined high power distance with a collective framing for moderate renewal rather than radical change.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to the issue-selling literature by showing that discontent can be a motivational force for change—conditional on a relational history of collective problem-solving and shared repertoires of open conflict resolution—rather than merely a fear-inducing inhibitor. It conceptualizes issue-selling as genres: collectively shared, recognizable patterns of framing, mobilizing, and pursuing that sellers and recipients jointly enact and reenact, demonstrating that the success of issue-selling cannot be understood from the seller's perspective alone. Collective moaning is identified as a previously unreported phenomenon.

**Practical Implication**
Issue-sellers can diagnose their context: those in low-power-distance settings are best served by framing issues as future opportunities, articulating explicitly, and daring to escalate; those facing high power distance should "read the wind," engage more actively in perspective-taking, and avoid self-depreciation. Managers should recognize that their reactions shape outcomes—acknowledging concerns and following up with clear decisions retains valuable staff, whereas deferring and leaving things open alienates issue-sellers. Facilitators can help teams focus on the shared object and address implicit fighting and moaning.

**Limitations**
Given the inductive design, the researchers could not know in advance how trajectories would unfold or what to look for, which they compensated for by returning to rich video recordings. Interviews focused on issue-sellers, so the subjective experience of recipients—who also play a key role—was underexamined. Spreading data collection across several companies strengthens generalization but means empirical differences between companies can have diverse causes, since the firms differed in innovation practices, scope, team composition, and senior-management roles.

**Future Research**
Future work should include recipients' subjective experience and emotional involvement in issue-selling trajectories. Researchers should examine whether the selling strategies and responses of the three genres are always empirically confounded, and test in experimental or intervention studies whether more successful strategies and responses can improve collective outcomes (e.g., counteracting moaning by explicating content and resource issues). Because the study focused on new product development, future research should further investigate the temporal dynamics of issue-selling in different contexts.
