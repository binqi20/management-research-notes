# With Head and Heart: How Emotions Shape Paradox Navigation in Veterinary Work

**Abstract**
Emotions are part of everyday life, and paradox scholars have long acknowledged that navigating paradox—that is, opposite, interrelated, and enduring tensions—is inherently emotional. Yet, how such emotions shape the ways in which individuals navigate paradoxes remains unclear. Drawing on an in-depth qualitative study of 63 veterinarians facing the business–care paradox, I develop an emotionally informed model of how individuals navigate paradox at work. I find that emotions: (a) make paradox salient by surfacing a personal connection to paradoxical tensions, (b) trigger flexible emotional labor while responding to paradox, and (c) leave emotional traces that foster ongoing learning. In so doing, I surface how emotions shape the way paradoxes are recognized and experienced. My model invites paradox scholars to consider the centrality of emotions in paradox navigation, thus moving beyond emotions as something to be avoided and instead embracing their generative potential. Foregrounding emotions offers rich theoretical opportunities for future work.

**Research Question**
How do emotions shape the ways in which individuals navigate paradox? The study examines this through veterinarians confronting the business–care paradox—the opposing yet interrelated business and care goals embedded in their work.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: The business–care paradox, made salient each time a client interaction pits profitability against the care of a pet.
- Phase 1 — Emotional salience of paradox: Social bonds (perceived deservingness of pet owners) surface social emotions (empathy, compassion, guilt), creating a personal connection to paradoxical tensions and an impetus to consider both sides.
- Phase 2 — Flexible emotional labor between demands: Vets intuit "room to maneuver" and either justify downplaying or justify following emotions, opting for cost or care; organizational billing norms influence the direction.
- Phase 3 — Emotional traces following response: Decisions leave emotional fatigue (focus on cost) or emotional energy (focus on care) that linger after the consultation.
- Phase 4 — Ongoing learning from emotions: Traces drive vets to construct "workable barriers" that block fatigue while preserving exceptions, enabling learning over repeated exposure.
- Influencing factors / boundary conditions: Social bond between vet and client; perceived strictness of organizational billing norms.

Pradies theorizes paradox navigation as an emotionally fueled process rather than a purely cognitive or rational act. Emotions make paradox personally felt (not merely "seen" cognitively), and intuition—a swift, unconscious process—drives flexible iteration between competing demands. The emotional traces left by each response accumulate into learning, so that over time individuals adjust their workable barriers to balance energy and fatigue while sustaining engagement with both poles.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive grounded-theory study (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Locke, 2001) of veterinarians in France navigating the business–care paradox; constructs were built inductively from the data rather than measured, and the design is interpretive — there are no variables, no estimation, and no causal identification strategy. Data corpus: semi-structured interviews with 63 veterinarians, plus follow-up interviews in 2019 with roughly half of the initial informants, for a total of 93 interviews; approximately 239 hours of overt, nonparticipant observation (Pratt & Sala, 2021) inside clinics, where the author shadowed nearly all the vets she interviewed and, with informant permission, recorded 73% of consultations using a camera (later a tablet) on a tripod or a voice recorder on the vet's desk; and archival data from the French veterinary press (Semaine Vétérinaire, Dépêche Vétérinaire, the Medical Board Journal), which was deliberately not coded to answer the research question and used only to understand the French veterinary context. Sampling used elements of theoretical sampling, starting from an "open sampling" strategy to retain theoretical flexibility; among the vets interviewed, 59% were partners, 30% employees, and 6% freelance professionals; average tenure was 14 years (range 1–49); 37% were men and 63% women; 70% worked exclusively with pets and small animals. Interviews lasted 70–80 minutes on average, were conducted in French, and were recorded and transcribed verbatim, with only relevant quotes translated into English. The analytic corpus was narrowed via a summary table of consultations: among 195 observations and 123 consultations recalled in interviews, 151 instances were identified in which clients had little economic means and business–care tensions surfaced (53 observed, 98 recalled); of these, 79 instances carried coded signs of emotional arousal (17 observed, 62 recalled), spread across 53 vets in 12 clinics, and those 79 were the primary focus of inquiry. The remaining tension instances showed no evidence of emotional arousal and were excluded from the formal analysis, serving instead as contrast cases. The focal construct — social emotions arising in interaction (Zietsma & Toubiana, 2018) — was operationalized through the signs catalogued in Table 1: nonverbal emotional display in interactions (facial expression, prosodic features such as pitch, volume, lengthened vowels, pauses and hesitations, and gestures and posture) and emotional statements while recalling exchanges (evaluative comments and quoted speech, in which a vet speaks as if she were the client). Coding proceeded in four steps in the Gioia et al. (2013) tradition: (I) open coding of interviews and field notes, plus a thick description per observed vet joining interview and observation transcripts, to identify consultations involving emotions; (II) refining first-order concepts, yielding codes such as "feeling that one's action may be causing harm and distress to the pet and pet owner" and "putting oneself in the shoes of the pet owner"; (III) integrating first-order concepts into second-order themes by iterating between empirical themes and relevant literature on social emotions, emotional labor (Hochschild, 1983), and emotional traces (Rivera, 2015) — with the author coining her own label, "intuiting room to maneuver regarding the balance between demands," because the prevalent intuition codes were not reflected in existing studies; and (IV) building a theoretical model, which produced four aggregate dimensions (Figure 2), with the full first-order/second-order/aggregate data structure in Figure 1 and exemplary quotes per theme in Table 2. Validity procedures were triangulation of observational and interview data (Jick, 1979), regular meetings with external colleagues to discuss first-order concepts and emerging constructs to reduce single-coder bias, and member checks in 2019 in which informants confirmed that the model accurately represents their experiences.

**Key Findings**
The study's central finding is that emotions make paradox salient by surfacing a personal connection to paradoxical tensions, trigger flexible emotional labor while responding to paradox, and leave emotional traces that foster ongoing learning — assembled into an emotionally informed model of paradox navigation with four aggregate dimensions.

Emotional salience of the paradox. Clinics had routine protocols for cost–care dilemmas (payment in multiple installments, or a lower level of care for less money), but these protocols were not always used in consultations where social emotions surfaced. The social bond between vet and client — manifested as the perceived deservingness of pet owners and pets, judged from a pleasant demeanor, loyalty to the clinic, or a strong connection with the animal — influenced whether vets experienced social emotions in the form of empathy, compassion, or guilt; vets said they would not "go out of their way" for clients they perceived as undeserving, and made the "strict minimum" effort for vindictive ones. Where those emotions arose, they surfaced a personal connection to tensions: vets described feeling attached to care and upset at the organizational routine focused on cost, and feeling personal ownership of the cost–care problem, treating the paradox as their own issue to solve rather than the client's. The contrast cases sharpen this: consultations without signs of emotional arousal ran much like nonparadox consultations — staying calm, asking diagnostic questions, no hesitation during payment — and there the client was left holding responsibility for the cost.

Flexible emotional labor between demands. Experiencing salience emotionally in turn led vets into flexible emotional labor that was fundamentally intuitive rather than deliberative: they intuited "room to maneuver," relying on gut feeling ("So much is based on how I feel about it in my gut") and on balancing care and cost on average across clients over a day, month, or year. The direction of the response tracked that intuition. When vets intuitively sensed room to maneuver, they justified following emotions — emphasizing that they were giving away their personal time, or emphasizing their values and humanity — and decided to focus on care, providing discounted or free services. When they sensed little room to maneuver, they justified downplaying emotions — emphasizing the value of their professional work and expertise, and that care comes with costs — and decided to focus on cost, billing in line with the organization's guidelines. Perceived organizational billing norms shaped the direction: where norms were perceived as strict, vets tended to justify downplaying their emotions; where perceived as loose, vets were more inclined to follow them. Neither intuition nor the organizational setting alone dictated the outcome, and the justificatory work was "far from linear" — one vet reported having "justifications that go both ways" and falling back into the paradox.

Emotional traces following response. Responses left feelings that lingered after the consultation ended. A focus on cost left emotional fatigue: vets described feeling drained when reducing care to focus on cost, and feeling fatigued when clients suffered as a result of expensive care they had chosen. A focus on care left emotional energy: vets described feeling energized when focusing on care and a spur of relief when prioritizing care over cost.

Ongoing learning from emotions. Those traces became a source of learning. Eager to avoid fatigue, vets realized the importance of constructing protective barriers — "armor myself a little more," a "warning signal" to "get back to your role" — while also learning to remain open to emotions by emphasizing exceptions, because occasional care-focused consultations were energizing and reconnected them to why they chose the profession ("those exceptions are important, as long as they stay exceptions"). The resulting "workable barrier" holds a double balance: too much protection forgoes energizing experiences, too little risks overexposure to fatiguing ones and to financial problems. Over time individuals adjust their workable barriers from repeated exposure to paradoxes, so learning to navigate paradox entails reaching a dynamic equilibrium not only between responses but also between emotional traces. The author is explicit that some links are suggested rather than demonstrated: the feedback from decision patterns over time into the intuitive process, and from the workable barrier back into experiencing social emotions, are drawn as gray dashed arrows indicating relationships the findings suggest but do not fully show, and the "decision to focus on cost" and "decision to focus on care" boxes are implicit in the vets' emotional labor rather than separately evidenced.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study enriches the microfoundations of paradox theory by explaining how emotions shape both the salience of paradox and individuals' responses to it. It theorizes an affective route to salience (driven by a desire to sustain social bonds), positions intuition as the key driver of flexible responses, and extends Smith's (2014) dynamic-equilibrium model to show that learning to navigate paradox entails balancing emotional traces, not just responses. More broadly it answers calls to address the dominant rational focus of paradox scholarship by foregrounding emotionality alongside rationality.

**Practical Implication**
Practitioners benefit from being attuned to their emotions when confronting paradoxical tensions, since learning to navigate paradox involves reaching a dynamic equilibrium between emotional traces (energy and fatigue), and building a "workable barrier" should be a conscious act. Management educators should highlight emotional awareness and intelligence as key skills in paradox navigation.

**Limitations**
As with other qualitative studies, the single French veterinary context raises questions about generalizability, though the dynamics may transfer to other professional settings where occupational values are pitted against business imperatives. Findings are likely most applicable to organizational members with high autonomy. It is also difficult to verify whether consultations categorized as lacking social emotions were truly devoid of emotion or whether emotions were repressed.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine how varying levels of emotional intensity produce different levels of salience over time, explore emotions and paradox at the organizational level (e.g., via multiple case studies of emotion norms), and investigate how individual backgrounds, work orientations, or affective commitments shape emotional experiences of paradox.
