# Inside-Out, Outside-In, or All-in-One? The Role of Network Sequencing in the Elaboration of Ideas

**Abstract**
The structure of advice and support networks within organizations has a profound impact on the elaboration of novel ideas. We explore how the sequence in which individuals expose ideas to their network contacts affects their innovation performance. We argue that, during idea elaboration, inside-out network sequencing—that is, mobilizing input and support from inner-circle ties before outer-circle ones—yields an innovation performance advantage over outside-in network sequencing and all-in-one mobilization of network contacts. Inside-out network sequencing generates valuable early feedback and support from inner-circle ties that actively engage with ill-defined, ill-structured, and uncertain ideas, and delays exposure to outer-circle ties until ideas can better withstand criticism from beyond the social circle where they emerged. We further contend that the benefits of inside-out network sequencing are amplified in environments that lack support for innovation. Using an analysis of survey data and archival innovation performance records for 301 research and development scientists and engineers in a large multinational firm, we find support for our predictions.

**Research Question**
How does the sequence in which R&D scientists and engineers mobilize feedback and support from colleagues for the elaboration of novel ideas affect their innovation performance? In particular, does mobilizing inner-circle ties before outer-circle ones ("inside-out" sequencing) outperform "outside-in" or "all-in-one" approaches, and is this advantage stronger in less supportive work environments?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1. Inside-out network sequencing in idea elaboration is positively associated with individual innovation performance.
- Hypothesis 2. The association between inside-out network sequencing and innovation performance is stronger when individuals operate in less supportive work environments.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Degree of inside-out network sequencing (IONS), an index combining the dispersion of tie mobilization across the four idea-elaboration stages and the consistency of mobilizing inner-circle (high shared-third-party) ties before outer-circle (low shared-third-party) ties.
- DV(s): Individual innovation performance rating (three ordered bands from Neptune's annual review); robustness uses a self-reported count of projects reaching the implementation stage-gate.
- Mediators: None tested (the paper notes it does not directly examine why sequencing differences arise).
- Moderators: Four indicators of a less supportive innovation environment — emphasis on accountability (over autonomy), leaders' lack of innovation support, lack of organizational slack, and reluctance to cannibalize existing assets.

Inner-circle ties (those sharing many third-party connections with the idea creator) bring greater commitment and information redundancy, so they patiently digest ill-defined, ill-structured early-stage ideas, supply candid constructive feedback, and surface logical inconsistencies before creators grow wedded to flawed directions. Exposing immature ideas to outer-circle ties too early risks dismissive reactions and "false negatives," whereas these nonredundant outsiders become valuable later, once ideas are robust enough to withstand external scrutiny and to validate fit with the wider organization. Sequencing inner-before-outer therefore lets individuals secure early feedback while shielding fragile ideas, mitigating the dual risk of dropping good ideas or persisting with bad ones. The contrast between circles—and hence the payoff to careful sequencing—sharpens in unsupportive contexts where outer-circle skepticism is most damaging.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from one large technology-intensive Fortune-500 multinational R&D organization (anonymized as "Neptune"). Three online surveys were sent to all 622 senior R&D scientists and engineers (61% response, n = 370), 969 R&D managers (43%), and 80 R&D directors (46%); responses were linked to Neptune archival HR records and individual innovation performance ratings via an algorithm converting respondent emails to anonymous IDs. After dropping cases with incomplete data on the study variables, the analytic sample is 301 scientists and engineers (48% of the original population). Individuals with higher innovation ratings were overrepresented relative to the full population, so population weights are included in the regressions (results are robust without weights). Exploratory interviews with 40 R&D scientists/engineers and 30 R&D managers preceded and informed the survey design.

DV: innovation performance is Neptune's annual performance review rating, in which a committee of line managers compares performance-review documents within rank and allocates individuals to one of three bands under a forced distribution; ratings were obtained six months after the survey. A robustness DV is the self-reported number of projects in the prior year that reached the implementation stage-gate (mean = 1.12, SD = 1.11), available for a subset (n = 212). IV: inside-out network sequencing (IONS), an index with two components — dispersion, the extent to which tie mobilization is spread across the four idea-elaboration stages (Stirling, 2007), and consistency (0-1), whether ties sharing more third parties are mobilized before ties sharing fewer. Network data came in three steps: four name-generator questions soliciting up to 11 contacts, a name-interpreter question (rank, division, communication frequency), and an alter-alter matrix. Inner- versus outer-circle status is operationalized structurally as f_ij, the fraction of i's ties also held by j (respondents averaged 2.3 shared third-party ties, SD = 1.63); relational tie strength is not measured. Timing comes from a scenario-based question asking, for each listed contact, at which of four stages of idea elaboration — wording adapted from NASA technology-readiness levels, from "Almost immediately after first getting the idea" to "After validation in a lab/trial environment" — the respondent would first disclose a recent idea. Moderators: emphasis on accountability (own scale; four-item accountability factor from the R&D managers' survey, α = .61, averaged by division and site); lack of innovation support (six-item supervisory-support scale adapted from Greenhaus, Parasuraman, and Wormley, 1990, α = .92, reverse-coded and averaged across the division leaders nominated as critical decision-makers); lack of organizational slack (four-item Danneels, 2008 scale, α = .81, reverse-coded, from the R&D directors' survey); reluctance to cannibalize (five-item Chandy and Tellis, 1998 scale as adapted by Danneels, 2008, α = .70, reverse-coded, R&D directors). Controls: relative speed to promotion, junior rank, job tenure, managerial support, research-portfolio time horizon, gender, intrinsic motivation, Burt network constraint, network prominence, and sectoral dummies.

Estimation is an ordered probit of the three-band rating (a Wald test rejected the parallel-regression assumption, χ2 = 35.92, p < .001), with standard errors clustered by rank because ratings follow a forced distribution within seniority ranks, and with IONS and the moderators mean-centered. The paper claims no causal-identification strategy: the design is associational, reporting associations between survey-measured sequencing and performance ratings collected six months later, and the scenario-based measure captures expected rather than actual mobilization behavior.

**Key Findings**
Hypothesis 1 is supported. In Model 2 the coefficient for inside-out network sequencing is positive and significant (β = 0.63, p < .01); an increase from its mean to 1 SD above the mean raises the probability of receiving the highest innovation rating by 9.3% (from 0.47 to 0.51) and reduces the probability of the lowest rating by 7.6% (from 0.28 to 0.24). The effect holds over and above network constraint, which is itself negatively associated with individual performance. The raw bivariate pattern is weaker and not linear: bottom-rated individuals show lower IONS (0.461) than middle- and top-rated ones (0.489, t = 1.289, p = .099), while bottom- and middle-rated (0.470) do not differ significantly from top-rated (0.485, p = .289).

Hypothesis 2 is supported for all four indicators of a less supportive work environment, added as interaction terms in turn across Models 3-6: IONS × emphasis on accountability (β = 1.83, p < .01), IONS × leaders' lack of innovation support (β = 0.42, p < .001), IONS × lack of organizational slack (β = 1.01, p < .001), and IONS × reluctance to cannibalize (β = 1.02, p < .001). The paper states two range restrictions on the marginal effects. For lack of innovation support, the difference in the predicted probability of a top rating between supportive and unsupportive contexts is significant only where IONS is low (< 0.35; 20% of the sample), which the authors read as a performance penalty for not sequencing inside-out rather than an amplified benefit of doing so, since the benefits of high IONS are similar in supportive and unsupportive contexts. For reluctance to cannibalize, the moderation is significant only for individuals with higher IONS (> 0.35; 80% of the sample).

Robustness: a Poisson model on the alternative count DV (projects reaching the implementation stage-gate, n = 212) also yields a positive IONS coefficient (β = 0.36, p < .001), a 1 SD increase raising the predicted count by 15.6%. Entered separately, both index components are positive and significant (dispersion β = 0.33, p < .001; consistency β = 0.32, p = .002). Hypothesis 1 continues to hold when controlling for the previous year's self-reported rating (β = 0.84, p < .001, n = 251) and when junior R&D professionals are excluded (β = 0.83, p < .001). When IONS is recomputed weighting shared third-party ties by communication frequency, all results remain substantively unchanged except the moderating effect of reluctance to cannibalize — the one reported result that is not robust to that respecification.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study introduces "network sequencing" as a temporal, behavioral dimension of networks distinct from network structure and position, showing that *when* individuals involve which contacts—not just *whom* they are connected to—explains differences in innovation performance over and above network constraint. It connects to and extends research on strategic networking behaviors by demonstrating that creative individuals outperform peers by making "savvy choices of who to involve when." Third, it shows that network mechanisms such as feedback and support are time- and alter-dependent and may backfire if mobilized at the wrong time, unpacking idea elaboration into substages in which strong/inner and weak/outer ties play different roles.

**Practical Implication**
Because network sequencing matters yet many individuals make poor mobilization choices, organizations should inform and train R&D talent to deliberately sequence their networks—seeking early input from committed inner-circle ties to nurture ideas in "safe spaces" before exposing them to outer-circle scrutiny, while avoiding cleaving too closely to the inner circle and engaging outer-circle ties once ideas mature. Such training is especially valuable in units lacking innovation support, where engaging outsiders too early is costly; careful management of slack, autonomy/accountability balance, cannibalization concerns, and supportive leadership can each reduce the risk of squandering creative talent.

**Limitations**
The study is based on a single organization with relatively homogeneous HR practices, limiting generalizability, and it examines only intra-organizational networks. The scenario-based measure of mobilization timing captures expected rather than actual behavior and cannot establish whether the reported approach generalizes across different ideas, contexts, or situations. The innovation-performance measure has a short (single-year) time horizon, and inner/outer-circle ties are operationalized structurally (shared third parties) without directly observing tie strength; the paper also does not explore why sequencing differences arise.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine network sequencing in other organizational contexts and across organizational boundaries, and use more direct methods (e.g., diary-based monitoring) to capture how individuals seek feedback for different ideas. Researchers could disentangle tie strength from social cohesion in sequencing effects, study how idea radicalness and longer-horizon (radical) innovation call for different sequencing strategies, and investigate antecedents of sequencing choices such as confidence, political skill, personality, and cognition.
