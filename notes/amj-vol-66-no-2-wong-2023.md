# Pay Transparency as a Moving Target: A Multistep Model of Pay Compression, I-Deals, and Collectivist Shared Values

**Abstract**
Drawing from research on the transparency-privacy dilemma in management, we theorize that firm-level pay transparency elicits a multistep process involving managers and employees that shifts the dispersion in remuneration from more to less observable forms, thus making pay transparency a “moving target.” We posit a serial indirect effect of pay transparency on firm-level rates of i-deal grants (a less observable form of remuneration) via variable pay compression and heightened rates of employee i-deal requests, with this indirect effect amplified in firms characterized by collectivist shared values. First examining the role of managerial agency and collectivist shared values in the pay transparency–compression relationship in a simulation-based experiment, we test the overall model in a multisource field study using a sample of 111 medical device distribution firms. Our findings demonstrate that: (a) firm-level pay transparency is predictive of greater pay compression, (b) firm-level rates of i-deal grants are largely explained by this pay compression via its effects on employee i-deal requests, and (c) this sequential effect is amplified in firms with more collectivist shared values. Accordingly, we explicate how transparency triggers unintentional hiding, and suggest that accompanying more transparent pay may be an increased reliance upon rewards that, by their very nature, are less transparent.

**Research Question**
Whether, how, and when does firm-level pay transparency become a "moving target" — that is, through what multistep process does transparency lead managers and employees to shift remuneration from more observable forms (variable pay) to less observable ones (nonmonetary i-deals), and under what conditions is this process amplified?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The quantitative components test six numbered hypotheses (Hypothesis 5 is split into 5a and 5b), summarized in the Figure 1 theoretical model. Study 1 tested only Hypotheses 1 and 5a; Study 2 tested the full model (Hypotheses 1–6).
- Hypothesis 1. Firm-level pay transparency is positively associated with variable pay compression.
- Hypothesis 2. Variable pay compression is positively associated with firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests.
- Hypothesis 3. Firm-level pay transparency is indirectly associated with firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests via variable pay compression.
- Hypothesis 4. Firm-level pay transparency is indirectly associated with firm-level i-deal grants via variable pay compression and firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests.
- Hypothesis 5a. Firm-level collectivist shared values moderate the effect of firm-level pay transparency on variable pay compression, such that this positive relationship is amplified among firms characterized by more collectivist shared values.
- Hypothesis 5b. Firm-level collectivist shared values moderate the effect of variable pay compression on firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests, such that this positive relationship is amplified among firms characterized by more collectivist shared values.
- Hypothesis 6. Firm-level collectivist shared values moderate the indirect effect of firm-level pay transparency on firm-level rates of employee i-deal grants via variable pay compression and firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests, such that this positive indirect relationship is amplified among firms characterized by more collectivist shared values.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Firm-level pay transparency
- DV(s): Firm-level rates of nonmonetary i-deal grants (developmental and benefit)
- Mediators: Variable pay compression (serial step 1) → firm-level rates of employee i-deal requests (serial step 2)
- Moderators: Firm-level collectivist shared values

Building on Bernstein's (2017) transparency-privacy model, the authors argue that making pay more observable pressures managers to avoid controversy over visible pay differentials by compressing variable pay. Consistent with compensating wage differentials theory, this compression motivates employees to seek redress through less observable, particularistic rewards (i-deals), and those requests are in turn granted — thereby "hiding" reward dispersion in discreet forms. Drawing on social comparison theory, firms with more collectivist shared values heighten the salience of observability and social monitoring, amplifying the compression-to-i-deal-request stage of the serial indirect effect. The field study supported Hypotheses 1–4 and 6 (and 5b), while Hypotheses 5a was not supported.

**Data & Measures**
The dominant component is the quantitative Study 2 field study; Study 1 is a preregistered experiment supplying randomized evidence for the first link only.
- Study 1 (preregistered online business-simulation experiment): 117 managers in the final sample (120 recruited via Prolific, screened on managerial experience; two failed embedded attention checks and one reported suspicion) from the United States and United Kingdom. A 2 × 2 between-subjects design randomly assigned participants to pay communication policy (pay transparency vs. pay secrecy) crossed with shared values (collectivist vs. individualist), both delivered as written framing of a five-person online sales team in which every participant was in fact assigned the manager role. After viewing an identical set of first-round performance scores for four "subordinates" (one made a high performer), participants allocated a £10 performance-based bonus pool, so the resulting distribution was attributable entirely to their own decision-making. The dependent variable, variable pay compression, was operationalized as the additive inverse of variable pay dispersion, that is, the Gini coefficient (Donaldson & Weymark, 1980; Pfeffer & Langton, 1993) multiplied by −1.0, so that 0 indicates complete compression and −1.0 absolute inequality. Manipulation checks used a 6-item pay transparency scale (α = .88) tapping pay process, pay outcome, and pay communication transparency, and a 2-item collectivist shared values scale (α = .84); both manipulations were effective, with no transparency × collectivism interaction on either check. Estimation: 2 × 2 ANOVA. Syntax, data, and materials are posted at https://osf.io/nuvge/.
- Study 2 (multisource, firm-level field study): 645 salespeople nested in 111 medical device distribution firms in China, collected in two phases about one week apart. Owners or CEOs of 135 firms were surveyed first (111 participated, firm-level response rate 82.22%), then the 805 salespeople whose contact details they supplied (645 responded, 80.12%, with three to 15 employees per firm). All items were back-translated into Mandarin Chinese, and the measurement reference period for every endogenous variable was the past year. Firm-level pay transparency (employer-rated) used Arnold and colleagues' (2018) 12-item scale covering pay process, pay outcome, and pay communication transparency across four pay components, modeled as a second-order factor (CFI = .97, TLI = .96, RMSEA = .09, SRMR = .04). Variable pay compression (archival) was computed from employer-reported bonus plus incentive pay per employee in RMB, again as −1.0 × the Gini coefficient. Firm-level i-deal requests (employee-rated) used Ho and Tekleab's (2016) 5-item frequency scale (four developmental items, one benefit item; 1 = never to 5 = frequently), aggregated to the firm (developmental ICC1 = .27, ICC2 = .68; benefit ICC1 = .31, ICC2 = .73). Firm-level i-deal grants (employer-rated) used the same five items rated for extent granted in the past year (1 = not at all to 5 = to a great extent; developmental reliability .91). Firm-level collectivist shared values (employee-rated) used two items from Wagner and Moch's (1986) collectivist values scale aggregated under a direct consensus model (mean rwg = .71, ICC1 = .29, ICC2 = .70). Controls: number of employees, firm revenue, tenure diversity, age diversity, education diversity, gender diversity, mean organizational tenure and its quadratic term, and base pay compression. Estimation: a 2-2-1-2 multilevel structural equation model (Preacher, Zyphur, & Zhang, 2010) fitted to item-level data in Mplus 8.4 with full-information maximum likelihood, plus Monte Carlo tests of the indirect and conditional indirect effects with 20,000 replications.
On identification, the authors treat Study 1's random assignment as evidence that pay transparency drives managers' compression decisions, and state that because Study 2 applied a field study design no comparable causal assertion can be made about the association between variable pay compression and i-deals; that part of the model is associational. To probe reverse causation they also collected an independent cross-lagged sample of 69 medical device distribution firms in China, and Appendix A reports a supplementary experiment testing whether compression drives transparency.

**Key Findings**
Support is reported per study because the two studies tested different subsets of the model. The dominant Study 2 supported Hypotheses 1, 2, 3, 4, 5b, and 6 and rejected Hypothesis 5a; Study 1 supported Hypothesis 1 and did not support Hypothesis 5a.
- Study 2 (645 employees in 111 firms; 2-2-1-2 MSEM). Hypothesis 1 supported: firm pay transparency was positively related to variable pay compression (γ = .05, p = .010). Hypothesis 2 supported: variable pay compression was positively related to both developmental (γ = 1.08, p = .000) and benefit (γ = 1.14, p = .004) i-deal requests. Hypothesis 3 supported: the Monte Carlo indirect effects of pay transparency via compression were significant for developmental (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [.01, .11]) and benefit (indirect effect = .05, 95% CI [.01, .12]) i-deal requests. Hypothesis 4 supported: requests predicted grants (developmental γ = 1.54; benefit γ = .94; both p = .000) and the serial indirect effects were significant for developmental (.08, 95% CI [.01, .17]) and benefit (.05, 95% CI [.01, .11]) i-deal grants. Hypothesis 5a rejected: the latent pay transparency × collectivist shared values interaction was not related to variable pay compression (γ = −.03, p = .242). Hypothesis 5b supported: the variable pay compression × collectivist shared values interaction was positively related to developmental (γ = 1.56, p = .001) and benefit (γ = 1.86, p = .002) i-deal requests, with the compression-to-request slopes steeper in firms with more collectivist shared values. Hypothesis 6 supported: the serial indirect effect on developmental i-deal grants was .15 (95% CI [.03, .30]) at high versus .01 (95% CI [−.06, .07]) at low collectivist shared values, difference .14 (95% CI [.02, .32]); for benefit i-deal grants it was .10 (95% CI [.02, .21]) versus .00 (95% CI [−.06, .05]), difference .10 (95% CI [.02, .23]). In the firm-level descriptive statistics, pay transparency correlated significantly with variable pay compression (r = .20, p = .037), but its correlations with both developmental and benefit i-deal requests and grants were not significant. The indirect-effect model explained 18%, 20%, 13%, 61%, and 39% of the variance in variable pay compression, developmental requests, benefit requests, developmental grants, and benefit grants respectively. Results were essentially unchanged when the control variables were removed.
- Study 1 (117 managers; 2 × 2 ANOVA). Hypothesis 1 supported: managers in the pay transparency condition compressed subordinates' variable pay more (M = −.17, SD = .11) than those in the pay secrecy condition (M = −.27, SD = .16), F(1, 113) = 18.93, p = .000, η² = .14, power = .99, recalling that higher values indicate greater compression. Hypothesis 5a not supported: the pay transparency × collectivist shared values interaction was only marginally significant, F(1, 113) = 3.22, p = .076, η² = .03, power = .43, and the interaction pattern ran contrary to the prediction. The collectivist-versus-individualist difference in compression was significant under pay secrecy (M = −.21 vs. M = −.33, difference in means = .12, p = .005) but small and nonsignificant under pay transparency (M = −.15 vs. M = −.18, difference in means = .03, p = .804).
Convergence and divergence: the experiment and the field study converge on the first link, with Hypothesis 1 supported in both, and converge on the failure of the manager-side moderation, with Hypothesis 5a unsupported in both; the collectivist amplification instead appeared only at the employee-driven compression-to-request stage in Study 2. Supplementary analyses reported in the appendices found no support for an association between variable pay compression and employees' requests or managers' grants for higher pay (financial i-deals), and confirmed robustness to the full three-item collectivism measure and to a coefficient-of-variation dispersion measure. In the independent 69-firm cross-lagged sample, pay compression was not associated with subsequent pay transparency (γ = .40, p = .573), while pay transparency was positively associated with concurrent variable pay compression (γ = .03, p = .011).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends Bernstein's (2017) transparency-privacy theory from the individual to the firm level, explicating a previously undescribed multistep mechanism by which transparency indirectly (rather than intentionally) produces hiding of reward differentials. It contributes to i-deals research by identifying pay compression as a firm-level antecedent of i-deal requests and grants and by isolating collectivist shared values as a boundary condition that counterintuitively amplifies the transparency–privacy paradox.

**Practical Implication**
Because pay transparency can trigger unintended pay compression and a downstream rise in nonmonetary i-deal grants, leaders should anticipate these externalities — e.g., emphasizing to managers that justifiable pay differences benefit their own performance, and monitoring i-deal allocation so hidden rewards are not used as a "back door" for gender- and ethnicity-based disparities. Effects are especially pronounced in collectivist contexts, where managers should make pay policies more transparent and procedurally clear.

**Limitations**
Study 1's experimental design cannot rule out reciprocal causation between compression and transparency, and its short-term managerial role may not generalize to real-life decisions. Study 2 used field data from small firms in a single industry (medical device distribution) and a single occupation (salespeople) in one country, limiting generalizability to larger firms, other industries, and contexts where vertical/tournament pay dynamics matter.

**Future Research**
Future work should test the causal link between pay compression and i-deals via experimental or quasi-experimental designs, examine pay compression as a mechanism linking transparency to outcomes like collective performance and human-capital sorting, and pursue cross-industry and cross-cultural samples. Researchers could also unpack the dominant mechanism behind the collectivist amplification effect and study pay "noticeability" at the individual level.
