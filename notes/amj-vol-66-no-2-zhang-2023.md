# Learning Down to Train Up: Mentors Are More Effective When They Value Insights from Below

**Abstract**
Although mentorship is vital for individual success, potential mentors often view it as a costly burden. To understand what motivates mentors to overcome this barrier and more fully engage with their mentees, we introduce a new construct, “learning direction,” which captures the beliefs people have about which individuals within a hierarchy— upward, lateral, or downward—are valuable sources of knowledge. Although most mentors believe knowledge resides higher up in hierarchies, the current research demonstrates mentors are more engaged and effective when they value insights from below. Our first three studies found that downward learning predicted mentor engagement (Study 1) because downward learning-directed mentors viewed mentoring as a worthwhile opportunity for their own learning (Studies 2A and 2B). A field study examined downstream consequences on mentees’ learning outcomes, finding that downward learning-directed mentors trained more hireable mentees (Study 3). A final experiment established causality while also demonstrating that learning direction is open to intervention: reflecting on a downward learning experience increased mentor engagement, which enhanced the mentorship experience for their mentees (Study 4). We extend theories on learning orientation to learning direction and demonstrate that beliefs about the hierarchical sources of learning are critical to successful mentoring.

**Research Question**
What motivates potential mentors to engage with and be effective for their mentees? The paper asks whether mentors' "learning direction"—specifically the belief that those lower in a hierarchy are valuable sources of knowledge—predicts mentor engagement and mentoring effectiveness, above and beyond related constructs like learning orientation and growth mindset.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1a. The downward learning of mentors will positively predict their engagement (i.e., time invested) with their mentees.
H1b. Mentors' downward learning will positively predict viewing mentoring as a worthwhile opportunity to learn knowledge and skills from their mentees.
H1c. Perceiving mentoring as a worthwhile learning opportunity will mediate the relationship between mentors' downward learning and their engagement with their mentees.
H2a. The downward learning of mentors will positively predict mentee learning experiences and outcomes.
H2b. Increased mentoring engagement will explain the relationship between mentors' downward learning and mentees' learning.
Additional unnumbered prediction developed for Study 4: the downward learning manipulation will have a greater impact on mentor engagement and on the learning experiences and outcomes of mentees when mentors have greater positional power.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Mentor downward learning direction (measured in Studies 1-3; manipulated downward vs. upward in Study 4), with lateral and upward learning direction as comparison directions
- DV(s): Mentor engagement (time/questions answered, advice word count); mentee learning outcomes (technical skill, hireability in Study 3); mentee learning experience (perceived quality of received advice and support in Study 4)
- Mediators: Perceiving mentoring as a worthwhile opportunity to learn knowledge and skills (Studies 2A-2B); mentor engagement (Study 4; in Studies 2A-2B engagement is itself the dependent variable)
- Moderators: Mentor hierarchical position / positional power (Study 4)

Believing that people below oneself in a social hierarchy hold valuable knowledge reframes mentoring from a costly obligation into a two-way learning opportunity. This reframing raises mentors' engagement (the time and effort they invest), which in turn improves mentees' learning outcomes and their perceptions of advice and support. Because power tends to reduce perspective-taking, the downward-learning manipulation has its strongest effects for mentors high in positional power.

**Data & Measures**
- Data: five studies. Studies 1 (n = 499), 2A (n = 776), 2B (n = 400 at Time 1, 364 at Time 2), and 4 (439 mentors, averaging 7.55 years of industry experience) recruited full-time working professionals from Prolific Academic, while Study 4's separate mentee sample of 295 were "individuals seeking jobs" recruited on the same platform; Study 3 matched survey responses from mentors at "MissionCode," an online programing boot camp, to archival records of student performance (278 mentor-mentee pairs from 50 matched mentors).
- IV, learning direction (Studies 1-3): three parallel items per direction, rated 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree, e.g., "I learn a lot by interacting with those who have [LESS/the SAME amount of/MORE] power than I do"; downward-learning alphas were .87 (Study 1), .88 (Study 2A), .91 (Study 2B), and .84 for the abridged two-item version used in Study 3.
- IV, learning direction (Study 4): manipulated. Mentors wrote three to five sentences about "a time in which you learned from someone with less power than you" (downward condition) versus "more power than you" (upward condition).
- DV/mediator, mentor engagement: the number of a prospective mentee's questions (out of 20) that mentors said they would take the time to answer (Studies 1-2B); the word count of the advice message actually written to a mentee (Study 4).
- Mediator, mentoring as an opportunity to learn knowledge and skills: six items adapted from Ragins and Scandura (1999) (alpha = .85). A competing mediator, mentoring as an opportunity to learn social and cultural information, used five items (alpha = .87).
- DV, mentee learning outcomes (Study 3): hireability rated by a third-party mock interviewer who had never met the student (0 = "no," 1 = "maybe," 2 = "yes") and technical competence rated on the same interviews (1 = poor to 5 = excellent).
- DV, mentee learning experience (Study 4): mentees rated the quality of the advice received (overall quality, useful, clear, actionable, detailed, concrete; adapted from Zhang & North, 2020) and the support received (an adaptation of Edmondson's 1999 psychological-safety items plus encouraging/motivating/supportive), each collapsed into a single highly correlated dimension (alpha = .98).
- Moderator, mentor hierarchical position: a 1-10 ladder measure of positional power within the organization (Anderson et al., 2012).
- Controls: learning orientation (Vandewalle et al., 2001), growth mindset (Dweck, 2000), power distance (Farh et al., 2007), intellectual humility (Leary et al., 2017), Big Five traits (Lang et al., 2011), mentor self-perceived coding knowledge (Study 3), and demographics.

Estimation follows the design of each study: OLS with Tobit and Poisson specifications as robustness checks in Study 1, OLS with Tobit specifications in Studies 2A and 2B, multilevel ordinal logistic regression (hireability) and multilevel OLS (technical skill) with test and interviewer fixed effects plus mentee and mentor random effects in Study 3, and multilevel models treating each piece of advice and each mentee rater as random intercepts with bootstrapped mediation in Study 4. Only Study 4 is a randomized experiment, and the paper presents it as the study that "sought causal evidence"; the other four designs are associational. Study 2A collected all variables at one time point, which the authors note may have overinflated the relationship, motivating the pre-registered two-wave Study 2B in which predictors were measured at Time 1 and the outcome and mediators two weeks later. In Study 3, matches were based only on overlap in scheduling availability rather than self-selection, but the authors still acknowledge that the mentor-mentee relationship could run in the reverse direction.

**Key Findings**
- H1a supported in all three survey studies: downward learning positively predicted mentor engagement in Study 1 (b = 0.16, t(494) = 3.15, p = .002; b = 0.13, t(486) = 2.53, p = .01 with related constructs and demographics controlled), Study 2A (b = 0.20, t(772) = 4.71, p < .001; b = 0.15, t(760) = 3.52, p < .001 with controls), and Study 2B (b = 0.15, t(360) = 2.37, p = .019; b = 0.14, t(348) = 2.18, p = .03 with controls).
- Lateral learning never significantly predicted engagement in any of the three studies. Upward learning did so only in Study 1 before controls (b = 0.11, t(494) = 2.12, p = .03) and was no longer significant once the related constructs were added.
- H1b supported in both studies that tested it: downward learning positively predicted viewing mentoring as a worthwhile opportunity to learn knowledge and skills in Study 2A (b = 0.22, t(772) = 5.15, p < .001) and Study 2B (b = 0.32, t(360) = 5.57, p < .001), with the effect surviving controls in both.
- H1c supported in both studies: the indirect effect through perceiving mentoring as a worthwhile learning opportunity was significant in Study 2A (95% CI [0.02, 0.06]) and Study 2B (95% CI [0.05, 0.14]). The competing mediator, mentoring as a chance to learn social and cultural information, produced no significant indirect effect in either study.
- H2a supported for hireability but only marginally for technical skill in the Study 3 field data: mentors scoring higher on downward learning had more hireable mentees (B = 0.39, t = 3.63, p < .001, odds ratio = 1.47; a one-unit increase raised the odds of a mentee moving from "maybe" to "yes, I'd hire" by 72%), while downward learning only marginally predicted mentees' technical ability (B = 0.09, t = 2.11, p = .05). Neither upward nor lateral learning predicted either outcome.
- H2a and H2b supported experimentally in Study 4: the downward-learning manipulation increased mentor engagement (word count M = 35.78 vs. 29.33; b = 0.14, t(437) = 3.02, p = .003), mentees' ratings of advice quality (4.49 vs. 4.24; b = .08, t(435) = 2.57, p = .01), and mentees' ratings of received support (4.39 vs. 4.19; b = 0.07, t(435) = 2.09, p = .04), with significant bootstrapped indirect effects of mentor engagement on perceived advice ([0.06, 0.29]) and on perceived support ([0.08, 0.27]). The manipulation check was significant but small (M = 4.86 vs. 4.62, t(437) = 1.93, p = .05, d = .18).
- Moderation by mentor positional power appeared only in the experiment, not in the surveys. In Study 4 the manipulation interacted with hierarchical position for engagement (b = 0.10, t(435) = 2.21, p = .03), advice quality (b = 0.07, t(434) = 2.17, p = .03), and support (b = 0.11, t(434) = 3.38, p < .001), with simple slopes significant at +1 SD and null at -1 SD. Exploratory tests in Studies 2A and 2B found no significant moderation by mentors' positional power.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces "learning direction" as a new construct that shifts learning from a task-related construct (learning orientation) to a relational one—who one believes one can learn from across a hierarchy. It extends work on learning orientation and growth mindset (which focus on the self) by showing that downward learning predicts mentor engagement and effectiveness independent of those constructs, and integrates research on social hierarchy, mentoring, and advice to explain what makes mentors effective.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations typically motivate mentors by emphasizing benefits to mentees, which is insufficient because mentorship still feels like a costly obligation. Instead, they should highlight the learning value mentees offer to mentors. The research identifies an easy-to-implement intervention: having mentors reflect on times they learned from someone below them in a hierarchy shifts their learning direction and boosts engagement, especially for those in positions of power.

**Limitations**
In the field study (Study 3), although mentor-mentee matches were based only on scheduling availability, the authors acknowledge the relationship between mentors' downward learning and student outcomes could partly run in reverse, with mentee performance shaping mentors' reported learning direction. The field sample of matched mentors was small and predominantly male, motivating the controlled, gender-diverse experiment in Study 4.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine downward learning beyond mentoring (e.g., how leaders' downward learning affects their own and their team's productivity and innovation), and explore optimal learning directions across career stages and roles. Researchers are also encouraged to study the conversational-level process through which downward learning improves mentee outcomes—disentangling content of knowledge exchanged from relational rapport—and to extend learning direction to nontraditional sources of learning.
