# May I Please Go the Extra Mile? Citizenship Communication Strategies and Their Effect on Individual Initiative OCB, Work–Family Conflict, and Partner Satisfaction

**Abstract**
Organizational citizenship behavior (OCB) contributes to organizational effectiveness, but it can also create a burden for employees and their partners. In three studies, we examine how employees communicate with their partner about engaging in OCB and how this affects employees' OCB, their work–family conflict, and their partner's satisfaction. In Study 1, we use qualitative data to identify five ways employees communicate with their partner in order to engage in individual initiative OCB (e.g., staying late at work, volunteering for special assignments). In Study 2, using dual-concern theory, we hypothesize that self-concerned citizenship communication strategies will not only increase OCB, but also increase work–family conflict, and we predict that other-concerned communication strategies will reduce work–family conflict and increase partner satisfaction. Using survey data from employees and their partners, these hypotheses were partially supported. Finally, in Study 3, we use experimental vignette methodology to provide causal evidence that citizenship communication strategies influence partner satisfaction. Together, these studies increase our understanding of how employees communicate with their partner about OCB, indicate that there is no single communication strategy that has positive implications for both OCB and home life, and suggest that employees and their partners tend to think of OCB as involuntary for achieving family goals.

**Research Question**
How do employees communicate with their partner at home when engaging in individual initiative OCB, and how do these citizenship communication strategies affect the employee's OCB, their work–family conflict, and their partner's satisfaction?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Study 1 was inductive and advanced no a priori hypotheses. The three hypotheses below were developed for the Study 2 survey, and Hypothesis 3 was retested experimentally in Study 3.
- Hypothesis 1. The self-concerned citizenship communication strategies of (a) projecting payoffs and (b) invoking prior conversations are positively related to OCB.
- Hypothesis 2. Whereas the self-concerned citizenship communication strategies of (a) projecting payoffs and (b) invoking prior conversations are positively related to work–family conflict, the other-concerned communication strategies of (c) providing early notice, (d) seeking permission, and (e) negotiating logistics are negatively related to work–family conflict.
- Hypothesis 3. The other-concerned citizenship communication strategies of (a) providing early notice, (b) seeking permission, and (c) negotiating logistics are positively related to partner satisfaction.

The authors state that they do not specify a prediction regarding the use of self-concerned strategies in relation to partner satisfaction, and that they do not directly test dual concern theory itself; the theory is used as a framework for categorizing the strategies and generating these predictions.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Five citizenship communication strategies, classified by dual concern orientation. Self-concerned: projecting payoffs, invoking prior conversations. Other-concerned: providing early notice, seeking permission, negotiating logistics.
- DV(s): Individual initiative OCB (partner-rated); work–family conflict (employee + partner averaged); partner satisfaction (relationship satisfaction in Study 2; communication satisfaction in Study 3).
- Mediators: None modeled (the conceptual model focuses on main effects; the authors did not find evidence that strategies are used in combination).
- Moderators: None supported; gender was tested as a moderator in Studies 2 and 3 and OCB type (depleting vs. enriching) in Study 3, but no significant interactions emerged.

Drawing on dual concern theory from the conflict-management literature, the authors argue that self-concerned strategies emphasize the employee's own career goals and the rewards of OCB, whereas other-concerned strategies prioritize accommodating the partner. Self-concerned strategies should facilitate OCB but strain home life, while other-concerned strategies should reduce work–family conflict and raise partner satisfaction. Empirically, the trade-off is asymmetric: invoking prior conversations and projecting payoffs raise OCB (and invoking prior conversations raises work–family conflict; projecting payoffs lowers partner satisfaction), while seeking permission and providing early notice raise partner satisfaction but do not reduce work–family conflict and (for seeking permission) lower OCB.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (qualitative strategy discovery): 22 individuals from 11 married couples, purposively sampled from personal and professional networks against inclusion criteria (married; the individual or spouse employed full time in a knowledge-work industry; at least one child under age 10). Semi-structured, 10-question interviews of 45 to 60 minutes, digitally recorded and professionally transcribed. Two of the authors applied modified principles of constant comparative analysis (familiarization, then data reduction, then iterative open coding into categories) to derive the five strategy categories. Validation used member checking with three participants plus an expansion sample of three demographically divergent couples (n = 6), in which all five strategies appeared and no additional category could be identified.
- Study 2 (multi-source survey, the hypothesis-testing core): 192 employee–partner pairs recruited via Qualtrics panels, retained from 428 initial focal-employee responses after exclusions for missing partner data, missing employee data, and a speed check (7.5 minutes average per person). The five citizenship communication strategies (IVs) were measured with a new 25-item scale, five items per strategy, developed following Hinkin (1998), Colquitt et al. (2019), and Anderson and Gerbing (1991), employee-rated from 1 = "never" to 5 = "always" (alphas .89 to .93; a five-factor structure fit best in CFA). Individual initiative OCB was Bolino and Turnley's (2005) 15-item scale, rated by the employee's partner on a 5-point scale (alpha .93). Work–family conflict was Netemeyer et al.'s (1996) 5-item scale, completed by both employee and partner on 7-point scales (alphas .95 each, r = .73) and averaged to capture the couple's shared experience. Partner satisfaction was operationalized as relationship satisfaction using Norton's (1983) 6-item scale completed by the partner (alpha .97). Controls: employee gender, weekly hours worked, job tenure, relationship length, number of children, employee job satisfaction (Cammann et al., 1983), partner-rated employee communication competence (Arroyo & Segrin, 2011), and OCB itself when predicting work–family conflict and partner satisfaction. Estimation was hierarchical linear regression with one-tailed significance tests, supplemented by relative weights analysis. The design is cross-sectional and associational; the authors state they cannot draw definitive conclusions regarding causality.
- Study 3 (between-subjects experimental vignette): 922 employed participants recruited on Prolific (962 recruited, 40 excluded as not currently employed), resident in the United States, working at least 30 hours per week, and married or living with a partner, randomly assigned to 1 of 10 conditions (roughly 92 per condition) crossing the five communication strategies with a more depleting OCB (staying late at the office) and a more enriching OCB (attending a work-related dinner together). The manipulation was a hypothetical message received from the participant's partner. The dependent variable was partner communication satisfaction, measured with the five-face Kunin (1955) scale plus an item on how satisfied the participant would be receiving the message, on a 5-point scale (alpha .88). Realism checks (mean 4.61) and items on whether the focal OCB was discretionary were also collected. Analysis was ANOVA with Tukey's HSD comparisons.

**Key Findings**
Support was partial and the pattern differs by outcome and by study, so it is reported per study rather than pooled.
- Study 2 (OCB): Hypotheses 1a and 1b were both supported. Projecting payoffs (b = .15, p < .05) and invoking prior conversations (b = .25, p < .01) were positively related to partner-rated individual initiative OCB. Unhypothesized, seeking permission was negatively related to OCB (b = -.20, p < .01, one-tailed).
- Study 2 (work–family conflict): only Hypothesis 2b was supported, with invoking prior conversations positively related to work–family conflict (b = .18, p < .05). Hypothesis 2a was not supported (projecting payoffs, b = .00, ns), and none of the three other-concerned strategies reduced conflict, so Hypotheses 2c (providing early notice, b = -.04, ns), 2d (seeking permission, b = .13, ns), and 2e (negotiating logistics, b = .04, ns) were not supported.
- Study 2 (partner satisfaction): Hypotheses 3a (providing early notice, b = .23, p < .01) and 3b (seeking permission, b = .20, p < .05) were supported; Hypothesis 3c (negotiating logistics, b = -.02, ns) was not. Unhypothesized, projecting payoffs significantly decreased partner relationship satisfaction (b = -.20, p < .05, one-tailed).
- Study 2 (relative weights analysis): invoking prior conversations (49%) and projecting payoffs (26%) were the strongest predictors of OCB; invoking prior conversations was the most potent predictor of work–family conflict (37%); for relationship satisfaction the employee's general communication competence dominated (61%), with providing early notice (19%) and seeking permission (11%) next. Post hoc tests found no significant interactions among the strategies.
- Study 3 (partner communication satisfaction): communication strategy had a significant between-subjects effect, F(4, 917) = 5.28, p < .001, partial eta-squared = .02. Seeking permission was the most satisfying strategy (M = 3.51) and invoking prior conversations the least (M = 3.13), while projecting payoffs (M = 3.34) did not differ significantly from seeking permission. Hypothesis 3b held here, but providing early notice (M = 3.24) and negotiating logistics (M = 3.14) were associated with relatively lower satisfaction, so Hypotheses 3a and 3c were not supported and Hypothesis 3 was partially supported. There was a main effect of OCB type (dinner M = 3.40 vs. staying late M = 3.15) but no significant strategy-by-OCB-type interaction, and gender moderated nothing in either Study 2 or Study 3.

Convergence and divergence across methods: only seeking permission's positive link to partner satisfaction held in both the survey and the experiment. Providing early notice was supported in Study 2 but not in Study 3, where it produced relatively lower communication satisfaction, and negotiating logistics was unsupported in both. The Study 1 qualitative finding that couples treat OCB as effectively non-discretionary converged with Study 3, where participants rated the scenario behavior as at least somewhat expected ("is an expected part of the job," mean 3.27; "is above and beyond job requirements," mean 3.97). The authors report two robustness caveats: the invoking prior conversations link to work–family conflict was not significant when work–family conflict was measured with the partner rating alone, and the projecting payoffs link to OCB became only marginally significant (p = .08) when controls were omitted. Taken together, no single strategy was positive for both OCB and home-life outcomes.

**Theoretical Contribution**
By identifying the *content* of partner communication specific to OCB, the paper extends partner-communication and work–family research—previously focused on how communication is enacted (e.g., demand–withdraw, assertive–passive)—into the citizenship domain. It demonstrates that there is no single communication strategy beneficial for both work (OCB) and home (reduced conflict, higher partner satisfaction), and shows that employees and their partners conceptualize OCB as involuntary for achieving family goals, deepening the literature on felt pressure to engage in citizenship.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations and managers should recognize that "extra-role" behavior is often experienced as non-discretionary by employees and their partners, raising justice concerns. Employees can strategically match communication to their priorities: emphasizing seeking permission and providing early notice to protect home life, or projecting payoffs and invoking prior conversations to enable OCB—while being mindful of each strategy's costs. Organizations should encourage, but not compel, good citizenship.

**Limitations**
The Study 1 qualitative sample was relatively young, well educated, predominantly White, and seemingly happily married, possibly surfacing mainly constructive strategies and limiting transferability. Study 2 data were cross-sectional, precluding causal inference, and OCB was partner-rated rather than fully objective. The research focused on one resource-consuming OCB type (individual initiative) and on one-way (employee) communication rather than dyadic exchange.

**Future Research**
Scholars could examine dysfunctional or post-hoc communication strategies, collect reciprocal dyadic data using the actor–partner interdependence model, and study how OCB may generate work–family enrichment rather than only conflict. Additional work could investigate antecedents of citizenship communication (e.g., personality, organizational norms) and whether these strategies extend to other OCB types and targets such as coworkers and managers.
