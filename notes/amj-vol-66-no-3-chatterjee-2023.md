# Double Weaving: A Bottom-Up Process of Connecting Locations and Scales to Mitigate Grand Challenges

**Abstract**
Grand challenges are system-wide problems. While top-down approaches to mitigating grand challenges may have potentially far-reaching impact, such initiatives from the higher scales can fail because of limited knowledge of ground realities. At the same time, resilient local efforts to address societal challenges can have limited reach. How can a grassroots organization work across locations and scales to address a grand challenge? We report the findings of a longitudinal case study of the Child In Need Institute, a nonprofit organization that successfully scaled its impact to reach over seven million beneficiaries. Based on 40 years of archival data and four years of intensive fieldwork, our findings reveal “double weaving” as a recursive process of diagnosing and addressing problems by connecting actors and resources across locations and scales. The double weaving process gives researchers a framework to explore the complex interplay between locations and scales that is necessary to address grand challenges. Our study contributes to research on grand challenges and scaling, advancing the spatial turn in organizational studies by developing the scalar dimension, and expanding research that has primarily focused on locations.

**Research Question**
How can a grassroots organization work within and across locations and scales to mitigate a grand challenge? The study uses a spatial lens that treats space as both horizontal (locations) and vertical (scales), asking how a resource-poor local organization can connect dispersed actors and resources to address a system-wide, "wicked" problem.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / dimensions: Scaling out, scaling up, scaling down (three directional components) plus reinforcing (a process driver) — the four broad activity types that comprise double weaving.
- Process / phases: Scaling out (local intervening, disseminating) → scaling up (synchronizing, strategic linking) → scaling down (collective capacity building, anchoring) → reinforcing (diagnosing, consolidating), iterating recursively through multiple cycles.
- Triggering conditions: Grassroots interventions lack financial resources and access to policy-making, which triggers a need to scale up to higher-scale actors; successful scaling up builds on credible disseminated outcomes of local interventions.
- Boundary conditions: A cooperative case with no contestation/conflict; norms of freely shared knowledge; and a long, nested four-decade scaling path from community to government.

Double weaving is theorized as a multidirectional, recursive process in which a focal organization "weaves" actors and resources horizontally across locations and vertically across scales at the same time, analogous to two fabrics woven and interwoven for strength. Reinforcing (continuous diagnosing of root causes and consolidating of what works while discarding what fails) drives and sustains scaling out, up, and down, giving the process continuity and durability. Rather than seeking shared consensus among all stakeholders, the organization weaves actors around specific problems on which agreement can be more easily managed, navigating scalar asymmetries of power and contextual familiarity.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive, longitudinal single-case study of CINI combining archival records with participatory fieldwork; the design is interpretive and process-oriented, and no causal identification is claimed. Archival corpus: CINI's records covering the 40 years since its inception (1974–2014), over 1,200 documents totaling around 9,000 pages — 160 quarterly board-meeting minutes (1,800 pages), 40 annual general meeting minutes, 39 annual reports, 31 audited financial accounts, 32 statutory filings, 147 government/donor grant agreements and related communications, three commemorative books, and 820 units of other materials (training materials, newsletters, project reports, audio-visuals) — supplemented by reports located in the National Library archives in Kolkata and by netnography. Fieldwork (participant observations and conversations, 2013–2017): 225 days as a participant-observer stationed at CINI's headquarters, 15 days of field visits to eight project locations, and five observed training programs for government fieldworkers (25 days), recorded in six field diaries (about 200 pages each), 117 hours of conversation audio recordings, and 100 photographs. Interviews (conducted during 2015–2017): 42 open-ended semi-structured individual interviews and 17 group interviews with CINI's cofounder and chairman, unit and functional heads, fieldworkers, board members, child development project officers, and a former state government secretary. Constructs were coded in five nonlinear, concurrent, iterative steps: (1) chronicling CINI's scaling activities as a narrative series of events and actions; (2) axial coding of the field data through questioning and constant comparison, using phrasal descriptors (e.g., "socio-technical intervention") and yielding 24 first-order categories after several cycles of refinement; (3) grouping those categories under eight second-order themes, some drawn from respondents' own language (e.g., "synchronizing"); (4) free-hand drawing and visual mapping to capture the space–time aspects of the themes; and (5) theoretical coding classifying the themes into four third-order aggregate dimensions — scaling out, scaling up, scaling down, and reinforcing — and settling on the double weaving metaphor. Triangulation across archives, observation, and interviews was reinforced by the third author, who never visited the field and coded as an outsider to balance "etic" and "emic" perspectives; coding disagreements were resolved by revisiting the data and often approaching informants for empirical validation.

**Key Findings**
The study surfaces "double weaving" as an ongoing recursive process through which a resource-poor grassroots organization mitigated the grand challenge of child malnutrition: three directional components—scaling out, scaling up, and scaling down—plus reinforcing as a process driver, iterating through multiple rounds. Scaling out (local intervening plus disseminating) diffuses social innovations within and across locations, but grassroots organizations lack the financial resources and policy access needed to extend it, so scaling out triggers a need for scaling up; successful scaling up builds on successful scaling out because disseminating outcome histories builds credibility and attracts the attention of higher-scale actors. Scaling up (synchronizing plus strategic linking) requires acknowledging the asymmetry of power across scales and complying with the mandates and expectations of higher-scale actors rather than lecturing officials on what they should do; it secures access to financial resources and policy-making, and the resulting policy changes and resource allocations are what allow the partnership to extend into scaling down. Scaling down (collective capacity building plus anchoring) channels those higher-scale policies and resources into superior grassroots interventions, completing one weaving cycle and enabling the next. Reinforcing—continuous diagnosing of root causes together with consolidating, which strengthens what worked and discards what did not (the Life Cycle Approach and, after decades of trial and error, Premix were retained; a farming project was dropped for lack of requisite knowledge and a university's large-sample randomized control proposal was rejected on ethical grounds)—runs throughout the process rather than preceding it, which is why the study treats diagnosing and mitigating as concomitant rather than sequential. The study also reports a second, opposite asymmetry: higher-scale actors have less familiarity with local contexts than lower-scale actors, an asymmetry lower-scale actors can use to access them. Double weaving does not rest on shared consensus among stakeholders; the focal organization instead weaves actors and resources around specific problems on which consensus can be more easily managed. Reported outcomes: CINI collaborated with four government agencies and 28 partners in total in 1980, rising to 18 and 95 by 2000 and 61 and 173 by 2016; after becoming Jharkhand's nodal agency for deprived children its outreach increased from two million to four million in two years; and by the end of the fieldwork it was reaching over seven million beneficiaries. As higher-scale actors developed confidence in CINI, weaving cycles that had initially begun at the grassroots often began at higher scales instead.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to grand-challenges research by showing that identifying a grand challenge and its root causes is inseparable from engaging with it, and by treating diagnosing and mitigating as concomitant rather than sequential. It advances the "spatial turn" in organizational research by developing the under-theorized scalar (vertical) dimension alongside locations (horizontal), and extends scaling research—which has treated scaling as a unidirectional process—by theorizing scaling down as a complement to scaling out and up and by foregrounding scalar asymmetry.

**Practical Implication**
For grassroots organizations with limited resources, the case offers lessons on scaling social impact: pair local interventions with disseminating credible outcomes to attract higher-scale actors, synchronize with the mandates and expectations of those actors (rather than lecturing them), and use scaling down to channel resources and policy into superior grassroots interventions. Lower-scale actors can exploit asymmetries—using higher-scale actors' resources and policy access while supplying the local contextual familiarity those actors lack.

**Limitations**
The authors flag three boundary conditions of a single-case study. First, the case features collaboration and cooperation without the contestation or conflict often inherent in grand challenges. Second, CINI members freely shared knowledge, whereas many settings involve closely guarded ideas and inter-firm rivalry. Third, the nested, four-decade scaling path highlights how slow the process can be and may not generalize to contexts with faster, politically accountable pathways.

**Future Research**
Future work could compare less emotionally intense and vivid issues (e.g., preventable diseases such as malaria) with child malnutrition; examine the conditions under which knowledge diffuses widely given the tension between sparse networks for idea brokering and dense networks for implementation; and study grand challenges from a higher-scale actor's perspective. Researchers could also examine more closely how actors exploit scalar asymmetries across scales.
