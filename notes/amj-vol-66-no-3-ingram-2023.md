# Identity Multiplicity and the Formation of Professional Network Ties

**Abstract**
How does personal identity multiplicity—a self-perception with more elements—help an individual connect with others? I argue that identity multiplicity increases the likelihood of establishing professional network ties through two mechanisms: individuation, or the tendency of a potential network partner to view one as an individual as opposed to a representative of a category; and identity homophily, or the attraction and interaction opportunities that emerge as a function of shared identity elements with a potential partner. The benefits of identity multiplicity are particularly strong for promoting interracial ties as individuation reduces racial stereotyping. I find support for these ideas in an analysis of the creation of network ties of mid-career professionals.

**Research Question**
How does identity multiplicity—the extent to which personal identities draw on multiple elements—predict the incidence of professional network ties, and does it particularly promote ties between individuals of different races?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The likelihood that two individuals will form a network tie increases as their identity multiplicity increases (positive).
- H1a: The relationship between identity multiplicity and the formation of a network tie is mediated by individuation of the people in a dyad (positive mediation).
- H1b: The relationship between identity multiplicity and the formation of a network tie is mediated by identity homophily, indicated by the similarity of personal identity elements of individuals in a dyad (positive mediation).
- H2: The likelihood that two individuals of different race will form a network tie increases as their identity multiplicity increases (identity multiplicity positively moderates the otherwise negative effect of racial difference).
- H2a: The effect of identity multiplicity to increase the likelihood that two individuals of different race will form a network tie is mediated by their individuation (positive mediated moderation).

All five predicted paths are signed positive in the paper's theory diagrams (Figures 1A–1C), with racial difference on its own entering negatively via similarity-based attraction.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Dyadic Identity Multiplicity (count of salient identity elements across ego and alter, from "identity maps")
- DV(s): Likelihood of a professional network tie within a dyad (sent and reciprocated ties; logit)
- Mediators: Dyadic Individuation (the dyad's sum of Uniqueness, an index of the extent to which the words a subject uses in their identity map are used less often by other subjects); Identity Homophily (LSA cosine similarity of ego and alter identity-map text)
- Moderators: Different Race (identity multiplicity moderates the negative racial-difference effect on tie formation)

Identity multiplicity makes a person harder to categorize, which prompts interlocutors to individuate them—perceiving them as a unique person rather than a category representative—and individuation drives attraction, engagement, and the initiation of ties. Simultaneously, more identity elements raise the odds of sharing salient elements with an alter (identity homophily), creating bases of attraction and interaction. KHB tests indicate individuation and identity homophily jointly mediate ~82% of the identity-multiplicity effect (full mediation). Because individuation reduces negative racial stereotyping, identity multiplicity is especially potent for forging interracial ties—what the author labels the "Grunitzky effect."

**Data & Measures**
Dyad-level analysis of 401 part-time MBA students at a prestigious university in a large U.S. city, drawn from three cohorts entering in summer 2020 (28% of subjects), fall 2020 (37%), and fall 2021 (35%): 26,234 dyads for sent ties and 13,082 for reciprocated ties. Every measure comes from assignments and surveys embedded in a required first-semester leadership course. Network data were collected by an online survey at the end of the first semester, approximately four months after subjects first came together, using the roster (or "whole network") method within class section: each subject was shown a list of all alters in their own section and asked to indicate who they considered part of their professional network.
- DV — Professional Network Tie: coded 1 if a tie existed between the members of a dyad. The average subject reported a tie with 41.6% of others; counting only reciprocated ties, where both ego and alter recognize the tie, lowers the rate to 25.2%. Both sent and reciprocated ties are analyzed.
- IV — Dyadic Identity Multiplicity: the count of gray and green elements on the identity maps of ego and alter, that is, the salient identity elements they share openly or would feel comfortable sharing. In a class exercise subjects drew "identity maps" (what Ramarajan called intrapersonal identity networks) with "me" as the central node and more important identities nearer the center, then color-coded each element gray (shared openly in the program), green (would feel comfortable sharing), or red (kept hidden). Red elements enter the models separately as Dyadic Hidden Identity; results are comparable if they are folded into the multiplicity count.
- Mediator — Dyadic Individuation: the sum of ego's and alter's Uniqueness, where an individual's Uniqueness is a function of the extent to which the (stemmed) words on their identity map are used less often by other subjects. The measure follows Maslach (1974), who found that experimentally individuated subjects gave more unique self-descriptions; the author needed a measure of individuation separate from identity multiplicity because the element count itself proxies both individuation and the chance of shared identity.
- Mediator — Identity Homophily: generated by latent semantic analysis (LSA) of all text a subject entered on their identity map, implemented with the semantic routine in Stata; the dyad's value is the cosine distance between ego's and alter's scores on the 110 LSA components. LSA captures semantic rather than literal overlap (e.g., matching "ALS" to "cancer", distinguishing "working class" from "upper class"). This operationalization is original to the paper.
- Moderator — Different Race: an indicator variable; Different Sex is carried as a parallel indicator.
- Foci and controls: three foci — Same Study Group (school-assigned through a process based on randomization with demographic and industry diversity constraints), Shared Club Memberships, and Seating-Chart Neighbors — plus Met Face-to-Face, Dyadic Self-Monitoring (Snyder and Gangestad's 18-item measure summed across the dyad), Dyadic Need for Cognition (Cacioppo et al.'s 18-item instrument in Cohort 3; four need-for-cognition items from the Growth Mindset Profile in Cohorts 1–2, scaled by the cohort mean), and cohort fixed effects. Table 4 adds each of 11 further psychometric and personality variables one at a time.

Estimation is logit regression, chosen because Professional Network Tie is dichotomous, with standard errors clustered simultaneously by ego and alter to handle the autocorrelation induced by the same subjects recurring across dyads; mediation is tested with the Karlson–Holm–Breen (KHB) method for comparing coefficients of nested nonlinear probability models. The design is associational rather than causally identified: identity multiplicity is measured from subjects' own identity maps, not manipulated, and the paper claims no experiment, instrument, or quasi-experiment for its focal predictors. What leverage the setting provides is organizational control — sections and study groups were assigned by constrained randomization, and seating charts, club memberships, and face-to-face contact are all observed. The reverse-causality alternative that ties reshape identity salience is addressed by robustness analyses rather than by design: adding indicators for MBA-program-specific identity elements (Models 7–8), restricting the sample to the 74% of subjects who agreed more with maintaining their professional self than with re-inventing themselves in the program (Model 9), and restricting to Cohort 3, whose identity maps were collected before subjects had met each other (Model 10).

**Key Findings**
The author concludes that "the models in Table 2 support all of the hypothesized relationships which are represented in Figure 1C," and every hypothesis test does return a coefficient of the predicted positive sign. The one qualification the results section itself records is that the mediation half of H2a did not reach significance.

H1 supported. In the base model (Table 2, Model 1) Dyadic Identity Multiplicity has a positive and significant coefficient (0.016, SE 0.006); a one-standard-deviation increase is associated with a 15% increase in the likelihood of a network tie.

H1a and H1b supported. With both mediators added (Model 3), Dyadic Individuation (0.115, SE 0.048) and Identity Homophily (3.001, SE 0.821) are both positive predictors of a tie: a one-SD increase raises tie likelihood by 17% and 7%, respectively. KHB tests indicate that individuation mediates 81% (p < .001) and identity homophily 19% (p < .001) of the identity-multiplicity effect on their own, and 82% (p < .001) combined; once the mediators are included the Dyadic Identity Multiplicity coefficient is not significantly different from 0, which the author reads as full mediation.

H2 supported. The Dyadic Identity Multiplicity × Different Race interaction is positive and significant (Model 2: 0.017, SE 0.006), and the magnitude is substantively important. Taking main effect and interaction together, a different-race dyad one SD below the mean on identity multiplicity is only 88% as likely to form a professional network tie as a same-race dyad; at the mean there is no significant difference; one SD above the mean it is 19% more likely to form a tie than a comparable same-race dyad. The author describes this as identity multiplicity effectively reversing the influence of racial homophily and names it the "Grunitzky effect."

H2a supported in part. Its moderation component holds: the Dyadic Individuation × Different Race interaction is positive and significant (Model 5: 0.073, SE 0.028), so that a one-SD increase in Dyadic Individuation raises tie likelihood by 15% in a same-race dyad and 27% in a different-race dyad — which the paper describes as in line with part of the H2a argument, that individuation moderates the negative impact of racial difference. Its mediation component does not hold: a KHB test indicates individuation mediated about 12% of identity multiplicity's moderation of racial difference, but that estimate is not significant (p = .37), so the unmediated influence of Dyadic Identity Multiplicity on racial homophily remains in place. The author speculates that the unexplained portion reflects a form of individuation not captured by an operationalization based on unique words in the identity map.

Ancillary and robustness results. Shared Club Memberships positively predicts ties (Model 4: 0.675, SE 0.129) and KHB indicates it mediates about 4% of the Identity Homophily effect (p < .01) — consistent with foci being one channel through which identity homophily operates, while leaving identity homophily with a substantial effect that shared club joining cannot explain. Model 6 re-estimates Model 5 on reciprocated ties with comparable results and a larger Identity Homophily coefficient (4.758, SE 1.176), where a one-SD increase makes a tie 12% more likely. Self-monitoring has a strong positive effect on tie formation and need for cognition is unrelated to it, and neither displaces identity multiplicity; the key results also hold across the four reverse-causality models of Table 3 and the addition of each of the 11 psychometric and personality controls in Table 4 (among which promotion, locomotion, and extraversion were positively related to tie formation, and prevention marginally so). Online Appendix replications using tie multiplexity and tie strength as dependent variables are consistent with Table 2, and indicate that identity multiplicity predicts the existence of a tie rather than its multiplexity or strength.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper brings personal identity directly into network-formation theory, advancing the "bring the individual back in" agenda by treating self-articulated identity (not demographic proxies) as a determinant of ties. It introduces "identity homophily," extending value homophily beyond race and gender to any self-identified element, and theorizes individuation as an original, tested mechanism linking identity to tie formation and to the transcendence of racial categories.

**Practical Implication**
Individuals who want richer, more diverse professional networks should cultivate identity multiplicity—e.g., by joining multiple affinity groups rather than focusing on one, and by experimenting with and refining authentic identity narratives. Organizations can leverage existing identity-based affinity groups to build cross-demographic ties that enhance creativity, decision-making, collaboration, and inclusion.

**Limitations**
The data do not capture off-campus socializing, so the exact location where each tie formed is unobserved (though the author argues this does not threaten the hypotheses). The reverse-causality concern—that ties reshape identity salience—is addressed through several robustness analyses but cannot be fully ruled out, particularly over longer time periods.

**Future Research**
Future work could analyze the structure of identity maps (e.g., path distance or eigenvector centrality of elements) to study how the importance and interconnection of identity elements shape networking. Researchers could examine networking agency and goal achievement as a function of identity multiplicity, apply text analysis of large identity corpora (e.g., college essays) to study identity homophily at scale, and explore implications for tie efficacy and overall network structure (e.g., modularity).
