# Structuring the Start-Up: How Coordination Emerges in Start-Ups through Learning Sequencing

**Abstract**
To succeed in growing and scaling their organization, start-ups must establish roles, routines, rules, and plans that coordinate organizational activities. However, early-stage start-ups often lack such coordination mechanisms. Through a longitudinal qualitative multiple-case study of five start-ups, we develop a theoretical framework for how start-ups develop and improve coordination over time. We find that start-ups establish coordination through a learning sequence consisting of four distinct learning styles. To develop coordination successfully, start-ups anticipate coordination problems before they escalate, steal ideas and frameworks from others, experimentally implement coordination, and combine and simplify coordination mechanisms. By providing a processual understanding of how start-ups develop coordination, we contribute to the literature on coordination in start-ups, which has tended toward static explanations. We also add to the literatures on emergent coordination and organizational learning, as we highlight the role of deliberate learning in developing coordination, and how different learning styles link together to create learning sequences.

**Research Question**
The paper asks: (a) How do start-ups develop coordination over time? and (b) How does successful coordination affect organizational performance?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: Early-stage start-ups lack the institutionalized scaffolding (roles, routines, rules, plans) that the emergent-coordination literature assumes is already present, so they must learn to coordinate from a rudimentary starting point.
- Process / phases (the "best practice" learning sequence): (1) *Anticipating coordination* (anticipatory learning) — foreseeing coordination needs before crises emerge; (2) *Steal with pride* (vicarious learning) — importing ready-made frameworks and practices from peers and competitors; (3) *Experimental implementation* (experimental learning) — minimal roll-outs, then scaling mechanisms that prove functional; (4) *Combine and simplify* (trial-and-error learning) — layering and simplifying mechanisms to fit the start-up and avoid over-formalization.
- Key constructs (coordination mechanisms scored per case): rules and plans, routines, and roles (Okhuysen & Bechky, 2009).
- Emergent outcomes: Coordination mechanisms that improve performance, indexed by market growth, received funding, and employee job satisfaction.
- Boundary conditions: Learning styles complement and prompt one another (path-dependent sequencing); start-ups may restart the sequence at junctures such as rapid scaling or pivots.

Across the five cases, the high performer (Freya) executed the full anticipatory→vicarious→experimental→trial-and-error sequence, while lower performers stalled by relying on improvised, unstructured solutions or by failing to combine and simplify. The central process insight is that, lacking pre-existing scaffolds, start-ups must learn coordination *deliberately*: each learning style makes a distinct contribution, and styles feed into one another (e.g., anticipatory learning motivates vicarious search; vicarious learning supplies frameworks for experimentation). Coordination is thus reframed as a state reached through learning rather than something that emerges from institutionalized scaffolds.

**Data & Measures**
Data corpus: five early-stage digital start-ups (pseudonyms Freya, Odin, Frigg, Balder, Thor), theoretically sampled on internal characteristics from an initial population of roughly 120 start-ups across eight coworking spaces, retaining ventures that were small (fewer than seven members), young (under three years), and immature (little or no revenue) and that "all had a digital aspect," after excluding accelerator participants, remote teams, and consultancy-focused ventures. Over 22 months the authors collected 52 participant-observation visits (over 240 hours) written up as field notes, 96 semistructured interviews across three rounds (93 hours of recordings, taped and transcribed), and archival sources including social media posts, newspaper articles, and publicly available revenue data from an official database. Operationalization: coordination was scored per case on Okhuysen and Bechky's (2009) three mechanism types (rules and plans, routines, roles) using a two-level score, one level for a mechanism being present and a higher level for a mechanism present and experienced by members as highly functional, with presence assessed from participant-observation field notes and members' experience from interview data, then aggregated into an overall rating (Table 2). Organizational outcomes were scored on market growth (somewhat successful = positive sales growth across the period; highly successful = yearly sales growth above 50%), received funding, and employee job satisfaction rated from interviews with employees rather than founders or top managers (Table 3). Each case was then scored across the four learning styles on a three-level scale adapted from Murray, Kotha, and Fisher's (2020) scoring system, running from no or very few examples, through some examples or many examples in individual teams, to many examples across the whole start-up (Tables 5–8). Coordination mechanisms and performance were scored at both the midpoint and the end of data collection. Analysis: within-case case stories written by the first author, independently read and checked by the second author acting as an "outsider" coder, then cross-case comparison using replication logic against the identified high performer, with triangulation across data sources, member checks in a follow-up interview round, and explicit consideration of alternative explanations. The design is inductive and interpretive; the authors report a "link" between coordination mechanisms and outcomes and claim no causal identification.

**Key Findings**
The study surfaces a four-stage learning sequence through which start-ups build coordination: anticipating coordination (anticipatory learning), then steal with pride (vicarious learning), then experimental implementation (experimental learning), then combine and simplify (trial-and-error learning), producing coordination mechanisms (roles, routines, rules and plans) that the authors associate with better performance. Each style makes a unique contribution and the styles both complement and prompt one another (anticipatory learning motivates vicarious learning; vicarious learning inspires experimental learning), so the authors suggest learning sequences may be path dependent. Support is heterogeneous across the five cases and is reported case by case: only the high performer Freya ran the full sequence; Frigg and Balder anticipated less vigilantly than Freya and stole with pride only in individual teams while mixing in improvised solutions, and both failed to scale mechanisms across the whole start-up (Frigg attempted to combine and simplify but not organization-wide; Balder never reached combine and simplify at all); Odin skipped anticipation and vicarious learning entirely, substituting improvisational learning, and only then implemented and simplified its own improvised mechanisms such as a client checklist, a slower path because it learned only from its own mistakes, and it did not combine mechanisms; the low performer Thor stopped at unstructured improvisation and built no meaningful coordination mechanisms. Outcomes tracked this ordering: Freya doubled its revenue every year from 2017 to 2020, raised four rounds of venture funding totaling 15 M USD, and had the most satisfied members, whereas Thor had no sales growth, received no funding, and closed down before the end of data collection. Two findings are flagged as surprising: coordination problems arose even in start-ups of four to eight people, and these small start-ups struggled to diffuse mechanisms organization-wide unless they deliberately tested them first (Freya designated a "shepherd" to own implementation), which contrasts with prior work assuming mechanisms spread through informal interaction. Start-ups may restart the sequence at junctures such as rapid scaling, as Freya did on reaching 28 members while expanding into new markets. The authors weigh three alternative explanations and judge each unlikely: Freya's market being less volatile, initial team formation, and founder characteristics or prior start-up experience (Freya's founders had no prior entrepreneurial experience, and the medium performers had similar outcomes despite large variation in experience).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper's core contribution is a processual theoretical framework explaining how start-ups learn to coordinate by sequencing complementary learning styles, addressing a literature on start-up coordination that has been dominated by static, top-down "blueprint" explanations. It extends the emergent-coordination literature by showing how coordination emerges from an immature state with underdeveloped routines and roles—through deliberate learning rather than from pre-existing scaffolds—and adds to the organizational learning literature by demonstrating how different learning styles interact in learning sequences, with one style compensating for shortcomings in another.

**Practical Implication**
Start-ups should not fear having clear roles, rules, plans, and routines and should deliberately seek to implement them rather than staying unstructured. Entrepreneurs are advised to anticipate coordination needs early, learn from peers, and continuously combine and simplify coordination mechanisms to fit their current situation while avoiding bureaucratic rigidity.

**Limitations**
As an inductive multiple-case study, the work is limited in its knowledge claims, especially regarding performance outcomes, and the authors cannot fully rule out alternative explanations (e.g., market volatility, team-formation effects, founder experience). Because only the high performer restarted its learning sequence within the study window, the authors' ability to theorize iterative sequences and recovery from coordination crises is constrained, and the data are limited on how start-ups recover from such crises.

**Future Research**
The authors call for larger-scale, quantitative work to test whether the learning styles are additive and sequential (does order matter, or can styles be reordered?) and whether the findings replicate in a larger population of start-ups. They also encourage research on how specific learning styles contribute to distinct aspects of coordination, how start-ups restart learning sequences as they scale, and how start-ups recover from coordination crises.
