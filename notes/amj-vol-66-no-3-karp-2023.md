# Gaining Organizational Adoption: Strategically Pacing the Position of Digital Innovations

**Abstract**
Scholars have long suggested that, to foster adoption for their innovations, entrepreneurs should engage with customers to better understand their unmet needs. Yet, customers frequently reside in organizations, and organizational members may not be aligned in their views regarding the benefits an innovation offers. Decision-making within organizations is rarely under the purview of a single executive and various members can wield influence. Given this plurality, how do entrepreneurial firms gain organizational adoption for their innovations? I explore how 54 entrepreneurial firms, all participating in a digital health accelerator, attempted to gain organizational adoption for their innovations. I show that, when entrepreneurs pursued an embedded process, they developed ways to expand the relevance of their innovations for new customers through new use cases, but strategically paced and withheld disruptive aspects of their innovations to appease different audiences within customer organizations. Firms that leveraged a market-centric process also expanded the relevance of their innovations. Despite sparking the interest of executives, firms using a market-centric approach did not gain adoption. By unpacking the process by which some entrepreneurs discovered how to strategically pace the deployment of their digital innovations, I contribute a mechanism that explains how digital innovations gain organizational adoption.

**Research Question**
Given that adoption decisions inside customer organizations involve a plurality of members with divergent views rather than a single executive, how do entrepreneurial firms gain organizational adoption for their (often disruptive) digital innovations?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: "new use cases" (extensions that expand an innovation's relevance); the single-versus-multiparty difference in organizational adoption; the tension between revealing value-adding disruptive functionality and threatening members' expertise, roles, and jurisdiction.
- Two approaches: an *embedded approach* (deep, on-site engagement with customers to co-develop use cases) versus a *market-centric approach* (scanning competitors and the market, demonstrating expertise, magnifying claims, comprehensively rolling out functionality).
- Practices of strategic pacing (embedded firms only): (i) concealing disruptive functionality, (ii) restraining claims that an innovation displaces work, and (iii) shifting the velocity of deployment based on impact to members' expertise.
- Triggering conditions: deep engagement surfaces how an innovation threatens members' jurisdiction, prompting firms to withhold or slow disruptive elements; threats from *outside* a focal business unit require concealing functionality, whereas threats from *within* a unit can be managed by restraining claims alone.
- Emergent outcome: firms using the embedded approach with strategic pacing generally gained adoption (paid pilots); market-centric firms sparked executive interest but generally failed to gain adoption.

Across 54 firms, identifying unmet customer needs was necessary but insufficient for adoption. The embedded approach gave entrepreneurs contextual insight into how diverse organizational members would receive an innovation, enabling them to strategically pace deployment—temporarily concealing disruptive, value-adding features and restraining claims that the innovation would automate or replace members' work—so as to appease audiences whose expertise or jurisdiction the innovation threatened. The digital nature of the innovations made this selective, reversible "switching on and off" of functionality feasible.

**Data & Measures**
The corpus is two years of field data collected while the author was embedded in the digital health accelerator Cure (a pseudonym) from 2016 to 2018, covering all 54 entrepreneurial firms that matriculated through the 2017 or 2018 cohorts (28 firms in year one, 26 in year two; no selected firm declined). Each cohort was tracked for 12 months — a two-month vetting period before program entry, the six months of participation in Cure, and four months after program completion — a window calibrated to informants' report that closing a deal generally took seven months (10 at most). Three types of field data were collected: (1) ethnographic observation of entrepreneurs' internal team interactions and of their interactions with customer executives and customer users as they developed and attempted to sell new use cases; (2) 150 interviews, ranging from 30 minutes to two hours, with the CEOs or founders of all 54 firms (involving multiple members of each firm) plus executives and influencers at prospective customer organizations; and (3) firm-level data comprising applications at program entry and exit, revised technology development plans, product strategies, and periodic alumni surveys of revenue, new contracts, funding, and hiring after program completion, together with strategic planning documents, acceptance evaluations, blog posts, press releases, product requirements, and product design documentation. Table 1 reports descriptives for all 54 firms: digital health sector, cohort, CEO gender, firm age, initial revenue and funding at program entry, number of founders, and prior startup experience.

Analysis began with open coding of field notes and interviews; all field data were then temporally ordered and coded (Van de Ven & Poole, 1995), with attention to whether and how entrepreneurs identified and developed new use cases. To rule out less plausible opportunities, only use cases "at risk for adoption" — those to which firms had actually committed resources in the form of capital or labor — were traced, yielding 58 new use cases (most firms pursued adoption for one; four firms pursued two). Coding the adoption activities firms enacted produced two patterns: an embedded approach (vetting knowledge extensibility, dedicating team members, strategic pacing) and a market-centric approach (market scanning, demonstrating market expertise, magnifying claims with comprehensive rollout), with activity definitions, representative data, and frequencies in Tables 2 and 4 and firm-level activity distributions in Appendix A (Table A1). Adoption was assessed in two steps. First, the desirability of each new use case was checked with customers and with other experts such as investors and accelerator mentors — deliberately not customers alone, following Christensen and Bower (1996) — by asking whether it addressed "real" problems, could solve unmet needs, and filled gaps in the market. Second, adoption itself was operationalized as whether an entrepreneurial firm contracted with a customer for a paid live pilot during the study window, a proxy justified by informants' report that pilots resulted in fuller deployment in nearly 80% of cases. The process model was then induced by comparing all process differences between the embedded and market-centric approaches, including activities entrepreneurs avoided as well as those they conducted. The design is inductive process research: the paper reports descriptive adoption rates and qualitatively evaluates alternative explanations rather than estimating causal effects.

**Key Findings**
All 54 firms identified ways to extend their innovations into new use cases capable of satisfying unmet customer needs, so developing a desirable use case was necessary but not sufficient to gain adoption; what separated outcomes was the process used. Twenty-one of the 54 firms employed an embedded approach and strategically paced deployment, while approximately two-thirds pursued a market-centric approach. Table 5 reports that 70% of the 21 embedded-process use cases were adopted by at least one customer versus 30% of the 37 market-centric use cases: firms deploying an embedded process generally gained organizational adoption, and firms using a market-centric process, at least during the study window, generally did not, despite sparking the interest of customer executives. The pattern is general rather than uniform — Appendix Table A1 records both embedded-process firms that did not gain adoption and market-centric firms that did. The share of use cases targeted at new customer segments was similar across processes (55% embedded vs. 60% market-centric), so the difference is not one of market ambition.

The central emergent finding is strategic pacing, and the puzzle it explains is that customer executives explicitly demanded innovations that would tangibly show how their work processes are disrupted, yet firms that comprehensively fulfilled those calls generally did not gain adoption, whereas firms that withheld disruptive, value-adding functionality did. Strategic pacing unfolded in three ways — concealing functionality embedded within a use case that some organizational members found threatening to their expertise and role, restraining claims that a use case could displace or substitute for members' work, and shifting the velocity at which a use case could be fully introduced — and was observed in 85% of embedded-process firms. The embedded process supplied the contextual insight that made pacing possible: dedicating team members built relationships with organizational members spanning roles, levels, and professions, which revealed that executives were rich sources of information on an innovation's future potential while middle managers, line workers, and professionals had a stronger grasp of what an organization would currently adopt and could and did successfully advocate against adoption. The digital nature of the innovations was what made pacing practicable, since functionality could be built robustly and then switched off or on customer by customer.

A conditional finding refines the model. When features diminished the perception of users' expertise held by those working outside the focal department or business unit, entrepreneurs both concealed functionality and restrained claims that the innovation substituted for the organization's processes or technology; when the threat came from within the business unit, rolling out full functionality while restraining substitution claims and positioning the use case as a tool for the affected users was sufficient. There were no cases in which a use case threatened a department or business unit without also unsettling views of a user's expertise, and identifying a pathway for affected members to demonstrate expertise at new tasks was a critical input into how quickly a use case could be fully rolled out. Several alternative explanations for the adoption difference were evaluated and not supported: initial funding and revenue levels, founder gender, subsector alignment, founder experience, and whether firms sold to existing or new customer organizations did not explain which firms gained adoption, and market-centric firms did develop use cases in high demand among customer executives.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The core contribution is a theoretically grounded process model explaining how entrepreneurial firms gain organizational adoption for digital innovations through strategic pacing, extending research that typically picks up after the adoption decision or assumes adoption is made by a few individuals. It reconceives pacing as a *strategic* tool (slowing the introduction of disruptive elements to manage internal stakeholders) rather than only a reflective device for managing market uncertainty, and shows that threats to members' expertise and jurisdiction from outside versus inside a focal business unit call for different tactics (concealing functionality vs. positioning alone).

**Practical Implication**
Entrepreneurs seeking organizational adoption should engage deeply with customers not merely to identify unmet needs but to gain contextual awareness of how diverse organizational members will react, and should strategically pace deployment—building disruptive functionality robustly but withholding or slowly releasing it, and positioning innovations as complements or aids—until affected members can demonstrate expertise in new ways.

**Limitations**
The study examines digital health and does not test whether the observations generalize to other contexts (e.g., financial services or fields further afield), nor whether strategic pacing works for non-digital innovations whose features cannot be flexibly turned off. It also does not address the long-term effects of an embedded process and strategic pacing.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine how these dynamics transition over time as firms gain scale or market dominance and may shift from an embedded to a market-centric process, whether an embedded process and strategic pacing enable other forms of entrepreneurial growth such as funding, and how entrepreneurs foster adoption of disruptive innovations in other regulatory and industry settings.
