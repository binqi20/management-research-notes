# Betwixt and Between: National and Organizational Identification of Host Country Managers Working in MNE Subsidiaries

**Abstract**
This study investigates the patterns of dual identities of host country managers working in multinational enterprise (MNE) subsidiaries and how these patterns relate to their feelings of identity-related stress within the foreign-owned firms. Drawing on the acculturation literature, we classify the patterns of dual identities based on membership in nation and organization into four identity profiles: (a) diffuse (weak national and organizational identification), (b) national (strong national and weak organizational identification), (c) organizational (weak national and strong organizational identification), and (d) integrated (strong national and organizational identification). We submit that perceived conflict between the dual identities renders MNEs' host country managers less likely to present the integrated and the organizational identity profiles and more likely to present the national identity profile than domestic firm managers. MNEs' host country managers also identify more strongly with their nation and less strongly with their organization than domestic firm managers do. The combination of strong national identification and weak organizational identification among MNEs' host country managers exposes them to greater acculturative stress within foreign organizations. The results of an analysis of a sample of 843 Korean managers working in 19 Korean firms and 60 MNE subsidiaries in Korea corroborate our hypotheses.

**Research Question**
How do the dual identity patterns of host country managers working in foreign MNE subsidiaries—in terms of the interrelationship and relative strength of their national and organizational identities—differ from those of domestic firm managers, and how do these distinctive patterns relate to the identity-related (acculturative) stress they experience within the foreign-owned firm?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Compared to those working in domestic firms, host country managers working in MNE subsidiaries are less likely to present the integrated identity profile than the national identity profile.
Hypothesis 2. Compared to those working in domestic firms, host country managers working in MNE subsidiaries are less likely to present the organizational identity profile than the national identity profile.
Hypothesis 3. The level of national identification among host country managers working in MNE subsidiaries is stronger than that among those working in domestic firms.
Hypothesis 4. The level of organizational identification among host country managers working in MNE subsidiaries is weaker than that among those working in domestic firms.
Hypothesis 5. The difference in the level of national identification between host country managers of MNE subsidiaries and domestic firm managers is smaller when their perceived career prospects in the organization are more positive.
Hypothesis 6. The difference in the level of organizational identification between host country managers of MNE subsidiaries and domestic firm managers is smaller when their perceived career prospects in the organization are more positive.
Hypothesis 7. National identification and organizational identification interact in predicting the acculturative stress of MNEs' host country managers, such that the relationship between national identification and acculturative stress is more positive when organizational identification is lower.
The authors state two deliberate non-predictions: they have no a priori reason to expect foreign and domestic firm managers to differ in the likelihood of presenting the diffuse identity profile, and they proposed no separate hypotheses about differences in the level of acculturative stress across the four identity profiles.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Foreign-owned firm (dummy: 1 = managers in MNE subsidiaries, 0 = domestic firm managers); national identification and organizational identification (for the acculturative-stress model).
- DV(s): Dual identity profile membership (diffuse / national / organizational / integrated, via LPA); level of national identification; level of organizational identification; acculturative stress.
- Mediators: None formally modeled.
- Moderators: Perceived career prospects (moderates the foreignness → national/organizational identification links, H5-H6); organizational identification × national identification interaction predicting acculturative stress (H7).

The paper argues that because host country managers' national and organizational identities cross-cut each other (whereas domestic managers' identities are nested), foreign ownership and control heighten the salience of national identity and pose distinctiveness/value threats to it. These threats promote subgroup (national) identification and inhibit superordinate (organizational) identification, making the national profile more likely and the integrated/organizational profiles less likely than for domestic managers. Strong national plus weak organizational identification then exposes managers to greater acculturative stress; high organizational identification neutralizes the positive national-identification–stress link, while more positive perceived career prospects attenuate the foreignness effects.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from a single online questionnaire survey administered in 2015 to Korean national managers working in large domestic firms and in MNE subsidiaries in South Korea. To keep the two groups comparable, the sampling frame was limited to domestic firms and to Korean subsidiaries of firms on the 2015 Forbes Global 2000 list; a firm counted as domestic if no single foreign entity owned more than 10% of its stake (the threshold defining foreign direct investment in Korea's official statistics) and as an MNE subsidiary if it was majority or wholly owned by an MNE headquartered abroad. Contact managers recruited from professional MBA programs at two Korean business schools enlisted managers at 19 domestic firms and 60 MNE subsidiaries; the authors listed the identified managers alphabetically and selected every second person. Of 1,512 questionnaires sent, 897 (59.3%) were returned, and after excluding responses with missing data the final sample is 219 managers of 19 domestic firms plus 624 managers of 60 MNE subsidiaries headquartered in 11 countries. All respondents were Korean nationals with at least one year of tenure in their firm. Survey items were back-translated following Brislin (1986) and rated on 7-point Likert-type scales from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

Operationalization of the focal constructs: national identification uses three items from Mlicki and Ellemers (1996) (a = .88), including "I am glad that I am Korean" and "I feel strong ties with Koreans"; organizational identification uses five items from Mael and Ashforth (1992) (a = .87), including "This firm's successes are my successes", with "this firm" specified to MNE subsidiary respondents as the subsidiary rather than the MNE as a whole. Dual identity profile membership is not measured directly but derived from a latent profile analysis using the national and organizational identification items as indicators. Acculturative stress uses five items adapted from Rodriguez et al. (2002), retaining only the pressure-to-acculturate dimension (a = .90), including "It bothers me because I feel pressured to conform to foreign [the parent country's] ways of doing things"; the other dimensions of that instrument were dropped as less relevant to managers working in their own country. The independent variable, foreign-owned firm, is a dummy coded 1 for managers in foreign MNE subsidiaries and 0 for those in domestic firms. The moderator, perceived career prospects, uses three items from Reiche, Kraimer, and Harzing (2011) (a = .90). Controls are organizational prestige (five items from Mael & Ashforth, 1992; a = .87, aggregated to firm level), corporate social responsibility (five items from Lichtenstein et al., 2004; a = .90, aggregated to firm level), perceived organizational support (six items from Shanock & Eisenberger, 2006; a = .86), female gender, age, organizational tenure, job level (six categories), job function (11 categories) and 21 Korean Standard Industrial Classification industry dummies; organizational identification is controlled in the national identification models and vice versa. The acculturative-stress model adds foreign language anxiety (two items adapted from Miller et al., 2011; a = .94) and cultural distance computed from GLOBE country scores using Kogut and Singh's (1988) formula.

The design is a cross-sectional, single-source survey and the paper claims no experimental or quasi-experimental identification. What it does address is the nonrandom assignment of managers into foreign versus domestic employment: Hypotheses 1 and 2 are tested with a three-step BCH covariate analysis of latent profile membership (Bolck, Croon, & Hagenaars, 2004) and supplemented by propensity score matching; Hypotheses 3-6 with treatment effect regression (Maddala, 1983); and Hypothesis 7 with Heckman selection regression (Heckman, 1979) estimated on the 624 MNE-subsidiary managers only, because domestic firm managers rarely face the need to adapt to a foreign organizational culture. The treatment and selection equations use female gender, previous international experience (logged years abroad) and foreign language anxiety, and cluster-correlated robust variance estimates address nonindependence among responses within a firm. The authors state that the cross-sectional data make it difficult to verify the causality between identification patterns and acculturative stress, so the estimates should be read as associational; they judge the risk of common method variance to be low because the independent variable is objective and because common method variance deflates rather than manufactures interaction effects.

**Key Findings**
The paper reports that the results corroborate its hypotheses; Hypotheses 1-5 and 7 are supported at conventional levels and Hypothesis 6 only marginally.

Hypotheses 1 and 2 supported. Latent profile analysis favored a four-profile solution (the Lo-Mendell-Rubin test was significant at p < .01 for four profiles and insignificant for five), and the profile means for national and organizational identification (3.32 and 2.76; 5.32 and 3.62; 4.25 and 4.89; 5.88 and 5.66) corresponded to the diffuse, national, organizational and integrated profiles. In the BCH covariate analysis with the national profile as the baseline, the foreign-owned firm coefficient is negative and significant for the integrated versus national contrast (b = -1.26, p = .02; odds 0.28 times lower than for domestic counterparts) and for the organizational versus national contrast (b = -1.18, p = .02; odds 0.31 times lower). Consistent with the paper's explicit non-prediction, the coefficient for the diffuse versus national contrast is small and not significant (b = 0.13). Propensity score matching reproduced both signs: the average treatment effect is -0.15 (p < .01) for the integrated versus national profile and -0.13 (p = .03) for the organizational versus national profile.

Hypotheses 3 and 4 supported. In the treatment effect regressions, the foreign-owned firm coefficient on national identification is positive and significant (b = 1.07, p < .01), meaning MNE-subsidiary managers score 1.07 points higher on the 7-point national identification scale than domestic firm managers, and the coefficient on organizational identification is negative and significant (b = -1.51, p < .001), i.e., 1.51 points lower.

Hypothesis 5 supported, Hypothesis 6 marginal. The foreign-owned firm x perceived career prospects interaction on national identification is negative and significant (b = -0.20, p < .01), so the positive effect of the firm's foreignness on national identification is weaker for managers with more positive perceived career prospects. The corresponding interaction on organizational identification is positive but only marginally significant (b = 0.10, p = .08); the authors treat this as corroborating Hypothesis 6, note that the interaction is not significant in the focal-variables-only specification (which they attribute to a suppression effect from omitting national identification), and separately discuss the lower statistical significance of this moderating effect. The plotted effects show the foreign-domestic gap in both national and organizational identification narrowing as perceived career prospects rise.

Hypothesis 7 supported. In the Heckman selection model on the 624 MNE-subsidiary managers, national identification is positively and significantly related to acculturative stress (b = 0.38, p = .02) and the national identification x organizational identification interaction is negative and significant (b = -0.07, p = .04). The marginal-effects plot shows national identification positively and significantly related to acculturative stress when organizational identification is low but insignificantly related when it is high; the authors report that the interaction effect size was not large but was substantial enough to change the direction of the effect, neutralizing the positive national identification-acculturative stress relationship at high organizational identification.

Robustness and post hoc results. Reconstructing the four profiles with midpoint, median and mean cut-offs instead of latent profile analysis reproduced the Hypotheses 1-2 results; separately excluding subsidiaries of U.S.- and Japanese-headquartered MNEs yielded similar results; and adding pay satisfaction as a control did not significantly affect the results. A post hoc latent profile analysis on the foreign firm managers alone produced mean acculturative stress of 3.08 (diffuse), 3.49 (national), 3.31 (organizational) and 3.08 (integrated), with stress significantly higher among those presenting the national profile than among the integrated (p < .01) and diffuse (p = .09) profiles.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to international management in three ways: it uses a counterfactual framework to show how host country managers' dual-identity patterns differ from domestic managers' (rather than studying host country managers in isolation); it identifies an internal, human-resource source of the liability of foreignness (low organizational identification and acculturative stress) overlooked by prior work focused on external-environment sources; and it shows that host country managers' persistent strong national identity implies the MNE is a multicultural "mosaic" rather than a "melting pot," so imposing a common corporate identity may backfire.

**Practical Implication**
Rather than substituting host country managers with parent country nationals, MNEs should adopt measures that raise host country managers' organizational identification and reduce acculturative stress—notably well-developed global talent-management programs that provide clear, credible career development paths across the worldwide organization. A dual identity approach that acknowledges distinct national affiliate identities while cultivating a common ingroup identity is recommended over a "one-company" global identity approach.

**Limitations**
The theoretical premise that nationality is a subjectively important identity may not hold where perceived "nation-ness" is low or in less-developed host countries, so foreignness may constitute less of a liability in such settings. The authors also raise Korea's collectivist culture as a possible influence, but argue there is little reason to expect individualism–collectivism to change how people react to identity threats, so the main thesis "could still hold in individualistic countries as well"—something they note needs testing in future studies. The single-source, cross-sectional data likely deflate the interaction effect (common method variance) and make it difficult to verify the causal direction between identification patterns and acculturative stress.

**Future Research**
Future work should explore how perceived nation-ness and sociocultural divides shape attitudes toward foreignness, and test whether the main thesis holds in more individualistic and in considerably less-developed host countries. Longitudinal designs collecting identification and acculturative-stress data at different time points are needed to address common method variance and establish causality in MNE subsidiary contexts.
