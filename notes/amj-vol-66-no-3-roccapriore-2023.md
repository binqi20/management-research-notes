# I Don’t Need a Degree, I’ve Got Abs: Influencer Warmth and Competence, Communication Mode, and Stakeholder Engagement on Social Media

**Abstract**
In this study we consider whether (a) image- and word-based communication modes and (b) warmth and competence cues vary in their relative influence on different levels of stakeholder engagement on social media. Specifically, we explore social media fitness influencers’ abilities to attract followers and get followers to positively interact with them via posts and comments. We theorize that differences in the ways each communication mode is processed, and differences in how competence and warmth cues are perceived, will lead to different relative effects on lower- and higher-engagement behaviors. Using the social media platform Instagram, we followed 488 social media entrepreneurs in the fitness and nutrition industry for six months, and found that images have a positive relationship with less cognitively effortful engagement (following) whereas words do not have a significant relationship, and words have a stronger relationship than images with more cognitively effortful engagement (positive interactions). We also found that competence cues have a stronger positive relationship than warmth cues with the number of followers, and warmth cues have a positive relationship with positive interactions, whereas competence cues do not. Our findings have implications for research on multimodal communication, social judgments, and entrepreneur–stakeholder engagement.

**Research Question**
(a) Do images and words vary in their relative influence on stimulating different levels of stakeholder engagement, and (b) do warmth and competence cues vary in their relative influence on these outcomes? The paper asks how communication mode and content shape behaviors that differ in the cognitive effort they require.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- Hypothesis 1a. Image-based cues will have a stronger positive relationship with the influencer’s number of followers compared to word-based cues (predicted positive, images > words).
- Hypothesis 1b. Word-based cues will have a stronger positive relationship with followers’ positive interaction with the influencer compared to image-based cues (predicted positive, words > images).
- Hypothesis 2a. Competence cues will have a stronger positive relationship with an influencer’s number of followers compared to warmth cues (predicted positive, competence > warmth).
- Hypothesis 2b. Warmth cues will have a stronger positive relationship with followers’ positive interaction with the influencer compared to competence cues (predicted positive, warmth > competence).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Image-based cues and word-based cues (communication mode); competence cues and warmth cues (communication content), each a standardized composite index of LIWC-coded text and rater-coded images
- DV(s): Number of followers (lower-level engagement, less cognitively effortful) and positive interactions (higher-level engagement, more cognitively effortful)
- Mediators: None modeled
- Moderators: Engagement level / cognitive effort of the behavior is the conceptual contingency that flips which mode and which cue dominates (tested via cross-equation Wald comparisons rather than interaction terms)
- Controls: Post/reply/comment word counts, posting activity, profile age, traditional credentials, rater-coded attractiveness and fitness, video and deleted posts, gender, race

Because images are processed rapidly and unmediated and offer heuristic "proof" (iconicity, indexicality), they exert more influence on the low-effort decision to follow, while words' syntactic determinacy lets them convey the specific arguments and trust cues needed for the higher-effort decision to interact. Parallel logic applies to content: competence cues credibly signal underlying ability and matter most for following an experience-good provider, whereas warmth cues build the liking, trust, and authenticity that drive cognitively effortful positive interaction. Statistical-significance patterns supported H1b and H2a; the relative effect-size hypotheses (H1a, H2b) were not significant due to wide confidence intervals.

**Data & Measures**
- Data source: secondary Instagram data on fitness and nutrition industry influencers, scraped via an API six days a week (Sunday excluded) from July 1 to December 31, 2019, yielding 52,148 influencer posts, 8,730,714 follower comments, and 620,505 influencer replies to comments, aggregated to influencer-months. The sample was built from key-term Instagram searches (trainer, online trainer, online coach, fitness coach, fitness coaching) plus stratified random sampling on pre-study followership size, giving an initial pool of 1,002 and a drawn sample of 502; removing influencers who stopped posting in the final three months left 488 influencers and 2,928 influencer-month observations (models are estimated on n = 2,916). A March 2019 visit to the Arnold Fitness Expo motivated the study but is not part of the analytic data.
- DV 1 (lower-level engagement): number of followers at the end of each month, taken from the influencer’s profile and log-transformed because of extreme right-skew (raw skew 7.19, reduced to −0.21; raw range 40 to 12,895,427).
- DV 2 (higher-level engagement): positive interactions = the count of positive affect words used by others in the comments on the influencer’s posts, coded with the LIWC positive affect dictionary.
- IV word-based competence cues: three-variable index of analytic words in the influencer’s posts, analytic words in replies to comments, and first-person singular pronouns in posts, coded with the LIWC analytic and pronoun dictionaries.
- IV word-based warmth cues: three-variable index of positive affect words in posts, positive affect words in replies, and other-inclusive first-person plural and second-person pronouns in posts.
- IV image-based competence cues: rater-coded counts of before-and-after transformation posts and of self-portrait or workout-demonstration posts; three raters each independently coded 500 posts (Cronbach’s α = 0.91 for before-and-after images, 0.73 for self-portraits).
- IV image-based warmth cues: rater-coded counts of group images (three or more individuals) and personal-life images; images fitting neither the warmth nor the competence category were excluded.
- Composite construction: every component variable was converted to a z-score. Word-based cues = mean of the six word z-scores; image-based cues = mean of the four image z-scores; competence cues = mean of its three word-based and two image-based z-scores; warmth cues = the same for its warmth components. Construct validity was checked with 93 Instagram users recruited on Prolific who rated eight messages on 1–5 warmth and competence scales.
- Controls: post, reply, and comment word counts; posts, comments, and replies in the period; video posts; deleted posts; traditional credentials (binary, any certification or degree listed in the profile); rater-panel attractiveness and fitness dummies (eight raters; α = 0.61 and 0.79); age of profile; gender; race (α = 0.81).
- Estimation: Hausman–Taylor random effects regression in Stata 16 (xthtaylor), chosen because the theory concerns between-influencer rather than within-influencer variation and the data contain time-invariant and nearly time-invariant measures. Variables were assigned to exogenous and endogenous, time-variant and time-invariant categories so the estimator could absorb an unobserved fixed effect — Instagram’s proprietary placement algorithm. The four focal cue measures were standardized so their coefficients could be compared with Wald tests.

The design is observational and associational: the paper reports relationships between cues and engagement, and its own language throughout is relational rather than causal. Hausman–Taylor does construct instruments for the endogenous regressors automatically, but the paper frames this as a way to retain time-invariant variables while accounting for the unobserved algorithm effect, not as a causal identification strategy. Endogeneity is addressed defensively rather than by design, through a robustness of inference to replacement (RIR/ITCV) analysis using Stata’s konfound command, alongside winsorizing skewed variables at a 1% cutoff, models with lagged dependent and independent variables, models dropping controls, tests for curvilinear cue effects, and substituting Apple and Android emoticon dictionary text before LIWC coding.

**Key Findings**
- Hypothesis 1a not supported. Image-based cues have a positive, significant relationship with number of followers (b = 0.02, p = 0.04) while word-based cues do not (b = −0.02, p = 0.54); because the hypothesis is about relative effect sizes, the decisive test is the Wald comparison, and the two coefficients are not significantly different (χ² = 1.29, p = 0.26), which the authors attribute to the wide confidence interval (−0.08 to 0.04) on word-based cues. For the significant path, each 1 SD increase in image-based cues corresponds to 1,498 additional followers, a 1.74% increase.
- Hypothesis 1b supported. Word-based cues have a positive, significant relationship with positive interactions (b = 545.49, p = 0.00) and image-based cues a positive, marginally significant one (b = 50.99, p = 0.07); the Wald test shows the word-based coefficient is significantly larger (χ² = 11.89, p = 0.00). Each 1 SD increase raises positive interactions by 36.8% for word-based cues and 3.4% for image-based cues.
- Hypothesis 2a supported. Competence cues have a positive, significant relationship with number of followers (b = 0.05, p = 0.02) while warmth cues do not (b = 0.00, p = 0.64), and the Wald test shows the competence coefficient is significantly larger (χ² = 6.26, p = 0.01). Each 1 SD increase in competence cues corresponds to 4,353 additional followers, a 5.1% increase.
- Hypothesis 2b not supported. Warmth cues have a positive, significant relationship with positive interactions (b = 141.43, p = 0.00) while competence cues do not (b = 78.00, p = 0.29), but the Wald test finds no significant difference between the two coefficients (χ² = 0.66, p = 0.42), again attributed to the wide confidence interval on competence cues (−67.05 to 222.99).
- Net pattern: the direction and statistical-significance pattern is consistent with all four predictions, but only the two comparisons that cleared the Wald test — Hypotheses 1b and 2a — are counted as supported. The authors characterize the overall result as general support for the expected pattern of relationships even though some effect sizes were not significantly different from one another.
- Post hoc within-mode and within-content comparisons (online supplement). For number of followers: image-based competence cues are positive and significant (b = 0.03, p = 0.01) while word-based competence cues are not (b = 0.02, p = 0.38), with no significant difference between them (χ² = 0.13, p = 0.72); image-based warmth cues are nonsignificant (b = 0.01, p = 0.17) while word-based warmth cues are negatively and significantly related (b = −0.04, p = 0.03), a sign opposite to what was anticipated, and the two differ significantly (χ² = 4.75, p = 0.03). For positive interactions: word-based competence cues are significant (b = 244.67, p = 0.02) while image-based competence cues are not (b = −0.94, p = 0.98); both image-based (b = 41.00, p = 0.03) and word-based (b = 307.31, p = 0.00) warmth cues are significant, with the word-based coefficient significantly larger (χ² = 8.75, p = 0.00). Overall, image-based competence cues had the largest positive relationship with lower-level engagement and word-based warmth cues the largest with higher-level engagement.
- Robustness. Winsorizing at 1% left the pattern intact except that image-based cues lost significance with followers and competence cues became slightly less significant (still p < .05). With lagged dependent variables, and separately with lagged independent variables, the only change was that the marginally significant image-based-cues-to-positive-interactions relationship became nonsignificant. Tests for curvilinear cue effects were all nonsignificant. The RIR/ITCV analysis identifies only image-based cues as a possible omitted-variable concern (5.03% of cases for followers, 7.38% for positive interactions), and the authors conclude that endogeneity does not appear to be an issue.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study advances multimodal communication research by separating communication mode from content—conflated in prior work—and showing that a mode's or cue's effectiveness depends on the cognitive effort the targeted engagement behavior requires. It extends the "Big Two" social-judgment literature by demonstrating that the long-assumed primacy of warmth over competence is conditional: competence cues dominate low-effort engagement, warmth cues dominate high-effort engagement. It also extends entrepreneur–stakeholder engagement research from one-to-one dyads to one-to-many online interactions.

**Practical Implication**
Influencers and entrepreneurs should use images and videos demonstrating competence to grow their following, and deploy both image- and word-based warmth cues to stimulate higher-level engagement such as positive interactions. They should avoid self-focused rhetoric and general cheerleading, instead using other-directed language; posts need not be long, but regular, attentive interaction is crucial for cultivating online relationships and monetization opportunities.

**Limitations**
Findings may not generalize beyond the visual, business-to-consumer fitness and nutrition context, and may differ in B2B settings or where visual evidence is less dramatic. Because data covered a fixed window with small monthly variation in followership and predictors, the authors could not compare within-influencer changes over time. Some images contain words, and Instagram's proprietary algorithm is an unobserved factor only partially addressed by the Hausman–Taylor design.

**Future Research**
Longitudinal designs tracking entrepreneurs from founding onward could reveal how engagement processes evolve as followerships grow. More granular measures of image-based cues could give a more robust understanding, given that some images also contain words. Scholars should also examine how image-based competence cues affect investor decisions in computer-mediated settings such as crowdfunding.
