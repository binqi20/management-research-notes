# Creative Jolts: Exploring How Entrepreneurs Let Go of Ideas During Creative Revision

**Abstract**
Creative revision involves sharing nascent ideas and soliciting feedback. This process has been portrayed as providing instrumental feedback that enables autonomous creators to shape and progress their ideas in a mostly positive, developmental way. However, creative revision also entails destruction—letting go of some ideas to make way for the new. Research has suggested that abandoning some aspects of ideas will be affectively challenging for creators, but has yet to elaborate how they navigate that challenge. In a longitudinal qualitative study of entrepreneurial ventures over a one-year period, we uncovered a novel phenomenon: the creative jolt—an episode of cognitive and emotional upheaval through which creators let go of their original ideas and allowed new ones to emerge, so that their ideas shifted in dramatic ways. We elaborate the processes through which creative jolts shape the trajectories of creative ideas. Our study captures the rollercoaster of heightened affect and cognitive disorientation experienced by autonomous creators as they try to bring nascent ideas to life.

**Research Question**
How do creators let go of ideas during creative revision (in order to allow new ideas to emerge and develop)? The paper studies how autonomous creators—early-stage entrepreneurs—navigate the disruptive episodes through which they relinquish ideas.

**Mechanism Process**
- Triggering condition: *Trigger feedback* — negative, subtractive, core-directed feedback from a credible source (trusted/high-status mentor) that is perceived as threatening the idea or the venture's core mission.
- Key constructs / tensions: the *creative jolt* (an episode of cognitive and emotional upheaval); *assumptive worlds* that must be broken down before letting go; the persistency paradox between flexible revision and rigid commitment.
- Process / phases: (1) *existential crisis* — shattered assumptions plus a heightened emotional state, branching into *existential dread* (threat to idea) or *existential awakening* (threat to mission); (2) *reappraisal* — sensemaking via *reaching out* (diversifying through new networks) or *reaching in* (reflecting on core values with close peers); (3) *resolution* — discarding core aspects and reorienting via *recreation* (a fundamentally new idea) or *restoration* (return to the original mission).
- Emergent outcomes: a dramatic, generative shift onto a new idea trajectory, accompanied by emotional repair.

Creative jolts loosen creators' attachment to ideas by breaking down the assumptive worlds underpinning them, opening a "gap in meaning" that motivates a sensemaking search through which a new idea emerges. Negative affect (dread) and emotional ambivalence (awakening) supply the motivation, while reaching out and reaching in supply the cognitive raw material for reconstruction—so that the remains of the old idea define the opening into which the new one grows.

**Data & Measures**
Inductive multiple-case design (Eisenhardt, 1989) following 12 early-stage entrepreneurs — founders or cofounders who were the main inventors of the focal venture idea and so operated as relatively autonomous creators — recruited through four London coworking spaces. Scoping interviews produced structured profiles of 20 potential participants, of whom 12 met the criteria of having worked one to 12 months on a nascent idea they had conceptualized and of being regular users of a space they had joined in the previous three to six months (seven solo founders; five members of two-person founding teams). Observation ran approximately one year per venture, varying from six to 13 months. Three complementary data sources were used, in a design inspired by the experience sampling method (Larson & Csikszentmihalyi, 2014): 85 detailed semi-structured personal interviews of one to two hours tracking the entrepreneur's changing business model, interactions, salient conversations and emotions; 42 phone interviews of 10 to 20 minutes; and 376 substantive mobile-app entries over the 13-month observation period (average 31 per entrepreneur, range two to 70), prompted each evening and used mainly to flag key interactions for follow-up in the next interview. Constructs were built through a multistep inductive analysis. (1) In-depth within-case analysis of each of the 12 entrepreneurs, without theoretical preference or a priori hypotheses, yielding temporal narratives (Langley, 1999) of each idea journey. (2) The challenging feedback interactions the authors label trigger feedback were treated as events (Abbott, 1990), and episodes were built that began with trigger feedback and ended when the entrepreneur viewed the challenge to the idea as resolved; these episodes were labeled creative jolts and became the study's unit of analysis, with three authors independently charting each candidate episode into expansive quote tables and case narratives that were then reconciled across the group, plus comparison tables of interactions that did not trigger a jolt. (3) Systematic coding of emotional expressions, drawing on the emotive-response literature, the PANAS-X affect schedule (Watson & Clark, 1994) and the 2015 LIWC dictionary, produced a coding structure of 16 affective responses — six positive, six negative, four other — with LIWC-flagged emotion words used as a guide to subsequent manual inductive coding rather than mechanically counted, supplemented by interviewer notes. (4) Cross-case comparison of the individual jolt episodes identified consistent themes, temporal patterns and shifts. Data analysis took approximately six months. The design is interpretive and process-based; no causal identification is claimed.

**Key Findings**
Of the 12 entrepreneurs followed, six engaged in ordinary creative revision — elaborating and refining ideas with relatively neutral emotions and incremental changes while retaining the core of the focal idea, with a few also ignoring or dismissing highly critical feedback — while the other six changed their ideas more radically, letting go of core aspects of the original idea; one of these experienced two episodes, yielding seven creative jolt episodes in total. Jolts were rare: across a year of observing many feedback interactions for every entrepreneur, only seven instances led to a creative jolt. Jolts were initiated by trigger feedback that creators perceived as threatening their idea or their venture's core mission. A credible source (someone deeply trusted, or high in status or power) was a precondition, but criticism from a credible mentor alone was insufficient: trigger feedback also focused on the core of an idea rather than a specific strategy or implementation plan, was subtractive rather than additive, and was aimed at an idea the creator was clear and confident about. Critical feedback lacking those characteristics — additive, or aimed at a missing plan rather than the core — did not trigger a jolt: Emily (Zelda) elaborated and improved her idea by developing a strategy for scaling, while Clayton (Digital Lemon), whose respected football-chairman critic was suggesting he wait or offer a larger equity portion rather than purely subtracting, "did not substantially change his plans in response to this feedback." Jolt episodes then unfolded through three phases — existential crisis, reappraisal, resolution — along two pathways that creators tended to follow consistently, without shifting between pathways from phase to phase, although the constituent activities were not exclusive to a pathway. Where trigger feedback was read as threatening the idea, creators experienced existential dread: shattered assumptions plus emotional turmoil, with LIWC-driven coding showing a shift from dominant positive emotions (self-assurance, hope, excitement) to dominant negative emotions (fear, anger, frustration), and actions that arrested or even reversed development of the idea. They reappraised by reaching out — diversifying their information and networks and seeking disconfirmation of the trigger feedback — and resolved through recreation, letting go of the original idea and embracing a fundamentally new one (GymForUs moved from matching gym buddies to connecting users with personal trainers; BaysWater narrowed to connecting Syrian refugee students in Turkey with peers and refugee academics after learning a large university already delivered its value proposition). Where trigger feedback was read as threatening the mission, creators experienced existential awakening: assumptions were shattered but also opened up to new possibilities or to a side-tracked original mission, accompanied by emotional ambivalence in which relief and hope coexisted with uncertainty and fear. They reappraised by reaching in — reflecting on core values and rationale, sometimes alone and sometimes with a close circle who understood the founding mission — and resolved through restoration, discarding significant and sometimes successful aspects of the venture in order to return it to its original core mission (Doorstep Radish forsook the sales drive that had grown it to roughly 600 home and business customers and 2,000 boxes per month; SuperCharge shifted from B2C to B2B to pursue its cultural-shift mission). Both resolutions were generative and both entailed letting go of something fundamental to the idea, but the study cannot establish that the new path was more novel or more useful than the original. Resolution was accompanied by emotional repair, with LIWC coding showing emotional expressions becoming more positive and increasing relief, hope and happiness. The overarching finding is that jolts did not stop creators from progressing along their idea journeys; they jolted those journeys onto a different trajectory, abruptly pushing creative work in substantially new, even radical, directions.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper conceptualizes the previously unexamined *destructive* counterpart of idea generation—how letting go of ideas unfolds through creative revision—and introduces the creative jolt as a distinct, affectively charged, multi-phase episode that pushes ideas onto radically new trajectories. It shifts research attention from how ideas are generated and selected toward the processes by which "openings" for new ideas are constructed, arguing that ideas alone are not enough and that commitment/rigidity can paradoxically enable radical change once shaken.

**Practical Implication**
Autonomous creators and the mentors, accelerators, and coworking communities supporting them should recognize that highly critical, core-directed feedback can trigger an agonizing but generative period rather than mere failure. Supporting creators through the emotional upheaval—via opportunities to reach out to diverse networks and reach in to core values—can help them work through the process of letting go, though the study cannot establish whether letting go improved outcomes for creators.

**Limitations**
The analysis deliberately focused on cases where creators let go of ideas, so it cannot establish whether letting go improved outcomes or whether persistence would have succeeded. The study is confined to autonomous creators (individual founders or primary cofounders), so collective- or team-level jolt dynamics could not be elaborated. Findings rely partly on retrospective interviewing, mitigated but not eliminated by the longitudinal, near-real-time design.

**Future Research**
Future work should map creators' emotional journeys more completely and examine how upheaval is prompted across different creators and contexts (e.g., organizational settings where support is withdrawn after a project is greenlit). Scholars could probe the nature of assumptive worlds and how creators emotionally prepare for their destruction, whether jolts can arise from positive triggers, and how jolts unfold at the team or collective level.
