# Rekindling the Fire and Stoking the Flames: How and When Workplace Interpersonal Capitalization Facilitates Pride and Knowledge Sharing at Work

**Abstract**
Sharing positive events with others is a phenomenon referred to as “capitalization.” Extant theory on capitalization considers the process of disclosing positive events as socioemotional in nature. However, individuals capitalize in the context of workplace relationships that serve instrumental functions, pointing to the possibility that the process of capitalization may at times be instrumental as well. In this paper, we integrate theory on capitalization with the social-functionalist model of emotions to develop a model that explains how workplace interpersonal capitalization serves the instrumental function of spurring knowledge sharing. We identify pride as a linking mechanism between workplace interpersonal capitalization and knowledge sharing, and we argue that experiencing pride is contingent on employees disclosing positive events that they attribute to their own efforts. We also identify perceived coworker responsiveness as a boundary condition qualifying the association between pride and knowledge sharing. A sourceand time-separated field study and an experience sampling field experiment with a daily intervention provide support for our hypothesized model. Overall, our work broadens the concept of capitalization and contributes to the social-functionalist model of emotions.

**Research Question**
How and when does workplace interpersonal capitalization (sharing personal work-related positive events with coworkers) serve the instrumental function of spurring disclosers' knowledge sharing? The paper asks whether capitalization is not only socioemotional but also instrumental, with pride as the linking mechanism, effort attribution as a first-stage boundary condition, and perceived coworker responsiveness as a second-stage boundary condition.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: The association between workplace interpersonal capitalization and pride is moderated by effort attribution, such that the association is positive when effort attribution is high, but not when effort attribution is low.
H2: The positive association between pride and knowledge sharing is moderated by responsiveness, such that the association is stronger when responsiveness is high (versus low).
H3: The positive indirect association of workplace interpersonal capitalization on knowledge sharing via pride is moderated by effort attribution and responsiveness, such that the association is stronger when effort attribution and responsiveness are both high (versus when either or both are low).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Workplace interpersonal capitalization (Study 1: 5-item self-report propensity; Study 2: daily randomized capitalization manipulation)
- DV(s): Knowledge sharing (the sharing of expertise or know-how)
- Mediators: Pride (authentic, achievement-oriented self-conscious emotion)
- Moderators: Effort attribution (first stage: capitalization → pride); perceived coworker responsiveness (second stage: pride → knowledge sharing)

Integrating capitalization theory with the social-functionalist model of emotions, the authors argue that disclosing a positive work event "rekindles the fire" of past achievement, drawing attention to the self and activating self-enhancement that elicits pride—but only when the event is attributed to one's own effort, a marker of work-goal progress. Pride in turn motivates disclosers to "stoke the flames" by sharing knowledge, a prototypical prestige-seeking behavior through which individuals convert valued achievements into higher social status. This prestige-seeking pathway holds only when disclosers perceive coworkers as responsive, because responsiveness signals that knowledge sharing will be rewarded with status. The full model is a two-stage moderated mediation in which the indirect effect is strongest when both effort attribution and responsiveness are high.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 data: a two-wave, source- and time-separated survey (waves one month apart) at a privately owned mechanical-gear manufacturing company in Eastern China; 524 full-time employees across 44 working teams were invited, yielding 448 matched focal disclosers and 1,989 round-robin coworker reports (85.50% response rate). All English scales were back-translated into Chinese. IV: workplace interpersonal capitalization = the 5-item Watkins (2021) measure referenced to the past two weeks, self-reported at Time 1 (α = .93). First-stage moderator: effort attribution = two Tracy and Robins (2007) items, "caused by your effort" and "caused by your behaviors and actions," at Time 1 (α = .94). Mediator: pride = the seven achievement-oriented pride items from Tracy and Robins (2007) (accomplished, achieving, confident, fulfilled, productive, self-worth, successful), self-reported at Time 2 with the capitalization referenced in the item stem (α = .96). Second-stage moderator: responsiveness = the 3-item Gable et al. (2012) scale at Time 2 (α = .90). DV: knowledge sharing = the 7-item Srivastava, Bartol, and Locke (2006) scale, completed by each discloser's teammates about the discloser in a round-robin design and aggregated to the discloser (α = .97; Rwg(j) = .96, ICC(1) = .68, ICC(2) = .95). Controls: Time 1 pride, coworker friendship, task interdependence, positive event frequency, team tenure. Estimation: multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8.3 with the MLR estimator accounting for individuals nested in teams, fixed slopes and grand-mean-centered predictors, with moderated mediation tested by parametric bootstrapping (20,000 resamples, 95% CIs).
Study 2 data: a 10-day experience sampling field experiment with UK working professionals recruited on Prolific and screened for regular coworker interaction, a full-time 9-to-5 schedule, and daily commuting; the final sample was 105 participants and 787 paired morning/afternoon observations (mean 8.77 pairs per person). Capitalization was manipulated daily via a constrained random matrix approach: each morning half the participants were assigned to the capitalization condition (coded 1; instructed to recall a past personal work-related positive event and share it that morning with one pre-selected coworker) and half to a control condition (coded 0; a typical conversation topic), with each participant assigned to each condition five times in a within-person randomized order. Effort attribution used the same two items in the morning survey (daily α = .91); because attributions do not apply to control cases, the authors followed Leslie et al. (2012) and multiplied the group-mean-centered raw score by the condition value, so the variable represents variation in effort attribution among capitalization cases against control cases as a baseline rather than a true interaction term. Pride used the same seven items in the afternoon survey (daily α = .96); knowledge sharing used three Srivastava et al. (2006) items self-reported in the afternoon (daily α = .91). Responsiveness was a Level 2 moderator measured in the entry survey with the 3-item active-constructive perceived responses to capitalization attempts scale from Gable et al. (2004) (α = .71). Controls: day of week, sine and cosine weekly-cycle terms, morning pride, event pleasantness, days since the positive event, trait self-esteem (α = .77), coworker tenure. Estimation: multilevel path analysis in Mplus 8.3 with a random slope for the pride-to-knowledge-sharing path, group-mean-centered Level 1 and grand-mean-centered Level 2 predictors, and parametric bootstrapping (20,000 resamples); a chi-square test of independence confirmed the manipulation (χ²(1) = 572.16, p < .001).
Identification: the authors state that Study 1 is correlational in nature and does not sufficiently establish causality. Study 2's randomly assigned daily manipulation and the temporal precedence of its measurement support only a partial causal claim; the authors say explicitly that they can speak to causality only in the front half of the model, where capitalization was manipulated. The pride-to-knowledge-sharing stage and the responsiveness moderation remain associational in both studies.

**Key Findings**
All three hypotheses were supported in Study 1 and again in Study 2, but the shape of the second-stage (responsiveness) moderation differed between the two studies.
H1 supported in both studies. Study 1: the capitalization × effort attribution interaction on pride was significant (γ = .09, p = .001, Δpseudo-R² = 3%), with the simple slope positive and significant at higher effort attribution (γ = .16, p = .001) and non-significant at lower effort attribution (γ = -.03, p = .488). Study 2: the effort attribution variable predicted pride (γ = .16, p = .001, Δpseudo-R² = 3%) alongside a positive capitalization-manipulation effect (γ = .28, p < .001), and the simple slope was positive and significant at higher effort attribution (γ = .47, p < .001) but non-significant at lower effort attribution (γ = .10, p = .181). The authors note that across both studies capitalization and pride were unrelated when effort attribution was lower.
H2 supported in both studies, with different interaction shapes. Study 1: responsiveness moderated the pride-to-knowledge-sharing association (γ = .10, p = .041, Δpseudo-R² = 1%); pride was significantly associated with knowledge sharing at higher responsiveness (γ = .14, p = .031) but not at lower responsiveness (γ = -.03, p = .775). Study 2: the cross-level interaction was significant in the predicted direction (γ = .13, p = .004; generalized R² = .07, i.e., 7% of Level 1 variance in knowledge sharing), and pride was positively associated with knowledge sharing at higher responsiveness (γ = .66, p < .001) and also, more weakly, at lower responsiveness (γ = .39, p < .001). So pride and knowledge sharing were positively associated only at higher responsiveness in Study 1, whereas in Study 2 they were positively associated at both levels but more strongly at higher responsiveness; the authors attribute the difference to the different levels of analysis (between-individual vs. within-individual) and report that the zero-order responsiveness-knowledge sharing correlation was positive in both studies (Study 1: r = .50; Study 2: r = .38, both p < .001).
H3 supported in both studies, though the Study 1 test was weaker. Study 1: the difference in indirect effects across moderator levels was only marginally significant (difference = .02, 95% CI [-0.002, 0.052]; 90% CI [0.002, 0.046]); the indirect effect differed from zero only when both moderators were higher (ab = .02, SE = .01, 95% CI [0.002, 0.051]) and was non-significant when both were lower and in both mixed cells. Study 2: the difference was significant (difference = .27, 95% CI [0.126, 0.435]); the indirect effect was positive and significant when both moderators were higher (ab = .31, SE = .07, 95% CI [0.177, 0.464]), non-significant when both were lower (ab = .04, SE = .03, CI spanning zero), still significant when effort attribution was higher but responsiveness lower (ab = .18, SE = .05, 95% CI [0.097, 0.287]), and non-significant when effort attribution was lower but responsiveness higher (ab = .07, SE = .05, 95% CI [-0.024, 0.163]), which the authors read as the indirect path being more sensitive to effort attribution than to responsiveness.
Variance explained: the Study 1 predictors accounted for 27% of the variance in pride and 30% in knowledge sharing; the Study 2 model explained an incremental 9% and 14%. In Study 2, days since the positive event was unrelated to pride, indicating comparable pride whether the disclosed event had occurred the previous day or weeks to months earlier.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper broadens the concept of capitalization by theorizing its instrumental potential, challenging the long-standing assumption that capitalization is a purely socioemotional process and that the characteristics of the disclosed event are inconsequential. By positioning effort attribution as a boundary condition, it shows that only effort-attributed events activate the instrumental process. It also contributes to the social-functionalist model of emotions by qualifying the assumed evolutionary, universal pride–knowledge sharing link with the boundary condition of responsiveness, demonstrating that the link is not as unbounded as previously theorized.

**Practical Implication**
Because sharing good news at work can indirectly promote knowledge sharing, organizational leaders—especially in knowledge-intensive and innovation-oriented organizations—should encourage employees to share positive events (e.g., in meetings or via workspace design) and coach employees to respond responsively when they are targets of capitalization. Leaders should be cautious, however, since capitalization can backfire through unfavorable social comparison or undermining, and overly controlling reactions (e.g., financial rewards) can reduce intrinsic motivation.

**Limitations**
Study 1 is correlational and cannot fully establish causality; it is set in a single manufacturing organization in China, limiting generalizability, and cannot fully assess whether a given team member was both a capitalization and a knowledge-sharing target. The model does not explain why or when disclosers come to perceive responsiveness in coworkers, and the authors did not directly theorize the instrumental downsides of workplace interpersonal capitalization.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine other characteristics of the disclosed positive event beyond effort attribution, explore the "half-life" of capitalization-elicited pride over time, develop theory on the antecedents of perceived responsiveness, and test whether the instrumental capitalization process generalizes to non-work contexts such as romantic relationships, religious groups, schools, or families. Researchers are also encouraged to study the potential costs and net effects of sharing good news at work.
