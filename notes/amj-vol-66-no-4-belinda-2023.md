# A Spillover Model of Dreams and Work Behavior: How Dream Meaning Ascription Promotes Awe and Employee Resilience

**Abstract**
Sleep and employee behavior are linked. While the dominant explanations for this link involve physiology and cognitive resources, we offer a different perspective. We suggest that dreams, or psychological experiences during sleep, spill over to affect employee behavior. This spillover effect, we argue, results from the interplay between dreaming, meaning creation, and emotion. Taking a morning-of perspective, we theorize that recalling and ascribing meaning to dream experiences elicits awe—an epistemic emotion produced by appraisals of vastness and a need for cognitive accommodation. In turn, because awe reduces individuals’ focus on themselves and their concerns, we argue that experiencing awe upon awakening increases employee resilience, and ultimately goal progress, throughout the workday. However, because awe entails a felt need to expand one’s existing ways of knowing, employees may vary in their receptivity to awe. We therefore argue that the link between awe and resilience hinges on trait epistemic curiosity, or employees’ inherent desire to seek and acquire new knowledge. Across three studies—including a morning-of field study, a single-day morning–afternoon study, and a two-week experience sampling study—we find that ascribing positive meaning to dreams elicits awe, which in turn promotes resilience, and ultimately goal progress, throughout the workday.

**Research Question**
How and for whom do dreams spill over to shape daily work behavior? Specifically, the authors ask how ascribing meaning to recalled dreams elicits waking awe that promotes employee resilience and goal progress throughout the workday, and whether trait epistemic curiosity moderates the awe-resilience link.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Positive dream meaning ascription is positively related to morning awe.
- H2: Morning awe is positively related to employee resilience.
- H3: Morning awe mediates the relationship between positive dream meaning ascription and employee resilience.
- H4: Employee resilience is positively related to work goal progress.
- H5: Morning awe and employee resilience sequentially mediate the relationship between positive dream meaning ascription and work goal progress.
- H6: Epistemic curiosity moderates the relationship between morning awe and employee resilience, such that this relationship is more (less) positive for employees who are high (low) in epistemic curiosity.
- H7: Epistemic curiosity moderates the indirect effect of positive dream meaning ascription on employee resilience, via awe, such that this effect is more (less) positive for employees who are high (low) in epistemic curiosity.
- H8: Epistemic curiosity moderates the sequential indirect effect of positive dream meaning ascription on work goal progress, via awe and resilience, such that this effect is more (less) positive for employees who are high (low) in epistemic curiosity.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Positive (and negative) dream meaning ascription upon awakening
- DV(s): Employee resilience (adaptive appraisal of work stressors) and, ultimately, daily work goal progress
- Mediators: Waking awe (sequential mediation: dream meaning ascription → awe → resilience → goal progress)
- Moderators: Trait epistemic curiosity (amplifies the awe → resilience relationship)

Integrating research on dream meaning ascription with appraisal theories of emotion, the authors argue that forming the belief that a dream carries meaning triggers core awe appraisals—perceptions of vastness and a need for cognitive accommodation—giving rise to awe. Through an appraisal-tendency mechanism, awe induces a "small-self" frame that makes subsequent work stressors feel less threatening, increasing resilience and downstream goal progress. Because awe requires expanding one's existing ways of knowing, its benefits accrue mainly to employees high in epistemic curiosity.

**Data & Measures**
Three self-report survey studies of full-time U.S. employees residing in Eastern Standard Time, each timed so that dream recall, meaning ascription, and awe were captured shortly after awakening: Study 1, a single morning-of survey of business-school alumni (119 alumni who recalled a dream); Study 2, a single-day morning-afternoon MTurk study (63 employees who recalled a dream); Study 3, a two-week twice-daily experience sampling MTurk study (411 participants passed the attention check; the final analytic sample was 1,321 dream-recall days across 215 participants who recalled dreams on at least four study days). Items were rated 1 (Strongly disagree) to 7 (Strongly agree) unless noted.
- Dream meaning ascription (IV, morning survey, all three studies): two single items adapted from Morewedge and Norton (2009) under the stem "To me, my dreams from last night ...", capturing positive ("Have a good meaning") and negative ("Have a bad meaning") ascription separately.
- Morning awe (mediator): the two items "Awe" and "Wonder". Studies 1 and 2 used a dream referent, "My dreams from last night made me feel a sense of ..." (α = .77 and .88); Study 3 shifted the referent to the morning itself, "This morning, I have felt a sense of ..." (α = .95), so that waking awe could be distinguished from awe felt during the dream.
- Resilience (mediator / proximal DV): Study 1 used three items adapted from Tugade and Fredrickson (2004) appraising a one-minute essay each participant wrote about their most important current work problem (α = .85); Studies 2 and 3 used four afternoon items adapted from Sinclair and Wallston (2004) under the stem "At work today, I ..." (α = .82 and .86).
- Work goal progress (distal DV, Studies 2 and 3): two afternoon items adapted from Wanberg, Zhu, and Van Hooft (2010) (α = .96 and .95).
- Trait epistemic curiosity (moderator, Study 3 preliminary survey): three items from Litman and Spielberger (2003) (α = .89). Study 3 additionally measured appraisals of dream vastness with two items adapted from Piff et al. (2015) and Shiota et al. (2007) (α = .94) as a manipulation-free check on the awe appraisal.
- Controls in every study: sleep quantity (self-reported hours slept), sleep quality, and trait positive affect (three items from Watson, Clark, and Tellegen, 1988). Studies 1 and 2 supplement the survey data with quantitative text analysis of open-ended problem, dream, dream-meaning, and dream-cause responses, including sentiment scoring against the bing and NRC lexicons.

The design is associational rather than causally identified: there is no randomization, instrument, or quasi-experiment anywhere in the three studies. What identification leverage exists comes from timing and centering — dream measures are collected between 5 a.m. and 10 a.m. and outcomes after work in Studies 2 and 3, controls for sleep and trait positive affect are carried in every path, and Study 3 group-mean centers its Level 1 predictors so estimates reflect within-person deviations from each employee's own average (Level 2 predictors are grand-mean centered). Studies 1 and 2 were estimated as path models with 10,000 bootstrapped samples for indirect effects; Study 3 was estimated as a multilevel path model in Mplus 8.4 (replicated in Stata's generalized SEM) with Monte Carlo confidence intervals based on 20,000 draws. Study 3's baseline mediation model was preregistered; the moderator hypotheses were not.

**Key Findings**
Every hypothesis a given study tested was supported in that study, but the studies tested different subsets: Study 1 tested only H1-H3, Study 2 tested H1-H5, and the moderation predictions H6-H8 were tested only in Study 3.

Study 1 (N = 119, standardized estimates): a 1-SD increase in positive dream meaning ascription was associated with a .29-SD increase in morning awe (p = .001; H1 supported), and a 1-SD increase in morning awe with a .25-SD increase in resilience (p = .005; H2 supported); the indirect effect of positive dream meaning ascription on resilience via awe was significant and positive (0.07, 95% CI [0.010, 0.174]; H3 supported). The model accounted for 11.65% of the variance in morning awe and 6.39% in resilience. Negative dream meaning ascription was unrelated to morning awe (-.00, p = .999) and had no significant indirect effect on resilience (-0.00, 95% CI [-0.048, 0.041]).

Study 2 (N = 63, standardized estimates): H1-H5 all supported — positive dream meaning ascription to morning awe (+.45 SD, p < .001), morning awe to resilience (+.28 SD, p = .015), indirect effect on resilience 0.09 (95% CI [0.002, 0.246]), resilience to work goal progress (+.41 SD, p < .001), and the sequential indirect effect of positive dream meaning ascription on goal progress via awe and resilience 0.05 (95% CI [0.005, 0.141]). Negative dream meaning ascription's indirect effects on resilience (-0.03, 95% CI [-0.129, 0.009]) and on goal progress (-0.02, 95% CI [-0.079, 0.004]) were not significant. In the text analysis, positive sentiment in participants' dream-meaning reports correlated with morning awe (r_bing = .36, p = .003; r_NRC = .36, p = .004), whereas positive sentiment in their general dream descriptions and dream-cause reports did not.

Study 3 (1,321 dream-recall days across 215 employees, unstandardized within-person estimates): H1-H5 replicated — positive dream meaning ascription to morning awe (γ = 0.21, p < .001), morning awe to resilience (γ = 0.07, p = .035), indirect effect on resilience 0.02 (95% CI [0.002, 0.029]), resilience to work goal progress (γ = 0.47, p < .001), and the sequential indirect effect on goal progress 0.01 (95% CI [0.001, 0.014]). Negative dream meaning ascription again produced no significant indirect effect on either outcome. The awe-resilience slope varied significantly across individuals (τ = .04, p = .005) and trait epistemic curiosity moderated it in the predicted direction (γ = 0.07, p = .010), reducing the slope variability by 21.32%: morning awe raised workday resilience for employees high in epistemic curiosity (γ = 0.12, p = .002) but had no appreciable effect for those low in it (γ = 0.01, p = .885). This is the H6 pattern, although the results text labels that paragraph "Hypothesis 5." The conditional indirect effects were likewise significant at high but not low epistemic curiosity, for resilience (0.03, 95% CI [0.012, 0.040] vs. 0.00, 95% CI [-0.012, 0.014]; H7 supported) and for work goal progress (0.01, 95% CI [0.005, 0.022] vs. 0.00, 95% CI [-0.009, 0.008]; H8 supported).

These relationships hold over and above the sleep quantity and quality controls, which the authors report were of little empirical consequence in their studies. Study 3 robustness checks reproduced the results under alternative operationalizations of positive dream meaning ascription, and neither openness to experience nor perspective taking interacted with morning awe to predict resilience, leaving epistemic curiosity as the operative moderator. A supplemental internal meta-analysis of dream-recall rates across Studies 1-3 and one supplemental study estimated that roughly 40% of employees would recall a dream on any given workday morning (40.18%, 95% CI [27.96, 52.39]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper augments the organizational sleep literature by delineating a psychological (rather than purely biophysiological) pathway—dream meaning ascription—through which sleep spills over into waking behavior. It advances research on emotions by positioning awe as a linchpin between dreams and behavior, and on resilience by showing that resilience can result from emotions occurring prior to stressful encounters, challenging the directional assumption of transactional appraisal theories that appraisal precedes emotion.

**Practical Implication**
Because roughly 40% of working people recall dreams on a given morning and tend to make sense of them, managers and employees benefit from treating sleep as a meaning-laden process. Low-effort practices such as keeping a dream journal or imagery-rehearsal techniques can elicit meaningful dreams and awe; organizations can also cultivate awe directly (e.g., biophilic job design, leadership storytelling).

**Limitations**
Convenience sampling in the time-separated studies (Studies 2-3) may have increased attrition, and analyses rely primarily on self-report data. The time-separated designs lacked a third measurement period, and dream meaning ascription was measured with single items; focusing on dream-recall days created potential selection bias addressed through multiple robustness checks.

**Future Research**
Future work could combine biophysiological and dream-spillover perspectives—e.g., examining dreams as a moderator that offsets or exaggerates the effects of sleep deprivation, or parsing other aspects of sleep. Researchers might use probability sampling, objective and other-report outcome measures, and additional measurement periods, and examine how work outcomes vary solely as a function of dream recall.
