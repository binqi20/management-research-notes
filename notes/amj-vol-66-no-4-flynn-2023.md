# Communication Miscalibration: The Price Leaders Pay for Not Sharing Enough

**Abstract**
Leaders may be seen by their followers as miscalibrating the quantity of their communication—sharing too much or too little. We propose that leaders are more likely to be seen as under-communicating than over-communicating, even though under-communication is more heavily penalized. In Study 1a, we examine 2,717 qualitative comments from archived leadership assessments and find that leaders are nearly 10 times as likely to be criticized for under-communicating than for over-communicating. In Study 1b, we obtain further evidence of this bias using a representative sample of U.S. adults. In Study 2, we manipulate communication (mis)calibration, showing that leaders who under-communicate are viewed as less qualified for a leadership role because they are viewed as less empathic. In Study 3, we use separate measures of employee perceptions of their manager's communication as well as their preferences. When there is a lack of congruence between perceived and preferred communication, employees judge their leaders as lacking empathy and, in turn, leadership ability.

**Research Question**
How does the perceived quantity of a leader's downward communication—specifically the (mis)calibration between how much employees perceive and prefer their leader to communicate—shape followers' evaluations of that leader's ability, and why is under-communication penalized more severely than over-communication?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Employees will identify under-communication as a leadership weakness more often than over-communication.
- H2: When leaders' communication is perceived to be well-calibrated, employees will provide more favorable evaluations than when it is miscalibrated.
- H3a: Under-communicating managers will be perceived as having less empathy than over-communicating managers.
- H3b: Under-communicating managers will be perceived as having less leadership ability than over-communicating managers.
- H4: The negative effect of under-communication on leadership evaluations will be mediated by a perceived lack of empathy.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Perceived communication (mis)calibration / quantity of downward communication (under-communicating vs. well-calibrated vs. over-communicating), operationalized via archival coding (1a), self-report scales (1b), experimental vignette manipulation (2), and perceived–preferred congruence via response-surface analysis (3).
- DV(s): Perceived leadership ability/effectiveness.
- Mediators: Perceived leader empathic concern.
- Moderators: None focal (gender and demographic characteristics tested as robustness controls).

Drawing on social judgment and helping-behavior research, the authors argue that employees read communication quantity as a prosocial signal of a leader's empathic concern. Because of the "curse of knowledge," leaders systematically underestimate how much their followers want and are therefore seen as under-communicating more often than over-communicating. Under-communication sends a stronger negative signal—that the leader neither helped nor even engaged the employee—whereas over-communication signals a misguided but well-intentioned effort. Consequently, perceived under-communication lowers perceived empathy, which in turn lowers perceived leadership ability, producing an asymmetric penalty.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1a: an archive of 360-degree leadership assessments from participants enrolled in an online executive education program at a large private university in the United States; the analysis used direct reports' written answers to an open-ended "areas for improvement" prompt, and two independent coders unaware of the study hypotheses applied a predefined rubric, coding each comment on a binary scale (1 = Yes, 0 = No) first for whether communication was mentioned and then for whether it referenced under-communication ("a lack of communication") or over-communication ("a surplus of communication"), yielding 2,717 usable comments for 924 leaders. Study 1b: a nationally representative U.S. panel recruited through Bovitz, Inc.; communication miscalibration was measured with three items on 1-9 scales whose midpoint represented well-calibrated communication, recoded so that larger values indicate over-communication and averaged into a composite (alpha = .86), then compared against the scale midpoint with a one-sample t-test. Study 2: a between-subjects vignette experiment with full-time employed Prolific Academic participants who read purported assessment feedback describing a fictional leader as communicating too little, too much, or just the right amount; perceived leader empathy was measured with seven items adapted from the empathic concern subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (Davis, 1983; alpha = .92) and perceived leadership ability with three items adapted from Haslam and Ryan (2008) (alpha = .96), both on 5-point likelihood scales, with the Study 1b communication items serving as the manipulation check (alpha = .96); mediation was tested with the PROCESS Macro Model 4 (Hayes, 2022) using a multicategorical predictor that compared each experimental condition to the well-calibrated baseline. Study 3: an online survey of alumni of an MBA program at a private university on the West Coast of the United States; perceived communication quantity (four original 9-point items covering frequency, duration, amount of information, and overall amount; alpha = .82) and preferred communication quantity (the same four items rephrased with "should"; alpha = .67) were measured separately, alongside leader empathy (the Study 2 seven items; alpha = .89), leadership ability (three items on a 7-point agreement scale; alpha = .96), and an exploratory job-satisfaction measure (alpha = .91); calibration was modeled with polynomial regression and response-surface analysis (Edwards & Parry, 1993) following Humberg, Nestler, and Back (2019), with mediation estimated through the Edwards and Cable (2009) block-variable approach and 5,000 bootstrapped samples. All four studies were preregistered. Only Study 2 manipulates communication (mis)calibration; the authors state that the correlational data in Studies 1a and 1b limit their ability to make causal claims, and note in the discussion that the experiment addresses reverse causality and omitted variables while the other studies offer greater generalizability and ecological validity.

**Key Findings**
H1 was supported in each of the three studies that tested it. Study 1a: under-communication was listed as a key leadership weakness in 421 comments (15.5%) versus 46 comments (1.7%) for over-communication, chi-square(1, n = 2,717) = 327.7, p < .001, a relative difference approaching a full order of magnitude. Study 1b: mean communication ratings (M = 4.26, SD = 1.41) fell significantly below 5, the midpoint representing well-calibrated communication, t(353) = -9.87, p < .001, d = .52; of the 273 managers rated as miscalibrated, 74.7% were seen as under-communicating versus 25.3% as over-communicating, chi-square(1, n = 273) = 131.6, p < .001. Study 3: perceived communication quantity (M = 5.66, SD = 1.63) was significantly lower than preferred quantity, t(221) = -8.74, p < .001, d = .59, a negative mean difference of -.88, 95% CI [-1.08, -0.68]. H2 was supported in Studies 2 and 3: in Study 2, perceived leadership ability was highest for the well-calibrated leader (M = 4.08, SD = .86) relative to the under-communicating leader (M = 2.20, SD = .97; p < .001) and the over-communicating leader (M = 3.60, SD = .79; p < .001), F(2, 309) = 130.0, p < .001, eta-squared = .46; in Study 3 the response surface met all four congruence criteria, with the first principal axis slope not differing from 1 (b = .86, 95% CI [0.35, 1.36]), its y-intercept not differing from 0 (b = .18, 95% CI [-0.60, 0.96]), the curvature of the line of incongruence significant and negative (a4 = -.49, p = .001, 95% CI [-0.78, -0.20]), and the slope of that line at the origin non-significant (a3 = -.06, p = .77). H3a was tested only in Study 2 and supported there: perceived empathy was significantly lower for the under-communicating leader (M = 2.93, SD = .78) than for the over-communicating leader (M = 3.64, SD = .65; p < .001) and the well-calibrated leader (M = 3.62, SD = .65; p < .001), F(2, 309) = 35.32, p < .001, eta-squared = .19, while empathy did not differ between the over-communicating and well-calibrated leaders (p = .99). H3b was supported in both studies that tested it: perceived leadership ability was significantly lower for under-communicating than for over-communicating leaders in Study 2 (p < .001) and in Study 3 (M = 4.98, SD = 1.61 vs. M = 5.72, SD = 1.38; t(201) = 2.93, p = .004, d = .47). H4 was supported, but asymmetrically in Study 2: the indirect effect of communication quantity on perceived ability through perceived empathy was significant for the under-communicating versus well-calibrated contrast (b = -.43, SE = .07, 95% CI [-0.58, -0.29]) but not for the over-communicating versus well-calibrated contrast (b = .01, SE = .06, 95% CI [-0.10, 0.12]); in Study 3, the block-variable indirect effect of calibration on perceived ability via perceived empathy was significant (b = .33, SE = .10, p = .001, 95% CI [0.16, 0.53]) and remained significant when controlling for employees' job satisfaction (b = .23, SE = .08, p = .005, 95% CI [0.09, 0.41]).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shows that the trade-offs of communication miscalibration are asymmetric in leadership evaluations: under-communication "looms larger" than over-communication and is judged as a more significant failing, so not all forms of miscalibration are equal. It documents a robust perceptual bias whereby leaders are seen as under-communicating far more often than over-communicating, and it identifies perceived empathic concern as the psychological mechanism linking communication quantity to leadership evaluations—remedying prior downward-communication research that offered no account of how much communication is "the right amount."

**Practical Implication**
Leaders cannot trust their intuition when calibrating the quantity of downward communication; they should adopt independent mechanisms—soliciting employees' idiosyncratic preferences, establishing routine rather than ad hoc communication patterns, and "pretesting" messages for calibration. In most cases leaders should err toward communicating more, accepting the relatively minor risk of being seen as over-communicating to send a stronger signal of empathy and concern.

**Limitations**
The samples were primarily American, so the findings cannot account for cultural differences (e.g., high-context or high-power-distance cultures) that might shape judgments of communication calibration. The theorizing and measures focus exclusively on leadership evaluations as the outcome, leaving aside other objectives such as supporting employee autonomy, and the studies isolate communication quantity while not modeling the qualitative dimension (content, style, delivery) of communication.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine how the quantitative and qualitative dimensions of leader communication interrelate and which matters more in particular cases, including whether qualitative miscalibration signals incompetence rather than low empathy. Researchers should also test whether under-communication can function as deliberate "tough love" that serves employees' developmental or autonomy needs, and how communication expectations vary with hierarchical distance between leaders and direct versus indirect reports.
