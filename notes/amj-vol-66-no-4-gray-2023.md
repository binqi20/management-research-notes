# The Technological Entrainment of Moral Issues: The Case of Genomic Data Markets

**Abstract**
This research considers the role of technology in shaping the moral foundations of emerging digital markets. Through an inductive process study, we examine the development of the market for genomic (DNA) data—an emergent digital market plagued by moral conflicts over privacy and ownership. Drawing on interview, archival, and observational data, we analyze how rules for exchanging genomic data emerged, and how entrepreneurial technology-development moves influenced those rules. Our findings reveal a four-step process, which we call the technological entrainment of moral issues, involving the progressive synchronization of framings, technical policies, technologies (software), exchange platforms, and market participant behaviors. This process baked particular moral stances on privacy and ownership into technological designs, which then dictated the rules market participants were required to follow to engage in exchange. We conclude that when markets are digital, technology processes central to exchange end up controlling many moral aspects of exchange. Our study contributes to theories of morally contentious markets and cultural or institutional entrepreneurship by illuminating how those theories can incorporate material (not only symbolic) aspects of technology processes and better explain the emerging moral order within the rapidly expanding realm of digital markets.

**Research Question**
What role does technology play in shaping the rules of exchange in digital markets fraught with moral conflicts? The authors investigate this in the emerging U.S. market for human genomic data, where exchange generates moral conflicts over privacy and ownership.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: technological entrainment of moral issues; moral conflicts over privacy and ownership of personal (genomic) data; exchange rules; technological framing (designers encoding values into technologies); competing actor clusters (Right-to-Science, Trusted Brokers, Walled Gardens).
- Process / phases: a four-step entrainment process involving the progressive synchronization of (1) framings, (2) technical policies, (3) technologies/software, (4) exchange platforms, and ultimately market participant behaviors.
- Triggering conditions: entrepreneurial technology-development "moves" by actors seeking to advance their preferred exchange rules in an emergent digital market.
- Emergent outcomes: particular moral stances become "baked into" technological designs, which then dictate the rules participants must follow; a technologically mediated coercive isomorphism that locks in some moral stances and forecloses others.

Through inductive grounded theorizing, the authors show that as actors develop technical policies, software, and exchange platforms, their preferred moral stances on privacy and ownership become progressively synchronized and embedded in the material technology itself. Once these interlocking technologies are widely distributed, they impose de facto exchange rules behind the scenes, rendering discursive or rhetorical contestation ineffectual and making the entrained moral order path-dependent and hard to reverse.

**Data & Measures**
The data corpus has three parts. First, 55 semi-structured interviews with 57 people (eight interviewed more than once) conducted between October 2016 and December 2021, comprising 4,314 minutes of audio: 16 GA4GH leaders plus staff and representatives of member and field organizations, and 39 representatives of other industry sectors (scientific researchers, bioinformaticians, health care practitioners and administrators, IT specialists, security experts, ethics specialists, venture capitalists, academic researchers); all were recorded and transcribed except five, for which contemporaneous notes were kept. Second, 19 days (224 hours) of field observation from October 2017 to November 2021 at GA4GH conferences and workgroup meetings, patient advocacy conferences and training workshops, organizational leadership meetings, a bioinformatics technical training workshop, and a GenoPri conference on genetic data cybersecurity, yielding 42 sets of field notes. Third, 914 archival documents, news articles, websites, and videos produced between 2008 and 2022, comprising 293 organizational documents, 141 websites and blogs, 101 scholarly journals and 32 reports, 45 transcripts and video files plus 3 unedited video recordings, 126 U.S. regulatory documents, 81 industry news articles, 82 mass media news articles, and 10 press releases. Sources are cited in the findings as I-XXX (interviews), O-XXX (observations), and A-XXX (archival documents).

Interviewees were identified first by snowball sampling and later by theoretical sampling as the authors' knowledge of the market evolved. The study period was bracketed from 2008, when genomic sequencing costs dropped over 1,000-fold, through May 2022, and the genomic data market was selected as an extreme case. Coding proceeded in stages: a chronology of the main actors and events; open coding of interview transcripts for key players, the moral stances they adopted, actions taken to create exchange rules, and any agreements that emerged, captured in a code book and supported by memo-writing; construction of narratives of events, moves, and proposed exchange rules for each moral issue; a deliberate shift away from narratives and templates toward temporal sequencing of the policies and technologies each cluster created or used; axial coding to tease out the relationships linking the coded constructs (supporting linkage evidence is reported in Online Supplement E); and finally the generation of a means-ends process model. The design is inductive grounded theorizing and claims no causal identification: entrainment is traced as a temporal, path-dependent progression linking framings, policies, technologies, platforms, and behaviors, not estimated as an effect. Only the Right-to-Science and Trusted Brokers clusters could be observed in detail; the secretive Walled Gardens cluster was excluded from the analysis.

**Key Findings**
The central emergent finding is a four-step process of technological entrainment, observed in both clusters studied. In Step 1 technical policies are entrained to the moral stances carried in entrepreneurs' framing of the purpose of exchange; in Step 2 technologies (software) are built to align with those policies, and in Step 2a further technologies are yoked to existing ones by interoperability requirements, so that adopting one technology entails adopting its predecessors and the policies they encode; in Step 3 exchange platforms adopt the resulting interlocking technology suites; and in Step 4 the behaviors of data providers and data users are passively entrained, so that participation itself enacts de facto exchange rules. The process baked particular moral stances on privacy and ownership into technological designs, which then dictated the rules participants had to follow, an outcome the authors characterize as a technologically mediated coercive isomorphism that operates behind the scenes and can render discursive or rhetorical argument ineffectual. Notably, this enactment happened independently of what market participants themselves thought was appropriate, and the rules were largely implicit rather than explicitly labeled as exchange rules.

The two clusters ran the same steps but with markedly different reach, so the downstream steps did not play out uniformly. In the Right-to-Science cluster, a framing of data providers as "partners" entitled to share in scientific advancement was encoded in GA4GH's Framework for Responsible Data Sharing, its Data Security and Infrastructure Policy, and its 2015 consent policy and broad-consent clauses, which in turn catalyzed Machine Readable Consent (2020), the Data Use Ontology (2018) and the Data Use Oversight System, the Direct Connect API, and Passports (2021). Exchange platforms such as the Broad Institute's AnVIL/Terra adopted interlocking suites of these technologies. By the end of 2021 GA4GH counted over 2,000 organizational members, mostly U.S.-based but with over 50 other countries represented, and by 2022 its technologies and standards were in use by the governmental genomics programs of 16 nations, by multicountry driver projects (ELIXIR, H3Africa, the 100K Cohort Consortium), by Amazon Web Services, and by the NIH in its All of Us initiative and its genomic database access tools. The de facto rules enacted were relatively broad consent and no compensation for data providers, and relatively expansive, less restrictive, and unpaid access for data users.

In the Trusted Broker cluster, a framing centered on preserving individual rights produced sticky policies, dynamic consent, and a bilateral consent and trusted mediator framework, implemented through blockchain immutable ledgers, smart contracts, micropayments, Blockstack, and data trust or "Mediator of Individual Data" arrangements. But no specific policies were universal across these firms, organizations disagreed over whether and where blockchain was appropriate (a trade group report concluded it was not appropriate for genomic data), and each organization assembled the components differently, leaving very little interoperability between organizations: entrainment here was intraorganizationally strong but interorganizationally weak. Step 3 was not a separate step for this cluster, because the organizations developing the technology were themselves the exchange platforms. Behavioral impact on the overall market was correspondingly limited: as of 2021 none of the Trusted Broker organizations had grown to the scale of the largest Right-to-Science initiatives (such as NIH's All of Us) or Walled Gardens (such as 23andMe or Geisinger's MyCode), although the cluster's technology choices did appeal to patient advocacy organizations (the Genetic Alliance chose to partner with LunaDNA in 2019) and to some indigenous groups.

The authors trace this asymmetry back to the clusters' moral stances: the Right-to-Science stance favoring greater data sharing led to policies geared toward maximal interoperability both between technologies and between organizations, whereas the Trusted Broker stance favoring data-provider control did not necessitate harmonization and instead favored technologies that limited the ease with which data could move outside a given platform's purview. Greater coordination of technology development in the Right-to-Science cluster appeared to have significant consequences for that cluster's relative growth and hence for the relative scope of impact of its idealized exchange rules, and more market participants appeared to be behaviorally enacting the Right-to-Science approach. The authors are explicit, however, that the exchange rules for the market were not settled once and for all: the rules and the moral outcomes they promote differed by cluster and simply coexisted as three separate market segments, counting those promulgated by the Walled Gardens.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces "technological entrainment of moral issues" to explain how the material (not merely symbolic) aspects of technology processes constitute the moral order of digital markets. It extends theories of morally contentious markets and cultural/institutional entrepreneurship by showing technology as a constitutive force—rather than only a cultural resource imbued with meaning—through which exchange rules and moral legitimacy are determined, and it surfaces exchange-platform organizations and technology developers as central (if less visible) moral entrepreneurs.

**Practical Implication**
Because exchange rules are set implicitly through upstream technology development, policy attention and early regulatory action should focus on the exchange-technology-development process before institutionalization, opening it to broader, inclusive, multiparty societal dialogue rather than leaving it to actors with vested interests and technical capabilities. Civil society and collective-action efforts (e.g., data trusts) could champion alternative exchange technologies that protect collective privacy and ownership interests.

**Limitations**
The study focuses on a single emerging market (U.S. genomic data) and could observe only the Right-to-Science and Trusted Brokers clusters in detail; the secretive "Walled Gardens" cluster (e.g., direct-to-consumer testing firms like 23andMe) was excluded from the analysis because of limited access to their exchange technology and its development.

**Future Research**
Future work could compare technology-centered entrainment with sociocultural models of moral and institutional entrepreneurship, examine how the two relate, combine, or compete to produce market- or field-level settlements, and explore whether entrainment constitutes a fourth form of moral-controversy settlement. Researchers could also investigate the role of platform organizations, the legitimacy and exclusionary implications of converting moral questions into technical ones, and the dynamics of Walled Gardens in market evolution.
