# Signaling Legitimacy: Why Mixed-Gender Coalitions Outperform Single-Gender Coalitions in Advocating for Gender Equity

**Abstract**
Employees often engage in collective grassroots efforts to bring about gender equity in the workplace. Such coalition-based advocacy is largely driven by women, which has led to debate about whether men's involvement as allies can help. Integrating literatures on signaling and legitimacy, we propose that the demographic composition of a gender equity advocacy coalition matters: men-only groups lack "coalition legitimacy," or the perception that they are the "right" spokespersons for gender equity issues, whereas women-only groups struggle to convey "issue legitimacy," or the perception that gender equity is of strategic importance within business organizations. By contrast, mixed-gender coalitions signal both forms of legitimacy, and are thus uniquely effective. We demonstrate these effects over three studies: managers reporting on advocacy coalitions comprising their colleagues (Study 1), an audio-based study showing that the proposed effects are unique for gender equity issues (Study 2), and a stimulus sampling study involving multiple policy proposals (Study 3). Our findings highlight the different forms of legitimacy that women and men bring to the table when selling gender equity issues. We discuss implications for who should be recruited to gender equity advocacy coalitions, and why.

**Research Question**
Does the gender composition of an employee advocacy coalition affect managers' support for gender equity issues, and why are mixed-gender coalitions more persuasive than women-only or men-only coalitions?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Women-only and mixed-gender coalitions are perceived to have greater coalition legitimacy in raising gender equity issues than are men-only coalitions.
- H2: Men-only and mixed-gender coalitions lend gender equity issues greater issue legitimacy than do women-only coalitions.
- H3: Mixed-gender coalitions receive greater managerial support when advocating gender equity issues via (a) greater coalition legitimacy than men-only coalitions and (b) greater issue legitimacy than women-only coalitions.
- H4: Overall, mixed-gender coalitions receive greater managerial support than both men-only and women-only coalitions via the sum of their effects on coalition legitimacy and issue legitimacy.
- H5: Mixed-gender coalitions earn greater managerial support for gender equity (vs. nongender equity) issues via (a) coalition legitimacy when compared with men-only coalitions, and (b) issue legitimacy when compared with women-only coalitions.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Coalition gender composition (women-only vs. men-only vs. mixed-gender); type of issue (control vs. gender equity, manipulated in Study 2)
- DV(s): Issue support (managerial support for the advocated gender equity issue)
- Mediators: Coalition legitimacy (the coalition is the "right" messenger) and issue legitimacy (the issue is strategically important)
- Moderators: Type of issue (gender equity vs. nongender equity); exploratory: managerial sexism

The paper integrates signaling theory and legitimacy theory to argue that a coalition's gender composition signals two distinct forms of legitimacy. The presence of women signals coalition legitimacy (the messengers have material stake and are the appropriate spokespersons), while the presence of men signals issue legitimacy (the issue is strategically important rather than a niche "women's" concern). Because women-only coalitions convey the former but not the latter and men-only coalitions the reverse, only mixed-gender coalitions signal both, making them uniquely effective at winning managerial endorsement for gender equity issues.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (n = 707 U.S. working adults in managerial roles, recruited via Qualtrics, November 2018): a field-based experiment in which each participant named three women and three men coworkers; two of those real colleagues were then randomly selected to form a women-only, men-only, or mixed-gender coalition proposing a salary-negotiation training program to address a gender pay gap.
- Study 2 (n = 974 U.S. MTurk participants, March 2019): a 3 (coalition gender composition: women-only vs. men-only vs. mixed-gender) x 2 (issue type: control vs. gender equity) between-subjects randomized experiment using an audio simulation in which participants played a store manager at "SoleStyle" and heard two voiceover actors advocate either the gender equity training or a customer-service training (control).
- Study 3 (n = 1,553 U.S. MTurk responses, August 2021): a pre-registered between-subjects experiment with stimulus sampling; participants played a regional manager at "Westfield" and saw one of four gender equity proposals (onsite childcare, eliminating salary negotiation as a basis for pay increases, a candidate pool for leadership at least 50% women, an annual women's leadership conference), with coalition composition manipulated via named and photographed employees drawn from the 10K U.S. Adult Faces database.
- Coalition legitimacy: three items adapted from Sherf et al. (2017) in Studies 1-2, e.g., whether the two employees together are the right spokespeople for the issue (1-7; alpha = .83 in Study 1, .87 in Study 2); five items from the authors' own content-validation study in Study 3 (alpha = .90).
- Issue legitimacy: four author-developed items in Studies 1-2 capturing whether the issue is critical, applicable, and of strategic relevance to the organization at large (1-7; alpha = .88 in Study 1, .85 in Study 2); five validated items in Study 3 (alpha = .94).
- Issue support: eight behavioral-intention items in Study 1 (1 = "Extremely unlikely" to 9 = "Extremely likely"; alpha = .97); dollars allocated to the program from a $50,000 discretionary budget in Study 2 (scaled in thousands); five items in Study 3 (alpha = .95).
- Exploratory moderators (Study 3 only): modern sexism (five items from Swim et al., 1995; alpha = .90) and social dominance orientation (eight items from Ho et al., 2015; alpha = .90).
- Identification and estimation: coalition gender composition was randomly assigned in all three studies (Study 1 a field-based experiment with real colleagues, Studies 2 and 3 fully randomized online experiments), and the authors note that Study 1's design allowed better causal inference than a purely correlational study while still leaving unmeasured coworker characteristics uncontrolled. Effects were tested with ANOVA for main and interaction effects plus bootstrapped indirect effects (10,000 replications, bias-corrected 95% CIs) in Stata 16.1; the legitimacy scales were validated in a separate three-sample study reported in Online Supplement B.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 (n = 707): H1 supported — coalition composition affected coalition legitimacy, F(2, 704) = 6.59, p = .002, with women-only (M = 5.10; contrast = 0.33, p = .004) and mixed-gender coalitions (M = 5.16; contrast = 0.39, p = .001) rated higher than men-only coalitions (M = 4.77). H2 supported but with a weak men-only contrast — F(2, 704) = 4.16, p = .016; mixed-gender (M = 5.47; contrast = 0.29, p = .005) and men-only coalitions (M = 5.38; contrast = 0.21, p = .046) raised issues seen as more legitimate than women-only coalitions (M = 5.18), a contrast the authors themselves call not very strong.
- Study 1 mediation: H3a supported (indirect effects on issue support via coalition legitimacy, relative to men-only: women-only 0.11, 95% CI [0.03, 0.20]; mixed-gender 0.13, 95% CI [0.05, 0.23]); H3b supported (via issue legitimacy, relative to women-only: men-only 0.12, 95% CI [0.00, 0.25]; mixed-gender 0.17, 95% CI [0.05, 0.30]); H4 supported (summed indirect effects favoring mixed-gender over women-only, 0.19, 95% CI [0.02, 0.36], and over men-only, 0.18, 95% CI [0.01, 0.35]). There was no main effect of composition on issue support, F(2, 704) = 1.42, p = .241.
- Study 2 (n = 974): H1 supported in the gender equity condition — composition x issue type interacted on coalition legitimacy, F(5, 968) = 14.67, p < .001, with women-only (contrast = 0.82, p < .001) and mixed-gender coalitions (contrast = 0.74, p = .001) above men-only (M = 4.77), and no differences in the control condition. H2 was only partially supported: the interaction on issue legitimacy was significant, F(5, 968) = 5.21, p = .006, and mixed-gender coalitions exceeded women-only ones (M = 4.92 vs. 4.47; contrast = 0.45, p = .002), but men-only coalitions did not differ from women-only ones (M = 4.53; contrast = 0.05, p = .697).
- Study 2 mediation: H3a supported in the gender equity condition (via coalition legitimacy vs. men-only: women-only 1.09, 95% CI [0.43, 2.04]; mixed-gender 0.99, 95% CI [0.39, 1.86]); H3b supported only for the mixed-gender comparison (via issue legitimacy vs. women-only: mixed-gender 1.41, 95% CI [0.41, 2.65]; men-only 0.17, 95% CI [-0.87, 1.23], nonsignificant); H4 supported (total indirect effects favoring mixed-gender over women-only, 1.30, 95% CI [0.04, 2.71], and over men-only, 2.23, 95% CI [0.94, 3.55]); H5 supported (indices of mediated moderation vs. women-only 2.05, 95% CI [0.56, 3.65]; vs. men-only 2.93, 95% CI [1.38, 4.52]), with none of these effects present in the control condition. There was again no main effect of composition on issue support, F(2, 971) = 0.51, p = .601.
- Study 3 (n = 1,553, pre-registered): H1 supported — F(2, 1,550) = 102.45, p < .001, with women-only (M = 5.66; contrast = 0.90) and mixed-gender coalitions (M = 5.60; contrast = 0.84) above men-only (M = 4.76), both p < .001. H2 supported — F(2, 1,550) = 12.13, p < .001, with men-only (M = 5.43; contrast = 0.35) and mixed-gender coalitions (M = 5.41; contrast = 0.33) above women-only (M = 5.08), both p < .001. H3a and H3b were supported (e.g., mixed-gender vs. men-only via coalition legitimacy, 0.27, 95% CI [0.20, 0.35]; mixed-gender vs. women-only via issue legitimacy, 0.23, 95% CI [0.11, 0.34]), and H4 was supported (mixed-gender over women-only, 0.21, 95% CI [0.07, 0.35]; over men-only, 0.26, 95% CI [0.12, 0.40]). H5 was tested only in Study 2, since issue type was manipulated there alone.
- Study 3 exploratory moderation: modern sexism interacted with composition on coalition legitimacy, F(2, 1,547) = 44.30, p < .001 — the women-only and mixed-gender advantage over men-only persisted but was weaker at high modern sexism (contrasts 0.55 and 0.63) than at low (1.65 and 1.24) — and on issue legitimacy, F(2, 1,547) = 3.25, p = .039, where men-only coalitions outscored women-only ones only among higher-sexism participants (contrast = 0.40). Social dominance orientation similarly attenuated the coalition-legitimacy advantage, F(2, 1,547) = 30.81, p < .001, but did not interact on issue legitimacy, F(2, 1,547) = 1.35, p = .26.
- Overall pattern: H1, H3a, H3b, and H4 were supported in all three studies; H2 was supported in Studies 1 and 3 but only partially in Study 2, where the men-only versus women-only difference in issue legitimacy was null (and it was weak, p = .046, in Study 1); H5 was supported in the one study that tested it. Coalition composition never produced a significant total (unmediated) effect on managerial support, which the authors attribute to the two mediators operating in opposing directions in a pattern of inconsistent mediation.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends signaling and legitimacy theories by distinguishing messenger legitimacy (coalition legitimacy) from message legitimacy (issue legitimacy) and showing that the characteristics conferring one need not confer the other, so advocacy coalitions must be composed to convey both simultaneously. It contributes to research on employee coalitions by specifying who should be part of them, and to work on men's allyship by moving beyond individual confrontation of sexism to the collective, coalition-based context.

**Practical Implication**
Employees advocating for gender equity should be strategic in selecting coalition partners, deliberately recruiting mixed-gender coalitions rather than relying on gender-homophilous (women-only) groups, because including men alongside women helps the coalition signal both that it is a credible messenger and that the issue is strategically important to the business. The authors caution, however, that their findings are descriptive rather than prescriptive.

**Limitations**
The studies were experimental and thus limited by their simulated nature despite using immersive paradigms. The threshold of male representation required for a coalition to appear sufficiently "mixed" remains an open empirical question, and the main effect of coalition composition on managerial support was not significant across studies because the two mediators operated in opposing (suppressing) directions.

**Future Research**
Future studies could use qualitative comparative case study approaches to examine how coalition gender composition shapes legitimacy and support over time, investigate the threshold at which a social group is viewed as properly represented rather than tokenistic, and explore additional boundary conditions of the theory.
