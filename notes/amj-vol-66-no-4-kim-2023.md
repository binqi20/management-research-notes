# Corporate Proximity and Product Market Reentry: The Role of Corporate Headquarters in Business Unit Response to Product Failure

**Abstract**
Understanding how organizations respond to failure is important to management research, yet prior studies have offered contrasting findings for whether, in a multiunit hierarchical organization, a corporate office improves business unit search following product failure. To better understand how a corporate office affects business unit search, we focus on the role of corporate proximity (hierarchical, geographic, and cognitive) between the corporate office and constituent units. We argue that corporate proximity improves a business unit’s local search process through two mechanisms—vertical linkages and corporate attention—that positively condition the likelihood of persisting; that is, reentering a product market after having experienced a prior product failure in that market. We find support for our theory using data on reentry in the U.S. medical device industry following exit from the market due to product failure. We also explore how age of the product market and characteristics of the failure—cause and severity— further moderate corporate proximity’s role in business unit reentry. Overall, our study offers a better understanding of how complex organizations respond to failure, thereby contributing to literatures on search, corporate headquarters, and product entry.

**Research Question**
Whether and how a corporate office shapes business unit search following product failure in multiunit hierarchical organizations—specifically, how the hierarchical, geographic, and cognitive proximity of business units to the corporate headquarters conditions a unit's likelihood of reentering a product market after a prior product failure.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1. Compared to market-related exits, product failure-related exits are associated with a greater likelihood of product market reentry.
- H2. Greater corporate proximity increases the (positive) relationship between product failure-related exit and product market reentry.
- H3a. The effect of corporate proximity increases more for products in established product markets compared to nonestablished product markets.
- H3b. The effect of corporate proximity increases more for product failure-related exits involving technical errors compared to those involving user errors.
- H3c. The effect of corporate proximity increases more for severe product failures compared to nonsevere failures.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Corporate proximity (composite of hierarchical, geographic, and cognitive proximity between corporate office and business unit)
- DV(s): Product market reentry following a product failure-related exit (measure of persistence in local search)
- Mediators: Vertical linkages; corporate attention
- Moderators: Product market age; cause of failure (technical error-driven); severity of failure (FDA severity categorization)

The paper draws on the behavioral theory of the firm, theories of search, strategic human capital, and the attention-based view to argue that variation in failure response stems partly from how proximate business units are to corporate headquarters. Corporate proximity improves a unit's local search process through two channels—vertical linkages that transfer corporate resources and assistance, and corporate attention directed at the unit's problem—thereby increasing the likelihood the unit persists and reenters the failed product market. These effects are amplified when the product market is established and when failures are technical and severe, conditions that increase the corporate office's ability and motivation to support problem-solving.

**Data & Measures**
Data sources: the Medical Device Register (MDR) supplies business unit product portfolios, employees, revenue, location and ownership at the business unit–year level; the FDA Medical Device Reporting database supplies adverse events; LexisNexis Corporate Affiliations supplies the corporate hierarchy; Compustat supplies corporate financials. Stand-alone firms and units that could not be matched to Corporate Affiliations are excluded, giving 4,760 business unit–product market–year observations (229 reentries, 157 business units, 686 product markets).

- DV — Product market reentry: binary variable set to 1 in the year a business unit offers a product in a market where it offered none the previous year but had offerings in prior years (coded from the MDR), 0 otherwise.
- IV — Experience of product failure: indicator set to 1 if the focal business unit had any FDA-reported adverse event in the focal product market within the preceding three years (including the focal year). Exits not involving a product failure are treated as market-related exits and serve as the base category.
- Focal moderator — Corporate proximity: composite built by z-standardizing and summing three subindicators. Hierarchical proximity is the number of hierarchical layers between unit and corporate office (distance 0–4, reverse-coded). Geographic proximity is great-circle distance in miles between the two offices, logged and reverse-coded against the sample maximum (8.84). Cognitive proximity combines corporate similar product experience (correlation in product shares across 19 medical specialty areas) and corporate product failure experience (0/1/2 shared adverse-event experience). Individual dimensions are also entered separately in the Online Appendix.
- Contingency moderators — Established product markets: age of the FDA product code since the product market was introduced. Technical error-driven vs. user error-driven product failure: cause coded from the FDA Medical Device Reporting database. Severe product failure: coded 1 when the adverse event involved serious injury or death and 0 when it involved only a malfunction, using the FDA severity categorization.
- Mechanism proxies — Vertical linkages: a link is assumed where a unit executive previously held or concurrently holds a corporate office position, tracked through the MDR executive directory (average 0.75 links per unit–year dyad). Corporate attention: mentions of the unit in the firm's annual report normalized by total report pages, from 479 annual reports covering 91 business units.
- Controls: performance above and below aspirations (splined), unit importance, unit age, number of products in the medical specialty, focus in medical specialty, reentry experience, product failure in other products, corporate size, corporate ROA, corporate slack, corporate reentry experience, number of business units, device regulatory class, number of incumbents, and exit and entry by other firms, plus year and medical specialty fixed effects.

Estimation is a discrete event history analysis using a complementary log-log model, with all explanatory and control variables lagged one year and mean-centered before forming interactions, and robust standard errors. The design is explicitly non-experimental: the authors state that random assignment was infeasible and argue that omitted variable bias and reverse causality are less salient because the timing of product failure is largely exogenous and corporate structure and location rarely change. They address non-random exit with inverse propensity weighting (trimming non-overlapping propensity scores and weighting by 1/(1 − p)), report balance diagnostics, and run a falsification test using future values of the explanatory variables. No formal causal-identification design (instrument, difference-in-differences, or discontinuity) is claimed.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported. Experience of product failure is positively associated with the likelihood of reentry (Model 2, b = 0.755, p = .015). Product failure-related exit more than doubles the reentry likelihood, raising it by 2.2 percentage points, from 2.0% to 4.2%.
- H2 supported. Corporate proximity positively moderates the relationship between product failure-related exit and reentry (Model 3 interaction b = 0.253, p = .002). Product failure raises the reentry likelihood by 4.1 percentage points (from 1.1% to 5.2%) for a unit one standard deviation above mean proximity, versus 2.2 percentage points (from 1.7% to 3.9%) for a unit at mean proximity.
- Unhypothesized result. Corporate proximity has a direct negative effect on reentry (Model 3, b = −0.142, p < .001): units closer to headquarters are less likely to reenter following a market-related exit. The authors did not predict this and offer it as a post hoc observation.
- H3a supported. The three-way interaction of product failure, corporate proximity and established product market is positive (Model 4, b = 0.042, p = .032). In established markets (one standard deviation above the mean) a one standard deviation increase in proximity raises post-failure reentry by 3.2 percentage points (1.8% to 5.0%), against 0.7 percentage points (1.9% to 2.6%) in less established markets.
- H3b supported. The proximity–failure interaction is positive only for technical error-driven failures (b = 0.360, p = .019; +2.8 percentage points, 2.9% to 5.7%); the authors fail to find statistical evidence of the interaction for user error-driven failures (p = .138).
- H3c supported. The interaction is positive for severe failures (b = 0.346, p = .005; +2.9 percentage points, 3.8% to 6.7%); no statistical evidence is found for nonsevere failures (p = .451).
- Mechanism evidence is supportive but indirect. In the vertical-linkages split sample the proximity–failure interaction is positive for units with vertical linkages (p = .028) and negative for units without them (p = .006), though the authors caution that the latter coefficient is large and noisily estimated because of the small number of reentries in that subsample. In the mediated moderation analysis, corporate attention positively moderates the effect of product failure (p < .001); when proximity and attention are entered together the proximity moderation is no longer statistically distinguishable from zero (p = .270) while the failure-by-attention interaction remains positive (p = .032); and corporate proximity is positively associated with corporate attention (p < .001).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study contributes to the behavioral theory of the firm and organizational learning by drawing renewed attention to corporate proximity as a determinant of business unit response to failure, theorizing and empirically demonstrating two operative mechanisms (vertical linkages and corporate attention). It advances the corporate headquarters literature by treating proximity as a multidimensional construct (beyond geographic distance) and by showing corporate influence as a contingent phenomenon shaped by failure characteristics. It also contributes to the literature on determinants of persistence in product entry by positioning corporate proximity as a driver of persistence.

**Practical Implication**
Understanding how structural variation shapes reentry is of value to managers making decisions about proximity via structural design, location, and managerial hiring across units. Although executives rarely relocate corporate headquarters or units, they may channel and redirect attention through changes to structure or cognitive ties, and may configure proximity levers to support performance-enhancing reentry following failure.

**Limitations**
Because the authors only have access to annual data on entry and exit, they cannot capture more granular reentry events (e.g., reentry in the same year as exit) that may also reflect learning. While product failures may cause exits, they do not always do so, so the authors cannot claim exits were caused by a preceding failure in every case (though such "false positives" make the test more conservative). The empirical setting is a single industry over a historical window.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine conditions under which the effects of hierarchical, geographic, and cognitive proximity might diverge (e.g., successful exploration may require close hierarchical but distant cognitive proximity). More work is needed on the performance consequences of proximity's effects on failure response, how and when firms should adjust proximity choices, and how different configurations of proximity levers can create performance-enhancing levels of reentry.
