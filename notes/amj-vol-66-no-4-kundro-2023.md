# The Benefits and Burdens of Work Moralization on Creativity

**Abstract**
Although morality has long been considered an important and integral element of organizational life for employees, research has suggested that moral considerations reduce creativity. However, evidence has hinted that morality may not always be a burden and instead could spark creativity. I develop a model that investigates how work moralization—or the degree to which individuals integrate moral considerations into their work tasks—influences employee creativity. Drawing on regulatory focus theory, I argue that work moralization can lead to prevention-focused cognitions (i.e., moral rumination), which impair and redirect the cognitive resources needed for creativity. However, I also posit that work moralization can lead to promotion-focused cognitions (i.e., cognitive flexibility), leading employees to adopt cognitive approaches that increase creativity. Moreover, integrating research on value congruence, I suggest that work moralizers are the least likely to ruminate—and the most likely to become cognitively flexible—when they perceive that their values are aligned with those of their organization, which ultimately fosters higher levels of creativity. I test my theoretical model with two field studies and a preregistered experiment. This research challenges the consensus that moral considerations generally have a negative impact on creativity, and contributes to research on organizational ethics and creativity.

**Research Question**
How does work moralization—the degree to which individuals integrate moral considerations into their work tasks—influence employee creativity, and under what conditions (value congruence) is its effect a benefit versus a burden?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1. Work moralization is positively related to rumination.
Hypothesis 2. There is a negative indirect effect of work moralization on creativity through rumination.
Hypothesis 3. Work moralization is positively related to cognitive flexibility.
Hypothesis 4. There is a positive indirect effect of work moralization on creativity through cognitive flexibility.
Hypothesis 5. The indirect effect of work moralization on creativity through rumination is less negative (positive) when value congruence is high (low).
Hypothesis 6. The indirect effect of work moralization on creativity through cognitive flexibility is more positive (negative) when value congruence is high (low).
Hypothesis 7. The effect of work moralization on creativity is more positive (negative) when value congruence is high (low).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Work moralization
- DV(s): Employee creativity
- Mediators: Moral rumination (prevention-focused cognition); cognitive flexibility (promotion-focused cognition)
- Moderators: Value congruence (alignment between employee and organizational values)

Grounded in regulatory focus theory, work moralization is theorized as a double-edged sword: it can trigger prevention-focused moral rumination that drains the cognitive resources creativity requires, while simultaneously prompting promotion-focused cognitive flexibility that enables creative associations. Value congruence is the critical moderator that tips the balance—when employees perceive their values align with the organization's, they are least likely to ruminate and most likely to become cognitively flexible, so work moralization yields higher creativity; when values are misaligned, the burden side dominates.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 surveyed employees of an organization responsible for environmental management in 2019: approximately 250 employees were invited and 134 provided complete responses to the core measures (approximate response rate 50%). Unless otherwise noted, all measures used 7-point scales from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Work moralization was captured with a three-item measure the author developed for this paper following Hinkin's (1998) recommended procedures (a = .75), retaining the items "In my eyes, performing my work tasks correctly is a matter of right and wrong; I see ethics as a critical component of my work; To me, performing my job correctly is morally important." Content validation with 120 MTurk working adults showed strong definitional correspondence (average 6.27 out of 7), and a 10-factor CFA on 232 MTurk working adults supported convergent and discriminant validity against calling orientation, workplace meaning, reflective and perceptual moral attentiveness, internalized and symbolic moral identity, moral awareness, and performance and family considerations. Moral rumination used three items adapted from Trapnell and Campbell (1999) to target moral topics at work (a = .85; sample item "At work, I ruminate about the morality of my behaviors"), and cognitive flexibility used three items from Martin and Rubin (1995) (a = .63; sample item "I consider alternatives when dealing with work problems"). Study 1 was estimated by path analysis in lavaan, modeling work moralization as a simultaneous predictor of rumination and cognitive flexibility.

Study 2 invited all 862 employees of a sanitation plant in the southeastern United States in 2019; 622 returned complete employee surveys and supervisors rated 590 of them six weeks later (total response rate 68%). Work moralization (a = .79), moral rumination (a = .89), and cognitive flexibility (a = .61) reused the Study 1 items. Creativity was supervisor-rated with three items from Zhou and George (2001) (a = .97; sample item "This employee comes up with creative solutions to problems"). The model was estimated by path analysis with observed variables in MPlus using clustered standard errors to handle employees nested within supervisory units (average four employees per supervisor), with indirect effects calculated from 10,000 bootstrapped samples. Demographic controls were excluded from the core analyses because they were not correlated with both the predictors and the dependent variable and had no specific theoretical rationale; supplementary models added age, gender, race, and tenure, and separately added moral identity internalization and cognitive engagement as controls.

Study 3 was a preregistered 2 (considerations: moral versus logistical) x 2 (value congruence: high versus low) between-subjects online experiment; 400 working adults were recruited from MTurk and 379 retained after preregistered exclusions (one participant who did not generate three ideas, 17 who wrote nonsense responses, three who failed an attention check). Participants generated three ideas for a municipal park. Work moralization was manipulated by instructing participants to apply moral (versus logistical) considerations to the flora and fauna on the site, and value congruence by telling participants that a consultant "Q-Sort" audit had rated each value they had personally listed as "very highly" valued by the organization (85th-88th percentile) or "not at all" valued (12th-15th percentile); both manipulation checks were significant. Moral rumination (a = .96) and cognitive flexibility (a = .88) used the same three items as the field studies, and creativity was scored by two trained research assistants who rated each idea from 1 (not at all creative) to 5 (very creative), with substantial interrater agreement (ICC2 = .74) and composites formed by averaging the two judges per idea and then the three ideas. Moderated mediation for Hypotheses 5-7 was tested with first-stage models using PROCESS Model 8 and 10,000 bootstrapped samples, a deviation from the preregistered Model 7 so that the direct effects in Hypothesis 7 could be estimated.

On identification, the author states plainly that the two field studies are cross-sectional, that time-lagged surveys were not possible given field-site constraints, and that causal inferences could not be assessed from them, so those estimates are associational. Causal evidence rests on the randomized experiment, in which work moralization and value congruence were manipulated; the author describes this as providing stronger evidence of causality than survey data alone and as an episodic test of the model, and frames the three studies together as a constructive replication.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (n = 134) supported both hypotheses it tested. Work moralization was positively and significantly related to moral rumination (b = .21, p = .01), supporting Hypothesis 1, and positively and significantly related to cognitive flexibility (b = .25, p = .003), supporting Hypothesis 3; both effects held in supplementary models controlling for age, gender, and organizational tenure (b = .19, p = .038 and b = .25, p = .005). Because supervisor evaluations of creativity could not be collected at this site, Study 1 did not test the indirect effects on creativity.

Study 2 (n = 590 employee-supervisor dyads) supported Hypotheses 1 through 4. Work moralization was again positively and significantly related to rumination (b = .15, p < .001) and to cognitive flexibility (b = .13, p = .007); in the path model, rumination was negatively related to supervisor-rated creativity (-.08, p < .05) and cognitive flexibility was positively related to it (.08, p < .05). The indirect effect of work moralization on creativity through rumination was significant and negative (indirect effect = -.02, 95% CI [-.043, -.002]), supporting Hypothesis 2, and the indirect effect through cognitive flexibility was significant and positive (indirect effect = .02, 95% CI [.001, .046]), supporting Hypothesis 4. Both indirect effects persisted in supplementary models controlling for demographics and, separately, for moral identity internalization and cognitive engagement, although several of these robustness estimates are reported at 90% rather than 95% confidence intervals. The author reports that work moralization had an overall nonsignificant effect on creativity in this study, consistent with the two offsetting pathways. A one-way ANOVA on the subset who reported job type found no significant differences in work moralization across job types (F[8, 287] = 1.71, p = .096).

Study 3 (n = 379) reproduced the pattern under random assignment and added the moderation, but with weaker support on the cognitive flexibility pathway than the field studies gave. Moralization positively predicted rumination (b = .49, SE = .18, p = .006), supporting Hypothesis 1, and produced a significant negative indirect effect on creativity through rumination (indirect effect = -.03, SE = .015, 95% CI [-.062, -.004]), supporting Hypothesis 2. Its effect on cognitive flexibility was only marginal (b = .24, SE = .13, p = .064), and the positive indirect effect through cognitive flexibility was likewise marginal (indirect effect = .02, SE = .012, 90% CI [.001, .043]); the author explicitly records Hypotheses 3 and 4 as supported at p < .10 in this study rather than at conventional significance. All three moderation hypotheses were supported. The work moralization x value congruence interaction on rumination was significant (b = -1.98, SE = .35, p < .001), and the indirect effect through rumination was more negative when value congruence was low (indirect effect = -.07, SE = .04, 95% CI [-.141, -.003]) than when it was high, where it was not significant (indirect effect = .02, SE = .02, 95% CI [-.001, .059]); the index of moderated mediation was significant (.09, SE = .05, 95% CI [.004, .187]), supporting Hypothesis 5. The interaction on cognitive flexibility was also significant (b = .63, SE = .25, p = .01), with the indirect effect through cognitive flexibility significant and positive under high value congruence (indirect effect = .04, SE = .02, 95% CI [.01, .08]) but not significant under low value congruence (indirect effect = -.01, SE = .02, 95% CI [-.04, .03]); the index of moderated mediation was significant (.044, SE = .02, 95% CI [.01, .10]), supporting Hypothesis 6. The interaction on creativity itself was significant (b = .37, SE = .14, p = .01): the effect of moralization on creativity was positive and significant when value congruence was high (b = .28, SE = .10, p = .01) but nonsignificant when it was low (b = -.09, SE = .10, p = .39), supporting Hypothesis 7. As in Study 2, the overall effect of moralization on creativity was nonsignificant. Supplementary analyses ruled out effort as an alternative explanation: the interaction did not predict time spent writing ideas (b = 43.12, SE = 29.18, p = .14) or the length of the responses (b = 51.30, SE = 55.94, p = .36). Parallel analyses on global regulatory focus measures showed the same interaction pattern on prevention focus (b = -1.86, SE = .32, p < .001) and promotion focus (b = .73, SE = .28, p = .01).

Taken together, the author's stated integration is that work moralization has a negative indirect effect on creativity through rumination and a positive indirect effect through cognitive flexibility, a double-edged sword with no reliable overall effect on creativity in either study that measured it, and that value congruence determines which side dominates: when value congruence is high the cognitive flexibility path is significant and positive while the rumination path is not, and when value congruence is low the reverse holds. The support pattern is not uniform across studies. Hypotheses 1 and 2 were supported at conventional significance wherever they were tested, whereas Hypotheses 3 and 4 were fully supported in the two field studies but only marginally (p < .10) in the experiment, and Hypotheses 5 through 7 were tested only in Study 3.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper challenges the largely held assumption that moralizing work generally harms creativity, showing instead that work moralization can—and often does—positively influence creativity through offsetting cognitive pathways. It contributes to regulatory focus theory by demonstrating that morality is itself a key predictor of promotion- and prevention-focused cognitions, and it introduces a conceptualization of work moralization that advances research on organizational ethics.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations should not assume that encouraging employees to view their work in moral terms will dampen creativity. Because value congruence determines whether moralization sparks cognitive flexibility or rumination, cultivating alignment between employee and organizational values can help employees who moralize their work translate that orientation into creative, rather than draining, cognitive outcomes.

**Limitations**
The author notes that the field studies could not establish causal inference, motivating the preregistered experiment as stronger causal evidence. Further evidence is needed to investigate the effects of work moralization on creativity and the role of the proposed moderator across additional contexts.

**Future Research**
Future work should investigate how work becomes moralized in the first place, how the content of work moralization influences an individual's behavior, and the antecedents of work moralization in more depth, and should test whether the effects found here apply to other domains outside creativity.
