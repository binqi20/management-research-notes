# The Glass Wall and the Gendered Evaluation of Role Expansion in Freelancing Careers

**Abstract**
People pursuing careers outside organizations often face a career progression paradox: they must develop expertise to find new work opportunities, but such expertise is largely acquired through accumulating work experience to which they seldom have access. Research has proposed that progressive role expansion—starting one's career specialized in a single work role and later expanding into new work roles—would be helpful to navigate this challenge. However, we propose that when women engage in lateral role expansion within professional careers, they face a "glass wall"—an invisible barrier that limits their work opportunities due to a gendered evaluation of role expansion. Specifically, role expansion by female (vs. male) freelancers is seen as less agentic, and such difference undermines female freelancers' perceived competence and commitment. Three studies using different methods (archival data and experiment), industries (music and film), and samples (Korean and American) show that female (vs. male) freelancers who expand into new work roles are seen as less agentic and have less chance of finding new work opportunities. Although women who pursue careers outside of organizations may become unshackled from gender biases embedded within traditional organizational hierarchies, we suggest that they still face obstacles in the new forms of work.

**Research Question**
How is gender bias manifested in freelancing careers that advance outside formal organizations, and specifically, does progressive role expansion (starting as a specialist and later expanding into new work roles) yield the same career benefits for women as for men, or do women face a "glass wall"?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1: Gender will moderate the relationship between role expansion and perceived agency, such that role expansion for female freelancers will be perceived to be less agentic than for male freelancers.
H2a: Perceived agency has a positive relationship with perceived competence.
H2b: Perceived agency has a positive relationship with perceived commitment.
H3a: The indirect relationship between role expansion and perceived competence via perceived agency is moderated by gender, such that this indirect effect is weaker for women compared to men.
H3b: The indirect relationship between role expansion and perceived commitment via perceived agency is moderated by gender, such that this indirect effect is weaker for women compared to men.
H4: Compared to men's role expansion, women's role expansion will result in a lower increase in the chance of having new work opportunities.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Role expansion (specialist who later expanded into a new work role vs. remained a specialist); freelancer gender (female vs. male)
- DV(s): Hazard of having a new work opportunity / new release (Study 1A); perceived agency, perceived competence, and perceived commitment (Studies 1B, 2)
- Mediators: Perceived agency (mediates the effect of role expansion on perceived competence and commitment)
- Moderators: Freelancer gender (moderates the role-expansion → perceived-agency relationship)

The paper argues that progressive role expansion can signal agency—taking control of one's career—but that gender stereotypes act as an evaluative anchor on how that expansion is read. Because women are stereotyped as communal, emotional, and lacking self-control, their role expansion is attributed to situational pressures or impulsiveness rather than purposeful career-crafting, so it is perceived as less agentic than men's. Reduced perceived agency in turn lowers perceived competence and commitment—the cues clients use to award work—producing an invisible "glass wall" that blocks women from converting role expansion into new opportunities. The effect is conditioned by situational ambiguity, which is endemic to freelancing.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1A (archival panel): the Korean Music Copyright Association (KOMCA) copyright-holder list, cross-checked against ManiaDB.com, Melon.com, and Bugs.co.kr, yielding 73,753 songs written between January 2000 and September 2012 and a monthly panel of the 8,168 songwriters who debuted between January 2003 and September 2012. DV = the hazard rate of having a new release, with months since the latest release as analysis time. IVs: Generalist debut = 1 if the songwriter debuted performing more than one of the three copyright-recognized roles (melodist, arranger, lyricist); Role expansion = 1 for periods during and after a specialist debutant first performed a role they had never performed before; Female = 1 for female songwriters, coded from the three public databases plus a majority vote of three native-Korean coders (the three coders agreed in 82.42% of cases and matched the public databases in 93.42%). Controls: Recent activity, Tenure, Prior releases, Performer experiences, Genre specialization (the sum of squared genre shares of a songwriter's songs over the prior three years), Prior success (hit songs on Melon.com's monthly top-100 chart), Experiences with majors, Success as a performer, Success of performers, plus Genre, Year, and Role fixed effects. Estimation: Cox proportional hazard regression with robust standard errors clustered by songwriter, time-dependent coefficients for the covariates that violated the proportional-hazard assumption (Schoenfeld residual test), and a stratified songwriter-fixed-effects model as a robustness check. This study is archival and observational; the paper presents it as a field study and makes no experimental-identification claim for it.
- Study 1B (survey experiment): 78 K-pop songwriters recruited from Korean online platforms (Mule.co.kr, KakaoTalk Open Chats, Instagram, Facebook, Clubhouse) in a 2 (target gender: male vs. female, between-subjects) x 2 (roles: specialized vs. expanded, within-subjects) mixed design, giving 156 participant-scenario observations. Perceived agency = eight items (e.g., "willing to take a stand," "independent," "dominant"), 1-5, alpha = .80; Perceived competence = five items ("competent," "capable," "skillful," "masterful," "intelligent"), 1-5, alpha = .88; Perceived commitment = four items adopted from Heilman and Okimoto (2008), 1-9, alpha = .86. The survey was run in Korean using Brislin's (1970) back-translation, and participants rated how people in the K-pop industry would generally respond rather than their own opinions, to alleviate social-desirability concerns. Estimation: OLS with participant fixed effects, standard errors clustered by participant, and bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals from 10,000 resamples (Preacher & Hayes, 2008).
- Study 2 (resume experiment): 400 U.S. participants recruited from Prolific Academic, 313 retained after a manipulation check, in a 2 (gender: male vs. female) x 2 (role expansion: yes vs. no) between-subjects design. Participants read the resume of a cinematographer, Steven or Sarah, who either continued taking cinematographer projects or had recently added a production designer role while keeping a cinematographer role. The same three scales as Study 1B were used (agency alpha = .70, competence alpha = .84, commitment alpha = .84). Estimation: linear regression with heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors, plus the same 10,000-resample bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals. Random assignment gives Studies 1B and 2 experimental control over the manipulated cues, and the paper states that Study 2's fully between-subjects design "allowed us to draw a more conservative causal claim than the fixed effects model used in Study 1B."

**Key Findings**
- Study 1A (8,168 debutants; 1,847 specialist debutants for the role-expansion test): Generalist debut reduced the hazard of a second release by about 21%, consistent with the value of starting specialized; Female had a significant negative effect on the likelihood of a second release (p < .01) but the Female x Generalist debut interaction was not significant (p = .15). In the role-expansion analysis, the simple effect of Role expansion was positive and significant on the hazard of a new release (b = 0.41, p < .01), the simple effect of Female was not significant (p = .28), and the Role expansion x Female interaction was negative and significant (b = -0.45, p < .01), supporting H4. For male specialists, expanding into a new role increased the hazard of a new release by approximately 40%, whereas for female specialists there was no statistical difference between remaining a specialist and expanding. Results were robust to controls and to songwriter fixed effects.
- Study 1B (78 K-pop songwriters, 156 observations): H1 supported - the Expanded x Female interaction on perceived agency was negative and significant (b = -0.31, p < .05) while the simple effect of Expanded was positive and significant (b = 0.24, p < .01), and women who expanded were rated less agentic (M = 2.73, SD = 0.61) than men who expanded (M = 3.18, SD = 0.66), t(76) = 3.12, p < .01. H2a supported (perceived agency to perceived competence, b = 0.42, p < .01). H2b not supported - no predictor had a statistically significant effect on perceived commitment. H3a supported - the indirect effect of Expanded on perceived competence via perceived agency was significant in the male condition (b = 0.102, 95% BC CI [0.015, 0.253]) but not the female condition (b = -0.027, 95% BC CI [-0.144, 0.026]), and the two differed significantly (b = -0.129, 95% BC CI [-0.329, -0.014]). H3b not supported - neither indirect effect on perceived commitment was significant and their difference was not either (b = -0.039, 95% BC CI [-0.389, 0.294]).
- Study 2 (313 U.S. participants): H1 supported - the Role expansion x Female interaction on perceived agency was negative and significant (b = -0.31, p < .05); here the pattern ran through the female condition, where role expansion lowered perceived agency (M = 2.48, SD = 0.53) relative to no role expansion (M = 2.75, SD = 0.56), t(153) = 3.06, p < .01, while the male comparison was null (M = 2.66 vs. 2.62), t(160) = -0.40, p = .69, and female role expansion was rated less agentic than male role expansion, t(152) = -2.07, p < .05. H2a supported (agency to competence, b = 0.30, p < .01) and H2b supported (agency to commitment, b = 0.48, p < .01). H3a supported - the indirect effect of role expansion on perceived competence via perceived agency was negative and significant in the female condition (b = -0.082, 95% BC CI [-0.152, -0.031]) but null in the male condition (b = 0.011, 95% BC CI [-0.040, 0.068]), difference significant (b = -0.093, 95% BC CI [-0.192, -0.023]). H3b supported - the indirect effect on perceived commitment was negative and significant in the female condition (b = -0.130, 95% BC CI [-0.274, -0.045]) but null in the male condition (b = 0.017, 95% BC CI [-0.064, 0.115]), difference significant (b = -0.147, 95% BC CI [-0.334, -0.036]).
- Support was therefore partial and study-dependent: H1, H2a, and H3a held in both experiments and H4 held in the archival study, but H2b and H3b were supported only in Study 2 (U.S. general population) and not in Study 1B (K-pop songwriter peers), which the authors conjecture reflects industry insiders assuming a baseline level of commitment for any songwriter.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces the "glass wall"—an invisible barrier disadvantaging women who pursue lateral role expansion within professional careers—as a complement to the glass ceiling, glass cliff, and glass escalator that operate inside organizational hierarchies, extending gender-bias theory to nonstandard, organization-free work. It identifies the gendered evaluation of role expansion via perceived agency as the underlying mechanism, and contributes to the generalist-discount/advantage debate by showing that gender stereotypes anchor whether generalism is read as competent agency or as erratic situational drift.

**Practical Implication**
Freelancers (especially women) may mitigate the bias by presenting their practice under an incorporated business name to shift client attention from demographic cues to work portfolios. Clients are cautioned that stereotype-tainted evaluations lead to suboptimal hiring and that female generalists may be an undervalued labor pool; policymakers could accredit licensing schemes that let women credibly signal agentic investment in acquiring new work roles.

**Limitations**
The empirical setting is confined to high-skill creative freelancing (music and film), so generalizability to low-skill platform work (e.g., Uber, Mechanical Turk) and to part-time or transitional freelancing remains untested. Both focal industries are male-dominated, which heightens the salience of gender and may inflate the observed bias, leaving open whether the glass wall appears in female-dominated industries.

**Future Research**
Future work should test whether the glass wall extends beyond high-skill creative freelancing to low-skill gig work and to traditional careers being reformed toward greater worker autonomy, and whether it appears in female-dominated industries. Scholars are also encouraged to examine additional parallel paths to the glass wall—including demand-side algorithmic matching and supply-side self-selection into specialization—and how they may reinforce one another.
