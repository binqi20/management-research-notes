# How Organizations Influence Interpersonal Trust Repair: The Case of a French Antiterrorist Unit

**Abstract**
Organizations that increasingly rely on interdependent teams, such as project teams, often imply work that is important, improvisational, and interdependent. However, these are the very same task conditions where trust is most fragile and in need of repair. As a result, organizations may wish to intervene in repairing interpersonal trust among members. Through an inductive qualitative study of a French antiterrorist unit, we move beyond dyadic treatments of interpersonal trust repair to theorize a model that elucidates the organizational practices that can be used to guide members through the trust repair process. We also induce core mechanisms, such as establishing trust violation rules and providing trust templates, that explain why these organizational practices work to repair interpersonal trust. We suggest how this organizationally directed trust repair process can lead to full, partial, or failed trust repair for trust violators, with consequences on whether and how these violators may be reintegrated into the organization. We conclude by discussing the contributions of our research on trust repair, as well as on how organizations treat organizational wrongdoers, and suggest several avenues for future research.

**Research Question**
What role, if any, does an organization play in facilitating interpersonal trust repair among its members — that is, how might an organization broker the relationship between a wrongdoer and the person(s) whose trust has been violated? The authors examine this in an elite antiterrorism organization, the GIGN.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: trust as "capital" eroded by mistakes; the dilemma that high-stakes interdependent work both requires trust and makes it fragile; trust violations versus trust repair.
- Organizational practices: communicating trust standards through selection and socialization; conducting debriefing sessions about violations; using practice, drills, and simulations; team-leader assessments of trust repair; and role assignments on missions.
- Core mechanisms (the "why"): (a) establishing trust violation rules based on trust standards, (b) detecting violations and assigning blame, (c) providing a trust repair template and opportunities, and (d) rendering and communicating an organizational trust judgment (first by the mission team leader, then by all team leaders).
- Emergent outcomes: full, partial, or failed interpersonal trust repair, with downstream consequences for whether and how violators are reintegrated into mission teams.
- Boundary conditions: the "mesh size" (tightness) of trust violation rules and the rigidity of the repair template vary with the organization's task requirements (e.g., how consequential, complex, and interdependent the work is).

The paper theorizes a "Big O" (organizational) process model in which the organization, not just the trustor–trustee dyad, structures interpersonal trust repair. Because all GIGN mistakes are treated as trust violations and repaired through a single standardized process that mirrors selection and socialization (fix character before competence), the organization can repair and reintegrate scarce expert operatives relatively quickly and unambiguously, signaling restored trust to future teammates via mission assignments.

**Data & Measures**
Three data-collection strategies were used and triangulated: semi-structured interviews, semi-overt participant observation, and archival data gathering. The first author completed 42 formal, one-on-one semi-structured interviews — a final interview sample of 17 officers and 25 NCOs, all but two informants male — conducted in French in an isolated room at GIGN headquarters, averaging one hour (range 30 to 180 minutes), transcribed to English and back-translated for accuracy; three officers and three NCOs were interviewed repeatedly (three up to 30 follow-up conversations each over four years) to clarify aspects of the work and provide member checks, with informal conversations captured in field notes. The protocol was modified over time as the research question shifted toward trust repair, and the final interview count was set by "theoretical saturation." Semi-overt participant observation covered the selection process ("pre-stage") over a five-week period at an average of 12 hours per day (about 360 hours), living and training alongside about 50 candidates and 15 instructors, with video diaries, audio records, and field notes usually written within 24 hours of leaving the field. Archival documents — news articles about GIGN interventions, documentaries about the unit and its selection and training processes, and the specialized magazine Gend'info — supplied context but were explicitly not subject to formal analysis. Sampling was purposeful and evolved with the emerging theory: an initial wave of 10 interviews on trust formation redirected the study to trust repair, after which sampling broadened to former mission members who now train and evaluate operatives, junior and senior members (six months to over 15 years of tenure), and one psychiatrist.

Focal constructs were operationalized through grounded-theory coding rather than measurement instruments. Open coding stayed close to informants' language (e.g., losing "percentages" of "trust capital"), and open codes were then aggregated into axial codes: statements about carrying rocks as punishment or having a "Godfather" mentor were grouped under "socialization practices"; statements such as "all mistakes erode trust," "multiple mistakes eat up trust capital more quickly," and "there are no unforgivable mistakes" were combined into "trust violation rules"; and codes on looking for "character" during selection, the "necessity of admitting your own mistakes," the importance of "competence" in training, and the use of practices, drills, and simulations became "trust repair template." The focal outcome was coded from informants' own trust statements — "I would trust him on any mission," "I would only let that operative drive the car," "I would not go with that operative on a mission" — abstracted to full, partial, or no trust repair. Axial codes were then mapped visually on a whiteboard, organizational practices at the top and interpersonal trust repair dynamics at the bottom, and the mechanism linking each pair was induced. Trustworthiness procedures included an insider-outsider approach with the second author as "devil's advocate," regular peer debriefings during collection, multiple member checks with key informants, and newly gathered data on three "intact" trust repair incidents illustrating the whole model, obtained where possible from both the trust violator and the organizational actor running the repair. The design is inductive and theory-elaborating; it makes no causal-identification claim and reports no statistical estimates.

**Key Findings**
The central emergent finding is a process model in which the organization, not only the trustor-trustee dyad, structures interpersonal trust repair. Five organizational practices — communicating trust standards through selection and socialization, debriefing about trust violations, practice and training (drills and simulations) to reaffirm trust standards, team-leader assessment of trust repair, and role assignment on missions — work through four mechanisms: (a) establishing trust violation rules, (b) detecting trust violations and assigning blame, (c) providing a trust repair template and opportunities, and (d) rendering and communicating an organization trust judgment, first by the team leader of the mission in which trust was violated and then by all team leaders. Taken together, the organization supplies a process for how, when, and where individuals can repair trust following a violation.

Two trust standards operate at the GIGN: an operative has the trust of others if they demonstrate excellence in character and competence, in that order, and if their "trust bank" is at 100% prior to a mission. These standards underlie three trust violation rules: all mistakes, whether in drills or missions, are trust violations that erode trust capital; violations become harder to rectify as they recur, with dishonesty and lying by omission treated most severely; and no mistake is unforgivable, so the trust bank can be restored to 100%. Because missions and training cannot be stopped mid-stream, detection and blame assignment occur collectively in post-mission debriefings involving everyone who took part, with the team leader charged with ensuring that all mistakes are identified and blame assigned. That collective judgment is expected to be final and is not open to debate, so justifying, rationalizing, or deflecting blame is treated as unacceptable — which, the authors argue, keeps the team from being bogged down by blame denial.

The trust repair template is standardized and public: violators must first acknowledge the violation and take the blame, reaffirming good character, and only then perform flawlessly in subsequent practice, drills, and simulations, reaffirming competence. This two-stage sequence mirrors the character-then-competence ordering of pre-stage and stage probatoire, and reaffirming character alone is necessary but not sufficient — "the debriefing alone is not enough." The same template applied to all violators regardless of the personal relationships they had with team members or leaders. Against the dyadic literature's expectation that repair tactics shift with violation type and relationship, the authors report being struck by the similarity in patterns of how trust was repaired across all situations, and found that violators who successfully went through the standardized organizational process were fully trusted again on subsequent missions.

Repair outcomes are rendered and signaled through role assignment. Full trust repair is communicated by allowing the violator back on missions without restrictions in an important role, at which point mistakes are forgotten and the violator's "slate" is wiped clean. Partial trust repair is communicated by assignment to less central, more peripheral roles. Failed repair is communicated by no longer assigning the violator to missions or by cancelling missions already assigned; the person is then socially ostracized (not greeted in corridors, not invited to eat with others in the cafeteria) and ultimately transferred out of the GIGN or quits. Partial trust repair is explicitly provisional and transitory, converting over time into either full repair — if the operative shows "progress and willingness to improve" — or failure if the template is ignored again. The difference between partially repairing trust and failing to do so resides primarily in the number of current infringements against the template, not the number of mistakes committed over time: many mistakes can all be repaired, but continual failure to repair them via the template makes trust irreparable. Outright failure was described as very uncommon, and the authors note that the GIGN does attempt to keep its operatives given the rarity of their expertise.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study develops a process model of how organizations influence interpersonal trust repair, contributing to the "third wave" of trust research that looks beyond the trustor–trustee dyad to third parties such as the organization. It shows that what is usually treated as a dyadic, idiosyncratic phenomenon can in fact be collective and standardized, and it bridges the trust-repair literature with research on how organizations deal with wrongdoers (discipline, reinstatement, reintegration).

**Practical Implication**
Coworkers are unlikely to fully trust reinstated violators on the strength of an organizational "stamp of approval" alone; managers should attend to reintegration, not just punishment. The authors suggest that shared agreement on what counts as a violation and how it is repaired, transparency in detection and the remediation process, and trust in the organization and its practices are conditions that make organizationally directed trust repair effective.

**Limitations**
As a theory-elaborating qualitative study of a single antiterrorist unit, transferability and generalizability are constrained; findings apply most directly to similar elite, paramilitary, or project-team organizations that must retain experts after trust violations. The sensitive nature of the missions prevented the researchers from attending violation-detection and blame-assignment meetings or tracking specific incidents in real time, and the studied organization already had functional trust standards, rules, and templates in place.

**Future Research**
Future work could track individuals longitudinally to identify additional ways organizations become involved at different points in the repair process, follow a comparable organization to see how trust standards, rules, and templates form, and examine cases where organizational attempts to repair interpersonal trust fail. Researchers might also probe boundary conditions such as the "mesh size" of violation rules and the rigidity of repair templates across contexts.
