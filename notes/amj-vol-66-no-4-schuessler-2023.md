# “We Can’t Compete on Human Rights”: Creating Market-Protected Spaces to Institutionalize the Emerging Logic of Responsible Management

**Abstract**
To what extent are multinational corporations able to address societal challenges through voluntary corporate social responsibility (CSR)? Based on an in-depth qualitative study of how apparel multinational corporations have addressed labor standards violations since the deadly 2013 Rana Plaza factory collapse, we show how CSR managers navigate the tension between the emerging responsible management logic and the highly institutionalized market logic, revealing how some go beyond accommodating responsible management within the market by prioritizing responsible management in market-protected spaces. We theorize the construction of market-protected spaces as a multilevel mechanism for institutionalizing an emerging logic in the context of a field dominated by the market logic via three forms of institutional work: restraining the jurisdiction of the market logic, infusing the responsible management logic with nonmarket elements, and maintaining market-protected spaces against resistance. A “marketprotected space” is an institutionally bound space that suspends the dominance of the market logic on selected issues based on a binding institutional infrastructure that allows prioritizing responsible management—unlike voluntary CSR, which still prioritizes the market logic. The concept of a market-protected space maps a path for policy-makers, managers, and other actors interested in institutionalizing responsible management in the global economy.

**Research Question**
To what extent can multinational corporations address societal challenges through voluntary CSR, and how can firm- and field-level actors overcome the systemic constraints of the dominant market logic to institutionalize the emerging logic of responsible management?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: tension between the emerging responsible management logic and the highly institutionalized market logic; the "market-protected space" as an institutionally bound space that suspends the dominance of the market logic on selected issues.
- Approaches identified: (a) prioritizing the market logic, (b) accommodating responsible management within the market logic, and (c) prioritizing responsible management in market-protected spaces.
- Institutional work (process): restraining the jurisdiction of the market logic; infusing the responsible management logic with nonmarket elements; maintaining market-protected spaces against resistance.
- Boundary conditions: construction relies on pre-institutionalized market-constraining actors (e.g., trade unions) to act as legal representatives of rights holders; spaces are fragile, contested, and require continuous maintenance.

Through three interlocking forms of institutional work, firm- and field-level actors jointly construct a binding institutional infrastructure that brackets the market logic on selected issues (e.g., fire/building safety, living wages) so that responsible management can be prioritized rather than merely accommodated. The authors theorize this as a multilevel mechanism for institutionalizing an emerging logic within a field dominated by a highly institutionalized rival logic, framed as cyclical embedding and disembedding of the market (Polanyi) rather than linear progress.

**Data & Measures**
- Firm-level corpus: at least two semi-structured interviews per firm across the 20 German and UK apparel retailers and brands — 20 with CSR managers (782 pages of transcript) and 20 with purchasing managers (405 pages) — conducted between March 2016 and August 2018, plus 13 follow-up interviews (69 pages) covering 13 firms in 2018–2019; 83 firm communication documents (corporate, sustainability and progress reports, supplier codes of conduct, responsible purchasing and human rights policies, modern slavery statements) plus corporate website content and press releases.
- Field-level corpus: 40 formal stakeholder interviews (743 pages) with trade unions, NGOs, industry experts, investors, legal experts, government representatives and importers, plus 33 informal talks, collected 2015–2019; notes from 63 industry events (430 pages) attended between 2016 and 2019; and 230 newsletters (210 pages of text) systematically collected from the UK Ethical Trading Initiative and the German Textile Partnership.
- Coding of the focal constructs: an inductive process moving iteratively between data, emerging themes and academic literature (Gioia, Corley, & Hamilton, 2013; Locke, 2001; Strauss & Corbin, 1998). Field material was first read closely to build a timeline of key events. Firm-level interviews and CSR-related firm documents were then coded in the actors' own terms as in vivo codes — capturing the aims of their responsibility efforts, to whom they attributed responsibility for labor standards, and where they saw the locus of intervention — which were grouped into first-order concepts, then second-order themes, then three aggregate dimensions corresponding to the three approaches (Figure 1). Several rounds of coding used NVivo plus coding in tables.
- Second analytic stage: with the construction of market-protected spaces identified as the core phenomenon, the authors zoomed into data from responsibility-prioritizing firms, added the entire field-level dataset, and related second-order codes to one another to specify conditions and actors, core mechanism, and consequences, connecting these to the institutional-logics dimensions of Thornton and Ocasio (2008) and Thornton et al. (2012).
- Identification: no causal-identification strategy is claimed and none is used. The design is interpretive and inductive, theorizing a process from qualitative data rather than estimating effects. Interview data were triangulated against documentary evidence, NGO studies and news coverage, and initial findings were tested at stakeholder workshops in Berlin, Geneva, Oslo and Dhaka and with the ILO Better Work program.

**Key Findings**
- Three approaches by which MNCs addressed responsible management demands were apparent in the data, differing in the meaning given to each logic and in organization- and field-level structures and practices: (a) prioritizing the market logic — deflecting responsibility, baseline CSR standards and minimum supplier compliance, joining business-led initiatives and lobbying against binding regulation; (b) accommodating responsible management within the market logic — acknowledging responsibility, formalizing CSR procedures (CSR veto rights on supplier onboarding, CSR information systems, win-win investments), and supporting voluntary industry standards and MSIs; (c) prioritizing responsible management in market-protected spaces — redefining labor standards as "pre-competitive", changing core business practices (notably responsibility-focused KPIs for purchasing staff), and developing a binding institutional infrastructure through lobbying for stricter regulation and forging sectoral agreements with unions.
- The pattern across firms is skewed rather than uniform: the first two approaches were pursued by the majority of firms and only a small number pursued the third; within that group, responsibility-focused purchasing KPIs were practiced by only a handful even of the most advanced firms. Only the third approach counts as an attempt to institutionalize responsible management as separate from the market logic. All market-prioritizing firms in the sample were based in Germany and were members of the Business Social Compliance Initiative (BSCI).
- Two field initiatives are the paper's prime examples of market-protected spaces: the Accord on Fire and Building Safety in Bangladesh (2013, signed by over 200 mostly European MNCs and the global union federations IndustriALL and UNI) and ACT (2015). Their "bindingness" rests on union enforcement — for the Accord, a multistage grievance procedure ending in final and binding arbitration — which prevents signatories from undercutting one another on safety.
- Four enabling conditions were identified: the Rana Plaza collapse as a focusing event that shifted the basis of attention; the UNGPs creating a deepening "shadow of the law" and a new source of legitimacy beyond profit; strengthened market-restraining actors (global union federations, the ILO as independent chair, international human rights lawyers) becoming a new source of authority; and strengthened progressive CSR managers gaining new internal power and status. The focusing event alone was not sufficient — contestation, including union threats of public campaigns, was required to push reluctant firms to accept union involvement and sign the Accord.
- The construction of market-protected spaces ran through three forms of institutional work: restraining the jurisdiction of the market logic (a new meaning of responsible management prompting a search for alternative templates, found in binding sectoral agreements); infusing the responsible management logic with nonmarket elements (a binding institutional infrastructure providing a new basis of norms and strategy, and changed core business practices providing alternative formal and informal control mechanisms such as supplier-list transparency and responsibility-focused purchasing KPIs); and maintaining the spaces against resistance.
- Resistance was persistent and the spaces proved fragile: German business associations lobbied aggressively against due diligence legislation, BSCI initially withheld any recommendation that members join the Accord, and the BGMEA opposed extending the Accord beyond its five-year mandate, with the Accord's presence in Bangladesh subsequently challenged in the courts. This pushback strengthened firm-level actors sceptical of the responsibility agenda, so responsible management continued to be resisted even inside responsibility-prioritizing firms, whose CSR managers feared that without continued maintenance work the spaces "might simply fall back to business as usual".
- The authors are explicit about the limit of what they observed: field-level transformation requires time, and they state they were not able to fully observe the downstream institutionalization effect in their data, seeing only the "seeds" of institutionalization from which nonmarket dimensions of the responsible management logic could eventually diffuse. They likewise conclude that catastrophic events such as Rana Plaza do not, on their own, catalyze a change in dominant logics.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper contributes to institutional theory by specifying an alternative way to address tensions between conflicting institutional logics when one logic (the market) is highly institutionalized and dominant. It advances the concept of a "market-protected space" held in place by a binding institutional infrastructure as a distinct alternative to voluntary CSR, and theorizes the multilevel institutional work required to construct and maintain such spaces, informing debates on the limits of CSR.

**Practical Implication**
Binding sectoral agreements—rather than the proliferation of small-scale voluntary projects—can restrain the market in ways that materially benefit peripheral stakeholders such as workers. Firms must align internal incentives (e.g., purchasing managers' KPIs) with responsible management demands, and policy-makers should strengthen market-restraining actors and provide binding regulation, since catastrophic events alone do not shift dominant logics.

**Limitations**
The study examines only one issue (labor standards) in one industry (apparel), so the observed dynamics may be context-specific and not immediately transferable to other grand challenges. The market-protected spaces studied were temporary and fragile, leaving open how the institutional work can scale and stabilize beyond these spaces.

**Future Research**
The authors call for comparative studies of market-protected spaces across grand challenges (e.g., environmental issues), examining which stakeholders can become key allies in restraining the market. Further work could analyze the multilevel political struggles around creating and maintaining such spaces from an open-polity perspective, how practices diffuse from these spaces into organizations, and why firms adopt different approaches cross-nationally.
