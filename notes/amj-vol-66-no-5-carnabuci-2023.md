# When People Build Networks That Hurt Their Performance: Structural Holes, Cognitive Style, and the Unintended Consequences of Person–Network Fit

**Abstract**
Extant research shows that individual cognitive style affects whether employees benefit most from a brokering or a dense network. But do people build the network structure in which they perform best? We address this question by advancing a novel two-stage explanatory model that explicitly disentangles the network formation process from its performance effects. We hypothesize that "adaptors" (i.e., individuals inclined to focus on implementable solutions through commonly accepted and well-defined approaches) perform best when their network spans structural holes. Yet, these same individuals systematically forego opportunities to build relations across structural holes. By contrast, "innovators" (i.e., individuals inclined to focus on envisioning creative solutions that break away with established approaches) draw no or even negative performance returns from structural holes. Nevertheless, their inclination is to build ever-new bridging relations. We test and find support for this counterintuitive hypothesis through a randomized longitudinal field experiment enabling us to disentangle empirically both stages of our theorized process model. Our findings help illuminate why people may build networks that hurt their performance, shed a new light on the role of individual cognitive style in shaping network advantage, and bear concrete implications for organizations aiming to leverage networks to enhance employees' performance.

**Research Question**
Do people build the network structure in which they perform best? Specifically, are adaptors more likely than are innovators to build bridging relationships across structural holes, even though brokerage benefits adaptors' performance more than innovators'?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1 (baseline hypothesis; stage 2): The more adaptive (innovative) an individual's cognitive style, the more (less) building bridging relations across structural holes will enhance his or her innovative performance.
- H2 (main hypothesis; stage 1): The more adaptive (innovative) an individual's cognitive style, the less (more) likely he or she is to build bridging relations across structural holes.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Individual cognitive style on the adaptor–innovator continuum (Kirton adaption–innovation inventory); a randomized executive-education treatment providing exogenous bridging opportunities
- DV(s): Stage 1 — propensity to build bridging ties across structural holes (network constraint); Stage 2 — supervisor-rated innovative performance
- Mediators: Network constraint / brokerage position (treatment-induced bridging ties, instrumented, carry the performance effect in Stage 2)
- Moderators: Cognitive style moderates the brokerage–performance relationship (adaptors benefit from brokerage; innovators do not)

The paper advances a two-stage person–network fit model. People perform best in networks that "complement" (combine productively with) their cognitive style, but they tend to build networks that "supplement" (align with and reinforce) their cognitive style. Because adaptors prefer well-defined, consensus-based, stable interactions, they avoid forming the bridging ties across structural holes that would most benefit their idea-implementation strengths; innovators, who gain little from brokerage, nonetheless keep building ever-new bridging relations. When complementary and supplementary fit are misaligned, people build networks that detract from rather than enhance their performance.

**Data & Measures**
Data come from a 2.5-year research project in the Italian business unit of a global semiconductor company (roughly 170 employees, 13 functional departments, five hierarchical levels), combining about 30 months of field observation and 25 half-hour interviews with four online survey waves at Time 1 (13 months before the treatment), Time 2 (six months before), Time 3 (one month after) and Time 4 (seven months after), plus a Time 1' mini-wave collecting time-invariant traits. Treated is a binary variable coded 1 if the employee took part in the full-day executive-education intervention. Network constraint, the stage 1 dependent variable and the stage 2 explanatory variable, is Burt's (1992: 62–64) constraint computed on the valued information-exchange network at each wave (roster-based single name generator with a 1–4 frequency name interpreter), where higher values indicate fewer bridging relations across structural holes; the natural logarithm of constraint is used in the analyses. Innovative performance is the average of the innovation-dimension items of Welbourne, Johnson, and Erez's (1998) performance scale, rated by each employee's direct supervisor at every wave, translated and back-translated into Italian, and demeaned at the supervisor level to absorb supervisor-level rating heterogeneity. Cognitive style is the Italian version (Prato Previde, 1991) of Kirton's (1976) adaption–innovation inventory, 33 items rated from 1 (very hard) to 4 (very easy) (Cronbach's alpha = .80), where high values indicate innovators and low values adaptors (observed range 45–95, mean about 75, SD 9.2); it is mean-centered before the multiplicative terms are constructed. Balance-check and control variables include departmental dummies (the reported models control for manufacturing), tenure, hierarchical rank, gender, network size, and self-monitoring. Identification rests on the paper's randomized longitudinal field experiment: employees were allocated by disproportionate stratified random assignment at the department level, with 74 invited to the treatment and 102 to the control group (44 actually attended). Estimation uses random-effects panel regressions with standard errors clustered around departments; individual fixed effects are not possible because treated and control groups do not change over time. Model 4 is a random-effects instrumental-variable panel regression using treated as an instrument for post-treatment constraint and for its interaction with cognitive style, and an intention-to-treat correction (invitation to the treatment as an instrument for actual treatment) handles the 33 noncompliers and three always-takers. The authors report that the instrument set is strong for constraint but weak for the constraint x cognitive style interaction (Sanderson–Windmeijer F = 1.67) and add a LIML estimator as a robustness check. Analytic samples are 268 observations (Model 1), 209 (Model 2) and 180 (Models 3 and 4).

**Key Findings**
The randomized treatment worked as intended: Model 1 shows a negative and significant effect of treatment on post-treatment constraint (b = -0.05, SE = 0.03), mean post-treatment constraint was 0.27 (SD 0.16) in the treatment group versus 0.35 (SD 0.18) in the control group (t test of the difference in means, p = .002), treated employees' networks were over 20% more brokering, and treated employees formed on average 2.6 more new bridging ties one month after the intervention and three more after seven months. Stage 1 (H2) is supported: the Treated x Cognitive style (innovator) interaction is negative and highly significant for post-treatment constraint (b = -0.05, SE = 0.02), so that given the same set of bridge-formation opportunities adaptors were significantly less likely than innovators to leverage them. Predictive margins show that treated and untreated adaptors (one SD below the mean) had statistically indistinguishable post-treatment constraint, whereas treated innovators (one SD above the mean) reduced their constraint by about one third relative to control innovators; descriptively, adaptors created an average of 2.5 new bridging ties against 7.4 for innovators. Stage 2 (H1) is supported, but only at the 10% level: the Cognitive style (innovator) x Constraint interaction on innovative performance is positive both in the random-effects panel model (b = 0.14, SE = 0.08, p < .10) and in the instrumental-variable model (b = 0.43, SE = 0.26, reported p = .071), while the constraint main effect is negative (b = -0.12, SE = 0.06 in Model 3; b = -0.36, SE = 0.17 in Model 4). Because higher constraint means fewer bridging ties, the positive interaction means adaptors' performance benefits from bridging structural holes while innovators draw no or even negative returns: a markedly adaptive employee (two SD below the mean) moving from a highly constrained to a highly brokering network is predicted to gain nearly 60% in innovative performance (from 2.74 to 4.35 on a 1-to-5 scale), and a slightly adaptive one (one SD below) gains 16% (3.41 to 3.81), whereas for an innovator one SD above the mean the same shift has hardly any effect (3.56 to 3.53) and for one two SD above the mean it lowers performance by 16% (from 3.85 to 3.23). Robustness checks using all 12 department dummies, two-way clustering, the intention-to-treat correction, a fully interacted stratification model, and lagged dependent-variable specifications are consistent in direction and significance, with the exception that the treatment x cognitive style interaction loses significance when all departments are controlled for (b = -0.04, SE = 0.02, p = .10).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends the cognitive-microfoundations stream of network theory by showing that cognitive style shapes the brokerage–performance relationship in a more complex, counterintuitive way than prior contingency accounts: it governs not only who benefits from structural holes but also who builds them, and the two are misaligned. By integrating network formation and network performance within a single explanatory framework, it explains why large numbers of employees build networks that are not good for their performance, contributing to both structural-holes and person–environment fit research.

**Practical Implication**
Organizations investing in network-building interventions should tailor them to employees' cognitive styles rather than encouraging brokerage uniformly. Adaptors should be helped to bridge structural holes (which they avoid but benefit from), whereas innovators are better served by deepening cohesive networks optimized for idea implementation; even a single day of executive education can shift employees' network positions.

**Limitations**
Evidence is from a single business unit of one semiconductor company in Italy, which may limit generalizability across settings and industries. Individual-level fixed effects could not be modeled because treatment varied between rather than within employees, so the design relies on random assignment and instrumental-variable estimation; noncompliance and always-takers required an intention-to-treat correction.

**Future Research**
Future work could test the two-stage complementary-versus-supplementary fit model by replicating the analyses across other empirical settings and with other dimensions of person–network fit, and examine additional cognitive or dispositional traits that drive how people build networks misaligned with their own performance.
