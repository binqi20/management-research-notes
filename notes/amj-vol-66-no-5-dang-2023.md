# On the Plurality and Politics of Ally Work: Liberalism and Self, Relational, and Organizational Ally Work

**Abstract**
We theorize that ally work (actions by employees belonging to advantaged social groups to support employees belonging to disadvantaged social groups) can be organized into three modalities: self ally work that is oriented toward developing skills and capabilities, relational ally work that is focused on building positive work relationships, and organizational ally work aimed at changing organizational practices to support disadvantaged coworkers. Drawing from the theory of political ideology as motivated cognition, across multiple pre-registered field studies we found that employees who were more liberal-leaning (vs. conservative-leaning) reported higher engagement in all three modalities. We also examined whether contextual affordances amplified the liberalism effect. Specifically, we tested whether personal accountability for diversity, colleague diversity, and organizational ideology directed liberals toward greater engagement in self, relational, and organizational work, respectively. As hypothesized, more liberal-leaning employees reported higher engagement in organizational ally work in more ideologically liberal organizations. However, the hypothesized effects of personal accountability were not supported. Notably, personal accountability had a positive effect on self ally work among more conservative-leaning employees. The moderating effects of colleague diversity were also mixed across studies. Highlighting both the plurality and politics of ally work, we discuss theoretical and practical implications of our findings.

**Research Question**
What motivates employees in positions of relative social advantage to engage in ally work, and how does political ideology—filtered through contextual affordances—shape the distinct self, relational, and organizational modalities through which ally work is enacted within organizations?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Hypothesis 1a. Political liberalism is positively related to self ally work.
Hypothesis 1b. Political liberalism is positively related to relational ally work.
Hypothesis 1c. Political liberalism is positively related to organizational ally work.
Hypothesis 2. The relationship between political liberalism and engagement in self ally work is strengthened when individuals perceive a higher (versus lower) degree of personal accountability for diversity efforts.
Hypothesis 3. The relationship between political liberalism and engagement in relational ally work is strengthened when individuals perceive colleague diversity as higher (versus lower).
Hypothesis 4. The relationship between political liberalism and engagement in organizational ally work is strengthened when individuals perceive that the organization is politically liberal (versus conservative).

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Political ideology (liberalism vs. conservatism, measured along a liberal–conservative continuum)
- DV(s): Engagement in three modalities of ally work — self ally work, relational ally work, and organizational ally work
- Mediators: None formally tested (political ideology is theorized as the motivational mechanism itself)
- Moderators: Perceived contextual affordances — personal accountability for diversity (self path), colleague diversity (relational path), and perceived organizational political ideology (organizational path)

The paper theorizes that political ideology operates as motivated cognition: a liberal (vs. conservative) ideology functions as a "higher-order want" and "perceptual filter" that leads advantaged employees to "see" their organizations as arenas of marginalization where progress requires challenging the status quo, motivating engagement across all three ally-work modalities. Perceived affordances act as a "push" that narrows liberals' choice set toward modality-specific action. Empirically, liberalism showed a consistent positive main effect on all three modalities, and perceived organizational liberalism amplified liberals' organizational ally work; however, the personal-accountability and colleague-diversity moderation hypotheses were not supported, and personal accountability instead promoted self work among more conservative-leaning employees.

**Data & Measures**
Four studies of U.S. self-report survey data collected in 2021. Study 1 built the measure: an initial pool of 15 self, 15 relational, and 12 organizational items (1 = Never to 5 = Always) was sorted by 20 practitioners and scholars, reduced by principal axis factor analysis with varimax rotation in two online-panel samples (MTurk n = 303; Prolific n = 331) to a 23-item scale (7 self, 7 relational, 9 organizational), cross-validated by CFA in Mplus 8.1 on a Lucid sample (n = 596), and checked for convergent and discriminant validity on a Prolific sample (n = 189). The hypothesis tests used Study 2a (124 undergraduate business students), Study 2b (319 MTurk working adults), Study 3 (374 working adults recruited through online classified advertisements in 12 U.S. cities, cross-sectional), and Study 4 (263 working adults completing both waves; n = 259 in the reported regressions), recruited via a university listserv, social media, and classified advertisements in 30 U.S. cities.
- DV(s): self, relational, and organizational ally work, measured with Study 1's 23-item self-report scale (alphas .90-.96 across studies).
- IV: political ideology, a single-item self-placement on a 7-point liberal–conservative spectrum (1 = Very liberal to 7 = Very conservative), reverse-scored so that higher values signify greater liberalism.
- Moderators: personal accountability for diversity (single item on whether all employees are responsible for developing and implementing solutions to diversity issues, 1-5); colleague diversity (self-reported percentage of day-to-day colleagues in each of seven racial/ethnic, gender, and sexual-orientation categories, averaged into an index and log-transformed for the interaction term); perceived organizational political ideology (single item on the same 7-point liberal–conservative spectrum, reverse-scored).
- Controls: gender, race/ethnicity, LGBTQ+ status, social class (10-rung ladder), and managerial status. Studies 2a and 2b additionally entered social identity, personal benefit, collectivism, social dominance orientation, activism orientation, locus of control, and relative deprivation as competing predictors.

Estimation was hierarchical linear regression with political ideology entered in the last step, with interactions tested using the PROCESS Macro for SPSS (v.4, Model 1) with 5,000 resamples and probed with the Johnson–Neyman technique. Studies 2-4 were pre-registered. The design is associational: the paper claims no causal-identification strategy, and Study 4's four-week temporal separation of the Time 1 predictors from the Time 2 ally-work measures is presented as a remedy for common method bias rather than as identification.

**Key Findings**
Hypotheses 1a-1c were supported in each of the four hypothesis-testing samples: greater liberalism predicted higher engagement in all three modalities. Study 2a incremental variance was ΔR² = .14 for self (p < .001), .04 for relational (p = .002), and .10 for organizational work (p < .001); Study 2b, ΔR² = .04 (p < .001), .01 (p = .039), and .01 (p = .020). Study 3 coefficients were b = .15 for self (p < .001), .10 for relational (p = .001), and .12 for organizational work (p = .001); Study 4, b = .18 (p < .001), .08 (p = .019), and .16 (p < .001). Social dominance orientation was the only competing predictor in Studies 2a and 2b that also predicted ally work.

Hypothesis 2 was not supported in either study that tested it. The political ideology x personal accountability interaction was significant but negative, opposite to the prediction (Study 3 b = -.07, p = .003; Study 4 b = -.07, p = .011): the positive effect of liberalism on self work declined as personal accountability rose and became nonsignificant at higher levels of accountability (above 4.11 in Study 3, above 4.12 in Study 4). Personal accountability's positive effect on self work was instead strongest among moderately-to-less-liberal and conservative-leaning employees and nonsignificant among polarized liberals.

Hypothesis 3 was not supported, and the pattern was discrepant across the two studies that tested it. In Study 3 the political ideology x colleague diversity interaction was significant and negative (b = -.13, p = .001): liberalism's positive effect on relational work weakened as colleague diversity increased and became nonsignificant above 3.34, the reverse of the prediction. In Study 4 the same interaction was nonsignificant with the log-transformed measure (b = .07, p = .062) and significant only with the untransformed measure (b = -.003, p = .033); the paper reports that when graphed this pattern differed from Study 3 and instead matched the hypothesis, with liberalism's positive effect on relational work strongest at higher colleague diversity (above 2.81).

Hypothesis 4 was supported and replicated. The political ideology x organizational ideology interaction was positive and significant in Study 3 (b = .06, p = .004) and Study 4 (b = .07, p = .002): the positive effect of liberalism on organizational ally work became increasingly positive as perceived organizational liberalism rose (above 3.47 in Study 3, above 3.14 in Study 4). Among the least liberal employees (below 2.40 in Study 3, below 3.39 in Study 4), higher perceived organizational liberalism was associated with a decline in organizational ally work.

Robustness checks left the results unchanged when controls were omitted, and the non-hypothesized affordance-by-ideology interactions were nonsignificant in both Studies 3 and 4. In Study 4, adding openness to experience and moral-foundations covariates left liberalism's effect significant for self (b = .15, p < .001) and organizational work (b = .14, p = .001) but rendered its effect on relational work nonsignificant (b = .06, p = .092).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper advances a pluralistic tripartite framework that disaggregates ally work into self, relational, and organizational modalities operating at different levels of the social self, and develops a validated measure of these constructs. It identifies political ideology-as-motivated-cognition as a general motivational mechanism for ally work that is portable across multiple intersecting social categories, moving beyond prior identity-specific or social-identity-based accounts. By introducing perceived affordances, it shows how organizational context conditions—and sometimes reverses—the behavioral expression of ideology.

**Practical Implication**
Managers can encourage allyship by creating affordances tailored to specific modalities (e.g., signaling organizational openness to structural change to elicit organizational ally work). Because self work is relatively accessible and self-focused, affording personal accountability can engage even conservative-leaning or politically ambivalent employees in self-directed change. Where employees resist organizational ally work, leaders are advised to move cautiously and use subtler messaging.

**Limitations**
Ally work was assessed only from the focal employee's self-reports, raising questions about whether liberals are "aspirational allies" whose self-reported ally work aligns with observers' reports. The colleague-diversity hypothesis yielded discrepant patterns, suggesting unmeasured moderators the data could not test; the authors call for further research into how task interdependence and other job characteristics shape these effects.

**Future Research**
Future work should examine how organizational factors such as leadership and culture might offset exogenous political influences on discretionary diversity behaviors, and study ally work within specific identity relations and from beneficiaries' perspectives. Researchers could investigate when it is useful to combine versus distinguish the three modalities, the strong self–organizational correlation, and the political ramifications and potential polarization effects that ally work may produce.
