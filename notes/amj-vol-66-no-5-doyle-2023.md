# Hierarchical Team Structures Limit Joint Gain in Interteam Negotiations: The Role of Information Elaboration and Value-Claiming Behavior

**Abstract**
Although teams of negotiators are widely assumed to be better at unlocking integrative solutions compared to individual negotiators, the interteam negotiation context is characterized by unique challenges that can make effective collaboration between teams difficult. We extend our theoretical understanding of interteam negotiations by offering novel insights about when and why teams realize their potential in integrative negotiations. Specifically, we propose a theoretical model that explains how hierarchical team structures reduce information elaboration within teams, which reinforces "fixed-pie" assumptions that prompt the reliance on value-claiming behaviors between teams and lower high-quality outcomes such as the joint gain achieved. Across four studies, each involving interactive team-on-team negotiations, we provide support for the hypothesized effects of formal intrateam hierarchies on joint gain, and test a useful intervention to mitigate the harmful effects of hierarchically structured teams at the negotiation table. Contributions to the literatures on team negotiations, interteam collaboration, and hierarchical differences within teams are discussed.

**Research Question**
When and why do formal hierarchical structures within negotiation teams shape a team's ability to maximize joint gain in negotiations between teams that have integrative potential?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Negotiations between two teams with formal hierarchical structures will result in lower joint gain as compared to negotiations between two teams with egalitarian structures.
- H2: The negative relationship between hierarchical team structures and joint gain will be mediated by intrateam information elaboration.
- H3: The negative relationship between hierarchical team structures and joint gain will be serially mediated by intrateam information elaboration and the use of value-claiming behaviors.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Formal intrateam hierarchy (hierarchical vs. egalitarian team structure; plus a "blended" condition where one team is hierarchical and one egalitarian, and a solo/control condition)
- DV(s): Joint gain achieved between the negotiating teams
- Mediators: Intrateam information elaboration (Path A) and interteam value-claiming behavior (Path B), tested as a serial mediation
- Moderators: Information elaboration instructions (manipulated in Study 4 as a moderation-of-process test of the proposed causal mechanism)

Formal intrateam hierarchy restricts the free flow and exchange of information within teams, reducing intrateam information elaboration. Lower elaboration reinforces the "fixed-pie" assumption that one party's gain is the other's loss, which fosters competitive value-claiming behavior between teams and ultimately lowers joint gain. Study 4's manipulation of information elaboration shows that providing elaboration instructions attenuates the harmful effect of hierarchy, supporting the proposed causal role of the elaboration-then-value-claiming pathway.

**Data & Measures**
Four randomized between-teams experiments with interactive team-on-team negotiations. Hierarchy was manipulated with written instructions telling teams either to designate one leader with decision-making authority over team decisions or to share leadership and influence equally, and was checked with three self-report items on equal power, influence, and leadership (α = .91–.94).
- Study 1: 318 MBA students (156 teams, 78 negotiation groups) negotiating the Cartoon multi-issue simulation; hierarchy (40 groups) vs. egalitarian (38 groups), with leaders self-selected by the team. Joint gain was calculated by aggregating the profit both parties earned from the agreement (maximum $5,080,000).
- Study 2: 990 undergraduates (949 retained; 390 teams, 195 negotiation groups) negotiating The Sweet Shop; hierarchy (63 groups), egalitarian (66), a control condition given no structure instructions (66), and a solo-vs-solo condition (70), with leaders randomly assigned. Joint gain = points summed across both teams (maximum 21,000). Intrateam information elaboration was coded from the video recordings by four assistants blind to hypotheses and condition, using five items reflecting behaviors identified in Van Ginkel and Van Knippenberg (2008) on a 5-point scale (α = .71); interteam value-claiming behavior was coded from the same videos by six different assistants on three items (α = .88).
- Study 3: 368 undergraduates (164 teams, 82 negotiation groups); hierarchy (27 groups), egalitarian (26), and blended (29, one hierarchical and one egalitarian team), with leaders self-selected. Information elaboration was video-coded with the Study 2 items (α = .72); value claiming was coded from written transcripts using the system of Weingart, Olekalns, and Smith (2004) and scored as the proportion of a team's statements that were value-claiming (Cohen's κ = .814).
- Study 4: 623 undergraduates (282 teams, 141 negotiation groups) negotiating The Player online via Zoom during the Covid-19 pandemic, in a 2 (team structure: hierarchy vs. egalitarian) × 2 (information elaboration instructions: yes vs. no) between-teams design. Joint gain = points summed across five issues (maximum 14,400); value claiming was video-coded with the Study 2 items (α = .81).

Team-level measures were aggregated to the negotiation group level before analysis. Mediation and serial mediation were estimated with PROCESS Models 4 and 6 using contrast codes, 5,000 bootstraps, and 95% confidence intervals; Study 4 used PROCESS Model 7 for moderated mediation. Random assignment identifies the effect of the hierarchy manipulation, but the paper states plainly that the relationship among information elaboration, value-claiming behavior, and joint gain is correlational in Studies 2 and 3 and limits causal inference; Study 4's moderation-of-process design (Spencer, Zanna, & Fong, 2005), which manipulates information elaboration directly, is the paper's basis for a stronger causal claim about the mediator.

**Key Findings**
H1 was supported in Studies 1–3 and held in Study 4 as a main effect that was qualified by the elaboration-instructions manipulation. Study 1: hierarchical negotiations achieved lower joint gain (M = 4,272,054.71, SD = 695,025.74) than egalitarian ones (M = 4,606,335.53, SD = 430,731.55), F(1, 75) = 6.40, p = .014, d = 0.58. Study 2: hierarchy (M = 16,674.60) was below egalitarian (M = 17,280.30), t(260) = 2.42, p = .016, d = 0.42, and below control (M = 17,393.94), t(260) = 2.88, p = .004, d = 0.50, while egalitarian and control did not differ; every team condition beat solo negotiators (M = 15,974.64), but the team advantage was 46% smaller when teams had a formal hierarchy. Study 3: hierarchy (M = 16,574.07) was below egalitarian (M = 17,826.92), t(79) = 2.36, p = .021, d = 0.69, and the blended condition (M = 16,672.41) also fell below egalitarian, t(79) = 2.21, p = .030, d = 0.61, without differing from hierarchy, indicating that one hierarchical team was sufficient to limit joint gain. Study 4: a significant main effect of team hierarchy on joint gain, F(1, 137) = 4.48, p = .036, alongside a significant hierarchy × instructions interaction, F(1, 137) = 10.98, p = .001.

H2 was supported in the two studies that tested it directly. Study 2: significant indirect effect of hierarchy on joint gain through intrateam information elaboration (effect = −385.55, 95% CI [−635.03, −178.86]); hierarchy lowered elaboration (b = −0.36, SE = 0.09, p < .001) and elaboration raised joint gain (b = 1,081.94, SE = 218.45, p < .001), with the direct effect no longer significant (b = −168.62, p = .532). Study 3: indirect effect = −369.56, 95% CI [−940.20, −38.56]; hierarchy lowered elaboration (b = −0.41, SE = 0.13, p = .003) and elaboration raised joint gain (b = 895.05, SE = 437.23, p = .044), with the direct effect not significant (b = −883.29, p = .114).

H3 was likewise supported in Studies 2 and 3. Study 2 serial indirect effect = −57.06, 95% CI [−133.70, −8.21], with elaboration reducing value claiming (b = −0.39, SE = 0.10, p < .001) and value claiming reducing joint gain (b = −413.96, SE = 159.05, p = .010); hierarchical negotiations showed more value claiming than egalitarian ones (M = 3.23 vs. 2.91), t(188) = 2.63, p = .009, d = 0.44, though exploratory contrasts found no hierarchy–control difference on value claiming (p = .271). Study 3 serial indirect effect = −272.00, 95% CI [−610.04, −53.49], with elaboration reducing value claiming (b = −0.06, SE = 0.02, p = .003) and value claiming reducing joint gain (b = −11,696.69, SE = 2,397.32, p < .001).

Study 4 supported the proposed causal role of information elaboration, but conditionally. Without elaboration instructions, hierarchical negotiations achieved lower joint gain (M = 11,422.86) than egalitarian ones (M = 12,682.35), t(137) = 3.80, p < .001, d = 0.96, and displayed more value claiming (M = 3.23 vs. 2.81), t(135) = 3.02, p = .003, d = 0.83. With elaboration instructions the two structures no longer differed on joint gain (M = 12,402.78 vs. 12,125.00), t(137) = 0.86, p = .394, or on value claiming (M = 2.94 vs. 3.04), t(135) = 0.76, p = .451. The indirect effect of hierarchy on joint gain via value claiming was significant only in the no-instructions condition (effect = −267.20, 95% CI [−620.88, −39.09]) and not in the instructions condition (effect = 65.48, 95% CI [−80.16, 392.60]); index of moderated mediation = 332.67, 95% CI [23.21, 929.64]. In Study 4 the main effects of hierarchy and of elaboration instructions on value-claiming behavior were both nonsignificant (p = .106 and p = .723), the main effect of elaboration instructions on joint gain was nonsignificant, F(1, 137) = 0.83, p = .364, and the hierarchy manipulation did not significantly shift coded information elaboration, F(1, 135) = 2.98, p = .086.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends team-negotiation research—which had focused predominantly on the benefits of teams over solo negotiators and treated teams as monolithic—by theorizing how within-team hierarchy undermines intrateam information elaboration and interteam joint gain. It bridges the literatures on intrateam hierarchy and interteam collaboration, showing that formal hierarchies can harm value creation across teams (not just within them), and contributes to work on hierarchical dynamics in teams and on multiteam systems by identifying when and why teams leave valuable resources unused.

**Practical Implication**
Contrary to the common advice from negotiation-training organizations that teams should appoint a single leader with decision-making authority, simply assigning members to leader and follower roles (or allowing self-selection) can inhibit information elaboration, promote competitive behavior between teams, and leave resources unused. Teams with leaders should deliberately take time to engage in information elaboration; egalitarian teams do not perform worse than competitive hierarchical counterparts, offering reassurance for sending more egalitarian teams to the table.

**Limitations**
The studies used MBA and undergraduate student samples in simulated business negotiations, raising generalizability concerns relative to more-complex, hostility-laden real-world negotiations. Effects of hierarchical differentiation may be contingent on the leader's negotiation expertise. The team sizes studied were small, and findings may not extend to larger teams, to status (rather than formal) hierarchies, or to negotiations lacking integrative potential.

**Future Research**
Future work could leverage real-world samples or more difficult-to-resolve negotiations with pre-existing hostilities, examine how leader expertise and leadership styles condition the effects, and study informal status hierarchies and alternative hierarchical shapes in larger teams. Researchers could also measure the fixed-pie bias more directly and examine how hierarchically structured teams perform across tasks with different coordination needs, including purely distributive (single-issue) settings.
