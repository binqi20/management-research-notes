# Wearing Your Worth at Work: The Consequences of Employees’ Daily Clothing Choices

**Abstract**
Do the clothes worn to work impact employees’ thoughts and behaviors? Despite the universal necessity of wearing clothes and the fact that employees make decisions about this daily, organizational scholars have not yet addressed this question. We integrate sociometer and enclothed cognition theories to propose that aspects of clothing—their aesthetics, conformity, and uniqueness—hold symbolic meanings that have implications for employees’ state self-esteem and subsequent task and relational behaviors (i.e., goal progress, social avoidance). We first provide evidence for the nature of the symbolic meanings associated with these three dimensions of work clothing in a set of within-person experimental studies. The results of a 10-day field study of employees from four organizations generally supported our predictions, showing that daily clothing aesthetics and uniqueness had effects on state self-esteem and downstream behavioral consequences. The effects of daily clothing conformity emerged under the condition of greater interaction frequency with others in the workplace. Our manuscript contributes to both major theories from which we draw, and further offers theoretical and practical contributions to the literature on organizational clothing.

**Research Question**
Whether, why, and when employees’ daily clothing choices have implications for their daily state self-esteem and subsequent task and relational work behaviors.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Within individuals, (a) daily perceived dress aesthetics is positively related to state self-esteem, and (b) this relationship is mediated by the symbolic meaning of personal attractiveness.
- H2: Within individuals, (a) daily perceived dress conformity is positively related to state self-esteem, and (b) this relationship is mediated by the symbolic meaning of belongingness.
- H3: Within individuals, (a) daily perceived dress uniqueness is positively related to state self-esteem, and (b) this relationship is mediated by the symbolic meaning of distinctiveness.
- H4: Within individuals, morning interaction frequency moderates the effect of daily perceived dress (a) aesthetics, (b) conformity, and (c) uniqueness on state self-esteem, such that when interaction frequency is high (vs. low), the relationships are stronger.
- H5: Within individuals, the positive effects of daily perceived dress (a) aesthetics, (b) conformity, and (c) uniqueness on goal progress are mediated by state self-esteem.
- H6: Within individuals, morning interaction frequency moderates the positive indirect effects of daily perceived dress (a) aesthetics, (b) conformity, and (c) uniqueness on goal progress (via state self-esteem), such that when interaction frequency is high (vs. low), the indirect effects are stronger.
- H7: Within individuals, the negative effects of daily perceived dress (a) aesthetics, (b) conformity, and (c) uniqueness on social avoidance are mediated by state self-esteem.
- H8: Within individuals, morning interaction frequency moderates the negative indirect effects of daily perceived dress (a) aesthetics, (b) conformity, and (c) uniqueness on social avoidance (via state self-esteem), such that when interaction frequency is high (vs. low), the indirect effects are stronger.

Study 1B tested the mediation predictions H1b, H2b, and H3b; Study 2 tested H1a, H2a, and H3a together with Hypotheses 4 through 8.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Daily perceived dress aesthetics; daily perceived dress conformity; daily perceived dress uniqueness
- DV(s): Daily goal progress; daily social avoidance (with state self-esteem as the proximal outcome)
- Mediators: State self-esteem; the symbolic meanings of personal attractiveness (for aesthetics), belongingness (for conformity), and distinctiveness (for uniqueness)
- Moderators: Daily interaction frequency with colleagues

Drawing on enclothed cognition theory, the authors argue that the clothes employees wear "embody" symbolic meanings that activate associated psychological processes. Integrating sociometer theory, those meanings (attractiveness, belongingness, distinctiveness) carry relational value that the sociometer registers, raising or lowering state self-esteem on a given day. Heightened state self-esteem then promotes motivated task behavior (goal progress) and reduces relational withdrawal (social avoidance). Because the sociometer is socially attuned, the authors predict that interaction frequency strengthens the effect of each of the three dress dimensions on state self-esteem (Hypotheses 4a–4c), since dress is more readily compared and observed on days with more interpersonal interaction.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1A (pre-registered online experiment): 65 employed US adults recruited from Prolific, 15 removed for failing attention checks, final n = 50. Participants viewed eight gender-aligned photographs of clothing that a pilot study had classified as high or low in aesthetics, conformity, and uniqueness (2 x 2 x 2) and selected, from a 21-item adjective list (9 focal terms, 12 distractors), the words the clothes would symbolize if worn to a business-casual office. Analyzed with chi-square tests for independence using Yates's correction.
- Study 1B (pre-registered within-person experiment): 140 new Prolific panelists, 17 removed, final n = 123, randomly assigned to an aesthetics, conformity, uniqueness, or control condition. All participants changed clothes twice — into a common baseline outfit and then into the condition outfit — and uploaded a photo each time; after each change they rated, on 5-point scales, personal attractiveness (three adjectives retained from Study 1A), belongingness (three terms), distinctiveness (three items), and state self-esteem (nine items adapted from Heatherton & Polivy, 1991). Analyzed with two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs and within-subjects mediational analyses using 10,000 bootstraps (Montoya & Hayes, 2017).
- Study 2 (10-day experience-sampling field study): 84 employees of four South Korean organizations returned 2,297 of 2,730 possible surveys (84.1%), yielding 808 complete daily observations. Three surveys per day, separated by at least two hours: T1 at the start of the workday (the three dress variables), T2 at lunch (interaction frequency, state self-esteem), and T3 at the end of the workday (goal progress, afternoon social avoidance). Items were rendered into Korean using Brislin's (1970) translation–back-translation procedure and answered on 5-point scales unless otherwise noted.
- Study 2 measures: dress aesthetics = three newly developed items ("Today, what I am wearing is aesthetically pleasing"; a = .93) and dress conformity = three newly developed items ("Today, what I am wearing conforms to norms at my workplace"; a = .83), both constructed with Hinkin and Tracey's (1999) procedure; dress uniqueness = three items adapted from Gurel and Gurel's (1979) clothing individuality measure (a = .78); interaction frequency = a single item asking how many times the participant had physically interacted with colleagues since arriving at work, on a 6-point scale from (1) never to (6) five or more times; state self-esteem = nine items from Heatherton and Polivy (1991), three each from the performance, social, and appearance dimensions (a = .81); goal progress = a 6-item measure from Wanberg, Zhu, and Van Hooft (2010) (a = .89); afternoon social avoidance = a 5-item measure from Woolum et al. (2017) (a = .92).

Identification rests on random assignment only in Study 1B; Study 2 is a non-experimental within-person field design with no manipulation. To isolate within-person effects and control between-person confounds the authors group-mean centered all predictors, temporally separated the T1/T2/T3 measures to fix the ordering of the variables, and controlled for survey day (1–10), the sine and cosine of the weekday, and prior levels of state self-esteem, goal progress, and social avoidance. Estimation used multilevel random-coefficient path modeling with a Bayesian estimator in Mplus 8.3, with random slopes on all hypothesized paths, fixed slopes on controls, full-information Bayesian handling of missing dependent-variable data, one-tailed p-values per Bayesian convention, and 95% credible intervals.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1A: chi-square tests confirmed the intended symbolic associations. High-aesthetics photos were rated significantly more often as "good-looking," "attractive," and "stylish"; high-conformity photos as "fits in," "belonging," and "included in the group"; high-uniqueness photos as "original," "unique," and "distinctive" (all p < .001 for both the male and the female stimulus sets).
- Study 1B: all three mediation predictions were supported. H1b — the aesthetics manipulation raised felt personal attractiveness relative to control (F[1, 58] = 26.26, p < .001, eta-squared = .31), and attractiveness mediated the effect on state self-esteem (indirect effect = .86, SE = .37, 95% CI [0.18, 1.64]). H2b — belongingness rose in the conformity condition (F[1, 57] = 6.18, p = .02, eta-squared = .10) and mediated the effect (indirect effect = .69, SE = .20, 95% CI [0.32, 1.09]). H3b — distinctiveness rose in the uniqueness condition (F[1, 58] = 43.75, p < .001, eta-squared = .43) and mediated the effect (indirect effect = .89, SE = .41, 95% CI [0.13, 1.71]). A supplemental analysis found that the need for competence also mediated the uniqueness–self-esteem link (indirect effect = .22, SE = .11, 95% CI [0.04, 0.47]); the identity and other self-determination variables were unaffected by the manipulations.
- Study 2, main effects on state self-esteem: H1a was supported (dress aesthetics, g = .17, p < .001, 95% CrI [0.116, 0.224]) and H3a was supported (dress uniqueness, g = .05, p = .02, 95% CrI [0.001, 0.100]), but H2a was not supported (dress conformity, g = .02, p = .25, 95% CrI [-0.043, 0.096]).
- Study 2, moderation by interaction frequency: only H4b was supported. The dress conformity x interaction frequency term was significant (g = .06, p = .02, 95% CrI [0.004, 0.115]), with conformity positively related to state self-esteem on high-interaction days (g = .16, p = .02) and negatively but non-significantly related on low-interaction days (g = -.11, p = .05; difference = .27, p = .02). H4a (aesthetics, g = .03, p = .17) and H4c (uniqueness, g = .01, p = .41) were not supported.
- Study 2, indirect effects on goal progress: state self-esteem was positively related to goal progress (g = .41, p < .001). H5a and H5c were supported (aesthetics, g = .07, p < .001; uniqueness, g = .02, p = .02) while H5b was not (conformity, g = .01, p = .25). Of the conditional indirect effects, only H6b was supported: conformity's indirect effect was stronger on high-interaction days (g = .07, p = .02) than on low-interaction days (g = -.05, p = .05; difference = .11, p = .02). H6a and H6c were not supported.
- Study 2, indirect effects on afternoon social avoidance: state self-esteem was negatively related to social avoidance (g = -.26, p < .001). H7a and H7c were supported (aesthetics, g = -.04, p < .001; uniqueness, g = -.01, p = .02) while H7b was not (conformity, g = -.01, p = .25). Only H8b was supported: conformity's indirect effect on social avoidance was stronger on high-interaction days (g = -.04, p = .02) than on low-interaction days (g = .03, p = .05; difference = .07, p = .02). H8a and H8c were not supported.
- Convergence and divergence across methods: the experiments and the field study agree that dress aesthetics and dress uniqueness raise state self-esteem through their symbolic meanings, but they diverge on conformity — the Study 1B manipulation did raise belongingness and, through it, state self-esteem, whereas in Study 2 conformity had no unconditional main effect and mattered only on days with frequent colleague interaction. A supplemental Study 2 analysis found no support for a conformity x uniqueness congruence effect; only the dress-uniqueness-squared term was significant (g = .08, p = .02), a slightly accelerating effect of dress uniqueness on state self-esteem.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends enclothed cognition theory from specific, universally meaningful garments (e.g., a doctor's lab coat) to ordinary, day-to-day work clothing applicable to employees in virtually any workplace, and adopts an actor-centric, within-person lens rather than the observer-centric view dominant in the organizational dress literature. It extends sociometer theory by showing that controllable daily choices—not just external events or stable physical attributes—drive state self-esteem, and by providing empirical support for the idea that both fitting in (belongingness) and standing out (distinctiveness) can foster self-esteem.

**Practical Implication**
Investing modest morning effort to dress in clothing that is aesthetically pleasing and unique—and, when interactions are expected, conforming to organizational norms—can meaningfully improve how employees feel about themselves and behave at work. Managers can offer soft guidance and organizationally branded items that help employees fit in while leaving room for self-expression, rather than imposing rigid dress codes.

**Limitations**
All Study 2 measures were self-reported, raising common-method-variance concerns, though the authors temporally separated variables, lagged mediators/outcomes, and group-mean centered within-individual variables to mitigate this. The field-study effects were somewhat small in magnitude, and the study did not use objective indicators of dress or behavior.

**Future Research**
Future studies could assess work behavior with objective indicators (e.g., sales) or other-source reports, and rate attire through a more objective lens (e.g., trained coders) to further reduce common-method-variance concerns. Researchers could examine the dimensionality of clothing with greater specificity, including how particular or personalized clothing attributes convey relational value differently across organizational and cultural contexts (a possible congruence effect).
