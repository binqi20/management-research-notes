# Tempering Temperance? A Contingency Approach to Social Movements’ Entry Deterrence in Scottish Whisky Distilling, 1823–1921

**Abstract**
What makes social movements successfully deter entry in contested industries? We develop a contingency framework explaining how movements' success depends on the internal fit between their private and public politics strategies with the tactics of mass and elite mobilization. We also highlight the importance of how these tactics fit with external conditions like the cognitive legitimacy of the industry and industry countermobilization. When movements rely on a private politics strategy to condemn an industry in the eyes of the public, social movement mass will be decisive. Alternatively, when movements use a public politics strategy to push for regulatory intervention, mobilization of elites is crucial. We develop our understanding of external contingency factors by exploring how cognitive legitimacy residuals from local ancestral populations affect both mass-driven private politics and elite-driven public politics, and how nationallevel industry countermobilization efforts affect elite-driven public politics strategies. We test these ideas in a historical study of the Scottish whisky distilling industry during the rise of temperance movements (1823–1921). We contribute to the social movements literature by showing how movements' entry deterrence in contested industries depends on the internal fit between their strategies and mobilization tactics, as well as on their engagement with external contingencies.

**Research Question**
How does social movement opposition affect entrepreneurial entry in contested industries, and how does the effectiveness of movements' contestation depend on the fit between their private/public politics strategies, their mass/elite mobilization tactics, and external contingencies (cognitive legitimacy and incumbent countermobilization)?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater the mass of a social movement engaging in a private politics strategy, the stronger its negative effect on entrepreneurial entry.
- H2: The greater the degree of political elite mobilization by a social movement engaging in a public politics strategy, the stronger its negative effect on entrepreneurial entry.
- H3: In counties with a large ancestral population, the negative effects of social movement contestation through both local elite-driven public politics and mass-driven private politics on entrepreneurial entry will be weaker.
- H4: In counties with stronger incumbent countermobilization, the negative effect of social movement contestation through national-level elite-driven public politics on entrepreneurial entry will be weaker.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Social movement mass mobilization (STL member proportion, private politics); elite mobilization (SPBTA local elite members and MPs, public politics); local ancestral populations (cognitive legitimacy residual); national-level industry countermobilization (whisky-specific associations).
- DV(s): Whisky distillery foundings (entrepreneurial entry), modeled as an annual county-level count.
- Mediators: None modeled (direct effects of mobilization and contingencies on founding rates).
- Moderators: Ancestral populations (moderating mass-driven private politics and elite-driven public politics); industry countermobilization (moderating elite-driven public politics).

Movements pursuing private politics deter entry by getting the public to internalize a negative frame, so a critical mass of supporters raises the social cost of entry; movements pursuing public politics deter entry by enlisting political elites who can signal forthcoming restrictive regulation. These effects are contingent on context: ancestral populations endow the industry with cognitive legitimacy, while incumbent countermobilization in the national legislative arena blunts elite-driven public politics — and, as Hypothesis 4 predicts, can even spur further entry.

**Data & Measures**
Hand-collected historical archival panel of Scottish county-years, 1823-1921 (2,744 county-year observations). Distillery founding and failure dates come primarily from Hume and Moss (2000), complemented by Smith (2002), Townsend (2004), and Udo (2006); independent and control variables were coded from yearbooks, census reports, and ledgers in the Glasgow University Archives and the National Archives of Scotland; temperance data come from The Scottish Temperance League Register and Abstainers' Almanac (1857-1921) and The Scottish Permissive Bill & Temperance Association records (1859-1921), with county-level STL membership traceable from 1856 and SPBTA membership from the association's formation in 1858. DV: Whisky distillery foundings, the annual count of all new distilleries founded in a county. IVs: STL member proportion (H1) = total STL membership in a county divided by the number of county inhabitants, lagged one year; SPBTA local elite members (H2) = county count of SPBTA members holding local office (provosts, council members, bailies, sheriffs, constables, justices of the peace); SPBTA MP members (H2) = count of SPBTA Members of Parliament. Moderators: Ancestral populations (H3) = count of all distilleries founded between 1680 and 1823 that failed before the relaxation of licensing constraints in 1823; SLTDA membership (H4) = county count of local organizations belonging to the Scottish Licensed Trade Defense Association; SWASS (H4) = count of Scottish Whisky Associations active in the county. The nonhypothesized cross-tactic mechanisms are controlled directly as SPBTA member proportion and STL reverend members (Church of Scotland reverends who joined the STL). Further controls: era dummies (1823-1840 reference, 1841-1860, 1861-1880, 1881-1900, 1901-1921) and whisky-region dummies, log county inhabitants, total whisky production, whisky retained for consumption and its change, technological advancements, economic depression year, industry age, local distillery failure, World War I, duty per gallon, numbers of positive and negative legislative acts, residual population, time-varying residual population density and its square, and De antiquo density and its square. Estimation is negative binomial regression, preferred over Poisson given overdispersion, with clustering on county; STL member proportion and STL reverend members have missing data and are handled by multiple imputation (Stata mi impute nbreg, 10 imputations, with M20 to M50 robustness checks); interaction terms are interpreted using Zelner's (2009) simulation method rather than from their standard errors. The design is associational. The authors state that because ancestral populations is a county-level constant they cannot estimate fixed-effects models, and substitute era and region dummies instead; no causal identification strategy is claimed.

**Key Findings**
- H1 supported: in counties with a greater ratio of STL members, organizational founding rates were lower (STL member proportion b = -0.364, SE = 0.156, Model 5). The nonhypothesized alternative for the private politics movement, elite enlistment captured by STL reverend members, had no significant downward effect on founding rates, which the authors read as further support for the contingency logic behind H1.
- H2 not supported: elite mobilization by the public politics movement had no significant direct effect on foundings at either level (SPBTA local elite members b = 0.007, SE = 0.024; SPBTA MP members b = -0.011, SE = 0.176; Model 5). The nonhypothesized alternative, SPBTA mass membership, also had no significant downward effect, ruling out a counterhypothesized effect.
- H3 rejected, with significant counterhypothesized effects in the opposite direction: large ancestral populations strengthened rather than weakened contestation. The interaction of STL member proportion with ancestral populations is negative and significant (b = -0.031, SE = 0.009, Model 6), and so is the interaction of SPBTA local elite members with ancestral populations (b = -0.009, SE = 0.004, Model 6). Zelner simulations show that where ancestral populations are large the effect of STL mass membership on founding turns negative and significant at higher membership levels, and that SPBTA local elite membership carries a sizeable negative effect once it exceeds the enlistment of 130 local elites.
- H4 supported, but only for whisky trade-specific countermobilization: the interaction of SPBTA MP members with SWASS is positive and significant (b = 5.635, SE = 1.716, Model 7), and including it turns the main effect of SPBTA MP membership negative and significant (b = -5.645, SE = 1.770). The parallel interaction with the broader-trade SLTDA is not significant (b = 0.024, SE = 0.019).
- The main effect of ancestral populations is consistently positive and significant across all regression models (b = 0.071, SE = 0.018 in the full model), which the authors interpret as a reservoir of cognitive legitimacy that aspiring entrants benefit from by association.
- Supplementary ARIMA analyses show that STL religious-elite membership positively predicted later mass membership (0.762, p < .001) and that SPBTA mass membership positively predicted SPBTA local elite membership (0.273, p < .001), indicating that mass and elite support are intertwined.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper builds a contingency theory of social movements' entry deterrence in established-but-contested industries, distinguishing the constituencies (mass vs. elite) that make private versus public politics strategies effective. It theorizes and tests two external contingencies — ancestral-population cognitive legitimacy and incumbent countermobilization — showing that contestation is a two-way, coevolutionary process whose outcomes depend on the internal fit between strategy and tactics and on engagement with these external conditions.

**Practical Implication**
For activists, the effective tactic depends on strategy: mass mobilization matters most for private politics, while enlisting political elites matters most for public politics, and elite leverage is strongest where an industry is historically well-anchored. For incumbents in contested industries, national-level countermobilization can defend sociopolitical legitimacy and may even encourage new entry by making the regulatory environment appear less hostile.

**Limitations**
The historical data preclude fixed-effects estimation and limit the ability to capture how movements adapted their framing or deployed specific tactics (petitions, boycotts, townhall meetings) over a long period. The authors also could not systematically capture the active lobbying behind incumbent countermobilization or countermobilization efforts aimed specifically at private-politics framing.

**Future Research**
Future work could develop finer-grained understanding of contestation strategies and their supporting constituencies, theorizing about the fit between new combinations of internal and external contingencies. Scholars could adopt a more granular historical approach to trace how movement and countermobilization dynamics, including specific tactical repertoires, unfold over prolonged periods.
