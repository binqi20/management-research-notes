# The Award Goes To… Someone Else: A Natural Quasi-Experiment Examining the Impact of Performance Awards on Nominees’ Workplace Collaboration

**Abstract**
Employee awards programs are prevalent in organizations. This research examines a group of employees who have received relatively less attention in the employee awards literature: those who are nominated for but fail to receive an award. Integrating social comparison theory with the functional theory of counterfactual thinking, we argue that nonwinner nominees go through complex motivational states and affective experiences, which influence their collaboration responsiveness to colleagues. We conducted a field quasi-experiment using 160,830 collaboration records of 494 employees over eight months (Study 1) and a multisource study using survey data and 34,911 collaborative records of 598 employees over two months (Study 2). The results revealed that, compared to non-nominees, nonwinner nominees have lower collaboration responsiveness to winners following the award announcement, especially when they have a higher structural proximity to winners. However, in the long run, nonwinner nominees have higher collaboration responsiveness to others in general. Furthermore, being nominated for but failing to receive an award simultaneously induces nonwinner nominees to experience higher causal-inference counterfactual thinking and negative affect, which exert opposing impacts on nonwinner nominees’ collaboration responsiveness. This research advances the understanding of the impact, influencing mechanisms, boundary conditions, and temporal dynamics of employee awards on workplace collaboration.

**Research Question**
How does being nominated for but failing to receive an award affect "nonwinner nominees'" collaboration responsiveness to colleagues—both to winners in particular and to others in general—compared with non-nominees, and through what mediating mechanisms, boundary conditions, and temporal dynamics?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
H1a. Following an award announcement, failing to receive the award has a significantly positive indirect effect on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to others in general through causal-inference counterfactual thinking.
H1b. Following an award announcement, failing to receive the award has a significantly negative indirect effect on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to others in general through negative affect.
H2a. Following an award announcement, failing to receive the award has a significantly negative effect on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to winners.
H2b. Following an award announcement, failing to receive the award has a significantly negative indirect effect on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to winners through negative affect.
H3. Following an award announcement, structural proximity moderates the negative effect of failing to receive the award on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to winners, such that the effect is stronger when the two parties are more structurally proximal to each other.
H4. In the long run, failing to receive an award has a significantly positive effect on nonwinner nominees' collaboration responsiveness to others in general.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Being a nonwinner nominee (nominated for but not receiving the award) versus being a non-nominee, following an award announcement.
- DV(s): Collaboration responsiveness (how promptly an individual addresses colleagues' collaboration requests, measured objectively as time to process received requests)—decomposed into responsiveness to winners and to others in general.
- Mediators: (1) Causal-inference counterfactual thinking (a positive, motivational pathway) and (2) negative affect (disappointment, frustration, anger; a negative pathway).
- Moderators: Structural proximity to winners (same department, section, or local office), strengthening the negative effect on responsiveness to winners.

Integrating social comparison theory with the functional theory of counterfactual thinking, the authors argue that a "near-miss" experience triggers two opposing forces in nonwinner nominees. Causal-inference counterfactual thinking ("if I had performed a little better, I would have won") motivates them to raise collaboration responsiveness to improve future winning chances, while the contrast effect generates negative affect that depresses responsiveness, especially toward winners. Because negative affect is short-lived but motivation is durable, the net effect is reduced responsiveness to winners in the short run (amplified by structural proximity) but elevated responsiveness to others in general in the long run.

**Data & Measures**
Study 1 draws on archival records from the company's collaborative work management system. DV: collaboration responsiveness, operationalized as the proportion of received collaboration requests processed within one working day; the raw processing-time lag was heavily right-skewed, so it was dichotomized at the company's implicit 24-hour response norm. IV: receiver-group indicators rNominee (nonwinner nominee) and rWinner (winner), interacted with After ("1" if the request was sent after the January 26, 2016 announcement) and, in the triple-difference models, with sWinner ("1" if the sender is a winner). Moderators: three structural-proximity indicators for whether sender and receiver are in the same department (M1), the same section (M2), or the same local office (M3). Controls: whether the collaboration was flagged important, whether it was routine, same-unit indicators, receiver demographics (gender, age, marital status, education), prior-year performance score, and tenure; the time-invariant controls are absorbed in the fixed-effects specifications. The paper names its own identification strategy: a difference-in-differences (DID) model for responsiveness to colleagues in general and a difference-in-differences-in-differences (DDD) model for responsiveness to winners, each estimated as a linear probability model with hour-of-day, received-date, sender, and receiver fixed effects and standard errors clustered at the receiver and sender level. Sensitivity analyses use placebo announcement dates, alternative conversion rules for the DV (winsorization at the 95th/90th/80th percentiles, log transformation, dichotomization at the median and 75th percentile), an attrition-restricted sample, and propensity score matching of nominees to comparable employees.

Study 2 matches a post-ceremony employee survey with archival collaboration records from the same company. The survey went to all research and sales employees immediately after the January 23, 2022 award ceremony (598 completed responses, 28.6% response rate) and was matched by email address and system ID to 34,911 collaboration records from the following two months. Mediators (5-point scales): negative affect, three items from the circumplex model of affect ("disappointed," "frustrated," "angry"; a = .73); causal-inference counterfactual thinking, a single item, "If I had performed just a little better, I would probably have won the award," adapted from Rye et al. (2008). Competing mediators were also measured: role modeling and rivalry (one item each) and benign envy and malicious envy (six items each; Cronbach's alpha .95 and .86). Controls: education and gender. Mediation was tested with multiple regression plus bootstrapped indirect effects (5,000 replications), and a supplemental Prolific validation study (n = 200) correlated the shortened scales with their full versions (r = .84 to .96). Because the Study 2 mediators are survey-reported and the mediation models are regression-based, these mediation estimates are associational rather than quasi-experimentally identified as in Study 1.

**Key Findings**
Study 1 (160,830 collaboration records; DID/DDD). The short-run effect of failing to receive the award on nonwinner nominees' overall collaboration responsiveness to others in general was nonsignificant relative to non-nominees (rNominee × After = .01 to .05 across specifications), matching the authors' expectation that the two mechanisms offset each other. H2a was supported: the DDD coefficient on sWinner × rNominee × After was significantly negative in all four specifications (-.25, -.24, -.25, -.23), meaning the announcement reduced the share of winners' requests that nonwinner nominees answered within one working day by 23 to 25 percentage points, about 30% of the average; the corresponding effect on winners was nonsignificant and statistically different from the nominee effect. H3 was supported for two of the three structural-proximity operationalizations: working in the same section (moderating effect = -.29, p = .02) and working in the same local office (-.29, p = .02) each strengthened the negative effect, each corresponding to a 29-percentage-point reduction, while the same-department moderator was nonsignificant, which the authors attribute to departments being too coarse a unit. H4 was supported: relative to non-nominees, the effect on nonwinner nominees' responsiveness to others in general was nonsignificant in months 1-2 (.02) but significantly positive in months 3-4 (.11, an 11-percentage-point increase, about 14% of the sample average) and months 5-6 (.12); in parallel, the negative effect on responsiveness to winners (-.25 in months 1-2) was no longer significant in months 3-4 (.03) or months 5-6 (.08).

Study 2 (598 survey respondents matched to 34,911 collaboration records; regression-based mediation) supported all three mediation hypotheses. H1a: being a nonwinner nominee was positively related to causal-inference counterfactual thinking (b = .18, p < .001), which was positively related to collaboration responsiveness (b = .01, p < .05); the indirect effect on responsiveness to others in general was positive and significant (indirect effect = .001, bootstrap 95% CI [.0001, .0017]), equal to 6.4% of the direct effect. H1b: being a nonwinner nominee was related to higher negative affect (b = .16, p < .001), which reduced responsiveness to others in general (b = -.05, p < .001); the indirect effect was negative and significant (-.0071, 95% CI [-.0089, -.0055]), equal to 49.1% of the direct effect. H2b: negative affect also reduced responsiveness to winners (b = -.05, p < .01), producing a significant negative indirect effect (-.0081, 95% CI [-.0144, -.0022]), equal to 6.3% of the direct effect. The authors report that both hypothesized mechanisms still hold after controlling for the competing mediators (role modeling, rivalry, benign envy, and malicious envy), and that winning an award did not significantly change winners' own collaboration responsiveness relative to non-nominees in either the short or the long run.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper makes a finer distinction within nonrecipients—separating nonwinner nominees from non-nominees—and shows that those who come closer to a desired outcome behave differently than those who do not, extending social comparison theory and the star-performer literature (e.g., how "losing stars" can harm "winning stars"). It introduces collaboration responsiveness as a novel outcome to the employee awards literature, identifies a dual mediating mechanism (causal-inference counterfactual thinking and negative affect) that holds beyond competing mechanisms such as role modeling, rivalry, and benign/malicious envy, establishes structural proximity as a boundary condition, and uses a longitudinal design to reveal the temporal dynamics of awards effects.

**Practical Implication**
Because awards programs act as a double-edged sword in the short term but yield net-positive long-run collaboration effects, managers should actively promote collaboration between nonwinner nominees and winners—especially immediately after announcements and especially when the two are structurally proximate—to protect winners' performance and retention. Managers can also help nonwinner nominees curb episodic negative affect by showing appreciation for their contributions and providing supervisory support, encouragement, feedback, and resources, lessening frustration and disappointment.

**Limitations**
The study examines only one facet of collaboration (responsiveness to received requests) and does not test other facets such as initiating collaborations, value contributed, or tie persistence. Data were collected in China, a highly collectivistic culture, though the authors argue collaboration is prevalent in organizations even in noncollectivistic cultures, and the awards combined social recognition, feedback, and financial incentives. The authors describe the two mediators' effect sizes as meaningful while acknowledging they are two of many potential mechanisms, and the DID framework cannot rule out individual differences that change after the announcement; the temporal turning-point results are exploratory.

**Future Research**
The authors call for examining additional facets and outcomes of collaboration (e.g., initiating requests, performance, withdrawal, unethical behaviors), additional boundary conditions (e.g., cultural context, award type including purely symbolic awards), and additional mediators (e.g., rivalry, perceived fairness/attributions). They also encourage replicating the quasi-experimental design to test the generalizability of the exploratory temporal pattern, studying "surprise" awards whose nominees are not notified, and exploring employee collaboration during the nomination stage.
