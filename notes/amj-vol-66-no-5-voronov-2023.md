# Distilling Authenticity: Materiality and Narratives in Canadian Distilleries’ Authenticity Work

**Abstract**
Authenticity is increasingly seen as a source of competitive advantage in many industries. Accordingly, authenticity work, the organizational efforts to develop and sustain believable authenticity claims, has emerged as an important organizational practice. We examined the interplay of materiality and narratives underpinning producers’ authenticity work in the context of incumbent and micro-distilleries operating in the Canadian whisky industry. We found that producers’ material endowments, especially central product features, anchored which authenticity claims they could credibly narrate. Other material endowments, such as key people and architectural design, were used to reinforce the integrity of authenticity claims. Our study extends our understanding of authenticity as a valued organizational resource. First, we identify two mechanisms, anchoring and reinforcement, through which materiality both constrains and facilitates organizations’ authenticity narratives. Second, our research brings to the fore how audience members’ experiential closeness to producers colors their perceptions of authenticity, and we show how material artifacts can enhance such closeness. Third, our findings highlight the competitive value of authenticity by unpacking how producers’ material endowments may constitute a resource or a liability.

**Research Question**
How do organizations’ material endowments enable and constrain their authenticity claims, and how does the interplay of materiality and narratives shape the authenticity work through which producers craft and sustain believable authenticity claims?

Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: authenticity work; authenticity claims (combinations of narratives and material artifacts); material endowments (product features, key people, architectural design); the tension between tradition-based and originality-based bases of authenticity.
- Process / mechanisms: *Anchoring* — central product features tie producers to a prefigured base of authenticity, making some claims natural and others requiring greater narrative effort; *Reinforcement* — other material endowments (human embodiments, architectural design) bolster the integrity and persuasiveness of claims.
- Triggering conditions: differential material endowments (e.g., incumbents’ access to aged liquid vs. micro-distilleries’ lack thereof) prefigure which authenticity claims are credible.
- Emergent outcomes: relatively stable authenticity profiles; experiential closeness between producers and audiences that heightens perceived authenticity; authenticity as a competitive resource or liability.

Materiality is prefigurative: rather than being a blank canvas, an organization’s material endowments inform the selection and audience reception of authenticity narratives. Producers exercise agency by skillfully (re)combining narratives and materiality to overcome the constraints of anchoring or to reinforce an existing base of authenticity, conferring plasticity to authenticity claims and generating audience buy-in.

**Data & Measures**
Data: 92 semi-structured interviews with distillery representatives and audience members, collected between 2015 and 2019 in the Canadian whisky industry. Distillery interviewees came from incumbent distilleries (several decades to more than a century old) and micro-distilleries (typically under ten years old), three of which sourced aged liquid from an incumbent under contract distilling; sampling deliberately spanned differences in age, size, ownership structure, geographical location, relative reputation, and reliance on in-house versus contract distilling, and deliberately targeted elite audience members who codify what counts as authentic (retailers, bloggers, restaurateurs, connoisseurs). Table 2 reports the audience interviews as critics and bloggers (10), restaurateurs and bartenders (11), retailers (9), government (2), connoisseurs (7), and others (4). Interviews used open-ended questions from a protocol reproduced in Appendix A, ran 1 to 1.5 hours, and were recorded and transcribed verbatim; protocols were refined as themes emerged, and interviewing continued until it added little new information. The first author additionally logged more than 70 hours of in situ observation of routine distillery operations and public events — 8 distillery tours, 17 tastings or informal visits, and 4 whisky festivals with masterclasses and tastings — and the team assembled archival material comprising 25 distillery websites, 180 newspaper articles, more than 500 whisky blog entries and reviews, 12 social-media accounts, and 11 books on the Canadian and international whisky industries. Distilleries are reported under pseudonyms except where quotes come from public-domain sources.

Operationalization: the focal construct — a distillery's authenticity claim — was coded inductively rather than scored on an instrument. Claims were classified into two bases: authenticity as tradition (continuity, loyalty to original roots, adherence to received practice and methods of production) and authenticity as originality (uniqueness, novelty, virtuosity in production or the resulting products). Table 4 then profiles every distillery on both bases across four evidence streams — "For each distillery, tradition and originality claims are assessed, as high, medium, low, or absent, for each data source" (interviews, observations, website or social media, and blogger or media coverage). Material endowments were coded into the three categories the authors identified as most salient in the whisky field: the aged liquid as manifest in whisky product features, the key people involved in whisky-making, and the architectural design of the distilleries. Each distillery was then assessed for which of these endowments it possessed or lacked, with follow-up interviews or emails where information was missing.

Analysis: a three-phase grounded analysis using constant comparison (Locke, 2001), iterating between raw data and emerging theory. Phase one open-coded authenticity claims, comparing narratives across interviews, website descriptions, and formal and informal presentations during distillery visits and whisky festivals, and searched the corpus for authenticity vocabulary such as "genuine," "traditional," "honest," "faithful," and "original," plus sensitizing synonyms drawn from prior management, sociology, and anthropology work ("pure," "real," "believable," "sincere," "skilled"); within-case comparison established that each distillery made relatively consistent claims, and cross-case comparison surfaced the incumbent–micro divide. Phase two identified the role of materiality and made cross-distillery comparisons of available and absent endowments. Phase three linked the phase-one narratives to the phase-two endowments. Trustworthiness was addressed by presenting tentative interpretations back to interviewees at several points, writing reports for distilleries and audience members, and resolving interpretive disagreements by consensus or by returning to informants (Lincoln & Guba, 1985). The design is inductive and interpretive: it builds theory from cross-case comparison of naturally occurring variation across distilleries and claims no causal identification strategy — there is no experiment, instrument, or quasi-experimental design, and the incumbent–micro contrast is analytic rather than statistical.

**Key Findings**
Audiences used two widely shared criteria to assess authenticity in whisky-making — tradition and originality — which in this industry are understood not as mutually exclusive opposites but as interdependent qualities. Within-case comparison showed each distillery tended to make relatively consistent claims emphasizing one base; cross-case comparison showed that most incumbent distilleries emphasized tradition while most micro-distilleries emphasized originality (Table 4). The pattern is a tendency rather than a rule: Innovative Spirits, an incumbent, claimed originality strongly in interviews, and two of the three micro-distilleries that sourced aged liquid from incumbents (Clean Air Spirits and Spirit of Adventure) claimed tradition strongly.

Anchoring: material product features anchored which claims a distillery could credibly narrate. The most important material manifestation was whether the product is made with well-aged liquid — on balance, abundant stocks of well-aged liquid made distilleries more likely to construct narratives emphasizing tradition, while limited access to such stocks made them more likely to emphasize originality (Table 5). Incumbents holding vast aged inventories (two owned more than 1.5 million barrels each) could narrate the Canadian whisky-making heritage naturally, but their interviewees reported that the same inventory limited their ability to construct desirable alternative narratives. That constraint was costly, because connoisseurs found the traditional smooth Canadian style "boring" and some dismissed it pejoratively as "brown vodka"; anchored to the aged liquid, incumbents were unable to construct flavor-profile narratives that would counter the perception. Micro-distilleries, conversely, had not produced whisky aged enough to credibly evoke the refinement that signals tradition — their releases were typically seen as less refined and were characterized as "harsh" — so most claimed originality instead, foregrounding higher alcohol content, unusual flavor profiles, grains that are difficult to work with or obtain, and appeals to a foreign (usually Scotch) whisky-making tradition.

Anchoring was strongly suggestive rather than deterministic, and both groups did authenticity work to mitigate it, with mixed results. Heritage Brands, an incumbent, recombined material elements into an annually released collection promoted as using extra-long barrel aging, unusual combinations of distillates, special distillation techniques, and higher alcohol content; these products generated positive reactions from most audience interviewees and showed that incumbents could appeal to connoisseurs. Micro-distilleries lacking aged stock sourced liquid from incumbents, but this mitigation carried a reported cost: because they made whisky in largely the same manner as the incumbents they sourced from, they were to a large extent re-anchored to tradition claims, lost part of their ability to claim originality, and increasingly constructed tradition-based narratives — responding by stressing their own contributions, such as finishing sourced liquid in unusual barrels, or by pointing to future originality from in-house experiments not yet released.

Reinforcement through human embodiments: what made distillery employees effective embodiments was not their mere existence but their visibility, accessibility, and willingness to interact, and audience interviewees generally concurred that master blenders and distillers were the most compelling embodiments. Incumbents mainly deployed historical figureheads and brand ambassadors, and both underperformed — most audience members found references to historical figures unconvincing, and brand ambassadors, whose background was typically bartending or sales rather than whisky-making, were often seen as less knowledgeable and less effective, with one whisky writer dismissing them as "salespeople." Incumbent informants attributed the dearth of active embodiments to organizational complexity that isolated master blenders in production from customer-facing activities; Alex, the master blender at Heritage Brands, was the only incumbent master blender regularly available for such interactions and was received warmly. Among micro-distilleries, founders or cofounders usually doubled as the whisky-makers, making them readily available embodiments of originality claims and allowing greater closeness with audiences, whereas incumbents' lack of present-day embodiments and emphasis on brand promotion often distanced them from theirs.

Reinforcement through architectural design: design reinforced claims mainly by conveying transparency and visibility of production. Incumbent buildings, many standing since founding in the mid-19th century and some classified as historically significant landmarks, are typically large structures in industrial districts built for efficient manufacturing rather than for tours; audience members described them as factories and as "industrial plants" with "no romance." The more consequential problem, according to audience members, was the failure to use the design to let visitors see the whisky-making process, which they named the most significant missed opportunity to showcase authenticity; over the study period incumbents responded by opening or planning visitor centers. Micro-distilleries, newly built and often sited in visitor-accessible areas or near the farms supplying their grain, were designed so that production could be entered or seen through a glass wall, reinforcing originality and the grain-to-glass story, and small size itself signaled authenticity to some consumers. One whisky writer dissented, arguing that entertaining visitors is not transparency and that some micro-distillers do not know enough to be transparent about anything else.

Overall, the findings suggest that micro-distilleries were more effective than incumbents at authenticity work: despite distinguished pasts, iconic founders, and abundant artifacts essential to the tradition of Canadian whisky-making, incumbents did not enjoy an authenticity advantage in the eyes of audience members over upstart micro-distilleries that seemingly had fewer of those material resources. Because material endowments anchored producers to a particular base of authenticity, relatively stable authenticity profiles emerged that generated clarity about producers' identities and conferred consistency to their action, so that authenticity-based advantage rested less on owning prestigious endowments than on the agentic work of combining narratives and materiality to anchor, reinforce, or mitigate claims.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends understanding of authenticity as a valued organizational resource by identifying anchoring and reinforcement as two mechanisms through which materiality both constrains and facilitates authenticity narratives. It theorizes materiality as prefigurative—shaping which claims are credible rather than merely amplifying chosen claims—and surfaces audience members’ experiential closeness as a route through which material artifacts enhance perceived authenticity. It reframes resource advantage as a matter of how organizations use endowments in authenticity work rather than mere ownership.

**Practical Implication**
For producers in industries where authenticity matters, competitive advantage depends less on owning prestigious material endowments than on skillfully combining materiality and narratives to construct credible claims. Incumbents anchored to tradition may need to demonstrate innovation, while newer entrants can leverage approachable makers and architectural design to build experiential closeness; material endowments can be a resource or a liability depending on the authenticity work performed.

**Limitations**
The study relied on audience members’ accounts of what they deem authentic and deferred to their subjective understandings of how materiality matters, building on an audience-centric view of authenticity. It focuses on a single, tradition-rich industry (Canadian whisky) where a product’s perceived authenticity is tightly tied to that of its producer, which may limit transferability.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how the challenge of balancing different bases of authenticity plays out across industries, and settings where material bases differ or where the product–organization tie is looser (e.g., high-technology or sharing-economy contexts). Scholars could also study how the meaning of material artifacts is negotiated in situ and how authenticity work and an organization’s primary authenticity claim change over time in response to internal and external factors.
