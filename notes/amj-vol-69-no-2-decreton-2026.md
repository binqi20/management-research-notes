# Deeply Rooted and Versatile? Knowledge Scouts and External Knowledge Integration in Multidivisional Firms

**Abstract**
Using knowledge created by startups can help firms boost innovation. Accordingly, many firms set up dedicated units tasked with facilitating the use of external knowledge in the development of new products across the organization. Research underscores the critical role of knowledge scouts within these units, as they span boundaries between external sources of knowledge and internal audiences. While deep roots in one division, developed through experience integrating external knowledge there, enhance a scout’s ability to support that division, it remains unclear whether scouts are sufficiently versatile to also leverage experience gained with other divisions. Given heterogeneity across divisions, a tension arises between the internal versatility expected of scouts and the complexity of achieving it. We argue that cognitive and relational challenges hinder a scout’s ability to build on experience gained with other divisions, adversely affecting new product launch. Further, we argue that cognitive challenges diminish when the external knowledge being integrated is similar to that previously encountered by the focal division. Relational challenges, conversely, intensify when experience was gained supporting divisions that have product-market overlap—and thereby compete—with the focal division. Extensive analyses using unique, fine-grained, proprietary data from a large multidivisional firm, alongside qualitative insights, support our arguments.

**Research Question**
How is the integration of external knowledge into a focal division shaped by the scout’s experience with other divisions? When do startup technological similarity and division product-market overlap attenuate or exacerbate this relationship?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: The greater a scout’s experience with other divisions in facilitating the integration of external knowledge to launch new products, the lower the likelihood that the scout will successfully facilitate the launch of a new product based on external knowledge in a focal division.
- H2: The similarity of a startup’s technology to the ones of startups the focal division previously engaged with weakens the negative relationship between a scout’s experience with other divisions and new product launch, such that this relationship becomes less negative as similarity increases.
- H3: Product-market overlap between the divisions with which a scout has gained experience and the focal division strengthens the negative relationship between the scout’s experience with other divisions and new product launch, such that the relationship becomes increasingly negative as overlap increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV: Scout experience facilitating completed external knowledge integration projects in other divisions.
- DV: New product launch in the focal division.
- Mediators: Cognitive and relational challenges are theorized mechanisms, not separately tested mediators.
- Moderators: Startup technological similarity to startups previously engaged by the focal division; product-market overlap between that division and divisions in which the scout gained experience.

Experience develops cognitive understanding and relational resources within a division, but differences in divisional needs, routines, priorities, trust, and political dynamics impede their transfer. Misapplied experience and costly adaptation can divert scouts from the focal project or reduce their willingness to sustain engagement. Similar external knowledge reduces adaptation demands, whereas product-market overlap creates competitive frictions that undermine trust and information sharing.

**Data & Measures**
The study uses proprietary records from Telcorp, a pseudonymous European telecommunications firm, covering 245 external knowledge integration projects involving 11 scouts during 2005–2018. Its five divisions were co-located in Europe, while its scouting unit operated in Silicon Valley. Each observation links one startup, one scout, and one division; startups are not repeated across scouts or divisions. Records include projects that did and did not produce launches. Interviews and field observations provide contextual evidence; patent, commercial database, web archive, LinkedIn, and company-report data supplement the internal records.

New product launch equals 1 when integration culminated in a new product and 0 otherwise; products include services. Scout experience with other divisions counts products previously launched by other divisions using startups identified by that scout, thus capturing completed projects rather than all attempts. Startup technological similarity uses four-digit Cooperative Patent Classification distributions and patents filed before scout identification: take the dot product of the focal startup’s vector and the aggregated vector for startups previously selected by the focal division, divide by the square root of the product of their self-dot-products, then multiply by 100. Division product-market overlap uses annual-report product lines and market segments to calculate division–year Jaccard overlap (intersection size divided by union size), referencing the divisions and years in which the scout gained prior experience. The experience variable and moderators are mean-centered.

Probit models estimate launch likelihood with division-clustered robust standard errors. Controls cover scouts’ focal-division launch experience, tenure, education, prior divisional employment, and concurrent startup identification; startup development stage, technological relatedness, age, employees, funding, and geographic distance; divisional concurrent projects, intangible capital expenditure, employees, EBITDA change, launches involving other scouts, dedicated project personnel, and scouting-unit age. The design is observational and does not eliminate residual endogeneity.

**Key Findings**
- H1 receives support: in Table 3, Model 5, other-division experience is negatively associated with launch (b = −0.50, SE = 0.07, p < .001). The reported average marginal effect is −0.04 (p < .001), approximately a 20% reduction relative to the sample’s mean launch probability of 0.20. Focal-division experience is positively associated with launch in the same model (b = 0.13, p < .01).
- H2 receives support: the similarity interaction is positive (b = 0.01, p < .001, Model 5), and the marginal-effect plot shows attenuation as similarity increases. The authors report that the negative effect is offset above a similarity score of 85, representing about 5% of observations.
- H3 receives support: the overlap interaction is negative (b = −0.06, p < .01, Model 5); marginal effects become more negative as overlap increases. The effect is nonsignificant below overlap of 3.07, about 57% of observations. The plotted confidence intervals are 90% intervals.
- The authors report no evidence of a curvilinear main relationship and no significant effect of scouts’ incomplete projects on launches. Including incomplete attempts in experience measures yields stable but weaker results. Supplemental selection and sensitivity analyses are described in the main text; the core findings remain subject to the authors’ residual-endogeneity caveat.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study shifts attention from identifying external knowledge to its downstream integration and depicts scouts’ sustained role throughout that process. It identifies a tension between becoming deeply rooted in a division and remaining versatile across divisions, thereby challenging treatment of the firm as a monolithic knowledge recipient. Technological similarity and interdivisional product-market overlap clarify conditions affecting the transferability of scouts’ experience.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend balancing specialization with broader divisional exposure, particularly across noncompeting divisions and with technologies similar to those divisions previously encountered. They also suggest broadening scouting units’ mandates to support cross-divisional application of external knowledge and providing targeted training in championing and political skills.

**Limitations**
Unobserved heterogeneity and endogeneity cannot be fully ruled out despite controls, selection checks, and contextual evidence. The data do not capture all process complexities, including differences in scouts’ activities and their fit with project needs or timing. Single-firm evidence entails an external-validity tradeoff: Telcorp’s structured scouting, longevity, performance, and internal competitive dynamics may differ from other settings.

**Future Research**
The authors propose tracking integration processes, potentially through qualitative cases, to establish when and why projects stall, and examining experience transfer across subunits within a division. Further inquiry could investigate other industries, corporate venture capital, less structured integration by non-dedicated individuals, firms with complementary rather than overlapping divisions, and other boundary spanners such as consultants.
