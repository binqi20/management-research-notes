# Who Avoids Punishment? How Discretion and Psychopathy Shape Leaders’ Responses to Misconduct

**Abstract**
Punishing misconduct is a key leader responsibility. However, when making punishment decisions, leaders may be overly lenient because of their personal concern for their subordinates’ wellbeing. We propose this mechanism prompts leaders to reduce their punishments when they have the discretion to, but that leaders vary in their likelihood of engaging in such behavior. Integrating insights from the job impact framework and the psychopathy literature, we further propose that discretion reduces the severity of leaders’ punishment decisions, but this effect is attenuated for leaders who are higher in psychopathy and therefore less personally concerned about their subordinates. We tested our theory across five studies. Studies 1 (multisource field survey) and 2a and 2b (experiments) supported our prediction about the relationship between discretion and punishment and the moderating effect of psychopathy on this relationship. Study 2b further established that the effect was mediated by greater prosocial motivation in lower-psychopathy leaders. Studies 3 (critical incident study) and 4 (experiment) further showed that punishment behaviors typical of higher-psychopathy leaders are rated as more effective and moral by both subordinates and third parties, even when these raters are aware of leaders’ psychopathic traits. Our research sheds new light on how leader behaviors that appear more organizationally oriented may nevertheless be driven by darker underlying traits.

**Research Question**
When and why do leaders use discretion over punishment to select less severe responses to misconduct, and how does psychopathy qualify this tendency? How do observers evaluate leaders whose punishments change by different amounts across discretion levels?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Punishment discretion is negatively related to leaders’ punishment severity.
- H2: The negative relationship between punishment discretion and punishment severity is attenuated for leaders higher in psychopathy.
- H3a–b: The moderating effect of psychopathy on the negative relationship between punishment discretion and punishment severity is mediated by (a) prosocial motivation and (b) harm aversion.
- H4: Leaders who reduce their punishment by a smaller amount, from lower- to higher-discretion environments, are rated as more effective.

**Mechanism Process**
- IVs: Punishment discretion in Studies 1, 2a, and 2b; differences in punishment across discretion levels in Studies 3 and 4.
- DVs: Punishment severity; perceived leader effectiveness and, in exploratory analyses, morality.
- Mediators: Prosocial motivation and harm aversion as proposed mediators of psychopathy’s moderating effect (Study 2b); perceived pro-organizational motives linking punishment changes to leader evaluations (Study 4).
- Moderators: Leader psychopathy; Study 4 also tests whether providing information about the leader’s psychopathic traits qualifies evaluations.

The job impact framework suggests that leaders’ interpersonal contact with followers and awareness of their impact foster concern for followers’ wellbeing. With more punishment options, leaders may act on this concern by choosing leniency; higher psychopathy is argued to weaken this response through reduced other-orientation. Study 2b supports the prosocial-motivation pathway for higher versus moderate discretion, but not harm aversion. Observers are theorized to interpret consistent punishment as reflecting organizational rather than personal priorities; Study 4 tests pro-organizational motive attributions, while the proposed task and relational bases of observers’ judgments are not directly measured.

**Data & Measures**
- **Study 1:** An English-language survey recruited 386 leader–follower dyads through India-based Maction Consulting across diverse industries. Leaders reported punishment discretion using three adapted decision-making-autonomy items and psychopathy using four Jonason–Webster items; followers rated expected punishment responses to their own norm violations with five Podsakoff et al. items. Regressions controlled for leader age, sex, income, caste, general autonomy, neuroticism, narcissism, and Machiavellianism. This design is observational.
- **Study 2a:** Of 400 Prolific participants, 399 remained after one attention-check exclusion. Participants were randomly assigned to higher or lower discretion when deducting bonus points for three falsely claimed anagram solutions: permitted deductions were 2–8 versus 4–6 points per false solution, centered on the same five-point standard. Recommended point deductions measured punishment; the four-item psychopathy scale followed the decision. Analyses included a perceived-power control and negative-binomial checks for the count outcome.
- **Study 2b:** Of 600 Prolific participants, 597 remained after three attention-check exclusions. Participants adopted a café-supervisor role and were randomized to lower, moderate, or higher discretion over an employee doing personal business at work. All received the same five-point punishment options, from ignoring the incident to termination; the moderate-discretion condition was the regression reference. Prosocial motivation used three adapted Grant items (α = .62), harm aversion two adapted items (α = .87), and psychopathy the same four-item scale. Mediated moderation multiplied the psychopathy-to-motive path by the discretion-by-motive interaction path, with 5,000 bias-corrected bootstrap resamples.
- **Study 3:** Prolific recruitment yielded 430 participants; excluding 11 attention-check failures, 206 equal-discretion reports, and 11 incomplete responses left 202. Each recalled two misconduct incidents involving the same leader and rated discretion, punishment severity, and misconduct severity on seven-point scales; leader effectiveness and morality each used three items. The source contains a sign inconsistency: Methods defines punishment reduction as higher-discretion severity minus lower-discretion severity (p. 369), whereas Results interprets nonnegative scores as unchanged or reduced punishment under higher discretion (pp. 369–370), implying the opposite subtraction. The primary nonnegative-score subsample contains 137 participants; the supplementary nonpositive-score subsample contains 91. The direction cannot be reconciled from the reported text alone.
- **Study 4:** There were 904 Prolific responses and 894 retained after 10 attention-check exclusions. A randomized 3 × 2 design crossed punishment change (decrease, increase, no change) with psychopathy information (absent, present). A café manager issued a warning in a lower-discretion incident and ignored misconduct, gave a written reprimand, or issued a warning in a higher-discretion incident. When disclosed, low, high, and moderate psychopathy descriptions were yoked to these respective behavior patterns; psychopathy level was not independently crossed with behavior. Effectiveness and morality used the Study 3 scales; perceived pro-organizational motives used four Grant items. Path analyses used 5,000 bootstrap resamples.

Collection dates and the Prolific samples’ countries are not reported. The paper states that all studies except Study 1 were preregistered; it also mentions a supplementary experimental replication of Study 3 without presenting its full results in the article.

**Key Findings**
- **Study 1:** H1 was supported: discretion was negatively associated with punishment severity (b = −.35, SE = .06, p < .001). H2 was supported by the positive discretion × psychopathy interaction (b = .10, SE = .05, p = .035); discretion slopes were negative at both lower psychopathy (b = −.42, p < .001) and higher psychopathy (b = −.20, p = .010). Results were substantively unchanged without controls.
- **Study 2a:** Higher discretion produced less severe punishment (M = 8.78 versus 10.51; F(1, 397) = 12.39, p < .001), supporting H1. The interaction supported H2 (b = 1.33, SE = .62, p = .033): the discretion effect was negative at lower psychopathy (b = −2.77, p < .001) but nonsignificant at higher psychopathy (b = −.66, p = .346). Power controls and negative-binomial regressions preserved the hypothesis-test conclusions.
- **Study 2b:** H1 was not supported (F(2, 594) = .70, p = .499), qualifying the abstract’s broader support statement. H2 received only directional support: the higher-versus-moderate discretion interaction was positive (b = .19, p = .010), but the lower-versus-moderate interaction was nonsignificant (p = .195). Higher versus moderate discretion increased punishment at higher psychopathy (b = .18, p = .013), while its negative slope at lower psychopathy was nonsignificant (b = −.09, p = .243). Prosocial motivation mediated the higher-versus-moderate interaction (indirect b = .06, 95% CI [.01, .13]), supporting H3a for that contrast only; the lower-versus-moderate indirect effect was nonsignificant. H3b was unsupported for both contrasts. Power controls yielded similar results.
- **Study 3:** The authors report support for H4: in the primary n = 137 subsample, punishment reduction was negatively associated with effectiveness (b = −.09, SE = .04, p = .033) and morality (b = −.12, SE = .04, p = .005). Their interpretation is that larger reductions under higher discretion receive worse evaluations, subject to the unresolved formula/sign discrepancy above. Although the prose calls supplementary results consistent, Table 6 shows only marginal evidence for effectiveness in the n = 91 subsample (b = .08, p < .10) and a nonsignificant morality coefficient (b = .05).
- **Study 4:** Consistent punishment was rated more effective (M = 3.62) than decreased (3.37) or increased punishment (3.30; both comparisons p < .001), supporting H4’s consistency-versus-reduction prediction. Consistency also received higher morality ratings (3.82 versus 3.42 and 3.33; both p < .001). Decreased and increased punishment each had negative indirect effects through pro-organizational motives on effectiveness (b = −.14 and −.05) and morality (b = −.15 and −.05), with all reported confidence intervals excluding zero. Psychopathy-information interactions were nonsignificant for effectiveness (p = .402), morality (p = .085), and pro-organizational motives (p = .445). Thus, the evidence favors consistency over both leniency and escalation, rather than a blanket advantage of higher psychopathy.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper distinguishes punishment discretion—the latitude of available actions—from power, showing how discretion can facilitate leniency and how psychopathy qualifies this relationship. It connects punishment patterns to observers’ favorable evaluations, suggesting that apparently desirable behavior can arise from otherwise undesirable traits. This contextual account qualifies general claims about psychopathy and leader effectiveness: consistency is valued, but escalating punishment under greater discretion is evaluated less favorably.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend helping leaders balance concern for transgressors with the deterrence and other benefits of appropriate punishment. Organizations can seek multiple individuals’ input, including skip-level leaders, especially for decisions with severe consequences. Leaders can also anchor decisions in appropriate responses to similar past incidents or seek colleagues’ experiential advice; these are proposed interventions rather than interventions tested in the studies.

**Limitations**
Study 1 is observational, and Study 3 relies on recall without experimentally controlling differences across incidents; psychopathy self-reports may introduce social desirability bias. The interaction’s simple slopes differ across studies, and the model is scoped to misconduct for which discretion can reasonably vary. The observer studies do not establish the full spectrum of behavior across psychopathy levels; Study 4 yokes psychopathy descriptions to behavior, and the proposed task and relational bases of evaluations were not directly measured.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining misconduct types, psychopathy facets and alternative measures, psychological distance and communication medium, and moderators such as moral identity, awareness, and rule knowledge/adherence, alongside causal-chain designs for mechanisms. They call for long-term observation across punishment incidents and contexts, examining relative discretion differences, punishment recipients versus third parties, and the mechanisms underlying observers’ judgments. Fully crossing psychopathy information with punishment change could clarify the effects of trait information on evaluations.
