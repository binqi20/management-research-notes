# Violence and Competition: The Effect of Mass Shootings and Domestic Terrorism on Organizational Risk-Taking in Response to Performance Shortfalls

**Abstract**
This study examines how violent traumatic events in local communities—such as mass shootings and domestic terrorism—affect organizational risk-taking in response to performance shortfalls. Integrating insights from the behavioral theory of the firm and regulatory focus theory, we hypothesize that, by evoking negative emotions, exposure to violent traumatic events lowers decision-makers’ promotion focus and, in turn, reduces their propensity to undertake risky actions aimed at closing the performance–aspiration gap. We further hypothesize that this effect is amplified when decision-makers have longer tenure in the community or are geographically proximate to the site of violence. We test our framework using two complementary empirical approaches: an observational field study in the NFL and two controlled experiments. The field study analyzes 39,343 fourth-down decisions made by NFL teams between 2009 and 2018, linked to data on violent events in the surrounding communities. The experimental studies replicate the primary findings and validate the proposed regulatory focus-based mechanism. Our study extends the behavioral theory of the firm by shifting the lens from dispassionate agents reacting to impersonal forces to decision-makers whose behavior is shaped by personally meaningful community-specific shocks and their emotional consequences.

**Research Question**
How do violent traumatic events in local communities change organizational risk-taking responses to performance shortfalls? Do shifts in decision-makers’ regulatory focus explain this relationship, and do community tenure and geographic proximity amplify it?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: A violent traumatic event in an organization’s community weakens the positive relationship between performance below aspirations and risk-taking.
- H2a: Lowered decision-maker promotion focus partially mediates the effect in H1.
- H2b: Heightened decision-maker prevention focus partially mediates the effect in H1.
- H3: Longer decision-maker tenure in the community strengthens the effect in H1.
- H4: Greater decision-maker proximity to the violent traumatic event strengthens the effect in H1.

**Mechanism Process**
- IVs: Performance below aspirations and exposure to, or experimental salience of, violent traumatic events.
- DV: Risk-taking in response to performance shortfalls.
- Mediators: Lowered promotion focus and heightened prevention focus are hypothesized; only promotion focus receives significant mediation support in both experiments. Negative affect is examined as an antecedent of the promotion-focus shift in supplementary analyses.
- Moderators: Decision-maker community tenure and geographic proximity to the violent event, tested in the NFL field study.

The authors argue that emotionally meaningful community shocks alter decision-makers’ situational motivational states. Dampened promotion focus reduces the pursuit of aspirations through risky remedial actions; heightened prevention focus is theorized to encourage preservation of current conditions. Longer community tenure and closer proximity are proposed to intensify the psychological response. The field data do not directly observe these psychological mechanisms; the experiments assess them.

**Data & Measures**
The NFL field study includes 39,343 fourth-down decisions in 2,526 regular-season games involving 32 teams during 2009–2018, excluding preseason, postseason, and overtime plays. Risk-taking equals one for a “go for it” decision and zero otherwise. Negative attainment discrepancy is the absolute score gap when the focal team trails, and zero otherwise; positive attainment discrepancy records the lead when it is ahead, and zero otherwise. Mother Jones and Global Terrorism Database records identify 386 violent incidents during July 2009–January 2019; the selection requires human casualties and excludes attacks attributed to foreign groups. Exposure equals one for teams within 100 miles during the 30 days following an event. Coach community tenure is years of professional residence in the area, and proximity is measured by team–event distance in miles. The authors use a generalized difference-in-differences design with conditional logit and team/time fixed effects, controlling for game situation, momentum, alternatives, team quality, and decision-maker attributes.

Experiment 1 randomized U.S. Prolific participants across event salience (mass-shooting article and reflection versus music festival) and performance feedback (below versus at aspirations). Of 510 valid responses, 11 suspicious participants were excluded, leaving 499. Participants played three rounds of a cold Columbia Card Task: choosing more cards increased possible gains but also the chance of receiving zero; the number selected measures risk-taking. Round 1 precedes performance feedback; the focal outcome averages rounds 2 and 3. State promotion and prevention focus use four-item scales, measured after the task, alongside affect, desire to win, and self-esteem.

Experiment 2, collected in August 2024, retained 514 of 523 valid responses after excluding nine suspicious participants. It used the same factorial design with an accounting-conference control and asked participants to imagine themselves as organizational decision-makers. Risk-taking is the number of numerical grids participants commit to solving within 45 seconds; solving all earns that number of points, whereas failure earns zero. Regulatory focus, affect, and desire to win were measured before the task; self-efficacy was also assessed. Both experiments use OLS and bootstrapped confidence intervals for mediation analyses.

**Key Findings**
- Field H1: The event × negative-attainment-discrepancy interaction is negative in Table 2, Model 6 (b = −0.097, SE = 0.016, p < .01). Predicted risk-taking at the conditional mean shortfall decreases from 11.96% without exposure to 5.13% with exposure. H3 receives support in the authors’ split-sample analysis: the interaction is stronger for above-median community tenure (b = −0.173, SE = 0.029, p < .01) than below-median tenure (b = −0.042, SE = 0.021, p < .05).
- Field reporting discrepancies: The prose describes odds ratios above one as decreases despite the negative Table 2 coefficients. The authors also report support for H4, with nonsignificant interactions in distance bands beyond 100 miles, but Table 3 prints positive focal interaction coefficients and negative discrepancy coefficients, opposite to Table 2. These source-internal inconsistencies remain unresolved.
- Experiment 1 supports H1: interactions are negative in rounds 2 and 3 and their average (Table 5, Model 4: b = −3.048, SE = 0.962, p < .01). Promotion focus mediates the interaction (H2a; bias-corrected 95% CI [−1.105, −0.153]); prevention focus does not (H2b; [−0.141, 0.436]). Salience has no significant direct effect on risk-taking. Table 5 marks the salience × performance effect on promotion focus as marginal (p < .10), although the prose describes no interaction effect.
- Experiment 2 supports H1 for round 3 and the two-round average (Table 7, Model 4: b = −0.472, SE = 0.173, p < .01). The round-2 interaction is only marginal (b = −0.323, SE = 0.190, p < .10), described as nonsignificant in the prose. H2a is supported (95% CI [−0.306, −0.032]); H2b is not ([−0.059, 0.033]). The prose calls the salience effect on prevention focus marginal, but Table 7 reports an unstarred coefficient of 0.268 (SE = 0.188).
- Negative affect mediates the salience–promotion-focus relationship in both experiments (Experiment 1: 95% CI [−0.189, −0.019]; Experiment 2: [−0.247, −0.071]), but does not directly mediate the salience × performance interaction on risk-taking in either experiment.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the behavioral theory of the firm by treating decision-makers as emotionally affected members of local communities, rather than dispassionate respondents to impersonal environmental forces. It presents regulatory focus as a changing motivational state that mediates external shocks, and extends research on mega-events to organizational competitive behavior.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that boards and investors attend to the possibility that weak responses to performance shortfalls reflect affective states induced by external shocks. Drawing on a supplementary association between pay equality and lower susceptibility to traumatic events, they suggest that inclusive cultures and equitable workplace practices may help managers buffer these effects.

**Limitations**
The NFL setting has short, 60-minute performance intervals and winner-takes-all competition that may not characterize other organizations; sports teams also exemplify action teams rather than teams performing intellective tasks. The experiments simplify real-world decision-making and assume centralized authority, treating individual participants’ responses as conceptually analogous to those of chief organizational decision-makers.

**Future Research**
The authors call for testing generalizability in other settings and examining other emotional shocks, including wars, natural disasters, economic crises, and political upheavals. They also propose studying specific emotions such as hope, anger, and relief, and how community embeddedness shapes locally oriented aspirations and comparison groups.
