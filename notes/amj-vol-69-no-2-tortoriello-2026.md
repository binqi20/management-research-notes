# The Ties That Nurture: Expressive Simmelian Ties, Instrumental Brokerage, and Individual Performance

**Abstract**
In this paper we develop the concept of structural complementarity to explain how individuals’ performance is shaped by their simultaneous positions in structurally distinct networks of different relational nature—specifically, instrumental and expressive networks. Using data from four diverse organizational settings, we demonstrate that the performance advantages of instrumental brokerage are significantly enhanced when individuals are also embedded in cohesive expressive networks characterized by Simmelian ties. These ties provide support, cognitive clarity, and social legitimacy, enabling brokers to better evaluate, mobilize, and act on diverse knowledge. Theoretically, we extend network research by moving beyond tie multiplexity to examine how cross-network structural configurations influence performance outcomes. Practically, our findings offer actionable insights for organizations seeking to design and support network structures that unlock the full value of informal relationships.

**Research Question**
How do individuals’ simultaneous structural positions in networks of different relational natures combine to shape performance? Specifically, do expressive Simmelian ties enhance the performance benefits of instrumental brokerage beyond the effects of tie overlap?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Simmelian ties in expressive or noninstrumental networks enhance the positive relationship between brokerage in instrumental networks and individual performance.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV: Instrumental brokerage, operationalized as instrumental structural holes.
- DV: Individual performance—supervisor evaluations or patent filings, depending on setting.
- Mediators: No mediation tests; evaluating options, obtaining early support, and projecting legitimacy are proposed mechanisms.
- Moderator: Expressive Simmelian ties—reciprocal relationships embedded in fully reciprocated triadic cliques.

Instrumental brokerage offers access to diverse knowledge but can impose cognitive demands, uncertainty, and emotional strain while providing limited trust and support. The authors argue that cohesive expressive ties provide a secure context for evaluating information, coordinating initial support, and signaling legitimacy to others. Structural complementarity combines these advantages across distinct networks; the empirical tests concern the interaction of network structures rather than direct measurement of these mechanisms.

**Data & Measures**
Four cross-sectional network surveys used an online free-choice–aided name generator, developed after field interviews, and were linked to company archival data:

- Consumer products HR: 385 respondents from 483 employees (80%); instrumental work-advice ties and expressive friendship ties; direct-supervisor annual performance ratings on a 1–4 scale.
- Luxury hotel: 165 respondents from 209 employees (79%); work-advice and friendship ties; direct-supervisor annual performance ratings on a 1–5 scale.
- Semiconductor R&D: 245 respondents from 276 employees (89%); work-advice and interpersonal-trust ties; individual patent filings from monthly R&D reports.
- Pharmaceutical R&D: 101 respondents from 113 employees (89%); science-related knowledge exchange and friendship ties; individual patent filings from monthly R&D reports. In both R&D settings, filings cover 6 months before and 12 months after the network survey; a check restricted to the subsequent 12 months reportedly leaves results unchanged.

Instrumental structural holes equal one minus summed dyadic constraint: first add the direct component Pij to the sum of indirect products PiqPqj through third parties, square that entire sum to obtain Cij, sum Cij across ego’s alters, then subtract from one. An expressive tie is Simmelian when ego, alter, and a third party have all six directed ties among them; the measure counts such ties per ego. The methods describe mean-centering this count, whereas Tables 2a–2c report mean 0 and SD 1, and Table 2d reports mean 0.04 and SD 1.03; the paper does not reconcile these scaling descriptions. Multiplexity is the proportion of ego’s instrumental ties that are also expressive, averaging 15%, 25%, 46%, and 26% in the four settings, respectively.

Controls include sex, tenure, education (job level as its proxy in the hotel), organizational affiliation, both network sizes, and setting-specific characteristics; R&D models include patent filings over the preceding 10 years. Alternative network structures enter additional models. OLS estimates supervisor ratings; negative-binomial GLMs estimate patent counts, with standard errors clustered by semiconductor laboratory and pharmaceutical specialization area. These are observational associations; endogeneity analyses use 2SLS for OLS settings and control functions for GLMs, with instruments based on hiring-year/location and sex/location opportunities for ties and their interactions with expressive Simmelian ties.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported in the baseline models (Table 3): the instrumental-structural-holes × expressive-Simmelian-ties coefficients are 0.484 (p < .01) for consumer products, 1.204 (p < .05) for the hotel, 1.976 (p < .01) for semiconductors, and 4.215 (p < .01) for pharmaceuticals. Expressive Simmelian ties have negative conditional main coefficients in these models; their contribution depends on instrumental brokerage.
- The positive focal interaction persists with alternative network-structure controls (Table 4, Model 1), but the hotel coefficient is only marginal (0.884, p < .10). Alternative cross-network interactions are nonsignificant in three settings; in pharmaceuticals, Simmelian-ties × Simmelian-ties and structural-holes × structural-holes are negative and marginal. Table 5’s endogeneity analyses report focal interactions of 0.635 (p < .10), 1.957 (p < .05), 1.538 (p < .01), and 9.885 (p < .05), respectively. Thus the tables qualify the prose’s broad claims of statistically significant support across settings and specifications.
- Marginal effects increase with expressive Simmelian ties; Table 6’s hotel effect at one SD below their mean is nonsignificant. Replacing Simmelian ties with expressive density does not reproduce positive moderation; the reported semiconductor interaction is significantly negative, qualifying the accompanying statement of no statistically significant impact. The authors report that controlling for expressive Simmelian brokerage does not substantively alter the findings, and that nonresponse checks and observed-versus-imputed-network ERGM comparisons reveal no detectable bias in the examined characteristics and structural measures.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper develops structural complementarity: performance advantages arising from occupying distinct structural positions in networks of different natures. It extends multiplexity research beyond overlapping dyads to the interplay of structures across networks, and identifies expressive cohesion as a condition under which instrumental brokerage yields greater performance benefits.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise senior executives and organizational designers to complement brokerage-promoting structures, such as matrix arrangements, rotational assignments, and cross-functional teams, with internal mentoring, peer-learning networks, and community-building initiatives that facilitate trust-based expressive ties. Middle managers and team leaders should cultivate both local expressive bonds and selective bridging, especially for people working across teams, departments, or knowledge domains.

**Limitations**
The cross-sectional designs cannot establish how instrumental and expressive relationships form, evolve, or depend on one another; unobserved personal characteristics and choices also create endogeneity concerns. Treating brokerage structurally leaves differences in boundary type, brokering orientation, tie origins, and long- versus short-term bridging insufficiently examined. The authors note that the interaction and its underlying mechanisms may vary by organizational context, with expressive ties appearing more consequential across the brokerage range in R&D settings than in the HR and hotel settings.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying instrumental–expressive network coevolution and variation in brokerage types, orientations, and temporal stability. Research across other organizations and innovative versus routine tasks could distinguish the proposed mechanisms, while other network types (formal/informal, positive/negative) and structures (centrality, peripheral embedding, structural equivalence) could reveal complementary, substitutive, or detrimental performance effects.
