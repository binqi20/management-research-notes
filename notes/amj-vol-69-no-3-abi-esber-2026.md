# Team Hierarchical Adaptability: Benefits for Team Coordination and Performance

**Abstract**
We introduce the concept of hierarchical adaptability, which we define as a team’s relative capability to repeatedly and bidirectionally shift between different shapes of its influence hierarchy (i.e., more hierarchical or flatter) across tasks, while the team’s formal hierarchy remains constant. We provide a first investigation of the effects of team hierarchical adaptability, proposing that team hierarchical adaptability enables teams to achieve better coordination and team performance outcomes as they move across different tasks, compared to consistently hierarchical or flat teams. Five multimethod studies, including field data of intact teams and a laboratory experiment of interacting teams, provide support for our hypotheses.

**Research Question**
Can teams improve coordination and performance by repeatedly and bidirectionally shifting the shape of their informal influence hierarchy between hierarchical and flatter forms across tasks, while keeping their formal hierarchy constant?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Hierarchical adaptability is positively related to team coordination.
- H2: Team coordination is positively related to team performance outcomes.
- H3: Hierarchical adaptability is positively related to team performance outcomes.
- H4: Team coordination mediates the relationship between hierarchical adaptability and team performance outcomes.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Team hierarchical adaptability; experimentally encouraged adaptability versus static hierarchy or static flatness in Study 4.
- DV(s): Team coordination and team performance outcomes.
- Mediators: Team coordination; exploratory serial pathways through implicit coordination then divergent thinking, and explicit coordination then convergent thinking, in Study 4. Psychological safety is an additional mediator in Study 3.
- Moderators: Not reported in paper.

Hierarchical adaptability concerns changes in the shape of informal influence, rather than changing formal positions or simply rotating the person at the top. The authors propose that activating different structural scripts aligns influence with task demands: flatter scripts facilitate lateral coordination for divergent tasks, whereas hierarchical scripts facilitate explicit coordination for convergent tasks. They also propose that shifting role-based scripts energizes coordination; cognitive energy is a theoretical explanation rather than a directly tested mediator here. Coordination then helps teams combine individual inputs into collective performance.

**Data & Measures**
- **Study 1A:** A retrospective MTurk survey of 405 full-time employees in the United States developed a five-item, seven-point hierarchical adaptability scale. Respondents described a recent team and rated adaptability alongside ten related hierarchy, flexibility, and leadership constructs. Factor models and correlations assessed construct distinctiveness.
- **Study 1B:** A retrospective Prolific survey of 194 participants in the United States used the same adaptability scale, three coordination items adapted from Lewis, and four team-performance items. The confirmatory factor analysis was preregistered; hypothesis tests and bootstrapped mediation were exploratory. These were individual reports about teams, not observations of 194 intact interacting teams.
- **Study 2:** A United States health services chain supplied 106 teams with at least three employee responses each: 1,125 employees and one manager per team. Employee ratings of five-item adaptability and three-item coordination were mean-aggregated to the team level and matched to managers’ four-item performance ratings. The main regressions were associational; additional process-conflict and hierarchy-steepness analyses are referenced in the online supplement.
- **Study 3:** In a virtual start-up idea competition, 954 working professionals with entrepreneurial experience recruited from across the United States completed the study in 348 leaderless teams. Participants were randomly assigned to three-person teams, with two-person teams proceeding when only two members returned. Following preparation and an intrateam negotiation, members rated adaptability and coordination. Performance was the sum of members’ points on five negotiated issues, comprising two distributive and three integrative issues. The expanded regression included coordination, open communication, the paper’s “transactional memory” measure, role clarity, trust, power struggles, psychological safety, and team conflict. Adaptability itself was measured, not randomized.
- **Study 4:** A London university laboratory recruited 237 participants into 79 three-person teams, including students and members of the local public. Every team had an assigned formal leader; teams were randomized to adaptability, static hierarchy, or static flatness instructions reinforced by a writing task. A hidden-profile logic task produced an objective score from 0 to 16; post-task ratings measured explicit coordination, adaptability, convergent thinking, and divergent thinking. An implicit-coordination scale was added after collection began and used a smaller subsample. The paper separately reports 235 observations for its CFA without explaining the difference from recruitment. Main directional comparisons of adaptability with each static condition used one-tailed tests; other between-condition analyses, including comparisons involving exploratory variables, used two-tailed tests.

**Key Findings**
- **Studies 1A–1B:** Measurement comparisons supported distinguishing adaptability from related constructs and from coordination and performance. Study 1A’s five-item scale had α = .84; its adaptability AVE is reported as .52 in the prose but .51 in Table 3. Study 1B’s exploratory associations were positive for adaptability–coordination (b = .55), coordination–performance (b = .85), and adaptability–performance (b = .59), all p < .001; the indirect effect was .45, 95% CI [.24, .63]. The authors acknowledged a higher-than-desirable RMSEA for Study 1B’s single-factor adaptability model.
- **Study 2:** H1 and H2 were supported (b = .72, p < .001; b = .18, p = .006, respectively). H3 was not supported: adaptability’s performance coefficient was .09, p = .266. H4 was supported by an indirect effect of .15, p = .041; the PDF prints its CI lower bound as “014,” so that bound is not interpreted here (Table 5 and p. 576).
- **Study 3:** H1–H4 were supported in the main tests: adaptability predicted coordination (b = .35, p < .001) and performance (b = 13.00, p = .002); coordination predicted performance (b = 16.19, p < .001), and the indirect effect was 5.15, 95% CI [1.51, 11.38]. Adaptability remained positively associated with performance in the expanded regression (b = 9.36, p = .029), although its coefficient was nonsignificant with coordination alone, and coordination was only marginally significant in the expanded model (Table 7). In parallel mediation, coordination and psychological safety both retained significant indirect effects; psychological safety was not ruled out as an alternative pathway.
- **Study 4:** Adaptable teams reported greater explicit coordination than hierarchical teams (5.30 versus 4.38, p = .006) and flat teams (4.68, p = .033). They also achieved higher task scores (10.71 versus 6.88 and 6.08, p = .010 and .003, respectively). Coordination positively predicted performance (b = 2.96, p < .001), and the indirect effect was 2.10, 95% CI [.57, 3.91], p = .009, supporting H1–H4. Hierarchical and flat teams did not significantly differ in explicit coordination or performance.
- **Study 4 exploratory qualifications:** Convergent thinking was higher in adaptable teams than in both static conditions. Divergent thinking and implicit coordination were higher than in hierarchical teams, but their differences from flat teams were nonsignificant (p = .189 and .177, respectively). The serial indirect effects through implicit coordination then divergent thinking (1.65, 95% CI [.50, 3.39]) and explicit coordination then convergent thinking (1.32, 95% CI [.45, 2.58]) were significant. Reversing the ordering yielded only marginal support for divergent thinking then implicit coordination (1.09, 95% CI [−.02, 2.55], p = .097), while the convergent-thinking-first pathway was significant (1.85, 95% CI [.82, 3.38], p = .005); the authors therefore recognize potentially bidirectional relationships (p. 583, footnote 7).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces hierarchical adaptability as a team capability for temporally combining hierarchy and equality through repeated changes in informal influence shape. It complements structural adaptation theory’s focus on one-time formal restructuring and extends rotating-leadership arguments beyond changes in who occupies the top position. Its input-process-outcome account links this capability to coordination and performance without equating structural scripts with coordination behaviors.

**Practical Implication**
Managers could teach teams to adapt their interaction structure to the task and use memorable mantras, physical artifacts, or rituals to signal shifts. Meeting agendas can mark a leader’s initial framing, a flatter brainstorming or debate phase, and a return to leader decision-making, helping teams practice these transitions.

**Limitations**
Study 2 did not establish a direct performance association; the authors suggest seasonal membership instability as a possible explanation rather than a demonstrated moderator. Studies 2–3 measured adaptability and coordination in the same survey, raising common-method concerns. The research does not directly compare hierarchical adaptability with rotating leadership, and Study 4’s exploratory analyses do not uniquely establish the ordering of coordination and thinking.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on how adaptability develops, persists, or diminishes, who initiates shifts, and how hierarchy bases, team context, and signals shape these transitions. They propose comparing and combining different dynamic team structures and investigating when the direct performance relationship emerges, including membership instability and other mechanisms. They also call for behavioral measures of coordination, potentially using natural-language processing and artificial intelligence to identify coordination cues.
