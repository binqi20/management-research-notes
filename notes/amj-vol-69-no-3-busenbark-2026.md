# Moderately Fast and Furious: A Screening and Behavioral Theory of New CEO Strategic Action Speed

**Abstract**
CEO successions induce considerable uncertainty for external stakeholders like financial analysts, who eagerly await insight from new CEOs about their first large-scale strategy. But emerging research offers equivocal implications about analyst evaluations of how long new CEOs wait to announce strategy, with conventional theoretical logic contending analysts appreciate expediency and other work suggesting new CEOs benefit from patience. To place this paradigm into sharper view, we build theory about “new CEO strategic action speed,” a construct we introduce constituting the amount of time that passes between the CEO appointment date and the first strategic action announcement. Fusing tenets of screening theory with the behavioral theory of the firm (BToF), we argue that analysts prefer faster new CEO strategic action speeds, except when they expect CEOs to undertake search before charting firm strategy. We specifically theorize that there exists an inverted U-shaped relationship between new CEO strategic action speed and analyst favorability when analysts expect CEOs to engage in greater degrees of search, but there is a negative association when analysts would not expect search. Our theory changes how scholars think about traditional logics from information economics, expands insights from the BToF, and illuminates key considerations for new CEOs.

**Research Question**
How do financial analysts evaluate the time new CEOs take to announce their first strategic action, and when do expectations of search make analysts appreciate an initial delay rather than immediate action?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: New CEO strategic action speed has an inverted U-shaped relationship with analyst favorability toward the first strategic announcement. Here, greater action-speed values mean a longer elapsed interval and thus slower action.
- H2: The relationship is inverted U-shaped when the prior CEO was dismissed and negative when the prior CEO was not dismissed.
- H3: The relationship is inverted U-shaped for outsider successors and negative for insider successors.
- H4: The relationship is inverted U-shaped when the firm is performing poorly and negative when it is performing strongly.
- H5: The relationship is inverted U-shaped when the announced action is new to the firm's repertoire and negative when it is already in the repertoire.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): New CEO strategic action speed, defined as elapsed time from appointment to the first strategic action announcement.
- DV(s): Net analyst favorability following that announcement.
- Mediators: Expected search is the proposed mechanism; it is examined through contingent predictions rather than a directly measured mediator.
- Moderators: Prior CEO dismissal, outsider CEO status, firm performance as assessed by analysts, and whether the announced action is new to the firm's repertoire.

Action speed serves as an information screen through which analysts infer the new CEO's strategic orientation, without requiring intentional signaling by the CEO. Analysts seek prompt information to reduce uncertainty, but the authors argue that they initially appreciate delay when they expect the CEO to need time for orientation and search. Beyond that interval, prolonged uncertainty outweighs the perceived benefits of search; where little search is expected, delay is predicted to receive increasingly unfavorable evaluations.

**Data & Measures**
The archival sample comprises 880 observations, each a non-interim CEO succession followed by a strategic action announcement, at S&P 500 firms with successions during 2005–2019. ExecuComp appointment and departure dates were checked against the Gentry et al. succession database; appointment dates were also verified using RavenPack. The authors excluded interim successors, multiple successions in the same year, and CEOs returning to a role previously held at the company. RavenPack announcements categorized as factual were manually examined to identify the earliest announcement of a new product or market expansion, acquisition, strategic alliance, capacity change, or large divestiture/spinoff. Analyst data came from I/B/E/S, supplemented by Compustat, ISS, CRSP, and Thomson 13f data merged with Bushee's investor classifications.

Net analyst favorability is the fraction of actively recommending analysts who upgraded the stock, using each analyst's first evaluative report within a month after the announcement. Its denominator includes upgrades, holds, and downgrades; subtracting downgrades from upgrades is a separate supplementary specification. The predictor first counts days between CEO appointment and the first strategic announcement, then takes the natural log; models include that logged predictor and its square. Dismissal and outsider status are binary; insiders include CEOs employed at the firm within the preceding two years or identified as heirs apparent. Firm performance is proxied by the median analyst recommendation at succession, coded from strong buy = 5 to strong sell = 1. A new action is one absent from the firm's repertoire over the preceding year.

The two-stage fractional response models use a Bayesian probit system and heteroskedasticity-identified instruments to address potential confounding, with strategic-action, year, and one-digit SIC industry fixed effects. Controls cover heir-apparent status, CEO duality, female CEO status and age, succession lead time, predecessor board membership, time since the predecessor's last action, market-to-book, ROA, debt, cash, logged assets, dedicated/transient investors, board composition, analyst recommendation dispersion, and prior-year strategic intensity. Variables are measured before succession unless otherwise noted in the paper. These are observational estimates; the proposed search mechanism is not directly measured.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: the overall relationship is inverted U-shaped, with a reported turning point of approximately 34 days. Table 2, Panel II reports a positive logged-speed coefficient (b = 0.034, C.V. = 0.013) and negative squared term (b = −0.005, C.V. = 0.039). C.V. denotes posterior relative uncertainty, not a standard error.
- H2–H4 are supported: the inverted U appears after predecessor dismissal, for outsider CEOs, and at low prior analyst recommendations, with reported turning points of approximately 182, 281, and 473 days, respectively. The corresponding non-dismissal, insider, and high-recommendation conditions show negative associations. Table 3 reports all displayed marginal-effect p values as < .05.
- H5 is only partially supported. Both new and existing action types show inverted U-shaped relationships, with the latter more pronounced, rather than the predicted negative relationship for existing actions. Table 3 reports turning points of 30.265 days for new actions and 39.528 days for existing actions. Figure 5 shows higher predicted favorability and a flatter decline for new actions; the authors interpret this as greater leniency and “more time for search,” although the reported new-action turning point is earlier. The table also reports a negative new-action slope at its labeled 28-day value despite the reported 30.265-day turning point; this source-internal inconsistency is retained without recalculation.
- The main text reports similar inference from models without instruments and alternative outcome measures. Its exploratory analyses suggest an inverted U under greater competitive aggressiveness or lower strategic diversity, and a negative relationship under the opposite conditions; the authors explicitly characterize these extensions as preliminary.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends the behavioral theory of the firm from managerial search to external stakeholders' expectations of search, including triggers beyond performance shortfalls. It gives screening theory a contextual account of why the same cue can attract favorable or unfavorable evaluations and introduces new CEO strategic action speed into research on stakeholder appraisals after succession.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that new CEOs might deliberate somewhat faster than they historically have to garner more favorable analyst responses. Their reported turning points vary with succession and firm circumstances, while many observed CEOs waited beyond those points; the practical argument concerns analyst evaluations of the first strategic announcement.

**Limitations**
The authors did not develop a sufficiently detailed theory of action-speed antecedents or of the particular strategic actions firms choose. They also acknowledge that a successor's first announced action may have originated with the predecessor, motivating their attempts to address confounding and their control for time since the predecessor's last action.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying how competitive conditions, executive characteristics, and investor preferences shape action speed, and how speed influences subsequent rivalrous exchanges. They also call for deeper work on action-specific analyst responses, the drivers and strategic implications of faster announcements over time, and whether initial action speed affects analysts' evaluations of later actions.
