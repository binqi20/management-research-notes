# Market-Stigmatized Groups and the Effectiveness of Stigma Management Strategies: Evidence from Washington’s Marijuana Market

**Abstract**
Some organizations in stigmatized markets are more successful than others at managing audience perceptions and attracting consumers. Despite extensive research on organizational stigma management, less is known about the conditions under which different strategies drive consumer patronage. This study examines two broad approaches—(1) distancing from the stigmatized market, and (2) displaying associations with the stigmatized market—that operate through opposing mechanisms and offer competing prescriptions for organizations. We consider how group stigma affects dynamics between organizations and consumers to influence the effectiveness of stigma management strategies. We introduce the concept of market-stigmatized groups and predict that the local concentration of these groups moderates the effectiveness of distancing and displaying in generating sales. Examining dispensaries in Washington state’s marijuana market, we find that displaying strategies are associated with lower sales in areas with higher concentrations of Black residents, a market-stigmatized group in this industry, whereas distancing strategies have diverging effects. Our study contributes to stigma research by demonstrating that the effectiveness of stigma management strategies is contingent on the local concentration of market-stigmatized groups, showing how stigmas interact across levels to affect organizational outcomes, and identifying a source of performance heterogeneity in stigmatized markets.

**Research Question**
In stigmatized markets, when and how does group stigma influence the effectiveness of organizations’ stigma management strategies? The study examines whether the local concentration of market-stigmatized groups conditions the relationship between distancing or displaying strategies and consumer sales.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a: The concentration of market-stigmatized groups positively moderates the effectiveness of distancing through medical language: dispensaries using medical language are more effective in generating sales as the concentration of Black residents increases.
- H1b: The same positive moderation is predicted for distancing through wellness language.
- H2: The concentration of market-stigmatized groups negatively moderates the effectiveness of displaying through vice language: dispensaries using vice language are less effective in generating sales as the concentration of Black residents increases.

**Mechanism Process**
- IVs: Distancing strategies expressed through medical or wellness language, and displaying strategies expressed through vice language in dispensary product names and descriptions.
- DV: Monthly dispensary consumer sales.
- Moderator: Local concentration of market-stigmatized groups, operationalized as the county percentage of Black residents; Hispanic and Asian resident percentages provide comparisons with less straightforward or distinct stigma content.
- Proposed mechanism: Greater salience of market stigma and heightened consumer concerns about stigma transfer; these concerns are not directly measured or tested as mediators.

Market-stigmatized groups are social groups whose stigmatizing labels share content with those attached to a market; group members need not themselves be consumers. The authors argue that shared stigma content makes market and group stigmas more cognitively accessible, increasing consumers’ sensitivity to stigma transfer in areas with greater concentrations of these groups. Distancing is predicted to reassure consumers by weakening association with market stigma, whereas displaying may reinforce transfer concerns rather than normalize the market. To interpret the unexpected wellness result, the authors suggest that medical framing may provide a broadly acceptable moral justification while wellness framing may reinforce racialized distinctions; these are proposed explanations rather than measured consumer responses.

**Data & Measures**
The full unbalanced panel contains 309 Washington dispensaries observed during eight periods between November 2015 and June 2019, yielding 1,366 dispensary-months; these dispensaries represent 71% of the 433 licensed dispensaries and appear on Weedmaps at least once. Weedmaps product data are linked to Washington State Liquor and Cannabis Board sales, suspension, location, and license records. The main analysis uses the 277 dispensaries with available county-level one-year American Community Survey estimates, yielding 1,207 dispensary-month observations; the ACS estimates span 2014–2019. Interviews with 10 dispensary owners or workers provided contextual information and confirmed dispensaries’ discretion over product descriptions.

Sales are the total monthly USD value of marijuana products sold to consumers, cube-root transformed following a Box–Cox procedure selecting λ = 0.33. Black, Hispanic, and Asian are county population percentages. For flowers and concentrates, the authors concatenate product names and descriptions and construct medical, wellness, and vice dictionaries through iterative hand coding and term-level coding. An initial 338 terms were validated with an industry expert and a pairwise word-comparison survey of 377 Washington respondents providing 15,080 responses, producing 313 final terms. For each dispensary and period, each language measure divides the number of terms belonging to its dictionary by all terms in the dispensary’s product names and descriptions, then multiplies by 100; the three measures are not mutually exclusive. Each language measure is interacted with each demographic percentage.

Controls cover logged dispensary age and product count, percentage concentrates, medical endorsement, commoditization (the proportion of products described solely by chemical composition), competition (the inverse-distance-weighted sum of other dispensaries in Washington), county density of residents aged 21 or older, the Trump-administration indicator, and monthly suspension days. The observational models use Baltagi–Wu mixed effects GLS with dispensary random effects, time-period fixed effects, and AR(1) disturbances. The Heckman selection check jointly estimates Weedmaps listing and sales using full information maximum likelihood and time fixed effects; its selection equation includes age, competition, medical endorsement, and county internet access, with internet access serving as the exclusion restriction. Table 5 reports n = 2,378 for this analysis.

**Key Findings**
- In Table 4, H1a receives support in the full model: Black × medical is positive and marginal in Model 4 (b = 0.40, p < .10), and positive and significant in Model 7 (b = 0.63, p < .01). H1b is not supported: Black × wellness is negative in Model 5 (b = -0.54, p < .001) and Model 7 (b = -0.38, p < .001). The prose on p. 662 describes the Model 7 coefficient as the same as Model 5, whereas Table 4 reports these different values; both sources agree on the negative, significant relationship.
- H2 is supported: Black × vice is negative in Model 6 (b = -0.77, p < .001) and Model 7 (b = -0.57, p < .001). These coefficients concern cube-root-transformed monthly sales. The authors report a positive conditional relationship between vice language and sales at low concentrations of Black residents and a negative relationship at higher concentrations. Their prose gives 4.30% as the positive-to-negative threshold, but Figure 1’s plotted point estimates cross zero earlier; this source discrepancy remains unresolved.
- Demographic comparisons are qualified. In Table 4, Model 7, Hispanic × vice is negative but marginal (b = -0.07, p < .10); Hispanic × medical and Hispanic × wellness are nonsignificant. Asian × wellness (b = 0.15, p < .01) and Asian × vice (b = 0.20, p < .001) are positive, while Asian × medical is nonsignificant. Thus, the principal comparison pattern differs from that for Black residents.
- The Heckman models retain the focal directional pattern but not uniform significance. In Table 5, Black × medical is nonsignificant in Model 4 and positive in Model 7 (b = 1.06, p < .05); Black × wellness is negative but only marginal in Model 7 (b = -0.43, p < .10), and Black × vice remains negative (b = -0.95, p < .001). Hispanic × vice is nonsignificant in Model 7, and Asian × medical is negative and significant (b = -0.84, p < .01), unlike the nonsignificant main-model comparison. The article reports additional checks using alternative time controls, estimators, ACS data, controls, and sample restrictions as consistent, referring their details to Additional Materials.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study identifies the local concentration of market-stigmatized groups as a boundary condition for stigma management, qualifying the normalizing promise of displaying and showing that medical and wellness distancing have different associations with sales. Its construct of market-stigmatized groups links conceptually distinct group and market stigmas across levels. By examining consumer sales, the paper connects these contextual strategy differences to performance heterogeneity among organizations within the same stigmatized market.

**Practical Implication**
Managers positioning products and services in stigmatized markets should assess how local group stigma and market stigma reinforce one another, because openly aligning with stigma to foster affinity can backfire. The authors recommend developing competence in managing both group and market stigma and recognizing that practices successful with some audiences or in some locations may not have universal appeal.

**Limitations**
Aggregate dispensary sales and county demographics cannot establish whether the observed patterns reflect market-stigmatized consumers, their neighbors, or both; consumers’ stigma-transfer concerns are inferred rather than measured, and county data miss more localized dynamics. Generalizability may be limited by the market’s recent transition from state illegality, continuing federal illegality and illegal operators, and discriminatory enforcement that may make stigma unusually salient. Washington’s relatively small Black population is another scope condition: the sample’s county Black percentage averages 5.15% and reaches 10.2% at its maximum.

**Future Research**
The authors propose experiments or surveys measuring stigma perceptions, comparisons of consumer subgroups’ responses to distancing strategies, and census-block or neighborhood analyses of local context. They also suggest studying market-stigmatized owners, managers, and employees within organizations, larger local concentrations of market-stigmatized groups, and whether less entrenched stigmas require larger population shares to produce similar effects. Further directions concern stigma dynamics during legalization, the different temporal trajectories of group and market stigmas, and how their historical sequence shapes destigmatization.
