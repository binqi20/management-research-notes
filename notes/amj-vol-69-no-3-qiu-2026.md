# When the Moon Is Jealous of the Stars: Implications of Informal Leadership for Supervisor Downward Jealousy and Their Treatment of Other Subordinates

**Abstract**
Informal leadership has received considerable attention over the last decade, yet the literature remains unclear about the interpersonal dynamics in teams where both informal and formal leadership coexist. Integrating the social functional view of emotions with recent theoretical advancements on workplace jealousy, we propose a theoretical model that explains how supervisors react emotionally and behaviorally to informal leadership. Specifically, we theorize that, when an informal leader is highly competent, their informal leadership will elicit supervisor downward jealousy stemming from the supervisor’s fear of losing valued relationships with the other subordinates; and this jealousy, in turn, will prompt more supervisor support and less abusive supervision toward the other subordinates who are also highly competent. We adopted a multiwave, multisource field study and an experiment using the critical incident technique to test our model and hypotheses. Results across these studies consistently supported our predictions. We discuss the theoretical and practical implications as well as future research directions of our work.

**Research Question**
Why, how, and when do supervisors respond emotionally to informal leadership, and how does supervisor downward jealousy shape their treatment of other subordinates beyond the informal–formal leader dyad?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Informal leader competence strengthens the positive relationship between informal leadership and supervisor downward jealousy.
- H2: Other subordinate competence strengthens the positive relationship between supervisor downward jealousy and supervisor support toward other subordinates.
- H3: Other subordinate competence strengthens the negative relationship between supervisor downward jealousy and abusive supervision toward other subordinates.
- H4: The positive indirect relationship between informal leadership and supervisor support through supervisor downward jealousy is stronger when both informal leader competence and other subordinate competence are higher.
- H5: The negative indirect relationship between informal leadership and abusive supervision through supervisor downward jealousy is stronger when both informal leader competence and other subordinate competence are higher.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV: Informal leadership, understood as leaderlike influence without formal authority.
- DVs: Supervisor support and abusive supervision toward the other subordinate, rather than toward the informal leader.
- Mediator: Supervisor downward jealousy—the supervisor's fear of losing valued relationships with subordinates to an actual or imagined rival for their attention.
- Moderators: Informal leader competence on the informal leadership–jealousy relationship; other subordinate competence on the jealousy–support and jealousy–abuse relationships.

The triad comprises the supervisor as jealous actor, the informal leader as rival, and the other subordinate as target. The authors argue that a competent informal leader can shift subordinates' dependence away from the supervisor, threatening valued relationships and eliciting jealousy. Jealous supervisors are then motivated to reinforce dependence through support and safeguard relationships by avoiding abuse, particularly toward competent subordinates whose relationships they value highly. These dependence-based explanations are the proposed theoretical processes, not separately measured mediators.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1 (pp. 631–636): A chain hotel in East China provided access to 93 teams; 75 nominated at least one informal leader. One nominated informal leader and one non-nominated subordinate were randomly selected per team. Excluding three supervisors and two subordinates for attention-check failures or unusually rapid completion left 70 independent supervisor–subordinate pairs. At Time 1, supervisors rated the focal informal leader's informal leadership and competence; one month later they rated downward jealousy, downward envy, and the other subordinate's competence; another month later the other subordinate rated supervisor support and abuse. Informal leadership used six items from Guo et al. (2022); each competence measure used six items adapted from Kim et al. (2021); jealousy used an eight-item scale developed from prior workplace-jealousy research. Support used three items adapted from Eisenberger et al. (2002), and abuse used five items from Mitchell and Ambrose (2007). The model included supervisor gender, categorical organizational tenure, and nine-item downward envy as a control/alternative mechanism. Mplus 8.0 path analysis used maximum likelihood with robust standard errors and full information maximum likelihood for missing data. Informal leadership, both competence variables, and jealousy were grand-mean centered; conditional indirect effects used 10,000-repetition Monte Carlo tests. This design is correlational.
- Study 2 (pp. 637–642): After prescreening 1,016 managers, 550 full-time managers from various industries in the United States and United Kingdom generated lists containing at least one high-informal-leadership subordinate and two low-informal-leadership subordinates. Eighteen attention-check failures and 13 inability-to-recall exclusions left 519 participants. Random assignment to eight conditions manipulated high/low informal leadership, the first subordinate's competence, and a different subordinate's competence through recalled incidents involving randomly selected listed subordinates. The term informal leader competence labels the first person's competence even when that person is in the low-informal-leadership condition. Jealousy used the adapted eight-item measure; support was intended personal time devoted to helping the other subordinate, rated from 1 (none) to 5 (all); abuse was selection among five increasingly abusive statements in a project-delay scenario. These are scenario responses, not observed workplace behavior. ANOVA and Mplus 8.0 path analysis tested the model, with 10,000-repetition Monte Carlo tests for indirect effects. Manipulation checks were conducted in a separate pilot, retaining 301 of 314 participants. Table 6's footnote labels high/low moderator values as ±1 SD, whereas the Methods specifies randomized high/low conditions; the paper does not reconcile these descriptions.
- Ancillary evidence: The article reports jealousy-scale development samples with final Ns of 49, 128, and 296, and separate support/abuse measure-validation samples of 141, 80, and 148; these are distinct from the two main model-testing samples. A further 65-supervisor recall exercise supplies illustrative narratives (pp. 626, 632, 638).

**Key Findings**
- Study 1: H1 was supported by the informal leadership × informal leader competence interaction (b = .09, SE = .04, p = .013). Informal leadership was positively associated with jealousy at high informal leader competence (b = .25, p = .045), but not at low competence (b = .04, p = .427). The jealousy × other subordinate competence interactions supported H2 for support (b = .42, p = .009) and H3 for abuse (b = −.42, p = .011). At high other subordinate competence, jealousy predicted more support (b = .61, p = .001) and less abuse (b = −.47, p = .003); at low competence, its association with support was nonsignificant (b = −.44, p = .111), but its association with abuse was positive (b = .58, p = .036). This latter, non-hypothesized result qualifies any general claim that jealousy improves leadership behavior (Table 2; pp. 633–634).
- Study 1: H4–H5 were supported. When both competence moderators were high, the indirect effect through jealousy was positive for support (.15, 95% CI [.01, .32]) and negative for abuse (−.12, 95% CI [−.27, −.002]). Each differed significantly from the corresponding indirect effect under each of the other three moderator combinations; those other indirect effects had CIs spanning zero and did not differ significantly from one another (Table 3).
- Study 2: H1 was supported by the informal leadership × informal leader competence interaction, F(1, 515) = 9.14, p = .003. At high informal leader competence, high versus low informal leadership elicited greater jealousy (M = 3.30 versus 2.51, p < .001); at low competence, the difference was nonsignificant (M = 2.60 versus 2.44, p = .294). The jealousy × other subordinate competence interactions supported H2 for support (b = .13, p = .020) and H3 for abuse (b = −.11, p = .022). At high other subordinate competence, jealousy was associated with more support (b = .11, p = .013) and less abuse (b = −.08, p = .002); at low competence, neither relationship was significant (support b = −.03, p = .530; abuse b = .03, p = .485). Thus, Study 1's positive jealousy–abuse association at low other subordinate competence did not recur significantly in Study 2 (Table 5; pp. 638–640).
- Study 2: H4–H5 were supported. With both competence moderators high, indirect effects through jealousy were .08 for support (95% CI [.02, .17]) and −.07 for abuse (95% CI [−.12, −.02]). Each differed significantly from its counterpart under each other moderator combination; the other three combinations yielded nonsignificant indirect effects and nonsignificant pairwise differences (Table 6). Reported path coefficients and indirect effects in both studies are unstandardized.
- Study 1 supplementary analyses, summarized in footnote 1, retained the results when controlling the informal leader's and other subordinate's leader–member exchange. Status threat did not emerge as a mediator, and the jealousy × other subordinate competence interaction did not significantly predict support or abuse directed toward the informal leader (p. 632).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates the social functional view of emotions with workplace jealousy to explain informal–formal leadership dynamics across the supervisor–informal leader–other subordinate triad. It identifies competence at two stages as a boundary condition under which jealousy can be linked to increased support and reduced abuse toward other subordinates, extending research beyond the informal–formal leader dyad. Its contextualized account of downward jealousy challenges a uniformly beneficial view of informal leadership and a uniformly destructive view of negative leader emotions.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend that organizations weigh informal leadership's functional benefits against its interpersonal effects and help supervisors identify ways to contribute unique value when competent informal leaders emerge. They also advocate emotion-management training for managers and employees to understand jealousy's social functions and respond effectively to discrete emotions. These are proposed practices; the studies do not test training interventions.

**Limitations**
Study 1's nomination procedure restricted informal-leadership variation, its modest sample limits effect-size precision, and its correlational design cannot establish the proposed causal relationships. Informal leadership and jealousy shared a supervisor-report source, although measured at different times; Study 2 relied on recalled incidents, for which the authors suggest more objective behavioral manipulations. The model does not directly examine other cognitive mechanisms, supervisors' interpretations of informal leaders' intentions, or the full evolving interplay among the three parties.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining cognitive calculations alongside emotional processes, supervisors' treatment of informal leaders, servant leadership as a moderator, and whether informal leaders are perceived as reinforcing the supervisor's vision. They call for longitudinal research on changing attitudes, behavior, and reciprocal reactions as informal leaders emerge, and for stronger informal-leadership measures incorporating behavioral indicators and more objective experimental manipulations.
