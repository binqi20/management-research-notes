# Multiverse Analysis to Advance Theory and Practice: Illustrating and Investigating Variability in CEO Gender Pay Inequality

**Abstract**
Despite using valid research designs informed by substantive and methodological theory, results across studies fail to converge at times. We introduce multiverse analysis as a tool to help catalyze theory development in situations with divergent theoretical explanations and associated evidence by (a) offering strong and precise inference testing, (b) identifying sources of variability across (dozens, hundreds, thousands, or even millions of) systematically and comprehensively derived valid alternative design decisions, and (c) allowing for the multifaceted nature of theoretical mechanisms. We use the theoretical and empirical divergence surrounding studies on how chief executive officer (CEO) gender relates to CEO pay to illustrate how multiverse analysis is a systematic and comprehensive method that is widely applicable, accessible, and flexible, making it a valuable catalyst for theory building.

**Research Question**
How can multiverse analysis help advance theory when studies using theoretically justified research designs produce divergent findings? The CEO gender–pay illustration asks how much results diverge, which research choices explain that divergence, and where the preponderance of justified estimates lies.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
No formal numbered hypotheses are stated. The illustration contrasts three existing theoretical expectations:

- Economic logic, including the resource-based view, suggests a female CEO pay premium because female CEOs are rare and valuable.
- Job-related schemas and stereotypes, including role congruity and social identity theories, suggest lower pay for female CEOs.
- Shifting policies and norms, or offsetting economic and stereotype mechanisms, suggest no gender pay difference.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV: CEO gender, coded female versus male.
- DV: CEO total direct compensation, measured in raw or naturally logged form.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper; the competing theoretical mechanisms are not tested through measured mediators.
- Moderators: No formal moderator hypotheses are reported; sampling frames, variable operationalizations, control inclusion, outlier treatment and fixed-effects structures are research-design alternatives whose individual and joint associations with estimated gender coefficients are examined.

Economic accounts emphasize rarity and value, whereas schema and stereotype accounts emphasize gendered evaluations; policies and norms or countervailing mechanisms may instead yield equality. Multiverse analysis evaluates these competing accounts across combinations of justified choices, identifying unexplained variability as theoretical opacity and limited variability as an opportunity for theoretical parsimony. The illustration remains associational: its aggregate patterns do not establish which substantive mechanism caused a pay difference.

**Data & Measures**
ExecuComp, Compustat, BoardEx and ISS supply the archival data. Methods reports 56,195 observations of 4,043 firms during 1996–2022; Table 2 instead reports 51,890 observations, with pairwise correlation cell sizes ranging from 12,384 to 51,890 (mean 42,272). The paper does not explain this count difference. The eight illustrative regressions in Table 3 use 3,387 or 3,381 observations from 2011–2022.

CEO gender is coded 0 for male and 1 for female. Pay is ExecuComp TDC1, total direct compensation, or its natural logarithm; the authors interpret raw-pay coefficients in thousands of dollars (pp. 869–870). Firm size is the natural logarithm of total assets or total employees at time t; firm performance is return on assets (ROA). Both size and ROA enter every specification. Other choices concern inclusion of total shareholder return, CEO–chair duality, CEO age, percentage of women on the compensation committee, board size and board independence; CEO tenure is measured as position tenure, firm tenure, or excluded. The design varies raw versus winsorized continuous data (1%/99% in Tables 5–6), firm versus firm-and-year fixed effects, and the three sampling frames 1996–2010, 2011–2022 and 1996–2022. Dodd–Frank motivates the temporal division; it is not used as a causal identification strategy.

Seven FT50 studies using ExecuComp informed the choices, generally retaining alternatives used in at least two studies, with separate derivations for sampling frames and analytical procedures. Listwise deletion was common to the reviewed studies and no alternative missing-data strategy was included; Table 2 separately uses pairwise deletion. Table 1 lists 13 decisions, including invariant ROA; multiplying their option counts yields 9,216 specifications, 4,608 for each pay measure. Specification curves order the gender coefficients; robust reweighted least-squares meta-regressions and relative weights analysis relate their variability to the choices. Relative weights partition explained variance and are normalized to 100%, rather than representing shares of total outcome variance. The exploratory expansion adds alternatives such as women on the board and CEO directorships, yielding 186,624 universes.

**Key Findings**
- The eight selected specifications in Table 3 all imply lower female CEO pay (raw-pay b = −11,211.77 to −10,538.70; logged-pay b = −0.85 to −0.63; all p < .001), yet occupy an extreme negative tail of the multiverse. This illustrates why agreement among selected robustness checks need not generalize across justified specifications.
- For each pay measure, the authors set aside two subordinate groups of 256 universes with extreme negative estimates, leaving a primary group of 4,096. They judge the subordinate estimates implausible in context while acknowledging that their constituent specifications remain theoretically justified and that selecting the primary group is itself an interpretational choice. For raw pay, the full-group mean coefficient is −315.23, whereas the primary-group mean is 573.94; 73% versus 82% of coefficients, respectively, are positive (Tables 4–5).
- Within the primary group, raw-pay estimates are significantly negative in 2 universes, nonsignificant in 3,276 (80.0%), and significantly positive in 818 (20.0%); the corresponding logged-pay counts are 105 (2.6%), 2,684 (65.5%) and 1,307 (31.9%). Table 4 defines nonsignificance as p ≥ .05, although p. 869 inconsistently prints p < .05 when describing it. The raw-pay median is 387.75 and its 80% credibility interval is [−164.09, 1,552.89], describing the distribution across specifications. The authors interpret the primary-group mean as an approximately $570,000 female CEO premium, with no universal gender pay difference because negative estimates and an interval spanning zero remain.
- Table 5 reports a larger primary-group mean premium for 1996–2010 than 2011–2022 (1,109.18 versus 135.39). Firm-size and tenure measures, age, board size and independence, outlier treatment, and fixed-effects choices produce variability; including equity returns, CEO duality or women on the compensation committee changes coefficients comparatively little. Its percentages describe positive coefficients regardless of significance, although p. 872 calls some of these percentages statistically significant. Table 5 reports 88% positive coefficients with raw data, versus 86% in the p. 873 narrative.
- Table 6's primary-group meta-regression has adjusted R² = .65; sampling frame and fixed-effects structure account for 60% and 13% of its explained variance. Winsorization relative to raw data has b = −216.33, SE = 10.65, 95% CI [−237.19, −195.46], contrary to p. 873's description of raw data as producing lower estimates. The source also differs on the most-positive configuration: p. 874 names position tenure alongside earlier sampling and CEO age, whereas p. 876 names firm tenure alongside earlier sampling. These conflicting accounts are not reconciled here.
- Among the expanded multiverse's raw-pay specifications, 54% have positive coefficients, according to the p. 875 report. The recent sampling frame combined with firm tenure again produces extreme negative estimates; CEO-directorship choices account for 2% of explained variance, with a large negative impact on gender coefficients when directorships are measured as the historical number. Unlike the base analysis's variability for board size and independence, the expanded analysis assigns low relative weights to board-composition variables as operationalized. These expansion findings are reported in the main text; the detailed supplementary tables are not part of the supplied PDF.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper presents multiverse analysis as a catalyst for theory development through broader inference testing, identification of theoretical opacity and parsimony, and consideration of justified choices in concert. The CEO illustration favors economic pay-premium reasoning in the authors' primary-group interpretation while directing attention to unexplained temporal and measurement contingencies; it does not settle the relationship universally. The method can support deductive, inductive or abductive theorizing, but its use alone does not constitute a theoretical contribution.

**Practical Implication**
Researchers can use the paper's iterative guidance to justify alternative specifications, align data with those choices, inspect distributions and sources of variability, and report interpretational decisions transparently. For CEO pay, the authors argue that changing board composition alone would not resolve gender pay differences based on their base and expanded analyses; this is their interpretation, not a tested board-change intervention. Multiverse reporting can also make robustness checks and reviewer-requested analytical changes more transparent.

**Limitations**
The illustration concerns U.S. publicly traded firms in the specified periods and does not establish generalizability to other occupations or CEOs in other countries. Multiverse analysis does not replace strong theory or repair pervasive design flaws; the authors acknowledge that endogeneity could strengthen, attenuate or reverse the estimated gender gap, and that focusing on the primary group is debatable. Selective use can facilitate p-hacking, while expanding the number of choices raises computational and interpretational burdens and may exacerbate unequal access to resources.

**Future Research**
The authors call for theory explaining temporal changes in CEO pay differences and why alternative tenure and firm-size measures alter estimates. They propose extending multiverse analysis to experimental design choices, pooled surveys, meta-analytic search and coding decisions, nonlinear dynamics and simulations. Further directions include hypotheses about which choices explain variance or shift effects, configurational combinations and causal asymmetry, and transparent multiverse-based robustness assessment during peer review.
