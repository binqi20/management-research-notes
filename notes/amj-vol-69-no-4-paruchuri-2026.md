# CEO Regulatory Focus as an Interpretive Frame: An Empirical Examination of Investor Perceptions in the Context of Earning Deviations

**Abstract**
Investors perceive a CEO’s regulatory foci based on the language they use. Complementing earlier studies on the direct effects of CEO regulatory focus, we propose a novel theoretical paradigm in which CEOs’ regulatory-focused language act as interpretive frames shaping investors’ reactions to earnings deviations—the positive or negative earnings deviations from analysts’ expectations. We propose that investor reactions to earnings deviations will be amplified when investors perceive a CEO to have higher promotion focus, and dampened when investors perceive a CEO to have higher prevention focus. We employ a multimethod approach to test our theorizing; we use an archival study of S&P 500 firms from 2003 to 2017 to test our hypotheses, and two preregistered randomized vignette experiments to explore the underlying investor attributions theorized in our framework. Across all studies, we find broad support for our theorization regarding how CEO regulatory-focused language acts as interpretive frames for investors, which has important implications for research on the social view of financial markets and CEO regulatory focus.

**Research Question**
How does perceived CEO promotion or prevention focus, conveyed through regulatory-focused language, serve as an interpretive frame shaping investor reactions to earnings deviations? What attributions about CEO effort, influence on performance, and future engagement underlie these responses?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Perceived CEO promotion focus positively moderates the positive association between earnings deviations and investor reactions; the relationship is stronger for CEOs perceived as more promotion-focused.
- H2: Perceived CEO prevention focus negatively moderates that positive association; the relationship is weaker for CEOs perceived as more prevention-focused.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV: Earnings deviations from analysts’ expectations, including positive, neutral, and negative deviations.
- DV: Investor reactions to earnings announcements in the archival study; attributed CEO goal intensity in Supplemental Study 1; internal and external performance attributions and expected CEO future engagement in Supplemental Study 2.
- Moderators: Perceived CEO promotion focus and prevention focus, theorized as distinct, coexisting, independent dimensions.
- Mediators: The proposed attributional mechanisms concern perceived CEO effort, demonstrated influence on performance, and future engagement. The experiments examine underlying premises; the article does not report a joint mediation test linking these measures to archival returns.

The authors argue that promotion-focused language signals eager pursuit of gains, whereas prevention-focused language signals vigilance against losses. Positive earnings deviations should produce stronger internal attributions and expectations of future engagement for high- than low-promotion CEOs, amplifying investor rewards; differences should be smaller for non-gains. Negative deviations should be attributed relatively little to either high- or low-prevention CEOs, but high-prevention CEOs should be expected to become more engaged, tempering penalties; differences should be smaller for non-losses. These are predictions about investor beliefs, not claims that the language accurately reveals CEOs’ actual motivational attributes; negative earnings deviations need not mean accounting losses.

**Data & Measures**
- Archival study: The starting population comprised 799 firms appearing in the S&P 500 during 2003-2017. Excluding firms without CEO shareholder letters left 521 firms and 4,074 observations in an unbalanced annual panel, beginning with available data and ending by 2017 or loss of independent status. Sources included Compustat, CRSP, I/B/E/S, and shareholder letters. Investor reactions were Eventus cumulative abnormal returns over the day before through the day after the annual earnings announcement, summing daily observed returns minus expected returns from the Fama-French three-factor model; the stated baseline was 250 trading days, from 295 to 45 trading days before disclosure. Earnings deviations used standardized unanticipated earnings: Equation 1 subtracts the paper’s “deviation mean” from actual EPS, then divides by the “standard deviation of deviation.” Perceived regulatory focus was proxied by LIWC counts of promotion- or prevention-focused words per 1,000 words in the prior-year shareholder letter, using a dictionary of 27 promotion and 25 prevention words.
- Archival estimation: Firm random-effects regressions included year and industry fixed effects. Table 2 controls covered industry discretion and dynamism; actual earnings and letter length; firm size, shareholder returns, slack, and CEO change; board size, independence, and CEO duality; CEO tenure, age, sex, and compensation; and letter emotional tone, temporal focus, and vagueness. One control-description sentence instead names industry munificence, while the subsequent explanation and Tables 1-2 identify dynamism. Firm size was log annual sales, and slack was cash plus short-term investments divided by total assets; the firm attributes were lagged one year.
- Supplemental Study 1 (n = 202): A preregistered between-person randomized letter vignette examined attributed CEO goal intensity under high-promotion, high-prevention, and low-regulatory-focus language. Supplemental Study 2 (n = 626) combined the language conditions with media-report vignettes presenting positive or negative earnings deviations, or met expectations, examining internal/external performance attributions and future engagement through multivariate regression. Table 3 compares each high-focus condition with the low-promotion–low-prevention condition within the same earnings condition. Both experiments checked perceived letter tone on a seven-point prevention-to-promotion scale. Recruitment, exclusions, collection dates, and the outcome item wording and scales are not provided in the main article; it refers readers to its online appendices.

**Key Findings**
- Archival study: Earnings deviations were positively associated with investor reactions (Table 2, Model 2: b = .006, p < .01). H1 was supported by the positive promotion-focus interaction (Model 3: b = .001, p < .05), and H2 by the negative prevention-focus interaction (Model 4: b = −.003, p < .05); both retained these signs and significance when included together in Model 5. The reported high-versus-low promotion difference was significant only for SUE scores above 2, while the prevention difference was significant only below −3. Thus, stronger promotion focus amplified rewards for sufficiently positive deviations, whereas stronger prevention focus softened penalties for sufficiently negative deviations.
- Archival qualifications: The authors report similar directions and significance with firm fixed effects and a longer event window, from one day before to three days after the announcement. The curvilinear specification found no quadratic effects; its linear prevention interaction was also nonsignificant (Model 6: b = −.002, SE = .002), while the promotion interaction remained significant. Separate analyses found neither regulatory focus significantly predicted earnings deviations.
- Supplemental Study 1: Participants in the combined high-regulatory-focus conditions attributed greater goal intensity than those in the low-focus condition (M = 5.44 versus 4.91; F[1, 200] = 9.11, p < .01). Manipulation checks distinguished perceived regulatory-focused language across conditions in both experiments (Study 1: F[2, 199] = 79.50; Study 2: F[2, 623] = 426.44; both p < .01).
- Supplemental Study 2, positive deviations: Relative to low promotion–low prevention, high promotion increased internal attribution (b = 1.08, p < .01), reduced external attribution (b = −.78, p < .01), and increased future engagement (b = .49, p < .05). High prevention also increased internal attribution (b = .56, p < .05) and future engagement (b = .64, p < .01), but its external-attribution coefficient was nonsignificant.
- Supplemental Study 2, met expectations: Both high-focus conditions increased future engagement (b = .43; promotion p < .05, prevention p < .01), without significant internal- or external-attribution differences. Under negative deviations, high promotion reduced internal attribution (b = −.64, p < .01), but its external-attribution and future-engagement differences were nonsignificant; high prevention increased external attribution (b = .58, p < .01) and future engagement (b = .56, p < .01), without a significant internal-attribution difference (Table 3).
- Source qualification: The discussion on p. 766 broadly states that high levels of either focus lead to greater attributed engagement following both positive and negative deviations. Table 3 reports a nonsignificant high-promotion future-engagement coefficient under negative deviations (b = .09, SE = .25), so that broad discussion statement is not uniformly supported by the displayed comparisons. The positive-deviation prevention and met-expectations engagement findings also exceed the theory’s more restricted predicted pattern.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends regulatory focus theory to external audiences by treating CEO regulatory-focused language as an interpretive frame through which investors assess subsequent performance information. It develops attributional explanations for perceptions of CEO effort, performance influence, and future engagement, and contributes to the social view of financial markets by showing how prior CEO communications can condition responses to the same earnings information.

**Practical Implication**
The authors’ findings indicate that prior CEO communications provide frames for interpreting subsequent earnings information. In the archival results, greater promotion-focused language was associated with stronger rewards for sufficiently positive surprises, whereas greater prevention-focused language was associated with reduced penalties for sufficiently negative surprises.

**Limitations**
The archival design could not directly test the proposed investor-attribution mechanisms, motivating the supplemental experiments. Selection on shareholder-letter availability produced a sample differing from excluded observations in industry discretion, actual earnings, working capital, slack, board independence, and CEO compensation. The framework concerns perceived rather than verified actual CEO regulatory focus, and the authors acknowledge that post-outcome engagement attributions are more nuanced than their initial theoretical expectations.

**Future Research**
The authors propose studying communication timing and platforms, nonwritten cues, and other stakeholders’ communications and interpretations. They also suggest distinguishing perceived from actual CEO regulatory foci, examining stable versus transient perceptions and circumstances in which one focus dominates, and investigating how past outcomes shape subsequent motivation, behavior, and external attributions. Further research could examine whether these interpretive frames help explain mixed prior findings and which other CEO or firm communication attributes enable or inhibit their use.
