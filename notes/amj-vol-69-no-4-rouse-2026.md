# We’re Not in Kansas Anymore: Pragmatic Adaptation in Large-Scale Creative Projects with Underspecified Product Concepts

**Abstract**
Developing new creative products inherently involves ambiguity due to its focus on novelty. When organizations have high ambitions for novelty yet lack a specific product concept, they must coordinate complex work and manage possibilities alongside significant ambiguity. This longitudinal qualitative study of a video game project examines how a large-scale creative project team collaborated in the face of an underspecified product concept. I theorize how project teams employ two interconnected processes: an iterative compositional process that drives product development within periods, and a pragmatic adaptation process that shifts teams from creative idealism to realism across periods. The findings reveal three distinct but interrelated forms of work—product work, contribution framing, and adaptive psychological work—that enable creative workers to develop an ambitious product without a specified product concept. By examining both product work and the psychological experiences of creative workers across hierarchical levels, this research broadens our understanding of creative work beyond more exclusively product-focused perspectives.

**Research Question**
How do organizational members across hierarchical levels collaborate in the face of an underspecified product concept in a large-scale creative project?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
Not reported in paper

**Mechanism Process**

- Product work: discovering and defining product concepts and element parameters, making components, and integrating and evaluating components and prototypes through nested iterations.
- Contribution framing: understanding one's role and contribution through visionary idealist, tempered realist, creative author, creative producer, or supportive contributor frames.
- Adaptive psychological work: processing ambiguity and possibility, reconciling the disposability of work and reduced ambitions, and accepting an underspecified product concept.
- Temporal periods: creative idealism, creative reality checking, and creative realism, with changing balances between novelty and resource constraints.

Within each period, iterative composition connects senior managers' concept work, middle managers' element parameters and integration, and individual contributors' component making; senior and middle managers evaluate the developing work. Across periods, an external publisher's deadlines and decision gate prompt shifts from radical ambitions toward quality achievable within resource constraints. Contribution framing and adaptive psychological work foster distance from particular contributions or the product, helping workers sustain engagement, although those maintaining creative-author frames continue to struggle with ambiguity and discarded work.

**Data & Measures**
The case concerns the Oz project at Central Studio, a U.S. independent console-game studio employing approximately 225 people; the project involved more than 100 people over several years. Central Studio is a pseudonym, and the game content was recast as The Wizard of Oz to preserve confidentiality. The researcher invited current team members and approached newcomers; 38 actively participated through interviews, diaries, or both, while four others contributed through observed meetings and informal conversations. The analysis focuses on people primarily working on Oz and attending its meetings, rather than the separate software engineering team's broader data.

The corpus comprises 73 interviews in three waves: 34 in July–December, 29 in December–April, and 10 in October–November of the following year, within one month after release. It also includes 592 weekly diary entries collected over 29 weeks between August and March, and 38 hours of overt nonparticipant observation. The nine months of active fieldwork began after approximately a year of concept development and an early demo; primary collection ended after two further prototypes, during final production. The post-release interviews occurred separately from fieldwork; calendar years are not reported (Tables 1–2).

During collection, the researcher used open grounded-theory coding, contact summaries, a field journal, and memos, adapting interview questions to emergent themes. Subsequent analysis revisited relevant codes chronologically, compared key informants' experiences across disciplines and hierarchical roles, bracketed periods around the green-light crunch and publisher meeting, and integrated relationships through tables and figures. Constructs were derived qualitatively: product work concerns envisioning and realizing the product; contribution framing concerns making sense of one's role; adaptive psychological work concerns managing ambiguity and possibility (Table 3). The study develops a process explanation rather than estimating causal effects.

**Key Findings**

- Creative idealism involved generative prototyping under radical ambitions and few constraints, but extensive iteration did not yield a specified, coherent product concept. Repeatedly discarded work eroded efficacy and generated frustration; creative-producer and supportive-contributor frames helped middle managers and individual contributors, respectively, remain engaged.
- Creative reality checking began as the green-light deadline made time constraints salient. Senior managers adopted tempered realism, scoped down ambitions, and required a polished demo; middle managers became more directive. Workers confronted strategic disposability: the demo could secure funding even though much of its content would later change. Successful approval raised collective efficacy and temporarily reduced ambiguity despite frustration and diminished ambitions.
- Creative realism involved new creative leadership and bricolage using prior materials within resource constraints. Clearer element parameters and confidence that work would be used supported renewed engagement, while acceptance of an underspecified concept and psychological distance enabled continued contributions. Some workers nevertheless remained disappointed or demoralized by discarded work.
- The released game retained a loose concept essentially unchanged from the outset, met publisher expectations, and pushed some genre boundaries. Many team members were proud of it, and critical reviews were moderately successful; it did not realize all the original radical ambitions. The model connects within-period iterative composition with across-period pragmatic adaptation, rather than treating creative work as a single linear progression (Figures 1–2).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study theorizes an iterative compositional process in which concurrent, nested cycles of making, integration, and evaluation connect concepts, components, and prototypes, bridging stage-based and collective-creativity accounts. It extends Finke's idealism–realism distinction into a temporal account of how novelty is reconsidered as resource constraints become salient. By incorporating contribution framing and adaptive psychological work, it shows how sustaining creative engagement involves role-specific adaptations shaped by proximity to making and workers' contribution frames.

**Practical Implication**
For large-scale creative projects, the study highlights the importance of managing expectations and emotions across organizational levels while balancing ambitions for novelty with available time and money. Understanding how senior managers, middle managers, and individual contributors experience and adapt to ambiguity can inform efforts to sustain creative workers through demanding project work.

**Limitations**
The study follows a single project, limiting its ability to assess alternative trajectories such as rapidly finding a radical specified concept, starting with creative realism, or failing at reality checking. An external client imposed deadlines and served as gatekeeper for continued development, and successful approval buoyed efficacy. These contextual features qualify transferability to settings without an external decision-maker or with different outcomes.

**Future Research**
Future research could examine how teams move between periods without external pressure or under self-management, how long they should remain in creative idealism, and how to recognize when they are stuck and need reality checking. The paper also calls for examining unsuccessful reality checking and when and why projects should begin with creative realism rather than creative idealism.
