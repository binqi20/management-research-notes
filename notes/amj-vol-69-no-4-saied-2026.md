# Who I Am Not: Enhancing Self-Clarity through Cultivating Not-Me Identities

**Abstract**
How do people develop and leverage self-definitions of who and what they are not, or their “not-me” identities, to navigate their work lives? In contrast to dominant portrayals of not-me identities as negative or oppositional, we explore how they can serve as resources in identity work. Drawing on a grounded theory study of 80 interviews with independent workers and clients, we identify a process we call “identity filtering.” Through this process, individuals identify and disconfirm misaligned role or relational elements, cultivating not-me identity repertoires that serve as reference points for how they engage in and shape their role-relationships. We identify two distinct pathways: some individuals enact their not-me identities through setting boundaries and role screening, which in turn enhance their self-clarity. Others suppress their not-me identities by engaging in enmeshment, which generates emotional strain. These diverging paths emerge through distinct underlying mechanisms: self-worth and desire for control both enable enactment, while adaptability constrains it. By reconceptualizing not-me identities as resources, this study advances identity theory and deepens understanding of how (independent) workers navigate fluid role-relationships and uncertainty in contemporary work.

**Research Question**
How do people cultivate their not-me identities, and why do they actively engage with them? The study examines how independent knowledge workers develop, enact, or suppress these self-definitions while navigating role-relationships with clients.

**Mechanism Process**

- Triggering experiences: relational identity strain arising from perceived role devaluation and exposure to role variety.
- Identity filtering: identifying misalignments, disconfirming incongruent role or relational elements, and retaining them within a not-me identity repertoire as reference points.
- Enactment activities: boundary setting defines limits within existing role-relationships; role screening guides selection of prospective relationships and exit from misaligned ones.
- Suppression activity: enmeshment blurs self–other boundaries and increases dependence on particular role-relationships.
- Contextual mechanisms and outcomes: self-worth and desire for control support enactment and self-clarity, especially under relative security; adaptability as over-accommodation supports suppression and emotional strain, especially under precarity.

The grounded model treats not-me identities as resources that remain cognitively available even when their enactment is suppressed. Filtering excludes incongruent elements from the self while retaining them as guides for future role-relationships. The authors infer from participants' accounts that enacting these identities facilitates self-clarity, whereas suppression generates emotional strain. Enactment and suppression patterns can change, and the mechanisms are context-dependent rather than stable personal traits.

**Data & Measures**
The formal corpus comprises 80 semi-structured interviews with 65 independent knowledge workers living in the United Kingdom and 15 clients in senior management positions across industries. Theoretical sampling sought variation in occupations and independent-work experience; workers combining gig work with full-time employment were excluded. Interviews occurred in five rounds: March 2020–March 2021 (17), October 2021–February 2022 (21), April–October 2022 (21), June–October 2024 (15 clients and two workers), and April–May 2025 (four experienced workers discussing the emerging theorization). Interviews lasted 28–106 minutes, averaging 59 minutes. Informal conversations with 20 workers and seven clients informed the research but were not formally analyzed.

Each transcript was coded for outward enactment or suppression and its implications for the self-concept, including self-clarity and emotional strain. These are interpretations of participants' accounts in a grounded-theory design, rather than statistical measures or causal effect estimates.

**Key Findings**

- Identity filtering produced not-me identity repertoires, illustrated by not-work-role identities and not-value-aligned identities. The former often had neutral valence and the latter positive valence; not-me identities need not be negative, oppositional, or remnants of past roles.
- Workers engaged in identity filtering, but not all enacted these identities through boundary setting and role screening. Their accounts linked enactment to greater role clarity, self-knowledge, and confidence; suppression through enmeshment was accompanied by emotional strain and diminished clarity.
- Self-worth and desire for role-relationship control supported enactment and appeared more accessible under relative security. Under precarity, adaptability took the form of over-accommodating clients, suppressing not-me identities; this is a context-specific downside of adaptability.
- Client reactions differed: some valued clear limits, while others discouraged or actively penalized boundary setting, including terminating contracts. The model therefore does not imply that enactment always sustains a client relationship.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study reconceptualizes not-me identities as primary resources for self-clarity, extending accounts centered on negative, oppositional, or previously held identities. It advances relational identity theory by showing how defining the limits of the self through distance, boundary setting, and role screening complements identity construction through interpersonal connection. For independent-work research, not-me identity repertoires offer a stabilizing resource that complements holding environments and mechanisms for expanding or adapting identities.

**Practical Implication**
For independent workers, articulating and enacting who and what they are not can clarify role limits and guide boundary setting, client selection, and exit from misaligned relationships. These possibilities depend on the relational context: precarity can encourage over-accommodation, and some clients penalize the boundaries workers seek to maintain.

**Limitations**
The sample comprises relatively privileged independent knowledge workers, and the not-me identity types described are illustrative rather than exhaustive; the authors speculate that organizational identity regulation may constrain their content in other settings. The study does not directly establish how “me” and “not-me” identities shape one another. It also treats self-clarity as a resource, while acknowledging that rejecting opportunities could restrict identity expansion and openness to growth.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining other not-me identity types, following their evolution and relationship to actual behavior longitudinally, and comparing independent and standard workers with different career trajectories. They also call for research on when “me” and “not-me” identities reinforce or undermine each other, when reduced self-clarity is adaptive, and when not-me identities impede clarity or contribute to rigidity or fragmentation. Further directions include alternative triggers such as role transitions, conflict, and turbulence, and links with proactive behaviors such as job crafting and feedback seeking, including the costs of sustained proactivity under precarity.
