# Reckoning with the Ideal Worker Body: A Qualitative Study of Workers in Chronic Pain

**Abstract**
Implicit in the ideal worker norm is the assumption of the ideal worker body, a body that is always ready and willing to meet work demands. Yet, bodies vary in their ability to meet this ideal. Through a qualitative study of 66 workers in chronic pain, we build theory on the experience of a chronic, stigmatized deviation from the ideal worker body. Our analysis uncovered that as informants’ pain interfered with their work, they attempted to pass as ideal-worker-bodied. However, attempting to pass ultimately increased their pain and its interference with their work, thus catapulting them into a vicious cycle. For two thirds of our informants, this cycle led to their pain becoming intolerable, forcing them to reckon with pervasive ableist norms as they turned to medical professionals for help. Informants who received validating cues from medical professionals were better able to relinquish, albeit with difficulty, the ideal worker body as necessary. They came to accept an alternative to the ideal worker body: a sufficient worker body. Informants who received invalidating cues continued in the vicious cycle of striving for the ideal worker body. Our findings contribute to scholarship on bodies at work, stigma at work, and the ideal worker norm.

**Research Question**
How do workers experience and manage a stigmatized, chronic deviation from the ideal worker body?

**Mechanism Process**
- Central tension: The ideal worker body is physically ready, willing, and able to prioritize work above all else; chronic pain can make meeting its appearance and behavioral expectations difficult.
- Vicious cycle: Pain interferes with work, evokes shame, and prompts attempts to pass as ideal-worker-bodied through privately attending to pain or pushing through it; passing increases pain and renews interference with work.
- Influencing conditions: Stronger organizational emphasis on the ideal worker body intensifies shame and pressure to pass. Effective pain management mitigates, but does not stop, the vicious cycle.
- Reckoning with ableist norms: Intolerable pain creates a body–work impasse, prompting further medical help. The authors’ model links invalidating cues to continued striving and validating cues to relinquishing the ideal worker body as necessary and accepting a sufficient worker body.
- Alternative and outcomes: A sufficient worker body remains capable and worthy of prioritization. Perceived feasibility of enacting it, shaped by organizational emphasis on the ideal, separates a sustainable cycle of body–work reconciling behaviors and intermittent passing from organizational exit.

The emergent model explains how attempts to meet ableist bodily expectations can undermine workers’ capacity to function at and outside work. Validating medical cues can break shame by legitimizing pain, but a diagnosis serves this function only when the informant regards it as legitimate. Accepting a sufficient worker body is difficult and distinct from being able to enact it within an organization. When enactment is feasible, reconciling work and bodily needs permits occasional passing with temporary increases in pain, followed by a return to reconciling behaviors.

**Data & Measures**
The primary corpus comprises 66 semistructured interviews with 66 workers in chronic pain across varied occupations and pain conditions (Table 1). Initial recruitment targeted full-time workers reporting physical pain lasting longer than three months; the final accounts also include people who had left work. Phase one recruited 14 informants through the authors’ personal and professional networks. Phase two broadened purposeful sampling through physical flyers and online communities and added narrative interviewing, yielding 24 interviews. Phase three used theoretical sampling in online communities associated with extreme pain conditions and added 28 interviews focused on responses and outcomes. Interviews lasted 30–75 minutes, averaging 43 minutes, and were recorded and transcribed with permission.

Footnote 2 dates collection to fall 2019–spring 2022: phase one occurred in fall 2019–early spring 2020; collection paused at the pandemic’s onset, and phases two and three occurred in late fall 2020–spring 2022. These are retrospective accounts of pain journeys up to interview, rather than longitudinal observations of each person. All four authors read the initial transcripts; the first two authors subsequently combined line-by-line open coding, informant summary narratives, axial coding, and theoretical coding while iterating with relevant literature (Figure 1). After reporting theoretical saturation and member checks with two informants, the team concluded collection; a fourth phase during peer review brought ableism literature into further model refinement. Constructs such as shame, body–work impasse, medical cues, and reconciling behaviors emerged from this interpretive analysis, with illustrative data in Table 2 and Appendix B.

**Key Findings**
- Informants described conflict with ideal worker body expectations even in less physically demanding jobs and at lower pain levels. Passing could worsen pain; pain-management efforts varied in effectiveness. More than one third could remain in the vicious cycle day to day without making pain unbearable, whereas nearly two thirds reached a body–work impasse (the abstract reports “two thirds”).
- In the authors’ model, medical invalidation perpetuates shame and ideal-body striving, whereas validation supports accepting a sufficient worker body. Validation took the form of a diagnosis regarded as legitimate or help with managing pain; a diagnosis alone did not necessarily validate the experience.
- Acceptance did not ensure organizational feasibility. Informants in organizations with weaker emphasis on the ideal tended to see enactment as feasible; those facing stronger emphasis were more likely to see it as infeasible, which was associated with organizational exit. Some departing informants found a more suitable organization, while others had not found a context in which they could resume work.
- Body–work reconciling behaviors either enabled work without hurting the body or enabled bodily care while working. Together with intermittent passing and recovery, these formed the sustainable cycle; the finding is not that pain disappeared or passing ceased.
- A reporting tension remains: the discussion states that acceptance was possible only after medical professionals broke informants’ shame, but Appendix B labels Patricia’s account as accepting a sufficient worker body after describing invalidating cues, without narrating a later validating cue. The compressed narrative does not establish whether such validation occurred (pp. 792, 801).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study makes explicit the ableist bodily assumptions embedded in the ideal worker norm and introduces the sufficient worker body as an alternative standard. It extends stigma research by identifying decoupling of the ideal worker body from the ideal worker norm when passing and positive identity construction are difficult, rather than assuming workers must abandon the broader ideal worker norm. It also shows how bodily experiences traverse work and nonwork, bringing medical cues and organizational variation into explanations of how workers understand and manage their bodies.

**Practical Implication**
The authors encourage managers to question assumed physical requirements of tasks and support workers’ varying bodily needs through an individualized approach, rather than one-size-fits-all accommodations. For workers with chronic, stigmatized deviations, they suggest using the findings as validation to challenge shame and the necessity of the ideal worker body, creatively identifying body–work reconciling behaviors, and considering a better organizational fit when enactment remains infeasible.

**Limitations**
The extreme chronic-pain sample may overemphasize dynamics such as body–work impasse; shorter pain episodes or socially valued bodily deviations may entail different or more temporary processes. Retrospective interviews captured people at different points in their journeys, and the authors suggest that longitudinal evidence could further substantiate the temporal dynamics; no organization-level data were collected. The sample was 78% self-identified women: the authors report similar proportions of men and women accepting a sufficient worker body, while explicitly declining to rule out gender dynamics.

**Future Research**
Longitudinal research could examine workforce reentry after exit and repeated body–work impasses; observational, ethnographic, or repeated within-person methods could add evidence beyond retrospective interviews. The authors also propose closer examination of gender and comparative case studies of organizational context, including variation in emphasis on the ideal worker body.
