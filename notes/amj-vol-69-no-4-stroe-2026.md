# Algorithmic Facial Expression Analysis: A Novel Methodology to Advance Management Research on Emotions

**Abstract**
With the rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technologies, algorithmic facial expression analysis (AFEA) has emerged as a promising methodology to measure emotions. Despite rapid adoption across management subfields, however, the full scope of AFEA’s theoretical potential remains underexplored. This paper provides a framework that links the AFEA measurement innovation to major opportunities for theoretical advancement around emotions in organizations. We start by describing the methodological basis of AFEA and reviewing its applications in management research to date. We then outline two core capabilities of AFEA—namely, capturing the temporal structure of emotions and detecting inauthentic expressions of emotions—and illustrate how these capabilities can enable theory advancement in management research. After presenting an empirical demonstration of AFEA’s capabilities and a practical step-by-step guide for using it, we conclude by discussing key considerations for realizing the future potential of AFEA research.

**Research Question**
How can AFEA’s capabilities to capture the temporal structure of emotions and detect inauthentic expressions be translated into measurable constructs that advance management theories of emotion?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
The paper develops illustrative, untested predictions rather than a numbered hypothesis-testing model. Examples include:

- Temporal regulation and influence: shorter negative-emotion recovery half-life predicts more effective bargaining; negative-emotion area under the curve should have a stronger positive association with end-of-episode strain than peak intensity alone, controlling for peak intensity and duration. Shorter investor joy-alignment latency could predict funding; shorter teammates’ emotion-alignment latency could predict peer-rated leader emergence.
- Contagion pathways: short latency, a fleeting low-intensity peak, and rapid decay indicate an automatic route; longer latency, a sustained high-intensity peak, and slow decay indicate a deliberate cognitive route.
- Emotion sequences: sadness followed by anger could mediate negative feedback’s association with confrontation or advocacy; anger followed by sadness could mediate accommodation and withdrawal. Across newcomers’ recurring comparable tasks, adaptation is predicted to involve smaller peaks and faster recovery for negative emotions, sustained or higher task-facilitative positive responses, and faster resolution of surprise.
- Authenticity and regulation: greater micro–macro incongruence could amplify interpersonal costs, with effects differing by mismatch direction. A customer-service agent’s self-reported deep-acting attempt that AFEA flags as inauthentic should predict higher end-of-day strain, controlling for customer volume and customer-reported negativity.
- Competing contagion predictions: after controlling for exposure, authentic and inauthentic displays could generate comparable contagion; alternatively, contagion could be stronger or quicker for authentic displays and attenuated or reversed for inauthentic displays. In pitching, authenticity is proposed to moderate the relationship between investors’ positive displays and subsequent engagement.

**Mechanism Process**
- Temporal structure: onset, latency, peak, duration, recovery, ordered emotion sequences, and trajectories across repeated events make emotion processes observable at different time scales.
- Authenticity markers: micro–macro expression incongruence, Duchenne versus non-Duchenne action-unit configurations, and hemifacial asymmetry provide indicators of how expressions are regulated.
- Expression versus experience: AFEA captures expressed emotion; self-reports capture experienced emotion. Their convergence and divergence can inform theories of emotional labor and regulation.

The framework links these measurements to mechanisms in emotional contagion, affective events, emotional labor, and the interpretation of emotions as social information. Temporal signatures are proposed to distinguish competing processes, while authenticity markers can help separate regulation attempts from successful regulation. Temporal precedence improves the observability of event–emotion–behavior chains, but the authors explicitly state that establishing causality requires experimental designs that eliminate alternative explanations.

**Data & Measures**
This is a methodology paper with an illustrative demonstration. Professional actors portrayed an entrepreneur and two investors using a real venture script, specified roles, and a fixed interaction flow; they were asked to respond spontaneously to one another without prescribed emotional displays and to minimize detection artifacts. The simulated interaction enabled public sharing without compromising real participants’ privacy; recording dates and country are not reported.

For temporal alignment, DeepFace supplied emotion-classification confidence scores from 0% to 100%, representing estimated classification probabilities. Figure 2 displays dominant emotions meeting a 50% threshold. Alignment latency is the observer’s corresponding emotion onset minus the expresser’s onset; these confidence scores should be read as classification scores, rather than as the framework’s conceptual definition of emotional intensity. For smile configurations, iMotions Affectiva AFFDEX 2.0 supplied individual facial action-unit activations: AU12 is the lip corner puller and AU6 the cheek raiser. The demonstration contrasts their co-activation across all frames within the seconds labeled 00:32 and 00:50.

Beyond the demonstration, Table 1 defines derived metrics: area under the curve integrates the time-stamped arousal series from onset to offset, with units of intensity multiplied by seconds; recovery half-time runs from the peak until arousal reaches 50% of that peak, while full-time runs until return to baseline. Emotion sequences preserve onset order, and transition speed uses the previous emotion’s offset and the next emotion’s onset. The framework also defines micro–macro incongruence by absolute valence distance and by whether the micro-expression is more negative or positive than the concurrent macro-display. These are proposed operationalizations, not additional estimates from the demonstration; no inferential model or covariate-adjusted test is reported.

**Key Findings**
- Temporal illustration: the entrepreneur’s joy onset at 4:22.03 was followed by Investor 2’s joy onset at 4:23.16, giving an alignment latency of 1.13 seconds (Figure 2; timestamps in minutes and seconds). The reported joy classification confidence was 98.7% for the entrepreneur and peaked at 98% for Investor 2, whose sadness score declined. The authors interpret this sequence as consistent with rapid, reflexive emotional contagion; the staged illustration does not establish a causal contagion effect.
- Authenticity illustration: at 00:32, Figure 3 labels both AU12 and AU6 as always above 99%, identifying a Duchenne smile. At 00:50, it labels AU12 as always above 99% and AU6 as 13%–31%, averaging 19.90%, identifying a non-Duchenne smile. The prose describes the latter AU12 activation as “near 99%,” whereas the figure says “always above 99%”; both descriptions indicate high lip activation relative to cheek activation.
- The authors report substantial agreement in detected emotions when the video was also analyzed with AFFDEX 2.0, without reporting an agreement coefficient in the main paper. Associations with follow-up meetings, due diligence, investment, or subsequent engagement are proposed extensions, not demonstrated outcomes.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The capability-driven framework bridges AFEA measurement and emotion theory by shifting attention from snapshots to trajectories and from who is authentic to when and how authenticity is expressed and interpreted. It proposes observable, episode-level constructs for temporal mechanisms and regulation outcomes, enabling distinctions that aggregate affect measures or self-reported regulation intentions can obscure.

**Practical Implication**
The practical audience is researchers: the paper supplies an illustrative workflow, shared materials, and a step-by-step guide covering study planning, technical implementation, algorithm selection, robustness, and ethics. It recommends documenting algorithm versions, preprocessing, and variable construction rules and conducting inter-algorithm reliability analyses to assess whether findings depend on a particular algorithm.

**Limitations**
AFEA has limited contextual awareness and categorical scope, so facial movements can be misinterpreted and nuanced or compound emotions can fall outside current detection capabilities; expressed and experienced emotion are related but distinct constructs. Algorithm updates can change outputs, and the paper does not detail computational procedures for several proposed dynamic metrics. The actor-staged demonstration cannot fully reproduce organizational interactions’ complexity and spontaneity, while the review and guide require updating as algorithms evolve.

**Future Research**
The authors propose combining AFEA with time-locked self-reports, experience sampling, experiments manipulating situational context, and qualitative interviews or observations to clarify the meaning of facial displays. They encourage studying convergence and divergence between expressed and experienced emotion as theoretically informative data and linking temporal or authenticity measures to subsequent behavior, including post-pitch investor engagement.
