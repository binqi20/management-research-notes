# Role Rectification: How Hybrid Entrepreneurship Turns Entrepreneur Roles from Liability to Advantage in Hiring

**Abstract**
Research shows that certain work roles constrain labor market success when individuals transition to new work roles, as they are viewed as discrediting by potential role partners. However, workers increasingly hold multiple roles simultaneously, which may alter the inferences drawn from roles that are discrediting in isolation. Building on role accumulation theory, we theorize that work roles that are net liabilities in isolation, such as the entrepreneur role, can become net assets as part of a compensatory role set, and we identify a new theoretical mechanism of role rectification through which this effect occurs. Mobilizing verbal protocol, experimental, and archival data sources, we find evidence for role rectification using the case of hybrid entrepreneurs, but we also find that the benefits of role rectification are attenuated in contexts where role accumulation magnifies role nonconformity risks.

**Research Question**
How and when does holding a compensatory set of work roles enable individuals with a discrediting role to make successful role transitions? The paper examines whether hybrid entrepreneurs receive more favorable hiring evaluations and pay outcomes than full-time employees or full-time entrepreneurs, and how role exit and autonomy qualify these advantages.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1a/H1b: Role accumulators with compensatory role sets (hybrid entrepreneurs) experience more favorable labor market outcomes than individuals holding an isolated role from that set: (a) full-time employees and (b) full-time entrepreneurs.
- H2a/H2b: Maintaining rather than exiting an accumulated role at hire attenuates the positive effects of hybrid entrepreneurship on labor market outcomes relative to (a) full-time employees and (b) full-time entrepreneurs; exit augments those effects.
- H3a/H3b: Higher autonomy in the role sought attenuates the positive effects of hybrid entrepreneurship on labor market outcomes relative to (a) full-time employees and (b) full-time entrepreneurs; lower autonomy augments those effects.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Applicant work-role configuration: hybrid entrepreneur, full-time entrepreneur, or full-time employee.
- DV(s): Hireability in Study 2 and post-transition pay conditional on pre-transition pay in Study 3; Study 1 examines hiring managers' attribute inferences.
- Mediators: Role rectification is the proposed cognitive mechanism, examined through verbal protocols rather than a formal statistical mediation model.
- Moderators: Maintaining versus exiting the entrepreneur role at hire, and autonomy in the role sought; their joint operation is examined post hoc in Study 3.

A compensatory employee role contradicts negative entrepreneurial stereotypes, such as uncertain job-related skills or difficulty accepting authority, allowing positive attributes from both roles to shape hiring evaluations. However, multiple roles may weaken employers' sanctioning power, making role nonconformity a concern; exit and low autonomy are proposed to mitigate that risk. The source contains a directional inconsistency: one sentence on p. 690 calls nonconformity risks “inversely related” to autonomy, whereas the surrounding explanation and H3 state that greater autonomy magnifies those risks and diminishes hybrid entrepreneurs' advantages.

**Data & Measures**
- Study 1: The sample comprised 33 HR specialists and managers with formal hiring responsibility, recruited for a think-aloud résumé-screening exercise for an RV technician position; everyone who began the protocol completed it. Job titles and descriptions distinguished employee, full-time entrepreneur, and hybrid entrepreneur applicants; autonomy was also manipulated but was not the primary focus. Two coders independently classified positive and negative entrepreneurial attributes, rectification, and other statements, resolving discrepancies through discussion. One hybrid-assigned participant misunderstood the concurrent employee role and was analyzed as evaluating a full-time entrepreneur; the authors report substantively unchanged results using assignment. Descriptive word shares indicate salience, while multinomial logistic regression analyzes 1,037 discrete verbalizations nested within participants, excluding reading aloud: positive attributes including rectification, negative attributes, or other statements. Standard errors are clustered by individual.
- Study 2: Of 450 Prolific hiring managers screened for formal competitive-hiring responsibility, 392 passed the reported data-quality standards. The randomized 3 × 2 design crossed applicant role with high versus low autonomy, manipulated through freedom and supervisor oversight in the job description. Hireability used Roth et al.'s continuous scale of expected task and contextual performance (α = .84). Primary ANOVA and post hoc least significant difference tests omit controls; supplemental models add evaluator age, tenure, gender, and momentary positive and negative affect, with largely consistent results but some differences. This screening-stage study tests H1 and H3, not exit effects in H2.
- Study 3: The British Household Panel Study supplied about 27,000 initial transitions, yielding 17,199 complete-data job changes by 7,922 individuals, including workers with employment breaks. Methods describe the survey as running from 1991–2008; the cited dataset edition is labeled “Waves 1-18, 1991-2009.” Full-time entrepreneurs were restricted to one- to two-year entrepreneurial spells because their previous wage precedes the entrepreneurial spell. Hybrid entrepreneurs held two jobs, exactly one self-employed; full-time entrepreneurs were self-employed in their only work role. Exit means a hybrid entrepreneur was no longer self-employed after the transition. Annual gross primary-role pay was converted to year-2000 pounds; OLS predicts post-transition pay using pre-transition pay as a predictor, so coefficients represent conditional pay differences rather than a differenced-pay dependent variable. Autonomy averages O*NET ratings of freedom to make decisions and structured versus unstructured work on a 1–100 scale, matched to occupations and mean-centered for interactions. Models include industry dummies, individual-clustered robust standard errors, and demographic, education, previous-role skill, recession, employer-size, multiple-jobholding, and prior-entrepreneurial-experience controls. Observations exceeding 4.5 standard deviations in post-transition pay or pay change were dropped. The archival comparisons are observational.

**Key Findings**
- Study 1 supports the proposed cognitive process: hiring managers sometimes withdrew negative entrepreneurial inferences after encountering the simultaneous employee role. Relative to employee applicants, full-time entrepreneurs elicited more negative-attribute verbalizations (b = 1.97, exp(b) = 7.16, p = .002), but the positive-attribute contrast was nonsignificant (p = .146). Hybrid applicants elicited more positive-attribute verbalizations, including rectification (b = 1.43, exp(b) = 4.17, p = .001), with no significant negative-attribute difference from employees (p = .499; Table 2). These are verbalization comparisons, not actual hiring rates.
- Study 2 supports H1a/H1b: estimated hireability was 5.74 for hybrids versus 5.54 for employees (difference = .20, p = .020) and 5.56 for full-time entrepreneurs (difference = .18, p = .041). There was no overall founding penalty. The applicant-role × autonomy interaction was significant (p = .032), but support for H3 was mixed. Relative to employees, hybrids' advantage was significant at low autonomy (.26, p = .025) and nonsignificant at high autonomy (.14, p = .266), consistent with H3a. Relative to entrepreneurs, the advantage was nonsignificant at low autonomy (.02, p = .875) but significant at high autonomy (.34, p = .011), contrary to H3b (Tables 3–4; Figure 3). The authors found no significant evidence that hybrids were viewed as more likely to leave or that self-interested behavior explained the autonomy effect.
- Study 3 supports H1a/H1b in the authors' conditional-pay models: hybrids gained £532.87 more than employees (p = .010) and £1,544.38 more than full-time entrepreneurs (p = .003); entrepreneurs gained £1,011.51 less than employees (p = .040; Table 7, Model 2). Of 659 hybrid transitions, 440 involved exit and 219 did not. Exit hybrids' advantages were £682.74 over employees (p = .005) and £1,695.86 over entrepreneurs (p = .002); non-exit hybrids' corresponding differences were £230.06 (p = .520) and £1,243.18 (p = .039). The authors interpret this pattern as consistent with H2a/H2b (Table 8, Model 4).
- In Study 3, the hybrid × autonomy coefficient was negative (b = −68.12, p = .011), while the full-time-entrepreneur interaction was nonsignificant (b = 51.86, p = .417); plotted comparisons were interpreted as supporting H3a/H3b (Table 7, Model 3; Figure 4). Post hoc models separating hybrid exit statuses found attenuation for non-exit hybrids (b = −121.34, p = .009), but no significant interaction for exit hybrids (b = −39.83, p = .217; Table 8, Models 5–6). Thus, the autonomy pattern for full-time entrepreneurs differs between Studies 2 and 3. The paper reports similar archival results with Heckman correction and matching, while acknowledging data limitations.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper introduces role rectification as an interpersonal mechanism through which a discrediting role can become an asset within a compensatory role set, without requiring changes to the role's socially shared expectations. It extends role accumulation theory by emphasizing potential role partners' evaluations and nonconformity concerns as boundaries on benefits to role holders. It also advances the careers perspective on entrepreneurship by distinguishing hybrid role sets from isolated entrepreneur roles when explaining labor market advantages and penalties.

**Practical Implication**
The authors recommend hybrid entrepreneurship as a career-management tactic for aspiring entrepreneurs seeking to avoid the founding penalty, accompanied by willingness to exit ventures at strategic times or as a negotiated condition of employment. They suggest that others in discrediting roles may benefit from complementary roles, conditional on exit and autonomy, while cautioning against generalizing to highly stigmatized roles such as dirty work. They also suggest that entrepreneurship educators promote hybrid entrepreneurship as a lower-risk career path.

**Limitations**
Studies 1 and 2 use a single-occupation laboratory setting, and hybrid applicants' accumulated work histories cannot have perfectly equivalent tenures to those of nonhybrid applicants; manipulation checks do not eliminate this design limitation. Study 3's data do not permit full use of prior longitudinal matched-sample designs or observation of job-seeking behavior. Autonomy affects full-time entrepreneurs' outcomes differently across Studies 2 and 3, qualifying generalization of the founding penalty across jobs and hiring stages.

**Future Research**
The authors propose richer datasets to address Study 3's data limitations, and stimulus-sampling designs spanning occupations to investigate whether the founding penalty extends to low-autonomy jobs at screening. They call for tests of role rectification across occupations and industries and within career trajectories, where historical roles may shape interpretations of current role sets. Further directions concern role accumulators' intrapsychic evaluations and stress states, job-search confidence, and when and why they abandon roles.
