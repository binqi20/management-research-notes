# Hostile Frame Takeover: Co-Opting the Security Frame in the Nuclear Energy Debate

**Abstract**
Frames not only shape societal debates but underpin the legitimacy of industries and institutions. Yet, even the most resonant frames can be turned on their head— weaponized against their initial intent. While framing research has studied competition between distinct frames, less is known about how a single frame’s meaning can be strategically inverted to serve opposing agendas. We conceptualize this process as “frame co-optation,” a potent but underexplored mechanism of discursive power that can alter institutional trajectories. The striking reversal of Germany’s nuclear energy phaseout provides a unique opportunity to examine frame co-optation. Using a multimethod machine-learning approach, we analyze three decades of the German nuclear energy debate to understand how the security frame, initially mobilized by antinuclear activists to justify a nuclear phaseout, was co-opted by pronuclear actors to advocate for its reversal. Our analysis shows how the pronuclear actors co-opted the security frame by perforating, camouflaging, infiltrating, and overwriting the frame’s meaning to invert its direction while preserving its resonance. We contribute to framing theory by introducing frame co-optation as a mechanism of strategic meaning inversion that can restore the legitimacy of controversial industries, and, more broadly, destabilize shared meaning and thereby amplify discursive polarization.

**Research Question**
How do actors co-opt resonant frames to support positions antithetical to the established field-level discourse? The study examines how German nuclear energy proponents repurposed the security frame, previously used to justify a nuclear phaseout, to support its reversal.

**Mechanism Process**

- Key constructs: Frame co-optation strategically transforms a frame’s diagnostic, motivational, and prognostic meaning components to support an opposing course of action while preserving its discursive power. The security frame encompasses technological, climate, economic, and supply security.
- Perforating the frame: Sow doubts about the rationality of the initial advocates and call for a new debate, making the established frame porous.
- Camouflaging new diagnostic and motivational components: Broaden the frame through climate, economic, and supply concerns that appear compatible with opponents’ values, without initially challenging the phaseout itself.
- Infiltrating a contrary prognostic component: Highlight trade-offs between the old course of action and new meanings, then couple the reversal of the phaseout to those new meanings.
- Overwriting the frame: Downplay the initial components, construct discursive alliances around the new components, and placate concerns by portraying continued nuclear power as a temporary bridging technology.
- Emergent outcomes: The frame moves from initial to porous, broadened, polarized, and co-opted; coupling with the initial advocates weakens while coupling with co-optive actors increases.

The four mechanisms build sequentially but can overlap. Early meaning changes retain apparent proximity to the original frame, reducing resistance before a contrary course of action is introduced. Growing actor–frame coupling gives co-optive actors authority over the frame and recursively supports further meaning work; the model presupposes sufficient discursive power to initiate this process.

**Data & Measures**
The corpus contains 9,147 articles from 11 national German newspapers, covering 1995–2023 and spanning different political tendencies. Legislative texts, policy documents, and NGO and industry reports provide background and illustrative evidence but were not used for topic modeling. Analyses were conducted in German, with tables and quotations subsequently translated into English.

Stage one focused on paragraphs explicitly addressing nuclear power. The authors tokenized, part-of-speech tagged, and lemmatized the text; estimated LDA models with 10–400 topics; and combined perplexity/coherence assessments with qualitative interpretation to select a 100-topic model. Two authors independently labeled topics using the top 20 words, 10 random text examples, and LDAvis (Krippendorff’s α = .87); 93 interpretable topics were grouped into 10 themes. Security topics were classified by meaning component, dimension, and rationale for or against nuclear energy, and their frequencies were tracked over time.

Stage two used randomly selected full articles comprising 15% of the data in the focal period, requiring a stated 0.3 threshold for the sum of security-topic probabilities in at least one paragraph. Methods describes the focal shift as beginning in 2003 and ending in 2010; Findings labels the co-optation period 2004–2010. The exact qualitative article count is not reported. Two authors used ATLAS.ti and inductive, initially in-vivo coding, iterating between data, themes, and literature; nine first-order processes were aggregated into four mechanisms. Actors were linked to passages in which they were directly or indirectly quoted. Table 3 tabulates 442 actor–process instances, not the number of articles or unique people. This is inductive process theorizing supported by descriptive topic frequencies, not a causal effect estimate.

**Key Findings**

- Security was the predominant frame overall, with a reported topic-frequency range of 28%–48%. Its meaning incorporated technological, climate, economic, and supply dimensions and could support opposing courses of action.
- In 1995–2003, technological security predominantly supported the antinuclear rationale. In 2004–2010, proponents increasingly attached climate, economic, and supply meanings and a contrary prognostic to the frame; the shift accompanied the 2010 phaseout reversal without an exogenous shock. The qualitative analysis develops the four mechanisms above to explain this gradual inversion.
- The reversal was not permanent. Fukushima renewed the antinuclear rationale in 2011–2021; Russia’s invasion of Ukraine prompted a pronuclear supply-security shift in 2022–2023. A four-month extension preceded the shutdown of the remaining plants in April 2023.
- Conservative parties and nuclear energy providers led the co-optation coalition, assisted by other actors, while some Social Democratic and NGO actors also adopted proponents’ arguments. Table 3 reports conservatives at 37.3% and nuclear energy providers at 25.1% of its total actor–process instances; these are descriptive shares.
- The paper contains actor-share discrepancies. For coupling a contrary prognostic, Table 3 assigns 26.2% to nuclear energy providers and 54.8% to conservatives, whereas the prose on p. 1151 reverses those assignments. For constructing discursive alliances, Table 3 gives nuclear energy providers 29.7%, whereas the prose on p. 1152 gives 29.2%.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper shifts framing research from competition between separate frames to contestation over a single frame, conceptualizing co-optation as modular, oppositional meaning work that differs from ideologically congruent frame extension and lamination. It theorizes how changing actor–frame coupling and contradictory meanings can reshape industry legitimacy and amplify discursive polarization. Methodologically, it combines inductive topic modeling with grounded theory analysis to connect field-level temporal patterns with detailed process explanations.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations can contribute to frame co-optation both actively and as silent bystanders. In particular, strategic silence by initial frame advocates could further weaken their coupling with the frame and support its co-optation; this is a proposed implication of the process model, rather than an evaluated intervention.

**Limitations**
The model requires co-optive actors with sufficient discursive power; here, that power came from a coalition of conservative parties and industry incumbents, whereas more fragmented fields may produce stronger contestation, slower co-optation, or failure. The study concerns a Western democratic regime and national newspapers with editorial oversight, leaving different political regimes and algorithmically mediated discourse for further examination. Its focus on co-optive actors also leaves open how initial advocates resist or reclaim frames.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining field concentration, actor coordination, alternative bases of discursive power such as credibility and reach, and co-optation in authoritarian regimes. They also call for research on digital platforms’ ranking, recommendation, and virality mechanisms; nuclear power debates associated with AI energy demand; and multimodal social-media interactions and long-term consequences for industry legitimacy, institutional stability, policymaking, and public trust. Further directions concern resistance and reclamation through symbolic reassertion, silence, or defection, and the transformation of stigmatizing frames into empowering ones, including changes in their emotional array.
