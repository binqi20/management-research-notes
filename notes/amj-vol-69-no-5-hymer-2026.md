# Who’s Behind the Red Suit? Exploring Role Prototypicality within Calling Enactment among Professional Santas

**Abstract**

This study examines how role prototypicality shapes calling enactment. Taking an interpretivist grounded theory approach, we harness the professional Santa Claus context to investigate our research question. Drawing upon interviews, observation, archival documents, and survey data, we find that professional Santas provide different narratives— socially constructed stories that made points about themselves—according to their level of role prototypicality (prototypical, semi-prototypical, non-prototypical) to describe the intrapersonal processes through which they enact their calling. Whereas prototypical Santas internalize the Santa role prototype, semi-prototypical Santas reconcile felt deviation from it, and non-prototypical Santas reinterpret prototypical characteristics. Prototypical Santas generally express high identification with the professional Santa role, making way for continuous calling enactment. Semi-prototypical Santas tend to demonstrate low role identification, resulting in episodic calling enactment. Non-prototypical Santas uniformly express high role identification and continuous calling enactment. Role salience influences prototypical and semi-prototypical Santas’ level of identification with the professional Santa role, and thereby their pursuit of continuous or episodic calling enactment. These insights theoretically extend the callings literature by advancing conversations on role prototypicality, temporality, and role identification within called work.

**Research Question**

How does role prototypicality shape calling enactment, including among workers whose characteristics deviate from their role’s prototype?

**Mechanism Process**

- Key constructs / tensions: Role prototypes specify expected physical and nonphysical characteristics. Prototypical Santas align with the societal and community prototype; semi-prototypical Santas deviate in ways the community overlooks or permits; non-prototypical Santas deviate in ways the community does not deem permissible.
- Practices: Internalizing the role prototype involves switching into the Santa persona and embracing prototypicality. Reconciling felt deviation involves generating explanatory backstories and modifying characteristics to fit the prototype. Reinterpreting prototypical characteristics involves abstracting the prototype to higher-order values while muting deviating characteristics during portrayal.
- Emergent outcomes: Continuous calling enactment extends the role into year-round nonwork life; episodic enactment confines it to bounded periods, generally the Christmas season.
- Role salience: The priority of the Santa role relative to other roles within an identity hierarchy helps explain variation in role identification and enactment among prototypical and semi-prototypical Santas.

The emergent narrative model connects confidence, imposterism, and rejection with different intrapersonal processes through which workers see themselves reflected in the role prototype. These processes allow calling enactment without requiring uniform conformity: less prototypical workers both adapt aspects of themselves and reinterpret their relationship to the prototype. Role identification links these processes to how workers manage work–nonwork boundaries, with high identification accompanying continuous enactment and low identification accompanying episodic enactment.

**Data & Measures**

- Main interviews: 53 informants participated in semi-structured interviews averaging 66 minutes (Table 1). Wave 1 comprised 29 interviews with respondents to the May 2021 survey who volunteered for interviews, seeking heterogeneity in characteristics; wave 2 comprised 24 interviews in August–October 2021, using referrals and theoretical sampling to access less prototypical Santas. All had portrayed Santa for at least one Christmas season. Mean age was 65.27 years (SD = 8.69), and mean calling tenure was 20.14 years (SD = 18.82). Calling tenure was calculated as age at interview minus age when first portraying Santa, an approximation of perceived-calling duration rather than a measure of calling intensity.
- Supporting sources: Five pilot interviews in May–June 2021 averaged 67 minutes; the data partner was interviewed twice. Archival surveys from October 2018 and October 2020 provided 849 responses in Table 1, described in the Methods as data from 849 professional Santas; a May 2021 follow-up provided 382 responses and included four researcher-added open-ended questions. The corpus also included one Zoom leadership workshop observed from 9 a.m. to 4 p.m., five practitioner books, and four films. These are different data sources, not an aggregate count of distinct participants.
- Coding: Interviews were recorded through Zoom, professionally transcribed, and randomly checked for accuracy. Initial coding progressed to focused coding using informant language and literature-derived labels. Authors moved from joint coding and reconciliation to independent coding, memos, and regular discussions; Dedoose supported code-frequency and co-occurrence reports. Two authors independently classified prototypicality against the societal prototype while allowing community flexibility, reaching consensus on all classifications. Alternate templates, temporal bracketing of Christmas and off-season experiences, negative cases, peer debriefing, and member checks refined the model. Identification and enactment were interpreted through accounts of self-definition, identity markers, and work–nonwork boundaries rather than quantitative scales.

Appendix B contains a naming inconsistency: the Santa Serpens narrative names Santa Phoenix in one sentence about reconciling felt deviation, although its heading and surrounding account identify Serpens (p. 955).

**Key Findings**

- All informants experienced Santa work as a calling. Prototypical Santas offered unity narratives: confidence accompanied internalizing the prototype, generally high role identification, and continuous enactment. Semi-prototypical Santas offered dissonance resolution narratives: imposterism accompanied backstories and personal modifications, generally low identification, and episodic enactment.
- Non-prototypical Santas offered trailblazing narratives: feelings of rejection prompted reinterpretation through higher-order values together with muting deviating characteristics. Within the study, they uniformly expressed high identification and continuous enactment. They generally lacked access to Santa networks and off-season recognition, and took a more individualized approach to integrating Santa with other roles.
- Role salience qualified the typical prototypical and semi-prototypical patterns. Some prototypical Santas prioritized other roles after Christmas and reported low identification and episodic enactment; some semi-prototypical Santas prioritized Santa, strongly identified with it, and enacted it continuously. The non-prototypical informants showed no corresponding variation in identification or enactment. These are emergent qualitative patterns, not statistically tested hypothesis results.

**Theoretical Contribution**

The study challenges role prototypicality as a necessary condition for calling enactment by identifying affective and cognitive processes through which less prototypical workers enact called roles. It expands theorizing on temporality by distinguishing continuous from episodic enactment and showing how workers use seasonal detachment to manage work–nonwork boundaries. It also challenges high role identification as a prerequisite: workers can weakly identify with a role yet still experience and episodically enact it as a calling.

**Practical Implication**

The authors address called contract workers and leaders of member communities in called occupations. Workers should assess how their characteristics fit the prototype, which enactment path and resources help them see themselves reflected in it, and how the calling ranks against their other roles. Community leaders could reform role prototypes to support inclusion and help workers negotiate health tensions associated with meeting prototypical expectations; the paper does not test these recommendations’ effects.

**Limitations**

The context combines a clear role prototype, workers who all report a calling, seasonal contract-based work, and performance for beneficiaries; the authors expect greatest transferability to called roles sharing these features, such as musicians, actors, and athletes. Applicability is less clear for year-round, full-time, non-client-facing roles or more ambiguous prototypes. The older sample may have resources and work-legacy motivations that differ from younger workers; these are possibilities raised by the authors, not established explanations.

**Future Research**

The authors propose examining year-round and full-time workers, including whether organizational mentoring and training help them see themselves in role prototypes, and using longitudinal research to study episodic enactment under part-time work, sabbaticals, or flexible arrangements. They also suggest extending the model to non-client-facing callings, such as oncology lab researchers and software developers. Further work could examine physical versus nonphysical deviations, beneficiary reactions and the burden of sustained concealment, and how age-related resources or work-legacy motivations shape enactment.
