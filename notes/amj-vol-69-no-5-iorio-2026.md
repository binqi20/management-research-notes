# Tied In or Tied Up? The Contrasting Effects of Social Ties on Employee Turnover

**Abstract**
A key theoretical tension in organizational network research lies in understanding how interpersonal connections shape employee turnover. While extensive workplace networks can reduce turnover by granting access to valuable information and resources, they can also increase it by exposing employees to information overload, leading to cognitive strain and disengagement. Drawing on process theory, we bridge these two previously disconnected mechanisms—information access and information overload—to uncover how they jointly influence turnover. We propose that an intermediate number of instrumental workplace connections maximizes retention, as it balances the benefits of information access with the burdens of overload. Specifically, we theorize that information access exhibits diminishing marginal returns in mitigating turnover as additional connections provide less value, while information overload accelerates as employees struggle to process a growing volume of inputs, resulting in a U-shaped relationship between the size of an employee’s network and turnover. We test these arguments across four studies: two preregistered experiments and two field studies of employees in large organizations. Our findings challenge conventional wisdom that larger networks are universally beneficial, providing insights into how the structure of informal networks drives turnover dynamics.

**Research Question**
How do employees' instrumental networks both reduce and increase turnover? How do information access and information overload jointly explain the relationship between network size and turnover?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1: Instrumental network size increases information access at a diminishing rate.
- H2: Instrumental network size increases information overload linearly.
- H3: Information access reduces turnover at a decreasing rate.
- H4: Information overload increases turnover at an increasing rate.
- H5: Instrumental network size has a U-shaped relationship with turnover: employees with few or many connections have higher turnover than those with an intermediate number.
- H6: Information access and information overload mediate the U-shaped relationship between instrumental network size and turnover.

Studies 1–2 test H1–H6; Study 3 tests H5 and provides indirect evidence for H6; Study 4 tests H3–H4.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Instrumental network size in Studies 1–3; information access and information overload in Study 4.
- DV(s): Turnover intentions in Studies 1 and 4; quitting a simulated task in Study 2; observed departure from the employer in Study 3.
- Mediators: Information access and information overload, measured in Studies 1–2 and examined indirectly in Study 3.
- Moderators: Information homogeneity in Study 3, used in a mediation-by-moderation test of the proposed informational mechanisms.

Instrumental ties provide useful information, but their informational benefits diminish as maintaining ties takes effort and additional information becomes redundant. Network size is proposed to increase overload linearly, while overload's effect on turnover accelerates as cognitive demands become difficult to manage. Meanwhile, information access reduces turnover with diminishing marginal benefits. The parallel processes yield the predicted U-shaped network-size relationship: access is more important at low connectivity, whereas overload becomes more important at high connectivity.

**Data & Measures**

- **Study 1:** The final sample comprised 115 master's students in international management at a private university. Participants were randomly assigned to positions in networks of seven or eight members and completed an incentivized, 20-minute resource-exchange simulation with actual interactions. Network size was the number of direct connections; models included its linear and squared terms. A post-simulation survey used 5-point scales: information access was a four-item mean (α = .87), overload a five-item scale adapted from Misra and Stokols (2012; α = .88), and turnover intentions a four-item mean after reverse-coding the fourth item (α = .85), asking participants to imagine a future job resembling the task. Main models included whether participants completed the simulation; demographic controls and counts of messages sent and received were used in robustness checks.
- **Study 2:** Of 900 recruited U.S. Prolific adults, 850 remained after preregistered exclusions. Participants were randomly assigned to two, five, or ten programmed peers during a remote association test, with message pacing varying across conditions; network size was coded as the ordinal levels 1, 2, and 3. Behavioral access was the number of puzzles solved, from 0 to 10; behavioral overload equaled 1 when a participant failed to recall an unrelated pop-up word. Quitting equaled 1 when a participant declined another, harder and faster task and accepted two free points instead. The study also collected self-reported access, overload, and turnover intentions. Access-count models used Poisson regression and binary-overload models used logistic regression. Tests of overload's quadratic relationship with quitting used the self-reported overload scale, because squaring its binary behavioral measure would not yield a distinct predictor.
- **Nonlinear mediation in Studies 1–2:** Information access was modeled as a quadratic function of network size, and overload as a linear function. Study 1's outcome models included linear and squared network size and linear and squared mediator terms; Study 2's outcome models retained only the linear access term but both linear and squared overload terms. Mediator and outcome equations were jointly estimated with seemingly unrelated regressions, and instantaneous indirect effects were tested with `nlcom`: at the 10th percentile, median, and 90th percentile of network size in Study 1, and the three experimental conditions in Study 2.
- **Study 3:** Two survey waves and organizational records covered the human resources function of a large, vertically integrated multinational. The response rate was 80%, and the analytic sample was 298. Advice nominations generated degree centrality, with second-wave network size and its square as focal predictors and first-wave size as a control. LinkedIn records identified departures during the five years after the last survey; the observation period ended just before COVID-19. Rare retirements and moves into independent consulting were included as turnover. Footnote 5 separately reports 300 employees with turnover data and 32 without; these availability counts differ from the analytic sample of 298. Information homogeneity was proxied by similarity between employees and their contacts across 13 sub-functional expertise categories, using Yule's Q to adjust for uneven category representation. Logistic models used country-clustered robust standard errors and interacted homogeneity with both network size and its square; information access and overload were not directly measured. Controls included demographics and organizational position, baseline network size, tenure, job performance and its trend, incentive changes, experience, local unemployment, and unit turnover. Performance trend was the latest appraisal score minus the preceding score.
- **Study 4:** The 2023 Federal Employee Viewpoint Survey had 625,568 respondents and a 39% response rate. Table 5 reports 497,210 observations in Model 1, 495,557 in Models 2–3, and 493,901 in Models 4–5. Turnover intention equaled 1 for any affirmative response to considering departure within the next year. Access was the single item about having enough information to do the job well; overload was the reverse-scored item about reasonable workload, both on 1–5 scales. The authors explicitly distinguish workload from information overload and use it as a proxy. Agency fixed-effects logistic regressions used agency-clustered robust standard errors and controls for job attitudes, organizational factors, supervisory relationships, and demographics. Network size was unavailable. Although the prose calls the models nested, Table 5 lists access terms in Models 2–3 and overload terms in Models 4–5, rather than both together.

Random assignment supports causal interpretation of the experimental network-position effects; the field and archival analyses establish associations, and Study 3's mechanism test is indirect.

**Key Findings**

- **Study 1:** H1 was supported: network size predicted greater access with diminishing returns (Table 2, Model 2: linear b = 0.588, p < .001; squared b = -0.049, p < .01). H2 was supported by a positive linear overload coefficient and a nonsignificant square; however, the Model 4 linear coefficient of 0.390 carries p < .01 in Table 2, whereas the prose reports p < .0001. H3 was supported (Model 6: access b = -1.110, p = .004; access squared b = 0.177, p = .002). H4 was supported by positive curvature (Model 8: overload squared b = 0.104, p = .006), while the linear term was nonsignificant (b = -0.293, p = .12). H5 was supported (Model 11: network size b = -0.622, p = .004; squared b = 0.093, p = .001), with significant negative and positive slopes in the U-shape test. For H6, access's indirect effect was negative and significant at low and intermediate network size but nonsignificant at high size; overload's indirect effect was nonsignificant at low size and positive and significant at intermediate and high size.
- **Study 2:** H1 was supported by diminishing returns to network size in predicting access (Table 3, Model 2: squared b = -0.123, p = .012). H2 received support from the positive linear overload relationship (Model 3: b = 0.560, p < .001) and nonsignificant quadratic term (Model 4: b = -0.092, p = .54). H3's predicted nonlinearity was not supported: access was negatively related to quitting (Model 5: b = -0.091, p = .004), but its square was nonsignificant (Model 6: b = 0.003, p = .79). H4 received only marginal support in Model 8 (overload squared b = 0.110, p = .056); the square was significant when access was included in Model 9 (b = 0.118, p < .05). H5 was supported in Model 11 (network size b = -1.396, p = .021; squared b = 0.332, p = .027), with lowest quitting in the intermediate condition and significant slopes on both sides. After including the mechanisms, Model 12's network-size terms were only marginally significant (linear b = -1.115 and squared b = 0.262, both p < .10). The authors report H6 support: access's indirect effect was negative at low and intermediate connectivity but not significant at the .05 level at high connectivity (p = .097); overload's indirect effect was nonsignificant at low connectivity and positive and significant at intermediate and high connectivity.
- **Study 3:** H5 was supported by a U-shaped association with actual turnover (Table 4, Model 3: network size b = -0.102, p = .007; squared b = 0.004, p < .001), with a predicted minimum at approximately 13 contacts. Higher information homogeneity flattened the curve (Model 4: network size squared × homogeneity b = -0.029, p = .007), providing indirect evidence consistent with H6 rather than a direct mediation test.
- **Study 4:** The authors interpret the results as support for H3 and H4: Table 5, Model 3 reports access b = -0.359 and access squared b = 0.056, both p < .001; Model 5 reports overload b = -0.227 and overload squared b = 0.054, both p < .001. They describe diminishing turnover-reduction benefits of access and increasing turnover costs of overload. The explanatory prose also gives the access linear coefficient as -0.357, differing from -0.359 in the reported Model 3 result and table. The linear-only access model was positive and marginally significant (Model 2: b = 0.011, p = .058).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper integrates information access and information overload through parallel process theorizing to explain when instrumental networks retain employees and when they encourage exit. It adds an information-processing account to research on the liabilities of centrality and the turnover of highly connected employees, extending Oldroyd and Morris's (2012) argument about overload. The experiments also examine actual information exchanges and simulated information flows rather than assuming that structural position directly indicates informational advantage.

**Practical Implication**
Managers should monitor delays, errors, and withdrawal as possible signs of overload; the authors caution against seeking a universal optimal network size because the tipping point depends on individual and contextual factors. They propose attention-management practices such as time blocking and communication batching, along with request triage, clearer role boundaries, information gatekeepers, shared repositories, and automated routing to preserve access while reducing redundant demands. They also suggest overlapping expertise across employees to distribute information and reduce bottlenecks.

**Limitations**
Study 1 measures intended rather than actual quitting and may involve demand characteristics and self-report bias; the experimental networks also cap the number of ties. Study 3's LinkedIn outcome does not establish why employees departed or clearly distinguish voluntary from involuntary turnover, while Study 4 lacks network-size data and uses workload as an imperfect proxy for information overload. The authors focus on employees and psychological push factors without theorizing employers' active role, and their settings emphasize frequent interaction in dynamic environments.

**Future Research**
The authors propose comparing psychological push and market-pull explanations and examining how employers' perceptions of networks affect targeting, incentives, and retention. They call for tests in less interdependent settings and of differences in information complexity, codifiability, urgency, individual filtering and boundary-setting capabilities, corporate culture, geography, and inner- versus outer-circle ties. They also suggest studying informational trade-offs in creativity and extra-role behavior, including possible negative consequences of greater information access.
