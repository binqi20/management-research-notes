# How Mega-Threats Influence Workplace Cooperation: Effect of #BlackLivesMatter Salience on Cooperation among and between Black and White Coworkers

**Abstract**
Mega-threats—public, shocking, and identity-relevant events involving violence or injustice—can disrupt organizational life. Yet, research has largely overlooked the fact that such events may create asymmetric identity-based threats for employees who share identities with victims versus those who share identities with perceived perpetrators, leading to divergent psychological responses and workplace cooperation within and between groups. We examine how the salience of a race-based mega-threat shapes cooperation between Black and White employees. Drawing on self-categorization theory and intergroup interaction research, we propose that mega-threat salience more strongly increases intraracial cooperation among Black versus White employees, due to heightened in-group identification among the former. Conversely, we hypothesize that mega-threat salience more strongly reduces White employees’ willingness to initiate cooperation with Black colleagues, driven by perceptions of a morally tainted social identity and concerns about social rejection. We argue that this negative effect is attenuated when White employees hold relatively lower (vs. higher) professional status than their Black interaction partners. We find support for these predictions in over 124,000 dyadic interactions between Black and White players in the National Basketball Association during periods of heightened Black Lives Matter salience. Two experimental studies replicate these findings and provide evidence for the envisioned mechanisms.

**Research Question**
How does the salience of race-based mega-threats asymmetrically affect cooperation within and between Black and White employees, through which identity-based mechanisms, and when does professional status disparity attenuate White employees' withdrawal from interracial cooperation?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**

- H1a: The positive effect of mega-threat salience on intra-racial cooperative interactions is stronger for Black than for White employees.
- H1b: For Black employees, in-group identification mediates the positive effect of mega-threat salience on intra-racial cooperative interactions.
- H2a: The negative effect of mega-threat salience on interracial cooperative interactions is stronger for White than for Black employees.
- H2b: For White employees, fear of rejection mediates the negative effect of mega-threat salience on interracial cooperative interactions.
- H2c: For White employees, morally tainted social identity serially mediates this negative effect through fear of rejection.
- H3: The negative effect on White employees' cooperation with Black coworkers is weaker when the Black coworker has higher professional status than the White employee.

**Mechanism Process**

- IV(s): Mega-threat salience; focal employee and interaction-partner race distinguish intra-racial and interracial pathways.
- DV(s): Initiation of cooperative interactions within and across racial groups, operationalized as directed basketball passes or choosing to undertake a task jointly.
- Mediators: Black employees' in-group identification for intra-racial cooperation; White employees' fear of rejection for interracial cooperation; morally tainted social identity preceding rejection concerns in the serial pathway.
- Moderators: Professional status disparity, with higher-status Black partners attenuating White employees' withdrawal; Study 3 locates the supported moderation on the rejection-concerns-to-interaction link.

Shared identity with victims makes collective threat salient for Black employees and strengthens in-group identification and solidarity, without necessarily producing out-group distancing. For White employees, perceived association with perpetrators or exemption from victimization can activate a morally tainted social identity, heightening anticipated rejection and reducing willingness to initiate interracial cooperation. The authors theorize that motivation to engage with higher-status colleagues and expectations of status-consistent cooperation can buffer this withdrawal. These are differentiated mechanisms situated within racialized workplaces, rather than a prediction of uniform responses to category salience.

**Data & Measures**

- Study 1: NBA.com passing data, supplemented with play-by-play and basketball-reference data, cover 487 players and 1,255 games in the 2014–2015 season. The prose reports 124,953 dyadic observations, whereas Table 1 reports 124,951; Table 2 reports 79,082 intra-racial and 45,869 interracial observations. The DV counts passes from a focal player to a particular teammate within a game. Player race was independently photo-coded, with 98% initial intercoder agreement; non-Black, non-White players were excluded from the main analysis. Mega-threat salience uses a corpus of 40.8 million BLM-related tweets from October 27, 2014, to May 31, 2015. The detailed procedure on p. 1086 computes a three-day rolling mean of daily tweet counts, then subtracts the mean and divides by its SD; p. 1085 instead describes a rolling average of standardized daily counts. This difference in the stated operation order is retained. Status disparity is Black-player salary minus White-player salary, in millions, so positive values indicate higher Black-player status. Poisson models include dyad and month fixed effects, joint playing seconds as exposure, and controls for reciprocal passes, player touches, lineup and team demographics, leadership representation, home games, team performance history, starter roles, and team quality. The design is associational and does not test the psychological mediators.
- Study 2: A 2 × 2 × 2 design crosses salience (high/control), participant race (Black/White), and partner race (Black/White). Of 400 completed responses, 23 failed to recall the partner's race, leaving 377 U.S. Prolific participants: 184 Black and 193 White. High salience uses an article about George Floyd's death and BLM demonstrations; the control article describes the Beirut port explosion. Participants choose to estimate building ages alone or jointly with a partner, producing the binary cooperation DV; all ultimately complete the task individually, with those choosing cooperation told their partner is unavailable. After the choice, three-item scales measure rejection concerns (α = .83), in-group identification (α = .755), and interpersonal liking (α = .917), using 1–7 responses. Logistic regressions, ANOVAs, and bootstrapped mediation examine the pathways. Only the intra-racial and interracial cooperation hypotheses, not the mediation hypotheses, were preregistered. A separate appendix-only set of low-ambiguity conditions includes 188 participants; its detailed results are not in the supplied article.
- Study 3: A 2 × 2 × 2 experiment crosses salience, partner race, and partner status (higher/equal) among White U.S. Prolific participants. Of 480 completed responses, 14 failed partner-race recall, leaving 466. The demographic prose nevertheless lists 186 male, 273 female, and nine diverse participants, which does not reconcile with the stated sample. The same salience manipulation and cooperation decision are used; partner profiles come from Study 2. Higher status means prior successful performance on the task; equal status means first-time participation. Four-item morally tainted social identity (α = .90) and in-group identification (α = .74) are measured immediately after the salience manipulation, with rejection concerns (α = .87) and anticipated liking (α = .94) measured before the cooperation decision. Logistic models and ANOVAs test experimental effects; PROCESS model 6 tests serial mediation within equal-status interracial conditions, and model 92 tests moderated mediation. Collection dates for both experiments are not reported; the events described in the stimuli occurred in 2020.

**Key Findings**

- Study 1 supports H1a, H2a, and H3. In Table 2, salience is positively associated with Black-to-Black passing (Model 1, b = .043, reported p = .000) and also White-to-White passing (Model 2, b = .018, p = .024); the Black-to-Black differential is positive (Model 3, b = .019, p = .020). White-to-Black passing decreases (Model 4, b = −.043, reported p = .000; an estimated 4.20% decrease per SD of salience), whereas Black-to-White passing has no significant association (Model 5, b = .002, SE = .004). The interracial direction interaction is negative (Model 6, b = −.042, reported p = .000). Higher Black-relative-to-White status attenuates the White-to-Black decline (Model 7, interaction b = .002, prose p = .010; Model 8, three-way interaction b = .003, p = .006). Source discrepancies remain: Model 6's SE is .00 in prose and .007 in Table 2; Model 8's is .012 in prose and .001 in the table; Model 7 receives the table's p < .01 marker despite prose p = .010. The supplementary event-timing analysis reports that effects on White employees weaken and become nonsignificant after approximately four weeks; the article describes other robustness checks as consistent, with detailed tables relegated to the online appendix.
- Study 2 supports the predicted racial differences and reports support for H1b and H2b. For intra-racial choices, the salience-by-participant-race interaction is significant (b = −1.401, p = .023); Black participants choose cooperation at .57 under control versus .76 under high salience (pairwise p = .050 as printed), while White participants' .60 versus .48 difference is nonsignificant (p = .220). The Black in-group-identification indirect effect has a bootstrapped 95% CI of [.015, .200]. For interracial choices, the interaction is significant (b = −1.324, p = .026): White participants' cooperation falls from .59 to .35 (p = .020), whereas Black participants' .50 versus .59 difference is nonsignificant (p = .403). The authors report a negative indirect effect through White participants' rejection concerns, printing its 95% CI as [−.114, −.237]; that descending endpoint order is preserved here. Interpersonal liking shows no significant manipulation effects (ps > .27).
- Study 3 supports H3 and the serial pathway in H2c. The salience-by-partner-race interaction is negative (b = −1.224, p = .022), and the three-way interaction with partner status is positive (b = 1.580, p = .037). With equal-status Black partners, cooperation declines from .60 to .38 (p = .013); with higher-status Black partners, .56 versus .60 is nonsignificant (p = .717). Within equal-status interracial conditions, the serial indirect effect through moral taint and rejection concerns is significant (95% CI [−.430, −.036]), but neither the rejection-only path [−1.023, .008] nor the taint-only path [−.166, .504] is significant. Moderated mediation is significant for the serial path (95% CI [.0290, .4352]) and rejection-only path [.001, 1.0364], but not taint-only path [−.857, .2010]. In the full model, status moderates rejection concerns' effect on initiation (p < .001), but not the effects of moral taint (p = .253) or salience (p = .108). Anticipated liking and in-group identification show no significant manipulation effects (ps > .07 and ps > .12, respectively).

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends mega-threat research to the distinct consequences of identity threat for members of victimized and majority groups, linking Black in-group solidarity and White interracial withdrawal to different psychological pathways. It contextualizes self-categorization and intergroup relations within racialized organizations and status hierarchies, showing why heightened category salience need not produce symmetrical in-group cohesion and out-group distancing. It also connects episodic mega-threats with social-movement attention dynamics, treating salience as fluctuating over time and professional status as a potential buffer of relational withdrawal.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise managers not to discourage increased cooperation among Black employees, which may provide support and space to process mega-threats. They propose guided discussions, racial-literacy training, and psychologically safe opportunities for White employees to learn and address uncertainty about cross-racial engagement. They also suggest that defined roles requiring lower-status White employees to cooperate with Black colleagues may normalize such engagement; these are managerial proposals, rather than tested organizational interventions.

**Limitations**
The authors bound their theory to U.S. anti-Black racism and highly publicized police killings of Black civilians, whose history and moral framing may differ from other identity-based threats. The NBA offers unusually visible, structured cooperative actions, leaving generalization to other industries, team compositions, and global contexts unresolved. The field study cannot establish causality or directly test psychological mechanisms, motivating the complementary experiments.

**Future Research**
The authors propose examining other marginalized groups and international settings, allowing effects to vary with power, stigma, historical position, and moral framing. Longitudinal work could test whether White employees' initial avoidance persists, diminishes, or becomes constructive as organizations develop anti-racist initiatives, dialogue, and supportive norms. They also identify diversity climate, leadership communication, norms for discussing race, and individual differences in political beliefs and geographic context as possible moderators.
