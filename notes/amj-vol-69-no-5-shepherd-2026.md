# Are You Looking at Me? Orchestrating Voyeuristic Events to Add Value for Those Being Observed

**Abstract**
Voyeurism offers audiences a window into the private lives of others. Given the widespread appeal of voyeurism, it is unsurprising that organizations have sought to commercialize voyeuristic events across various industries. Prior research has focused on the audience’s role in voyeuristic experiences. However, those being observed (i.e., observees) play an essential role in shaping audiences’ voyeuristic experiences, making it crucial to explore how voyeuristic businesses strive to manage observees’ engagement. For this exploration, we used an inductive approach. We focused on the voyeuristic business of slum tourism and interviewed the observees (slum residents), audience members (tourists), and orchestrators (tour guides) in the Dharavi slum. We found that businesses attempt to orchestrate a voyeuristic event to ensure continued observee participation by scripting the event, claiming to improve the lives of those in the transgressed community, bounding audience behavior, personalizing the observees, and memorializing the voyeuristic experience. This orchestrated interaction can temporarily blur the traditional roles of audience and observee, with some observees momentarily taking on the perspective of the audience and the audience momentarily being observed. This temporary switching of roles can enhance the experience of the voyeuristic event for the audience and for some of the nonconsenting observees.

**Research Question**
How do businesses orchestrate voyeuristic events, and how does this orchestration influence the experiences and roles of observees and audiences? The study examines how businesses manage audience–observee trade-offs, how nonconsenting observees respond, and how roles can momentarily shift.

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs / tensions: Authenticity and transgression; businesses seek to satisfy audiences while reducing the costs borne by nonconsenting observees.
- Practices: Scripting the event; claiming to improve the lives of those in the transgressed community; bounding audience behavior; personalizing observees; memorializing the voyeuristic experience.
- Observee responses: Negative responses involve resisting intrusive overexposure, opposing perceived profit exclusion, and criticizing dehumanizing depiction. Positive responses involve recognizing potential financial and nonfinancial gains, reframing the everyday as special, and having the audience recognize observees’ culture, work, and lives.
- Momentary shifting of roles: Audiences catalyze some observees’ curiosity; observing the audience’s novelty provides amusement.
- Boundary conditions: Observees lack true agency to opt out under substantial structural power asymmetry; audiences, observees, and orchestrators are co-present and co-located, and observees are typically aware of being watched.

The emergent model describes how tour guides orchestrate encounters intended to preserve audience experiences while reducing transgression costs for residents. Scripting and behavioral boundaries regulate intrusion; personalization and memorialization facilitate interactions, while claims about community contributions seek to offset the transgression. Some residents experience recognition, potential gains, curiosity, or amusement and temporarily observe the tourists themselves. These exchanges can benefit some members of both groups, but do not imply residents’ consent or alter the fundamental power imbalance.

**Data & Measures**
The study concerns slum tours in Dharavi, Mumbai. Table 1 reports 35 interviews with observees, 25 with orchestrators, 15 with audience members, and 4 with NGO workers. The methods prose instead describes “15 audience members,” although eight audience interviews involved pairs or larger groups; the supplied paper does not establish the total number of individual audience respondents. Initial interviews took place in November 2023–February 2024; the researchers added 15 non-Hindi-speaking and five Hindi-speaking observees in January–March 2025 beyond theoretical saturation to check for missed cultural differences. The final observee interviews comprised Hindi (20), Marathi (6), Gujarati (5), and Tamil (4); audience interviews were in English, and orchestrator and NGO-worker interviews were in Hindi. Non-English transcripts were translated and checked by language-fluent coauthors or experts, as applicable.

Local experts interviewed residents near tour routes, guides between tours, tourists immediately after tours, and NGO workers at their offices. The 58 field notes came from the first wave and triangulated interview accounts; no second-wave field notes were collected. Secondary articles, documentaries, and a podcast provided context but were excluded from formal analysis. Analysis moved from open codes close to informants’ language to second-order categories and four theoretical dimensions: orchestrating practices, negative responses, positive responses, and momentary role shifts. The researchers revisited the voyeurism literature after coding 30 transcripts and returned to the field after analyzing 59 transcripts and 58 field notes. This is an inductive process account, without quantitative effect estimates or a causal-identification test.

**Key Findings**
- The five orchestrating practices structure encounters through routes and guide training, claimed community contributions, behavioral restrictions, personalized interactions, and opportunities to participate in activities or acquire souvenirs (Table 2). These practices seek to manage trade-offs; claimed benefits are not established as uniformly delivered.
- Some residents resist overexposure through withdrawal or confrontation, oppose exclusion from tour revenues, and criticize portrayals that sensationalize poverty and dehumanize their community (Table 3). Withdrawal from interaction does not prevent them from being observed.
- Other accounts identify potential business, employment, donation, and community benefits, alongside moments of joy and pride when tourists appreciate residents’ everyday activities, culture, and work (Table 4). Some residents remain skeptical of tour operators’ claims to reinvest profits; the authors acknowledge that claimed community benefits may be overstated.
- Curiosity about tourists and amusement at their unfamiliar appearance and behavior allow some residents to momentarily become observers, producing opportunities for learning and mutual exchange (Table 5). These shifts remain limited: tourists control what they reveal and can leave, while residents’ access to tourists’ private lives is neither authentic nor deeply transgressive. Positive responses and continued engagement do not establish consent.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study extends research on commercializing voyeurism beyond optimizing audience emotions to the orchestration of relationships involving both audiences and nonconsenting observees. It identifies practices for managing stakeholder trade-offs and contextualizes entrepreneurial opportunity attributes around authenticity, transgression, and observee engagement. It also challenges an exclusively unidirectional account of voyeurism by showing momentarily fluid roles within enduring structural power asymmetries.

**Practical Implication**
The authors advise businesses offering authentic customer experiences to develop practices that aim to reduce the costs of transgressing into others’ lives and to provide opportunities for observees to learn from audiences. Businesses should consider how to protect observees and create positive experiences while managing trade-offs with voyeuristic customers, and can contribute back to the communities on which they transgress.

**Limitations**
Transferability is bounded by observees’ limited agency and power and by the co-presence and co-location of audiences, observees, and orchestrators; extensions beyond these conditions require further work. Using local experts supported access, trust, and care, but may have produced less rich data and limited attention to nonverbal communication and interview context; residents who felt most transgressed may also have been more likely to decline interviews. Claimed community benefits are contested, and the temporary role shifts neither establish consent nor remove the underlying power asymmetry.

**Future Research**
The authors call for tests of the model’s boundaries, including settings with less vulnerable observees, remote encounters, or no guides, and for examining whether practices transfer to other voyeuristic businesses or businesses dependent on natural resources, such as Arctic expedition cruises. They also propose considering the trade-offs between researchers collecting data themselves and using local experts, and investigating additional languages. Finally, research could examine how voyeuristic opportunities form, who first constructs and pursues them and why, and who finds exploiting them desirable and feasible.
