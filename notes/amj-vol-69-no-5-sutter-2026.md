# To Be or Not to Be (Aligned with Elites): Navigating Systemic Power within Informal Institutions

**Abstract**
Social intermediaries play a critical role in addressing exclusion, but must navigate entrenched power relations within informal institutions. Drawing on comparative case studies of three social intermediaries, this study examines how intermediaries negotiate systemic power—manifested through domination and discipline—across intersecting caste–gender hierarchies. We identify three distinct approaches to elite engagement: elite delegation, elite co-optation, and creative accommodation. Each of these reflects a specific combination of communal positioning and power frame adopted by intermediaries. Elite delegation, grounded in a detached communal positioning and a zero-sum power frame, prioritizes rapid scaling but reproduces existing hierarchies. Elite co-optation, marked by pragmatic communal positioning and a conditional positive-sum power frame, fosters short-term cooperation yet achieves only partial inclusion. Creative accommodation, characterized by embedded communal positioning and a positive-sum power frame, enables social transformation by simultaneously empowering marginalized groups and constructively engaging elites. By demonstrating how communal positioning and power frames shape these approaches, the study advances understanding of how systemic power sustains exclusion and how it can be reconfigured from within. We contribute to research on informal institutions and inclusion by elucidating how intermediaries can leverage positive-sum engagements to reshape domination and discipline, fostering more equitable and enduring informal institutional change.

**Research Question**
How can social intermediaries negotiate informal institutions as they navigate systemic power relations between elite and marginalized community members?

**Mechanism Process**
- Key constructs: Communal positioning is an intermediary’s stance toward local intersectional hierarchies; its power frame interprets gains in power as zero-sum or positive-sum. Domination operates through structural advantages and resource control; discipline operates through internalized norms, surveillance and self-regulation.
- Elite delegation: Detached communal positioning and a zero-sum power frame combine pragmatic gatekeeping with strategic blindness, reinforcing elite control and marginalized groups’ invisibility.
- Elite co-optation: Pragmatic communal positioning and a positive-sum frame conditional on economic benefits combine economic-benefit framing with segregated group design; a price-gain virtuous loop coexists with a segregation vicious loop.
- Creative accommodation: Embedded communal positioning and a positive-sum power frame combine elite engagement with marginalized empowerment, using elite indifference, staged mixing and norm-setting to develop positive-sum gains, empowerment and solidarity.

The comparative process model links intermediaries’ orientations to different ways of reproducing or challenging domination and discipline. Creative accommodation begins with community-wide projects, gains access to marginalized groups through activities that do not threaten elites, and builds their capabilities and confidence. Carefully structured interactions then support incremental changes in power relations; the process takes time and encounters resistance.

**Data & Measures**
The study compares Vifal, Uttam and Daksh, three of five intermediaries initially studied in rural Madhya Pradesh, India. These organizations shared TechScreen’s video-based agricultural-extension methodology but retained autonomy over objectives, content, screening locations and participants, providing a basis for comparison of their different approaches. The eight-year field study involved 19 visits and 24 calendar months in the field, with 489 semi-structured individual interviews and 130 group interviews; these are interview counts, not a reported count of unique participants. Table 3 separately documents staff and community interviews, field observations, informal conversations and archival materials; group interviews were not traditional focus groups because researchers did not preselect participants. Data collection ended in December 2016; the Uttam account specifies a July 2009 start.

The researchers iterated among collection, analysis and interpretation. The second and third authors independently developed thick case descriptions, then collaboratively filled gaps; comparative analysis and triangulation of interviews with observations examined domination, discipline and intermediary–elite interactions. Communal positioning, power frames and the three engagement approaches emerged from this analysis. The study develops a qualitative process explanation rather than estimating causal effects or testing standardized construct measures.

**Key Findings**
- Vifal’s elite delegation supported rapid scaling while reinforcing exclusion. Elites controlled group formation and resource access, while internalized norms and restricted mobility made marginalized groups less visible. Screening groups were predominantly dominant-caste men’s groups, with a few dominant-caste women’s groups that rarely screened videos; there were no marginalized-caste or mixed-gender groups.
- Uttam’s elite co-optation secured economic cooperation and some material gains for relatively better-off marginalized-caste farmers, but inclusion remained conditional on elites’ material interests. Segregated groups limited cross-group interaction and reinforced disciplinary norms; the most economically marginalized remained excluded. Some marginalized-caste women and eventually less-well-off dominant-caste women participated, but there were no mixed-gender groups or groups of well-off dominant-caste women, and mixed-caste participation was limited.
- Daksh’s creative accommodation produced the most substantial shifts toward inclusive norms among the three cases. Community-wide projects secured elite access; initial work with scheduled-tribe women on forest produce used elite indifference to open space for participation. Recognizing marginalized women’s capabilities, gradually extending participation and structuring mixed groups helped challenge both domination and discipline. Men’s inclusion depended on women’s consent, with women speaking first and men not interrupting; cross-caste women’s interaction fostered solidarity around shared problems, including domestic violence. Changes were gradual, and elites initially restricted content and resisted some groups.

The paper contains an internal qualification discrepancy: Uttam’s detailed account and Table 2 report participation by some dominant-caste women, whereas the comparative summary says it did not successfully reach women from the dominant caste without retaining the economic-status qualification.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study explains the durability and possible evolution of informal institutions through intersecting hierarchies and systemic power, emphasizing group-specific roles as well as rules. It identifies making power visible, adopting positive-sum power frames and building marginalized groups’ capacity and confidence as mechanisms for shifting domination and discipline. Communal positioning and power frames explain three approaches to elite engagement, extending the cooperation-versus-resistance distinction and showing how creative accommodation can alter informal institutions through embedded, negotiated action.

**Practical Implication**
The authors argue that social intermediaries should engage elites and marginalized groups in tandem: exclusive elite engagement risks capture, while ignoring elites risks obstruction. Their guidance emphasizes understanding otherwise invisible power relations, constructing positive-sum opportunities while elites still perceive a relative benefit, strengthening marginalized groups’ capabilities and confidence, and tailoring interventions to the distinct but intersecting constraints of caste and gender.

**Limitations**
The authors caution against over-transferring findings from rural India’s distinctive social hierarchies to other settings. The high-level comparison omits granular variation within caste groups, local government actors and informal social networks, each of which may condition how change unfolds.

**Future Research**
The authors propose testing the transferability and boundaries of communal positioning, power frames and the three approaches across cultural or institutional settings, regions and sectors, including whether additional approaches emerge. They suggest studying reachability and mobility constraints in entrepreneurial inclusion of migrant or refugee populations. Further work could examine within-caste heterogeneity, local governments and informal village networks, extend intersectional analysis to class and religion, and investigate how formal institutions and policies amplify or dampen intermediary efforts.
