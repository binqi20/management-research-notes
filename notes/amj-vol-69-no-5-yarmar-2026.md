# When Women Stick Together: Network Dynamics and Shared Resources Following Disruptive Events

**Abstract**
Social networks play a role in exacerbating or ameliorating inequality. The persistence of network-based inequality is well documented. However, the mechanisms that disrupt this unequal access to resources remain poorly understood. This study investigates how structurally disruptive events, such as mergers and acquisitions (M&As), reshape gender homophily and resource sharing in networks. We examine how 59 M&As shape the evolution of 82,064 physicians’ referral and patient-sharing networks. We find that disruptive events enhance women’s access to resources by altering gender-homophily dynamics in formation, and maintenance. Specifically, acquisitions intensify men’s tendency to form ties with other men. In contrast, women are more likely to preserve ties with other women, with all-women dyads exhibiting greater resilience and higher maintenance rates than mixed-gender or all-men dyads. This dynamic results in richer networks for women, shielding them from the adverse effects of organizational disruption. These results highlight contrasting network behaviors following disruption: women winnow their networks, reinforcing stable, trust-based relationships with women, while men widen theirs, forming new distant ties with men. By demonstrating how gendered network dynamics evolve in response to disruptions, this study contributes to scholarship on gender inequality, network resilience, and conditions under which homophilous ties can mitigate disparities.

**Research Question**
How do disruptive organizational events reshape gender homophily through tie formation and maintenance, and when do these network dynamics attenuate gender inequality in access to network-based resources?

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Disruptive organizational events increase the formation of ties between men more than the formation of ties between women.
- H2: Disruptive organizational events increase the maintenance of ties between women more than the maintenance of ties between men.
- Corollary 1: Disruptive events intensify gender-homophilous ties for both men and women, albeit via different mechanisms.
- H3: Disruptive events increase the resource benefit of women’s maintained same-gender ties more than that of men’s newly formed same-gender ties.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Hospital acquisition exposure; physician-level counts of same- and different-gender ties formed and maintained.
- DV(s): Dyadic tie formation, binary tie maintenance, and dyadic shared-patient volume; individual physicians’ total shared-patient volume.
- Mediators: No formal mediation test. Gendered tie formation and maintenance are the proposed pathways connecting disruption with resource access; trust, reciprocity, and coping with uncertainty are theorized explanations.
- Moderators: Dyad or physician gender; tie embeddedness in the dyadic models; acquisition exposure in models of gendered returns to network formation and maintenance.

The authors argue that disruptive events heighten relational uncertainty, prompting men, who disproportionately occupy higher-status positions, to widen their networks through new, distant same-gender contacts. Women are theorized to winnow their networks by preserving embedded same-gender relationships offering trust, reciprocity, and dependable support. Under disruption, those established relationships may generate greater immediate resource benefits than men’s new ties, even though tightly knit networks can constrain longer-term access to diverse opportunities.

**Data & Measures**
- Data and timing: IQVIA LifeLinkLRx longitudinal prescriptions identify physicians prescribing mental health medications and their shared patients; the Measuring Hospital Ownership Transitions in the United States dataset supplies hospital acquisitions. Outcomes in 2005 and 2007 bracket acquisitions in 2006; the authors omit 2006 outcomes because acquisition dates are available only annually, exclude regions acquired in 2005 or 2007, restrict dyads to the same three-digit zip code, and remove physicians with incomplete data. The sample has 82,064 physicians and 178,252 unique dyads, with 94,872 active relationships in 2005 and 132,646 in 2007. The source reports 726 regions but also 56 exposed and 673 control regions; these counts do not reconcile.
- Ties and analysis samples: A patient–physician network is projected into physician dyads weighted by shared-patient counts; an active relationship requires at least six shared patients. Formation requires no shared patients in 2005 and at least six in 2007. Unformed comparison dyads share no patients during the observation period; sampling selects three per formed tie within the same region, favors dyads with more initial shared contacts, and adjusts inclusion weights. The prose reports 26,219 formed and 78,822 unformed ties, whereas Table 3 reports 106,059 observations; these figures also do not reconcile. Maintenance models follow all 94,872 active baseline dyads, measuring persistence or the number of patients shared in 2007.
- Exposure and gender: Acquisition indicates a hospital acquisition in the physician’s or dyad’s three-digit zip code in 2006. The 59 events comprise 56 acquisitions and three mergers. Gender is woman/man at the physician level and all-women/all-men/mixed-gender at the dyad level. Dyadic controls include regional hospital count, shared medical school, both physicians being primary-care providers, and both being psychiatrists; physician-level controls include birth year, primary-care and psychiatry specialties, and regional hospital count.
- Embeddedness and resources: Equation 1 defines tie embeddedness as `M_ij / (C_i + C_j - M_ij - 2)`, where `M_ij` is shared contacts and `C_i`, `C_j` are each physician’s direct contacts: first add the two contact counts, subtract shared contacts and two, then divide shared contacts by that denominator. This is structural overlap, not directly measured trust or emotional closeness. Network dynamics are absolute counts of same- and different-gender ties maintained between 2005 and 2007 or formed in 2007, entered simultaneously without normalizing by initial network size. Total shared patients sums a physician’s shared-patient counts across prescriber contacts in 2007, with zero imputation for physicians sharing patients in 2005 but not 2007.
- Estimation: Main analyses use logistic models for formation and binary maintenance and QML Poisson models for dyadic and individual shared-patient counts, with the clustering specified above. This is an observational study of regional acquisition exposure; difference-in-differences is reported as an Appendix D robustness analysis, rather than the main Tables 3–5 specification.

**Key Findings**
- H1 is supported: In Table 3, Model 2, acquisition × all-women has a coefficient of −0.33 (SE = 0.06, p < .001), relative to all-men dyads. Figure 7 reports positive acquisition marginal effects for all-men dyads (0.016, SE = 0.005), negative effects for all-women dyads (−0.044, SE = 0.010), and a nonsignificant mixed-gender effect. The acquisition × all-women × embeddedness interaction is positive (4.03, SE = 1.18, p < .001; Model 3): the pattern favors men’s formation of distant ties and women’s formation within overlapping contact networks.
- H2 is supported with a comparison qualification: Table 4, Model 2, reports a positive acquisition × all-women interaction for binary maintenance (0.16, SE = 0.08, p < .05); Figure 9 gives a positive acquisition marginal effect for all-women dyads (0.039, SE = 0.016), with nonsignificant effects for all-men and mixed-gender dyads. However, the paper reports that the adjusted all-women versus all-men comparison after acquisition is positive (0.06) but nonsignificant. Continuous maintenance likewise has a positive all-women acquisition interaction (0.13, SE = 0.06, p < .05; Model 5). Embeddedness strengthens the relative all-women acquisition response in both binary maintenance (0.53, SE = 0.26, p < .05; Model 3) and shared-patient volume (0.31, SE = 0.15, p < .05; Model 6). Together with formation, these patterns support Corollary 1’s distinct routes to homophily.
- H3 is supported: Table 5, Model 3, reports a positive acquisition × women × same-gender-maintained interaction (0.12, SE = 0.04, p < .01), whereas the corresponding same-gender-formed interaction is nonsignificant (0.03, SE = 0.04). The direct comparison of women’s maintained same-gender ties with men’s newly formed same-gender ties under acquisition yields an estimate of 0.31 (SE = 0.03, t = 9.03, p < .001). Separately, the maintenance-versus-maintenance comparison in Figure 11 associates an additional maintained same-gender tie in acquisition regions with 10.32 additional shared patients for women (SE = 1.02) versus 5.88 for men (SE = 0.54).
- Robustness is reported in the main text for alternative tie thresholds, acquisition measures, specialty and insurance-market checks, and lagged-resource controls; Appendix D’s difference-in-differences models reportedly confirm women’s post-acquisition resource gains. Other forms of homophily, including race, do not replicate the findings. The detailed online appendix tests are not included in the supplied paper text.
- Source reporting cautions: The p. 999 narrative reverses the references to Figure 2 (maintenance) and Figure 3 (formation). Table 4, Model 4, marks the acquisition main effect as marginal (0.05, p < .10), while the prose calls it nonsignificant. The p. 1002 description that weak all-women ties dissolve differs from Figure 10’s positive, nonsignificant acquisition marginal effect at zero embeddedness (0.016, SE = 0.026). The p. 1005 statement that Table 5, Model 2, excludes acquisition conflicts with its acquisition row. That page also describes a combined effect approaching 18% using a 0.30 maintenance coefficient, whereas Model 3 reports 0.29 and multiple interactions; no reconciliation is supplied. Its different-gender-formation interaction for women is reported at p < .01 in prose and marked p < .001 in Table 5.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The study connects status-dependent responses to uncertainty with network evolution, distinguishing men’s network widening from women’s embedded tie preservation. It identifies organizational disruption as a contingency under which women’s homophilous networks can become resource advantages: the value of existing ties can change even when their structure remains stable. Examining formation, maintenance, and resource flows together clarifies how network dynamics may interrupt rather than simply reproduce gender inequality.

**Practical Implication**
The authors suggest that organizations support relational continuity for underrepresented employees through women’s mentoring circles and peer-support programs that persist through restructuring, while mapping pre-merger collaboration and protecting high-trust connections across combined units. They also advocate cross-unit collaboration, rotational leadership programs, and inclusive professional development to extend access beyond existing circles. These recommendations balance short-term continuity with network expansion, given the possible long-term costs of tightly knit same-gender networks.

**Limitations**
The data cannot connect physicians to specific hospitals, preventing separation of directly affected hospital colleagues from physicians exposed through their surrounding region; area-level exposure may dilute estimates. Although the study documents gendered network patterns and resource sharing, the underlying sociopsychological mechanisms remain unclear. The authors also caution that same-gender networks adaptive in the short term may limit access to diverse contacts, novel information, and emerging opportunities over time.

**Future Research**
The authors call for research on the sociopsychological mechanisms driving gendered network resilience during disruptions, including the motivations underlying women’s supportive professional networks.
