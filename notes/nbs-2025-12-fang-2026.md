# Political Segregation in the Labor Market and the Persistence of Pay Gaps

**Abstract**
Many scholars assume that initiatives to promote gender and racial pay equality are largely performative, helping firms appear socially responsible while making little progress. However, the extent to which such initiatives are genuinely supported may depend on the ideological leaning of the field in which they occur. We propose that the higher the ratio of liberals in an industry or occupation, the more its members will be guided by logics that support the equal distribution of resources between gender and racial groups. Using large-scale archival samples of U.S. employees and recent data on U.S. firms and nonprofit organizations, we find support for our theory. The results indicate that membership in liberal-leaning industries and occupations is associated with stronger support for the use of pro-equality practices. Liberal-leaning industries and occupations also showed greater progress toward gender and racial pay equality over time. Notably, we controlled for various alternative explanations, such as demographic and geographic differences between fields. Thus, this paper suggests that the political composition of a field may give rise to logics and norms that shape how efforts to promote group equality are understood and pursued.

**Research Question**
Why have gender and racial pay gaps persisted despite increasingly common organizational equality initiatives? The paper asks whether the political composition (the ratio of liberals to conservatives) of an industry or occupation shapes whether pro-equality logics and norms emerge, and thereby whether equality initiatives are genuinely pursued and pay gaps narrow.

**Hypotheses / Propositions**
- H1: Membership in a more liberal industry is positively related to support for organizational practices that promote (a) gender and (b) racial equality.
- H2: Membership in a more liberal occupation is positively related to support for organizational practices that promote (a) gender and (b) racial equality.
- H3: Liberal industries tend to have smaller (a) gender and (b) racial pay gaps than conservative industries.
- H4: Liberal occupations tend to have smaller (a) gender and (b) racial pay gaps than conservative occupations.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Industry liberalism and occupational liberalism (average self-reported political ideology of a field's members, reverse-scored so higher = more liberal)
- DV(s): Support for pro-equality practices (preferential hiring/promotion of women and Blacks); gender and racial (Black–White) pay gaps in annual wages
- Moderators: Proportion of women / racial minorities in a field; geographic (state) ideology
- Controls: Personal liberalism, age, gender, race, education, region/state fixed effects, industry/occupational polarization, work-related variables; industry and occupational fixed effects

Drawing on field theory, the authors argue that industries and occupations function as fields in which interorganizational "hubs" (trade associations, conferences, unions, professional groups) enable members to consolidate and spread shared ideas and practices. A higher ratio of liberals lets pro-equality logics—shared understandings that gender and racial equality are valued and expected—emerge, spread, and become legitimate, so members support pro-equality practices and pay gaps narrow. In conservative fields, equality-supporting members encounter fewer like-minded peers and efforts challenge a meritocratic status quo, so pro-equality logics emerge weakly or not at all and gaps persist.

**Data & Measures**
- GSS: The survey comprises independent cross-sectional samples from 1972–2021. Field liberalism uses ideology reports from 1974–2021: reverse-score the seven-point personal ideology item so higher values indicate greater liberalism, then average across respondents and survey years within each industry or occupation. Retaining fields with at least 15 reports yields 250 industries and 394 occupations. These are continuous average-ideology scores. The support analyses use 14,696 respondents after listwise deletion and two separate, reverse-scored four-point items on preferential hiring and promotion of women and Blacks. OLS standardizes continuous variables and controls age, gender, race, education, personal liberalism, and region fixed effects; expanded models add field demographic composition, state liberalism, childhood class, and field polarization measured by the standard deviation of members' ideologies.
- CPS: The main pay analyses cover full-time employees aged 25–64 in 1994–2023, matching the same GSS field scores to respondents' 2010 Census industry and occupation codes across survey years. Prior-calendar-year pretax wage and salary income is first adjusted using the Consumer Price Index to constant 1999 U.S. dollars, then log-transformed. Gender is coded female = 1, male = 0. OLS tests gender/race interactions with field liberalism, standardizes continuous predictors, and progressively adds sociodemographic, geographic, and work controls. Tables 5–6 include education, marital status, citizenship, firm size, and survey-year fixed effects throughout; Models 2–3 add state fixed effects, and Model 3 adds occupation fixed effects in Table 5 or industry fixed effects in Table 6. The text and Table 4 report n = 1,458,666 (Table 4 specifies Black and White respondents), whereas Tables 5–6 report n = 1,455,285 and include additional racial categories. These observational models estimate adjusted associations, not experimentally identified effects.
- Supplementary cohort: The Youth–Parent Socialization Panel Study follows one cohort across four surveys from ages 18–50, pairing reported occupations with support for government aid to racial minorities. Analyses examine occupational switches, later attitudes, career exposure to liberal occupations, and changes with age.
- Supplementary firms: JUST Capital's 2022 survey asked Russell 1000 firms about four practices in 2021: pay-gap analyses, public pay-gap disclosure, diversity targets for hiring/promotion/retention, and public EEO policies. Glassdoor supplies employee diversity-and-inclusion ratings; reported firm controls include size and ideology.
- Supplementary nonprofit hubs: CauseIQ data provide occupation-level counts and revenues of hubs promoting women/girls or African Americans across 186 occupations. Controls include the total counts and revenues of all potential hubs and occupational gender/racial composition. Hub revenue serves as a proxy for size and status.

**Key Findings**
- GSS: H1 is partly supported and H2 fully supported. In Table 3, Model 1, industry liberalism is not significantly associated with support for preferential hiring/promotion of women (b = .00, SE = .01, p = .67), whereas occupational liberalism is positively associated with it (b = .03, SE = .01, p < .001). For support concerning Blacks, Model 2 reports positive industry and occupational associations (b = .03 and .06, respectively; both SE = .01, p < .001).
- CPS: The authors report support for H3 and H4. In the fully controlled Model 3 of Tables 5 and 6, industry and occupational liberalism each interact positively with female gender (b = .03) and Black race (b = .02), with p < .001, indicating smaller adjusted gender and Black–White pay gaps in more liberal fields. Table 8 reports female-to-male earnings ratios of .85 versus .76 in liberal versus conservative industries and .88 versus .80 in liberal versus conservative occupations; corresponding Black-to-White ratios are .96 versus .89 and .94 versus .89.
- Exploratory CPS analyses: Year-specific analyses covering 1968–2023 indicate faster overall progress toward pay equality in liberal fields. Of eight demographic/geographic moderation tests, only one is significant: the industry-liberalism association with gender pay equality is stronger in industries with higher proportions of women.
- Supplementary cohort: Three of four tests are consistent with the theory. Switching toward liberal or conservative occupations accompanies corresponding increases or decreases in support for racial equality; occupational liberalism predicts later support; and greater career exposure to liberal occupations is associated with stronger support. However, the study does not find increasing support with age among people in liberal versus conservative occupations.
- Supplementary firms and hubs: Firms in liberal industries report greater use of the four equality-promoting practices and receive higher employee diversity-and-inclusion ratings, with controls including firm size and ideology. Separately, liberal occupations have more hubs promoting women and Blacks and higher revenues for these hubs after the reported controls. These findings are consistent with the proposed account, but the authors could not directly measure field-level pro-equality logics or norms and therefore did not directly test whether they explain the findings.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper extends research on pay gaps, organizational ideology, and political polarization by theorizing ideology as a field-level (industry/occupational) norm rather than only an attribute of individuals, teams, or firms. It shows that shared ideological logics can emerge even in loosely connected settings through informational hubs, and it identifies political segregation in the labor market as an overlooked driver of societal polarization, reframing slow equality progress as the uneven distribution of pro-equality fields rather than uniformly symbolic effort.

**Practical Implication**
Initiatives to reduce pay gaps should be more effective in liberal fields (where equality is more widely valued) and face greater barriers—but greater room for change—in conservative fields. Efforts are likely more effective when directed toward influential hubs such as industry associations, professional conferences, training centers, and accreditation agencies that can normalize fair-pay and objective-hiring practices across a field.

**Limitations**
The authors could not directly measure whether promoting equality is a logic or norm for each field, so they cannot fully confirm that differing liberal/conservative logics explain the findings. The data lack information on individuals' specific job levels, limiting analysis of pay gaps among similar roles at different hierarchical levels, and other omitted controls may refine the understanding of pay inequality. Data are limited to the United States.

**Future Research**
Future work could examine how actors embedded in multiple intersecting fields (e.g., an HR director in the energy sector) navigate conflicting ideological pressures, and whether powerful actors (executives, boards) exert disproportionate influence over a field's ideological leaning. Researchers could measure pro-equality norms directly, incorporate finer-grained job-level indicators, and conduct cross-cultural studies to test whether the patterns hold where institutional support for equality and political polarization differ.
