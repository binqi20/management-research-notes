# Investments that make our homes greener: The role of regulation

**Abstract**
Operation of residential buildings is responsible for roughly 22% of global energy consumption and 17% of CO2 emissions. We study the investments triggered by a regulatory intervention requiring rented properties to satisfy minimum energy efficiency standards. The analysis shows significant investments in low capital expenditure retrofits. Using an instrumented difference-in-differences methodology, we show that the investments do not have an economically significant impact on rents, so that landlords are not compensated for them.

**Research Question**
What are the magnitude, nature, and financial returns of the investments triggered by a regulatory intervention requiring privately rented properties to meet minimum energy-efficiency standards, and to what extent are landlords compensated through rents for the capital expenditures they incur?

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Introduction of the UK Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard (MEES) Regulations (approved March 2015) requiring privately rented dwellings to meet minimum SAP-based energy-efficiency thresholds; pre-regulation EPC status used as an instrument for post-regulation energy efficiency
- DV(s): Retrofit investments undertaken (type, capital expenditure, energy savings, internal rate of return); property-level rents; energy-efficiency (SAP) scores; CO2 emissions / carbon footprint of properties
- Mediators: Not reported in paper; the channels discussed below are not formal mediation tests.
- Moderators: No common moderation model is reported. The analyses compare pre-regulation threshold status, tenure and main fuel, and recalculate emissions using updated factors.

Building on the energy-efficiency gap and split-incentive literatures, the paper argues that in England and Wales utilities are paid directly by tenants, so landlords cannot internalize energy-bill savings from retrofits and — absent regulation — under-invest in energy efficiency. When mandated by MEES, landlords comply by selecting low-capex retrofits with the highest IRRs, but rent effects depend on how selection is addressed: the unadjusted DiD estimate could cover low-capex upgrades, whereas the IVDiD estimates show no economically meaningful rent effect and no compensation even for low-capex investments. Because EPCs score properties on a cost-based metric that combines fuel quantities and prices, regulation incentivizes shifts toward cheaper rather than cleaner fuels, so energy-efficiency gains in the rental sector do not translate into commensurate reductions in carbon emissions, especially once outdated carbon factors are used.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper provides large-sample evidence on the types, capital costs, and internal rates of return of residential energy-efficiency retrofits undertaken by both homeowners and landlords, informing the climate-finance and energy-efficiency-gap literatures on barriers to private investment in decarbonization. It quantifies the cash-flow (rent) effects of a major energy-efficiency regulation and shows that landlords are not compensated for their capital expenditures, clarifying the nature of regulatory risks faced by real estate investors. It further demonstrates that cost-based energy-efficiency metrics can misalign private investment with decarbonization goals, distinguishing energy-efficiency from environmental-impact improvements.

**Practical Implication**
If energy-performance certificates and regulations are to be used to tackle climate change, they should report and target carbon emissions directly rather than cost-based efficiency metrics, and carbon factors should be updated to reflect the decarbonization of electricity generation. Policymakers should recognize that landlord-tenant split incentives limit the pass-through of energy savings into rents, so mandating energy-efficiency upgrades will likely impose uncompensated costs on landlords and bias their compliance toward low-capex retrofits. Interventions should also coordinate consumption-side efficiency requirements with production-side decarbonization policies.

**Limitations**
The analysis is confined to England and Wales and to the specific MEES regulatory regime. Carbon-footprint recalculations using updated emission factors are constrained by the EPC data, which do not allow the authors to split energy consumption by fuel type for multi-fuel properties. Estimated internal rates of return rely on the projected energy savings and assumed investment lifespans, which may diverge from realized outcomes.
