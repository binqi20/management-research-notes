# The ESG-Innovation Disconnect: Evidence from Green Patenting

**Abstract**
We document that traditional energy firms are key innovators in the United States’ green patent landscape. These firms produce more, and significantly higher-quality, green innovation. In many green technology spaces, they appear to be influential first movers and to produce ongoing foundational aspects of innovation and commercialization on which other alternative energy producers build. They additionally invest significantly in labor and capital to complement these green innovations. These traditional energy firms, however, receive significantly lower environmental, social, and governance (ESG) scores and fund flows and are not rewarded for incremental green innovation. This behavior is consistent with a competitive response by traditional energy firms to preempt obsolescence of current technology by investing in future replacement technologies.

**Research Question**
Which firms actually contribute most significantly to green innovation in the United States, and do current ESG rating and capital allocation mechanisms effectively reward the entities best positioned to develop environmental technologies? The authors in particular ask whether traditional energy firms are systematically penalized by ESG ratings and divestment-oriented capital flows despite their role as producers of green patents.

**Mechanism Process**
- IV(s): Membership in the traditional energy sector (SIC 10, 12, 13, 14, 29, 49, excluding alternative-energy NAICS); number of green patents granted; interaction of Energy Sector with Green Patents Granted.
- DV(s): Industry and firm green patent ratio (green patents / total patents); Sustainalytics/MSCI environmental score; green patent quality (adjusted citations, blockbuster-patent indicator); first-mover status (USPTO patent number rank, top-10% pioneering indicator); patent-thicket measures; production of low-carbon products; revenue from low-carbon products; green hiring; fund holdings and flows.
- Mediators: Not reported in paper.
- Moderators: Energy-sector status (Energy Sector × Green Patents interaction). Additional comparisons split the sample before/after 2004 and compare energy with the top three non-energy green-patenting sectors.

The paper develops a conceptual framework grounded in industrial organization: when incumbent firms rationally anticipate the eventual technological displacement of their existing product lines, profit-maximizing behavior leads them to invest in the very innovations that could cannibalize their current business, aligning private incentives with environmental objectives without external ESG inducements. Empirically, traditional energy firms dedicate roughly 20.91% of their patenting to green innovation (vs. 9.2% for other green-patenting industries), produce patents with about 14% more citations and more top-5% "blockbuster" patents, enter green technology classes over 600,000 patent-sequence slots earlier and are roughly 80% more likely to produce pioneering patents, and accompany this with greater low-carbon product revenue and green hiring. Yet ESG rating providers systematically score them lower and penalize them further for each additional green patent, and environmentally focused funds underweight them, creating a disconnect between green innovation producers and where sustainable capital is directed.

**Theoretical Contribution**
The paper documents, for the first time at large scale, the landscape of U.S. green patenting and shows that incumbent "brown" firms — specifically traditional energy producers — are first-mover, high-quality, foundational green innovators, contradicting the implicit premise of ESG exclusion frameworks. It offers a competitive-dynamics / cannibalization mechanism from industrial organization under which private, profit-maximizing incentives can drive environmental innovation absent preferential capital allocation, and speaks to broader questions about harnessing market competition to achieve societal objectives. It also identifies gaps in ESG measurement: ratings are positively associated with green job postings on average, but this association does not hold for energy firms; low-carbon products and revenue are not significantly related to ESG scores.

**Practical Implication**
Evaluation frameworks should better account for firms' environmental innovation contributions, including green patenting, low-carbon products, and green hiring, rather than relying on sector-level exclusion or scoring focused on emissions and environmental penalties. A more balanced evaluation framework — one that recognizes both the current environmental footprint of traditional energy firms and their demonstrated role in producing technologies critical for the green transition — could improve capital allocation toward firms actually advancing decarbonization. The authors report that, following circulation of their paper, at least one major ESG rating provider (MSCI) introduced patent-based "Environmental Opportunities" indicators reflecting clean tech, green building, and renewable energy.

**Limitations**
The main firm-level analyses link USPTO patents to publicly traded Compustat firms; patent-quality tests also compare all patentors with public-firm subsamples. ESG score data only begin in 2008, limiting the ESG-specific regressions to 2008-2020. The authors also acknowledge that traditional energy firms remain substantial contributors to fossil-fuel extraction and emissions, so the findings do not imply that ESG investors should refrain from divestment — only that current evaluation frameworks may be incomplete.
